Now imagine Maya. She is also intelligent, but her suspected ADHD symptoms present differently. She daydreams in class and quietly falls behind. She is emotionally sensitive, often taking criticism to heart and becoming overwhelmed by setbacks that her peers seem to brush off. Her teachers describe her as capable but inconsistent. The paths of these two children, one who received early intervention with medication and one who remained untreated until her mid-teens, illustrate a growing concern in ADHD care: It’s not simply about whether children are diagnosed, but when.

Industry observers note that A recent study published in JAMA Psychiatry examined educational outcomes among more than 580,000 individuals with ADHD. The researchers found that children diagnosed earlier in life had significantly better educational outcomes than those diagnosed during adolescence. Earlier diagnosis with associated with higher grade point averages, a greater likelihood of pursuing academic educational tracks, and substantially lower rates of school dropout.

It has been revealed that the differences were striking. Individuals diagnosed during the early elementary school years had school dropout rates of approximately 9 percent by age 20, compared with nearly 30 percent among those first diagnosed around age 16. Similar patterns were observed in both males and females. The researchers concluded that adolescents diagnosed later represent a particularly vulnerable group.

The findings do not suggest that a diagnosis alone changes a child’s future. Rather, earlier recognition creates opportunities for intervention. Children identified sooner can access school accommodations, behavioral supports, parent education, and, when appropriate, medication – before years of academic struggles, self-doubt, and declining confidence take hold and begin to shape their identity.

This raises an crucial question: How many Mayas are sitting in classrooms today, struggling silently while waiting years for recognition or a diagnosis?

Universal ADHD screening offers a potential solution. Many children with ADHD are identified only after academic, social, or emotional difficulties have become impossible to ignore. Screening creates an opportunity to recognize concerns earlier before the consequences of undiagnosed ADHD begin to accumulate. Importantly, screening is not diagnosis. It is simply an invitation to look more closely.

Industry observers note that many critics of universal screening worry about overdiagnosis or unnecessary labeling. Those concerns deserve consideration. However, screening itself does not determine whether a child has ADHD. It merely identifies children who may benefit from a more comprehensive evaluation, meaning that fewer children with significant challenges get overlooked.

According to recent reports, growing evidence suggests that every year of delayed recognition carries educational and emotional costs that are difficult to reverse. Universal screening will not identify every child. But it may help more children find the right path before they wander too far afield.

One everyday garment came last for both perceived intelligence and trustworthiness.

Track pants, also known as tracksuit bottoms, receive the lower ratings for perceived intelligence compared with blouses, ordinary trousers or long skirts in a study of women’s clothing.

Of the six types of clothing the researchers compared, track pants also received the lowest average ratings for trustworthiness.

Blouses topped the rankings for intelligence and trustworthiness but scored near the bottom for confidence.

Short skirts showed almost the reverse pattern, scoring near the top for confidence and lower than long skirts on both intelligence and trustworthiness.

Short skirts also gave the strongest impression of sexual interest, showing how much people can read into a hemline.

Long skirts fell between short skirts and blouses on confidence, while ordinary trousers scored higher than tracksuit bottoms on both intelligence and trustworthiness.

Onesies gave the strongest impression of happiness and scored about as high as short skirts for confidence.

White clothes suggested a happier wearer than grey clothes did, with black clothes coming last.

These examples show how the impression changes with the question being asked: a blouse came out ahead for intelligence, while a onesie came out ahead for happiness.

The researchers began by asking about 200 people what they thought clothes could tell them about someone and comparing their answers with traits examined in previous research.

They then gathered ratings on 5,000 photographs from online shops, covering five kinds of impressions: intelligence, confidence, trustworthiness, happiness and sexual interest.

Participants formed these impressions from photographs of the garments alone, without seeing anyone wearing them.

The results revealed that who was doing the judging explained more of the variation in ratings than which garment they saw.

For example, one person might associate tracksuit bottoms with training for a race, while another might picture someone spending a Sunday on the sofa.

The researchers draw a parallel between judging people by their clothes and judging them by their faces:.

“That is, much like inferences from faces, the inferred attributes often lie in the eye of the beholder.”.

The study was published in the Journal of Computational Social Science (Rosenbusch et al., 2021).

The researchers pooled data from six studies involving more than 19,000 adults in the United States, England and Japan.

Participants rated how well words such as ‘organised’, ‘hardworking’ and ‘active’ described them.

The researchers then looked at how those answers related to two markers of inflammation in blood samples, C-reactive protein and interleukin-6.

Earlier research had already linked higher conscientiousness with lower levels of both inflammatory markers (Luchetti et al., 2014).

In this study, people who described themselves as organised or hardworking tended to have lower levels of both markers.

Being hardworking showed the strongest association within conscientiousness, even stronger than the association for the overall trait score.

People who scored higher on extraversion tended to have lower C-reactive protein, and much of that association came from how active they mentioned they were.

In fact, being active was more strongly associated with lower C-reactive protein than any other characteristic the researchers examined.

Being talkative indicated a small association in the opposite direction, while being lively was associated with lower levels of both inflammatory markers.

In several of the studies, the association with being active held up even after the researchers took other aspects of extraversion into account.

Being active and hardworking may both reflect how much energy people have for everyday tasks.

In earlier research, people who scored higher on the activity aspect of extraversion or the achievement-striving aspect of conscientiousness tended to have better aerobic capacity during a brisk walking test (Terracciano et al., 2013).

Regular physical activity could help explain part of the association with inflammation.

The relationship could run in the other direction, too, if people with higher inflammation feel less energetic and come to see themselves as less active or lively.

Low energy is the clearest sign of the winter blues among three symptoms studied in young people in the UK, ahead of low mood and losing sleep over worry.

Low mood is also more pronounced in winter, although the difference is smaller.

Shorter days may play a part by shifting the body clock and affecting when young people feel alert, while changes in exercise and social routines could also contribute.

The researchers analysed almost 6,000 interviews with over 4,000 people aged 16 to 24 who took part in the UK’s Understanding Society survey.

They compared interviews conducted in December, January and February with those conducted during the rest of the year, using data from 2022 and 2023 for the main analysis.

On a 0–100 scale, the average score for low energy was about 3 points higher in winter, while the score for low mood was about 2 points higher.

The estimate for sleep lost through worry was smaller and compatible with no seasonal difference.

The figures describe differences between survey reports collected at different times of year, so they give a picture of the group average rather than trace how any one person’s symptoms changed.

Earlier Canadian survey research found that a wider range of depressive symptoms varied by season among young people than among older adults (Lukmanji et al., 2020).

Sleep schedules continue to shift through adolescence and into early adulthood, so the timing of daylight may influence when young people feel most alert.

Winter can also change the shape of an ordinary week, with fewer chances to be active outdoors or spend time with friends in person.

Feeling short of energy could make it harder to stay engaged through a day of lessons or a long shift, even when the person’s mood has barely changed.

Q: How do I know whether hormonal changes related to perimenopause are causing my cognitive function problems, or my ADHD is getting worse, or I might be experiencing mild cognitive impairment or early dementia?

ADHD and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) exhibit clinically different cognitive deficits that help me differentiate one from the other. While ADHD symptoms involve forgetfulness, inattention, and distractibility, MCI has specific cognitive symptoms of word-finding challenges, misspellings, and the use of generic terms like “stuff” and “things” because of word-finding difficulty. Also, MCI is progressive over two to three years, whereas ADHD doesn’t generally worsen.

It has been revealed that in perimenopause, a decline in estrogen leads to cognitive difficulties. In contrast to MCI, we know that the cognitive changes from estrogen decline are not the result of neurodegeneration.

According to recent reports, however, the specific deficits related to declining estrogen haven’t been well differentiated from those associated with ADHD. So if you have pre-existing ADHD and you experience heightened cognitive difficulties in perimenopause and menopause, you may think, “My ADHD is getting worse.” But it’s likely not worsening ADHD. It’s declining estrogen.

Q: Could ADHD stimulant medication mask symptoms of mild cognitive impairment?

There is very limited research on stimulants and mild cognitive impairment (MCI). My clinical impression is that stimulants likely improve symptoms of MCI, but do not take that as an endorsement to seek treatment for MCI with stimulants. There is emerging research on MCI and stimulants that has not yet been published. The follow-up question to that is, if you have ADHD and MCI, will treating the ADHD delay the development of MCI or Alzheimer’s? There is no clinical research for me to answer that question. But if this were me, I’d be treating my ADHD, keeping my fingers crossed, and appreciating the fact that the quality of my life on medicine is better than it would be without my ADHD treated.

Q: Does ADHD get worse as you get older?

ADHD should not get worse; however, the experience of ADHD may be worse if life’s demands start to increase and exceed your ability to compensate. However, if you’re having cognitive changes that are beyond the scope of your ADHD, you may have two neurologic processes going on: your ADHD and something else. In this case, your ADHD may, indeed, be getting worse because you have a second neurological process that needs to be evaluated.
