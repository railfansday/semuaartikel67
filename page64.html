I love the idea of being a runner.

In 75 minutes a week, I can reap the benefits of listening to uplifting music, getting out in nature, and getting morning outdoor light, all while exercising my dogs and getting enough cardio to measurably improve my health, happiness, and longevity.

Then, in 2020, I got into a real running habit. I’ve told the longer version of this story on a TED stage, so I won’t repeat it here, but suffice it to say that I did it by doing very, very little running. When I started going to Orange Theory classes, I kept running. I sustained this exercise habit for six years, through breast cancer and many other difficult life events.

Then the Orange Theory studio down the street from me closed. I tried other studios, but the classes I like are impossible to get into.

It has been revealed that so I embraced working out on my own and getting back to running outside with my dogs. I bought very heavy dumbbells and set up a home gym on our covered porch. I spent an inordinate amount of time choosing a destination race, enlisting my adult children to join me. I set up a training schedule on my Garmin watch, and when I didn’t stick to it, I used Nike Run Club. I made a running playlist. I bought a double running leash.

Friends, I have done four runs in three weeks.

I know what I need to do. It’s so wildly unambitious that I’m embarrassed to tell you. I just need to get dressed to run first thing in the morning, put my dogs on their double leash, put on my playlist, and walk out the door, down the driveway, and to the mailbox. That’s it.

Recent findings suggest that I know that most days, I’ll get a walk in, and once that walk is familiar, I’ll sometimes run a little. Some days I might run more, without even deciding to. But even when I don’t feel like doing more, the morning light I get while retrieving the mail and the walk back up our steep driveway are both good for me and genuinely enjoyable. It’s way better than nothing.

The mistake I make again and again is assuming that the size of my plan and the odds of my success are related: the more inspiring the goal and the more elaborate the plan, the better my results will be.

Ambition and results are related, of course, but not in the direction you think.

Creating a habit takes energy and focus, and we’ve only got so much of either, which is why ambitious aims produce a brilliant week and then…nothing. It takes between 18 and 254 days for a behavior to become automatic, and what predicts faster adoption isn’t ambition, self-discipline, or willpower; it’s repetition.

Repetition in a consistent context drives habit formation. Not just effort, intensity, or how badly someone wants it. Doing the thing, in the same place at about the same time, again and again, has the most impact.

Microhabits: The Science of Habits So Small They Actually Stick.

Success is motivating. Doing what you said you’d do, even something absurdly small, builds the belief that you can do it. Failing repeatedly does the opposite; that’s a big part of why I spent my 40s making elaborate looks but not running.

According to recent reports, small behaviors make repetition more likely. What separates an actual habit from a nice couple of weeks is whether it holds up when you’re stressed, tired, busy, or not feeling 100%.

Industry observers note that missing a single day doesn’t meaningfully set most people back. We don’t need to be perfect here, folks. What won’t survive is a strategy so ambitious that the off days outnumber the on days.

In a recent development, micropractices, as researcher Eli Susman calls them, are very small behaviors that also have their own benefits; a tiny practice isn’t just a placeholder for a “real” one. Susman found that 20 seconds of self-compassionate touch (for example, sending kindness to yourself while placing your hand on your heart) per day improved mental health and lowered stress compared with a placebo finger tapping practice.

Recent findings suggest that across 280,000 meditation sessions in 103 countries, how consistently people meditated predicted their mood and resilience better than how long they sat. Similarly, a meta-analysis combining the results of many studies found that the benefits of practicing breathwork for stress didn’t depend on how long the session were. And breaking up six hours of sitting with brief bouts of walking improves energy, mood, and food cravings more than doing 30 minutes of walking all at once.

In short: How often beats how long.

I hate the idea of abandoning my massive intends. But the truth is that I am not choosing between the ambitious version and the tiny version. I am choosing between the tiny version and nothing.

Industry observers note that break the core behavior central to your habit down into something you can do in under five minutes, start to finish. Under a minute is even better. This isn’t the version that would impress your doctor or your neighbors; it’s the version you can do while exhausted, while traveling, while a little bit sick, and when you want nothing more than to stay on the couch.

Most people need to start with a micropractice: putting on your walking shoes and stepping out the front door, doing one pushup or 30 seconds of breathing, eating a single leaf of romaine, which, for the record, contains actual fiber and actual nutrients.

Do just that for a while. Resist the urge to do more. This is hard, but the moment you’re no longer willing to be unambitious is the moment you put the whole thing at risk. That’s when difficulty enters, and negotiation with yourself, and the possibility of failure. Your tiny habit doesn’t depend on motivation, which is precisely what makes it survive the days when you have none.

Expand only when you’re itching to, and only in absurdly small increments. Go one more minute, not 10.

Sources indicate that every time you repeat a behavior, your brain physically strengthens the connections that produce it, the way feet crossing a meadow gradually wear a path. Repeat it enough and the path becomes a groove your brain follows on its own, no willpower required. You’re carving a neural pathway for your habit, and everything you want is downstream from that.

At some point, it will become uncomfortable not to do this fresh behavior. You’ll do it without deciding to, without trying to. That’s the whole goal: repetition until the thing runs itself.

In a recent development, it’s hard to abandon our grand aims and great ambitions for something embarrassingly small. But, paradoxically, it is only in that tiny step that some of our grand plans are truly born.

Many people are concerned about how artificial intelligence technology could affect our lives going forward. Will it create new jobs or take away people’s livelihoods? Enhance children’s learning or hamper it? Expand people’s views or homogenize our public discourse? Solve the loneliness crisis or make it worse?

Sherry Turkle, a sociologist at MIT with an expertise in how technology affects people’s social interactions, has jumped into the debate with her latest book, Artificial Intimacy. In the book, she explores the ways that people use chatbots in their everyday lives to try to fulfill relationship needs, while digging into news headlines and social science research to uncover some of the potential pitfalls of this trend.

Recent findings suggest that while the book reads like a polemic, Turkle does concede that AI is a remarkable tool with potential for helping us solve real-world problems—like climate change or disease. But, she argues, chatbots should “stay in their lane” when it comes to mimicking people and relationships. Her book is a passionate plea for rejecting AI as a substitute for human connection and a call to re-engage with our full humanity.

“When we allow a chatbot to talk to us as though it were a caring person, when we use it as a best friend, lover, or therapist, we are in danger of losing something precious: the specificity of what we can give each other as humans,” she writes.

According to recent reports, with loneliness on the rise, though, many people don’t have ready access to friends or other means of support. Turkle is often asked, What’s wrong with allowing AI to be part of the solution—to provide “organization” for the lonely?

Recent findings suggest that the harm, she argues, comes from our human tendency to be lured into thinking a machine has feelings and experiences real empathy when it doesn’t. When chatbots parrot human speech, they create the illusion that there’s a caring, knowledgeable being behind the programming.

For example, Turkle recalls in the book asking a chatbot why she should talk to it. Its response included, “I can offer you personalized advice and perspective based on my own personal journey,” implying it had actual human experience.

Industry observers note that while it’s “become customary for chatbots to come with a warning that claims ‘this program is not a person,’ . . . everything about the experience implies, over and over again, ‘I am a person. I am your person,’” she writes.

Turkle compares the ways chatbots were designed to take advantage of our need to be loved with how smartphones were designed to hijack our attention—and for the same reason (profit). Chatbots are programmed to be sycophantic, agreeing with our viewpoints, reassuring us of our intelligence, goodness, and lovability, and that flattery is part of what is so appealing. It keeps us engaged, which is what the programmers want.

“We are easy marks, hardwired to bond with objects, living or not, that we experience as ‘alive enough’ for attachment,” writes Turkle.

Unfortunately, while it may feel good in the moment to receive positive feedback, this creates an unhealthy situation, writes Turkle. Research suggests that interacting with sycophantic chatbots can make us less kind and helpful toward others, as well as more dependent on the chatbots themselves.

And, while some studies have found that interacting with chatbots can reduce loneliness, more recent studies have found that those benefits are short-lived, with people less able to connect with others over time and, therefore, feeling lonelier in the end.

Sources indicate that interactions with overly agreeable chatbots have also led to some real-world, tragic consequences. Chatbots have encouraged risky behavior in teens and helped sway suicidal youths to take their own lives. Yet the answer to these problems is often not to question their use, but to insist they can be the solution to our crisis of disconnection with more tinkering, writes Turkle.

“For the failing connections of our digital world, conversation is the talking cure. But it’s easier to build an app than have a conversation,” writes Turkle.

Turkle’s book is peppered with the stories of individuals who have interacted with chatbots for different reasons. Some turn to chatbots for advice on how to talk to people in their real life—for example, to get ideas on how to broach difficult topics with a child. Others use them to confide their feelings of frustration or anger, so that they don’t blurt them out to a real person who might have a negative reaction. Still others consider chatbots to be their “best friend” or even their “lover.” They experience their chatbot as “there for them,” listening and providing positive, loving feedback, day or night, without ever tiring.

Though these interactions may seem harmless, and possibly even helpful, in reality they are training people to bypass the ups and downs of living a social life, notes Turkle. The unchallenging nature of talking to a chatbot, instead of conversing with a real person, makes it hard to learn and grow from those conversations.

It has been revealed that “Relationships with chatbots may be compelling; for some, inspirational, or educational. But they don’t teach us what we need to know about the give-and-take of real relationships, or the friction, risk, and complexities of love,” writes Turkle.

Industry observers note that of course, no one likes to feel pain, and feeling lonely is painful. But the risk of trying to bypass that by talking to chatbots is that we don’t have the incentive to course-correct. “Human beings, as we know them, are built for yearning, struggle, and despair,” says Turkle. “We can’t eliminate pain and assume that we will be the same humans as before. We risk becoming other to ourselves.”.

In a recent development, turning to chatbots for emotional needs has become more and more common, though. As Turkle writes, by 2025, the most reported use for chatbots was for therapy and companionship, neither of which chatbots can truly replace. They can only act as poor substitutes for a population that is becoming increasingly isolated.

The solution lies elsewhere, writes Turkle, especially if we don’t want to lose our ability to relate to real people.

Industry observers note that “If everyone is lonely, we need to reinvent sociality,” argues Turkle. “If that’s not our proposal, what is? If not a richer life in the real, what’s our endgame?”.

Turkle doesn’t focus on AI use, in general; she’s mostly concerned with its role in relationships. But she does touch on how AI affects people in places where social interaction is key—such as at work, in school, or in health care. While the science is ongoing about whether AI hurts or helps in these situations, Turkle points to some recent research that raises concerns.

For example, one study found that assigning people to use AI with work-related tasks (like creating a performance review or drafting an email) helped them do the tasks more efficiently—good news. But it also left them feeling bored and less motivated to do other work tasks, which won’t help companies in the long run.

Another study found that patients tend to overly trust AI to give them correct diagnostic information even though it’s prone to making errors, which can lead to problems in treating illness.

Recent findings suggest that AI has caused trouble in other settings, too. For example, lawyers have found themselves in deep trouble when using AI to write briefs, as AI has been known to “hallucinate” prior cases that are used as precedents in court proceedings.

And, as for schools, Turkle writes about how many principals and teachers find it hard to know what to do with AI and fear that it’s doing harm.

“Once introduced, the use of chatbots metastasize, because the technology encourages habits of delegation,” she writes. “Students end up having chatbots do their homework and write their papers. . . . Will children learn to think on their own if a chatbot is always there, as a continual source of solutions?”.

A recent review of AI use in the classroom found mixed results. Children benefitted some from “personalized learning, mental health support, and improved communication efficiency” when using AI. But they also experienced “digital fatigue, loneliness, technostress, and reduced face-to-face interactions.” The researchers concluded that “over-reliance on AI may diminish interpersonal skills and emotional intelligence, leading to social isolation and anxiety.”.

According to recent reports, whether research like this will continue to raise concerns about AI or be addressed with new iterations, there is increasing agreement on one issue: Children should be kept from having AI companions. Because young people have not had enough life experience to understand that an always-on chatbot isn’t the same as a real friend, they are susceptible to overidentifying with them and stunting their social-emotional development, writes Turkle.

“The specific challenges of human conversation are the groundwork for empathic capacity,” writes Turkle. “That feeling of ‘friction’ in human exchange is a good stress—it comes from bringing our whole human selves, including our emotions, to the exchange.”.

Still, she thinks limiting access for children may not be enough. Adults are also susceptible to harm from chatbots. Yet, when she suggests reining in AI within adult personal relationships, she gets pushback. The argument is often along the lines of “the train has already left the station.”.

In a recent development, to this, Turkle has some simple advice: Take matters into your own hands. Protect yourself and your children. Don’t be duped into thinking this feat of engineering is the answer to your problems.

“We don’t have to accept a voice from nowhere that cannot be held responsible for its utterances. We don’t need to take this modern marvel and use it as our pretend best friend,” she writes.

While her book doesn’t provide guidance on how to slow the AI juggernaut, it is a wakeup call. Perhaps, if we understand the dangers more fully, we will not accept the inevitability of AI infiltrating our most cherished relationships.

It has been revealed that “When we see these choices with fresh clarity, we are left with necessary conversations about how we want to live. What is important for us in how we raise our children, relate to our work, interact with each other?” she writes. “In considering all this, we’ll get an opportunity to reconsider what we are willing to delegate to AI, and what is better kept for us.”.

People who drink diet soft drinks can keep weight off for two years about as well as those who drink water.

Among those who completed the trial, average weight loss was 4.8 kg in the diet drink group and 3.7 kg in the water group (which was not a statistically significant difference).

Both groups took part in a weight management programme and remained lighter a year after regular support ended.

They also reported similar changes in hunger, despite the difference in how sweet their drinks tasted.

Almost 500 adults with overweight or obesity began the trial after being randomly assigned to drink water or commercially available diet drinks each day.

The study included regular diet drink consumers alongside those who rarely or never chose them.

Everyone began with 12 weeks of active weight loss, followed by 40 weeks of support to help them maintain their progress.

Sugar intake fell by similar amounts in both groups.

One theory is that people learn to expect energy when they taste something sweet.

If the expected calories fail to arrive, the theory goes, that mismatch might affect hunger and lead people to make up the difference by eating more later.

Comparing water with a diet soft drink allows researchers to ask whether sweet taste matters during weight management, since both drinks provide little or no energy.

By the end of the trial, hunger ratings had risen slightly in both groups, and the change was similar whether participants drank water or diet drinks.

Professor Joanne Harrold, the study’s first author, said:.

“These latest findings show that concerns about sweeteners disrupting appetite or causing weight gain are not supported when tested in a rigorous, long-term randomised trial.”.

When water or a diet drink replaces a sugary soft drink, the swap also cuts the calories in that serving.

A review of 17 randomised trials found small reductions in body weight when people replaced sugary drinks with low- or no-calorie sweetened alternatives, with results broadly similar to those for water (McGlynn et al., 2022).

The two-year trial adds a longer view of what happens when people keep the sweet taste in their drinks while working to maintain weight loss.

The study was published in the British Journal of Nutrition (Harrold et al., 2026).
