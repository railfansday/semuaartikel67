A 3,300Km Walk Across Japan in Winter

Japanese publisher Katsunari Tanaka has set himself the challenge of hauling a handcart 3,345km from Japan’s northernmost tip to the southern edge of Okinawa in winter.

Recent findings suggest that tanaka, 49, set off from Cape Sōya in Hokkaido on October 7 and is heading south to Cape Hedo in Okinawa. He hopes to complete the traverse in 100 days over five months. As he will have to contend with some challenging winter conditions along the way, he has described it as “playing chase with winter.”.

Rather than carrying everything on his back, he is pulling a handcart that weighs around 100kg. He also has a support team with a vehicle in case of emergencies. At some points, different members of his team have walked with him for a day or so. His daily target is around 35km, with rest days and organized stops built into the schedule.

In a recent development, the first few weeks have already brought strong headwinds, rain, and some steep climbs, making progress difficult at times. Temperatures have dropped close to freezing. At the end of October, he crossed a mountain pass between Yoichi and Iwanai. There, headwinds stirred up the gravel as he walked. Then the snow began. In the end, he took refuge with his support team until the weather improved.

It has been revealed that despite the conditions, Tanaka reached Hakodate Harbor on November 3, having covered 621km across Hokkaido. From Hakodate, he is crossing the Tsugaru Strait by ferry to Aomori, then will continue south along the Sanriku coast to Sendai.

It has been revealed that from there, he follows the Pacific coast through Fukushima to Tokyo before joining the Tokaido route toward Osaka before heading west to Kyushu. A second ferry crossing brings him to Okinawa, where he will walk to his final destination, Cape Hedo.

Along a road in Hokkaido. Photo: Katsunari Tanaka/Instagram.

This is not Tanaka’s first experience of traveling Japan with a handcart. Around a decade ago, he spent three years visiting all 47 prefectures, selling books directly to people he met along the way. Loading his publications onto a cart, he covered the country between 2013 and 2016, eventually selling 13,477 books. At the time, many people questioned what he was doing and whether books could really be sold on the street. But he indicates the experience shaped his approach to business and his belief in the power of personal connections.

Tanaka insists that he is not an adventurer. Instead, he is traveling the country as a “merchant.” This time, he is making the journey to support his mentor, Arthur Hollands, a Japanese evangelist known for undertaking lengthy walks while carrying a large wooden cross. Along the route, Tanaka looks to stop at 11 locations to give lectures. He welcomes people to join him for sections of the walk.
