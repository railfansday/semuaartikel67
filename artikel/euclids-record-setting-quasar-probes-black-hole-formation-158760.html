Quasars are the cosmos' most luminous objects, generated by supermassive black holes (SMBH) that are accreting enormous amounts of matter. Astronomers keep finding these massive objects further and further back in time. The ESA's Euclid just found the newest record holder: the telescope observed it 40 million years sooner than the previous record-holder, and it was found at redshift z=8.13, corresponding to about 630 million years after the Big Bang. That's firmly in the Epoch of Reionization.

The quasar's technical name is J1504+6252, but it's been given the more recognizable name DÃºn Aonghasa. DÃºn Aonghasa is an ancient stone fort on top of a cliff in Ireland, where it looks out over the Atlantic Ocean.

The discovery is in research published in Astronomy and Astrophysics titled "Euclid: Discovery of a quasar at z = 8.13 hosting a 360-million-solar-mass black hole." The lead author is Julien Wolf, who was a postdoc at the Max Planck Institute for Astronomy when the research was done.

It has been revealed that one of the hallmarks of modern astronomy is the discovery of massive objects in the Universe that formed much sooner than astronomers thought they could. The JWST is driving much of this.

"Quasars powered by rapid accretion onto supermassive black holes (SMBHs) have been uncovered deep into the first gigayear of cosmic history," the authors write.

Recent findings suggest that now the Euclid Space Telescope is contributing to that trend. Euclid was rolled out to study dark energy and the expansion of the Universe. A big part of that job is measuring galaxies at different redshifts in its Euclid Wide Survey, to see how their morphologies are different over time. That will reveal how dark energy has changed over time.

DÃºn Aonghasa and its high redshift brethren pose the same familiar question: how did they get so large so quickly?

"Assembling a black hole of several hundred million solar masses within the first few hundred million years, despite its at the moment sub-Eddington accretion rate, requires either a massive seed or sustained, highly efficient early growth," the authors write.

While an actual answer to their growth is elusive, finding them helps constrain an eventual answer.

Aside from their puzzling masses, these quasars also open different windows or probes into aspects of the early Universe. They give astronomers long sightlines into the intergalactic medium, letting them study its opacity during reionization, and its metal enrichment.

The quasars also serve as probes of how matter was distributed. "Quasars further mark rare, highly biased peaks in the underlying matter distribution and therefore trace the densest regions of the early large-scale structure," the researchers write.

Sources indicate that on galactic scales, these high-redshift quasars are probes of how SMBH masses and the masses of their host galaxies co-evolved. Both masses tend to correlate, but there's no obvious reason why. AGN feedback could play a role, so could mergers, or a shared source of fuel for growth. But there's no certainty, so finding more high-redshift quasars may lead to an answer.

These things are all related, but the main result of finding these quasars is facing the questions posed by their high masses.

It has been revealed that finding these quasars and supermassive black holes is forcing astronomers to consider different explanations for how they grew so massive so quickly. One explanation is super-Eddington accretion, but DÃºn Aonghasa isn't accreting matter that rapidly. Another is massive seeds, but current formation models struggle to accommodate those. Another explanation is that quasars experienced super-Eddington accretion very early in the Universe, under very different conditions.

Heavy seeds could explain these quasars, but what could they be? Astrophysicists don't know, but some candidates are Direct-Collapse Black Holes, or maybe sequential runaway stellar collisions in extremely dense star clusters.

Another, more exotic and theoretical possibility for heavy seeds is primordial black holes (PBH). These are theorized to have formed in the very first moments in the Universe, when dense pockets of gas collapsed directly into black holes. With such an early head start, these PBH could've formed into heavy seeds without needing physics-breaking levels of accretion.

Primordial black holes are only hypothetical. If they're real, they could've formed by direct collapse of dense pockets of matter shortly after the Significant Bang.
