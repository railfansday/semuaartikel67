Latest Update: This Breezy Los Angeles House Has “a Little Funk to It”

Recent findings suggest that above: The living room features a custom red marble coffee table, sofa, and side table by Mike Moser Studio. The wicker chairs are from Matahari; the vintage mirror is from A. Tyner Antiques, and the carpet is from Lawrence of La Brea.

When designer Mike Moser first saw the Santa Monica house, it hadn’t been touched since the ’90s, and no one had lived in it for over a decade. He knew it would require a full gut renovation. But Moser saw that as an opportunity for his clients—UTA’s chief communications officer Cassandra Bujarski, media executive Jason Bergsman, and their daughter—to get exactly the house they wanted. A place that’s at once a little bit Spanish, a tad preppy, and very Californian.

It has been revealed that moser, a Los Angeles-based interior designer who opened his eponymous firm in 2018, met the clients through their real estate broker. The agent, a friend of Moser’s, called him in while the couple was in escrow, asking him to help them imagine what this crumbling house could become with the right eye and guiding hand. They ended up moving forward with the house and hiring Moser to lead the renovation.

According to recent reports, bujarski’s parents were antique and art collectors and she had a vision for the home as a place that would honor that past and help her pass that legacy down to her own child. “Whatever we do,” Moser remembers her saying, “I want it to really feel collected and eclectic.” Other than that guideline, Bujarski and Bergsman gave Moser free rein—almost.

It has been revealed that “That was really her only directive,” he says. “She was like, just have fun and play and be colorful and try latest things.”.

In a recent development, moser leaned fully into this eclecticism, pulling references that span eras, regions, and styles. He cites 1920s and ’30s Los Angeles houses, Mexican architect Luis Barragán, Milan’s Villa Necchi, and hacienda style all as influences in the house’s design. In the dressing room, Moser added a “menswear, haberdashery, Ralph Lauren feeling,” he claims—fitting, as Moser once worked for the brand. He calls this potpourri of inspiration an “amalgamation”: “It’s easy breezy California and we wanted to add a little funk to it.”.

According to recent reports, los Angeles is the place to mix and match styles. It’s a city where modern mansions share blocks with Tudors and Craftsman; Spanish Colonials bump up against French Normandy houses. “The beauty of California, especially L.A. architecture, is it's a melting pot,” Moser says. “It's all really about these weird artists and creatives back in the ’30s, ’40s, ’50s that just were marching to the beat of their own drum. So I was like, let's do the same.”.

It has been revealed that moser and his team gutted the Spanish-style house and stripped it down to the studs. They had to modernize all the internal systems, bring in new steel and windows, open up the layout to create more “breathing room,” as Moser calls it, and replace the laughing plaster walls. Despite the major work required to make the house livable, Moser still wanted to honor what it used to be. He added Saltillo replica floors and was able to keep the original wooden beams in the living room. In the dining room, he added new wooden beams, calling back to those in the living area, but had artist Nicke Valle paint them with swirling yellow and red spirals.

In a recent development, if the structural elements pay homage to the space’s origins, the furniture is tailored precisely to its latest residents. Moser needed to find stylish places to store Bujarski’s collections. “I had an itch to scratch with the casegoods on this project. I was really obsessed with armoires,” the designer notes.

There’s a baroque cabinet from Giannetti Home in the entryway, an antique credenza in the dining room, and two vintage Deco Swedish dressers that act as nightstands. “Each one of these cabinets got to be a curio,” Moser indicates. The dining sideboard houses her china; a vintage Swedish armoire in the family room holds her mother’s Murano candlesticks. In the dining room, the dishware mounted on the wall is vintage Majolica, all with a seafood theme, that Moser and Bujarski hunted down—at auction, on eBay, in antiques stores—in the six months before the install. Throughout the home, “there’s a sense of reveal,” Moser notes.

“I always want to be a chameleon,” Moser adds. “I live as a joyful person. The first thing I say to a client when I take on a project is ‘If this isn't fun, we're doing it wrong.’”.
