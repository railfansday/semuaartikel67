Weight Gain During Long-Term Antipsychotic Use

Industry observers note that most antipsychotics cause some degree of weight gain. They are generally classified into three categories according to the degree of weight gain each causes. Weight gain caused by antipsychotics can be a deterrent to using them, or a motivation to go off antipsychotics prematurely. For someone like me who has been on antipsychotics for 26 years, I have experienced a lifetime of managing weight fluctuation, gaining a good deal of weight and then losing it a few times. Here is an account of my journey of weight gain and loss while taking antipsychotics and what I have gleaned from these experiences.

My first antipsychotic was Zyprexa, which I first started taking in 2001. I was not informed by my psychiatrist at that time that this drug causes weight gain, and I was on a higher dose. Accordingly, I had no idea I was taking the antipsychotic notorious for causing the most weight gain, and the psychiatrist never shared with me the fact that the medicine could be a factor in my weight gain.

Sources indicate that I was already so hard on myself for taking an antipsychotic in the first place, and the weight gain, which I blamed on myself, was another source of shame. I couldn’t understand why I was gaining weight when nothing had changed with how much I eat and how much I was exercising, so I felt like my life was out of control in multiple ways. It felt similar to having some sort of mental illness but not really understanding what I was up against or what I could do about it.

Sources indicate that it’s so easy to be hard on yourself about weight gain, and it’s also so incredibly hard to properly attribute your medicine for causing it. Because of stigma, we are taught that mental illness should be under our control, just like society tells us that weight gain is really always under our control as well. Taking an antipsychotic can tug at your self-esteem, and weight gain just intensifies any tendency for low self-esteem and self-blame.

Sources indicate that I was on Abilify and experienced almost no weight gain with it, so I was happy. However, I suddenly started having involuntary tongue movements with this drug after it had worked well for me for years, so I had no choice but to go back on Zyprexa, just a month or two before I started trying on wedding dresses after my engagement. As someone who was anorexic in college and still body dysmorphic, gaining a bunch of weight right before trying on wedding dresses, when most people try to be their thinnest, seemed so unfair. I took it as a sign that I would never be able to move on with my life.

I was thankful that my psychiatrist put me on a high dose of Metformin to help manage weight gain caused by Zyprexa. The Metformin felt to me like validation and acknowledgement that, yes, it’s not all my fault if I gain weight on this drug and that there are real, demonstrated ways that psychiatry acknowledges and helps us with this issue. Even with Metformin, I put on 15 pounds, but I successfully worked hard to lose it all before it was time for dress alterations. But it did feel like an unnecessary hurdle and annoying complication, a reminder that my illness is never over.

I’ve gained and lost so many pounds through antipsychotic use. The weight creeps in until one day I’m forced to address it, then I go through an organized effort to lose it. Growing up, I was a competitive athlete who thought weighing 140 pounds was a lot, but for most of my adult life on antipsychotics, weighing 140 is no longer achievable, or if so, unsustainable.

I’m convinced the reason I gained over 60 pounds while pregnant with my child was due to being on Zyprexa at the time. However, I did lose the weight through walking, calorie reduction, and nursing. With the help of Metformin, I did not get gestational diabetes. I’m now on Quetiapine, and it is supposed to be a moderate weight gainer, but I don’t think I have much of an easier time keeping weight off with this drug than with Zyprexa. However, since Quetiapine is perfect for avoiding a terrible side effect I get with every other drug, there is no way I will ever take any other drug again. This time, when weight loss was called for at age 47, I needed to go on a GLP to lose it.

According to recent reports, one thing I can say about this 26-year journey of weight gain and dieting since I've been on antipsychotics is that I am healthier for it and make healthier choices. Because I have less control over my metabolism, I have made other critical health choices that will pay off in the long run in multiple ways. Giving up gluten is a substantial deal for me, especially with thyroid diseases, as well as giving up almost all soda and alcohol. I’ve also been a vegetarian for 20 years, for a number of reasons in addition to health. Some of these choices started as ways I could mitigate weight gain caused by antipsychotics, but they turned out to have benefited me in other very real ways.

In a recent development, another huge shift for me is caring more about my health than fixating on the number on the scale. I’m no longer concerned with going back to 140—I’m more interested in longevity and keeping up with my 7-year-old son. Through my cycle of weight gain and loss, I’ve given up on the idea that success is a clothing size; it’s about my bloodwork all looking good, reducing inflammation, feeling energetic, and exercising regularly.

In a recent development, one of the toughest aspects of serious mental illness is the elusiveness. You can’t visualize it; there is no blood test, and you may get re-diagnosed by different doctors. Sometimes there is no clear answer on what to do at all. You can’t control what effects antipsychotics have on you, and sometimes discerning what is under your control and what is being dictated by the antipsychotic is difficult. Weight gain caused by antipsychotics can feel the same way, leaving you feeling powerless and frustrated.

Industry observers note that it’s hard and seems unfair that making the right and responsible choice for your mental health can have other health consequences. But staying on antipsychotics, even if they cause weight gain, has been critical for my full remission from schizophrenia for 15 years and for becoming a wife and mother. Unpredictable weight gain has also led me to make key health decisions for myself when it comes to food and drink that will benefit me for the rest of my life. I’ve also made other good health choices that I probably would not have made if I did not have a tendency to gain weight due to antipsychotics. I’ve also learned to lean on internal means of validation and self-esteem instead of superficial, external means.
