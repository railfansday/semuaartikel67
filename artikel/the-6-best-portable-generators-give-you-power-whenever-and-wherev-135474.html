Recent findings suggest that when you’re shopping for a portable generator, there are a few crucial things to consider, including power, weight, and fuel type. For portable power, models that output 2,000 to 5,000 watts will generally be simple enough to lift in and out of a truck, though some models that generate more are designed to be lightweight. Anything over 100 pounds starts to buck the idea of something being portable, even if it has wheels.

Fuel type matters, too. All generators run on gas, but dual-fuel models can also run on propane. The drawback is that propane delivers slightly less power output and shorter running time, but it runs a lot cleaner and is much less messy to deal with.

It has been revealed that if you’re powering sensitive electronics like digital equipment or TVs, you’ll also want to look for an inverter generator, which delivers more stable “cleaner” power that won’t damage your gear. They also run quieter than standard models. For these reasons, all of the picks on our list are inverter generators.

In a recent development, GENERATOR SAFETYWhile not hard to run, generators present dangers, so you need to follow basic safety rules. Never operate them in a closed space, like a garage or shed. Exhaust gases contain carbon monoxide (CO), a lethal, odorless, colorless gas. Most manufacturers offer models that shut down when they detect carbon monoxide building up. Do not run a generator in a garage with the door open, or in front of the garage with the door open, because carbon monoxide can still get trapped and seep into the house. Run generators at least 20 feet away, including from windows and doors, with the exhaust blowing away from the house.

It has been revealed that these generators, sometimes called backup generators, are used to provide temporary power when and where it is needed. Portable is a relative term; some are more portable than others. While the smallest models can be picked up and carried, most have wheels and a handle to make transport easier. However, at 100 pounds or more, picking them up to load or move may take two people. Appliances, power tools, or other devices can be plugged directly into standard outlets on the generator’s front panel.

Recent findings suggest that additionally, many models have a twist-lock plug that can provide up to 240 volts and be used to power circuits in a home via a manual transfer switch. You may hear generators like these referred to as “open frame” generators, which simply means that the engine, fuel tank, and generator components are not enclosed in bodywork.

Though inverter-type generators are—like their standard cousins—typically portable, we group them separately because they’re significantly and technically different in terms of how they work.

Like most generators, inverter generators provide 120/240 volts of AC power. They generate AC current just like the others, but it’s then converted to DC (Direct Current), and then inverted back to AC. The conversion and inversion is controlled by circuitry which acts like a filter, flattens surges, and cleans up the sine wave (or oscillating wave) of the electrical current.

In a recent development, typical generators have varying degrees of distortion in the sine wave of the alternating current. This isn’t usually an issue for most electrical devices, though sensitive electronics like tablets, laptops, televisions, and other smart devices can be damaged by current distortion or surges. These devices will last longer with “clean” power and steady voltage. Because of that added complication, inverter generators can be significantly more expensive.

Recent findings suggest that standby generators are permanently installed and connected to a home. These may power selected, critical circuits during a power outage, or may provide power for the whole home. Standby generators have systems that monitor power supplied by a utility and start automatically in the event of an outage. Since they’re not portable or easily moved, we didn’t include any here.

It’s vital to know what you intend to power with a generator in order to choose the right size. Manufacturers list two figures for generators: starting watts and running watts. Starting watts include the initial surge required to start appliances or tools with powerful electric motors or compressors. Once started, these devices require far less power to run continuously.

According to recent reports, many manufacturers have a chart with estimated power requirements for common appliances and tools to help you figure out what you need. For a more precise measurement, you can also calculate the figure yourself by adding up the power consumption of specific appliances and adding them together.

To calculate watts, multiply voltage (usually 120 volts) by the amps (amperage) required to run the appliance (usually found on a tag attached to the appliance). Do this for each item that the generator will power.

In a recent development, most portable generators are powered by gasoline, the most commonly available fuel. However, when using generators for emergency power, their infrequent use presents some concerns to consider.

It has been revealed that the first is that when big, catastrophic storms hit and power outages last for days, gas stations may struggle to meet demand, resulting in long lines. The second concern has to do with the infrequent use of backup generators, which may sit for months to years between uses.

Sources indicate that gasoline sitting in a carburetor will slowly evaporate over time, leaving gummy deposits that can block fuel from getting through, making it difficult or even impossible to start your generator without servicing. You can avoid this by closing the fuel valve and letting the generator run until it stalls, using up the remaining fuel in the carburetor. Also, over time, gasoline can oxidize or become “stale,” losing combustibility. It may also absorb water, which can then corrode fuel tanks and other metal components in the fuel system. Starting a generator regularly, as well as treating fuel with a stabilizer, can help fight these problems.

Ethanol-free gasoline, available at some gas stations, is also less susceptible to degradation over time than standard 87-octane pump gas; however, it’s still a good idea to mix the fuel going into your generator with stabilizer.

Recent findings suggest that dual-fuel generators can run on gasoline or liquified petroleum gas (LPG), commonly known as propane. Switching between the fuels is simple, and you won’t be tied to the availability of one type of fuel.

Industry observers note that A substantial advantage of propane is that it’s very stable when stored for long periods of time. It won’t go stale, and it won’t gum up carburetors from sitting. Plus, some homes that use propane for other purposes may already have large storage tanks on the premises, foregoing the need to make repeated trips out for fuel.

The downside to propane is that it creates fewer BTUs (British Thermal Units, a measurement of energy) than the same amount of gasoline. So, a generator running on propane will have slightly reduced starting and running watts. On the plus side, though, it will also run a little quieter than those that run on gasoline.

It has been revealed that while not as common, tri-fuel generators add an option to connect by natural gas. Some propane-powered generators can be converted to run on natural gas, as well. Like propane, natural gas can run cleaner and a little quieter. However, it also creates fewer BTUs than gasoline and propane, so it will also produce reduced starting and running watts.

Natural gas also requires a dedicated line for the generator, which most homes that have access to natural gas will not have, and thus will need to be professionally installed.

Recent findings suggest that if you’re powering appliances, tools, or other devices by plugging them directly into the standard outlets on the generator, the answer is no. If the generator is being used to power circuits in your home, maybe. It’s always best to consult the operator’s manual, a licensed electrician, or your local building officials about township ordinances—some localities may have requirements that differ from the National Electrical Code (NEC).

Electrical circuits need to be grounded to operate devices safely, so that any current that shorts out or has a fault is directed to the ground, i.e., the actual, literal earth, so the user doesn’t become the conduit to “ground.”.

There are two ways that generators are grounded: neutral-bonded and floating neutral. Neutral-bonded means the neutral wire is connected (i.e., bonded) to the generator frame. With a floating neutral, the neutral is not connected to the frame. In the latter case, the generator should be grounded by connecting the ground terminal to a grounding rod driven into the ground.

Industry observers note that generators with ground-fault circuit interrupter (GFCI) outlets, when used to power circuits in your home, require neutral switching and a manual transfer switch, and must be grounded by connecting the ground terminal to a ground rod driven into the soil. Neutral-switching transfer switches are also referred to as three-pole transfer switches.

In a recent development, there could be numerous reasons for difficulty starting a generator. The first things to check are whether or not there's any fuel in the tank, and if the fuel valve is turned open. Then, check for any obstructions in the air cleaner box. If that’s clean, move on to check that the engine has spark.

If your generator has sat for a long time without treated fuel, the carburetor may be clogged up and require cleaning—if you’re unfamiliar with any of these steps, it may be best to take it to a shop for service.

In an emergency, use what you can get, but as a general rule, 87 octane fuel will be fine—just don’t go lower than that.

Industry observers note that as long as we’re talking fuel, it’s always best to use ethanol-free fuel in equipment with small gas engines—if that’s not possible, nothing more than 10-percent ethanol. Ethanol can attract moisture, which will then separate and settle in the gas tank or carburetor, where it can corrode and clog the fuel system. For this reason, it’s also a good idea to treat gas with a fuel stabilizer.

Industry observers note that much like your lawn mower, generators need regular maintenance. However, since they often see less use, maintenance intervals tend to be farther apart. Typical maintenance would include changing oil, checking the air filter and changing it if needed, changing the fuel filter if so equipped, and checking the spark plug.

The best source of specific maintenance and troubleshooting information for your generator is the owner’s manual.
