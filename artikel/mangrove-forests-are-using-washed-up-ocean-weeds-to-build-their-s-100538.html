Mangrove forests are using washed up ocean weeds to build their soil and fight rising sea levels

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that coastal mangrove forests are critical for protecting shorelines from severe storms and trapping CO2. Unfortunately, they are losing the race against rising sea levels.

In a recent development, mangrove forests grow right at the edge of the sea, so rising water can drown them. To survive, the ground under the trees has to build up as fast as the ocean rises. A latest study finds that dead seagrass helps that happen.

Before sunrise on his monthly survey days, from May 2022 to May 2024, Florida International University (FIU) doctoral student Kevin Montenegro loaded a 16-foot (4.9-meter) canoe onto a truck and set off to paddle for an hour to the Biscayne Bay mangroves.

Sources indicate that he then waded in to see what the tide had left. Much of it was dead seagrass and macroalgae, the debris that scientists call “marine wrack.”.

When seagrass washes into a mangrove forest, it adds material to the forest floor and traps carbon that would otherwise warm the climate.

An isotope model estimated that seagrass supplied between 36 and 53 percent of the soil carbon near the surface at his two sites, according to FIU. It has not closed the gap, because the sea there is rising at least twice as fast as the ground.

Montenegro and a field partner worked at two sites in Biscayne National Park, on the Atlantic coast of Florida. The paper calls them BISC-1 and BISC-2. They lie about 5 miles (8 kilometers) apart, in a narrow band of forest along the shore.

BISC-1, the southern site, sits just south of the mouth of the Homestead Military Canal and east of the L-31E levee, built in 1966.

BISC-2 sits near the mouth of the Black Creek Canal, in the Cutler Wetlands, where roads and urban development run beside the shore.

Levees and roads stop these mangroves from moving inland, and little river sediment reaches them.

The National Park Service set up both monitoring stations with a surface elevation table, an instrument that records small changes in ground height from a fixed benchmark.

The readings run from February 2011 at BISC-1 and January 2012 at BISC-2 through September 2024. BISC-1 has 25 rounds of measurements and BISC-2 has 23.

Sea level in the bay is rising about 8.5 millimeters (0.33 inch) a year, based on data from the NOAA tide gauge at Virginia Key.

The ground at BISC-1 rose about 2.1 millimeters (0.08 inch) a year, and the ground at BISC-2 rose about 3.9 millimeters (0.15 inch).

That left BISC-1 about 6.4 millimeters (0.25 inch) short each year. Below the surface, the land at BISC-1 sank 0.7 millimeters a year and the land at BISC-2 sank 0.4.

In a recent development, hurricane Irma hit the area in 2017. Elevation at both sites had climbed sharply until 2014, declined from 2014 to 2019, and has risen steadily since 2018, possibly from fresh plant growth and sediment moved by the storm, the authors wrote.

In a recent development, yearly gains are 1.1 millimeters higher at BISC-1 and 2.2 higher at BISC-2 than in the last regional synthesis. Other research has warned that Florida’s mangroves may end up underwater as seas climb.

It has been revealed that montenegro made 23 monthly wrack surveys between May 2022 and May 2024. He and his partner marked three lines at each site, each one 66 feet (20 meters) long, and stopped at the shoreline, at 33 feet and at 66 feet.

At every stop they laid down a frame of about 11 square feet (1 square meter), wrote down how much seagrass, macroalgae, plastic, wood and mangrove litter lay inside it, and measured the pile with a meter stick. Then they photographed it.

It has been revealed that researchers usually study wrack on beaches, FIU said, since they can walk up to it or see it from satellites. Neither works well under a mangrove canopy.

For the decomposition test, the team filled nylon bags with about 5 grams of red mangrove leaves or the seagrass Thalassia testudinum.

They left the bags on the soil and collected them after six and 12 months, then repeated the experiment for a second year.

According to recent reports, the surveys also caught a regional event. Sargassum, a brown macroalga, reached its highest level on the plots in March 2023, the same month the University of South Florida‘s satellite system reported a peak across the region.

It has been revealed that south Florida’s mangroves are generally thought to build soil from their own leaves, wood and roots, according to FIU. The team pulled soil cores 4 inches (10 centimeters) deep during four sampling rounds and removed the roots where possible.

They then measured stable isotopes, forms of carbon and nitrogen that differ between plant sources, and ran a statistical model that compared the soil with red mangrove and seagrass tissue.

In the top 0.8 inch (2 centimeters), the seagrass share ranged from 36.1 percent at the innermost point of BISC-1 to 53 percent at the same point of BISC-2.

BISC-2, in the Cutler Wetlands, received more than 3.4 times as much wrack as BISC-1 at 66 feet inland, and its ground rose fastest. The seagrass share fell lower down the cores, likely because roots add more material at depth.

Where more wrack piled up, seagrass in the bags broke down more slowly at both sites, and mangrove leaves did the same at BISC-2.

Past measurements at the stations recorded single wrack deposits adding between 0.07 and 1.8 inches (1.78 and 46.38 millimeters) to the surface.

According to recent reports, montenegro knew both habitats before the project began. As an FIU undergraduate he worked in a seagrass lab that studied water quality damage, and he later measured mangrove elevation with the National Park Service.

“We know seagrass ecosystems are very sensitive to water quality,” he said in the FIU release. Losing those meadows, he added, would mean “losing a huge contributor to mangrove resilience.”.

The bay is one where climate stress has already changed the water. Mangroves worldwide also face a triple threat to their survival, and rising seas may push some forests to release stored carbon.

In a recent development, the authors wrote that management and restoration efforts should consider strategies that protect the route that carries wrack from seagrass beds into the forests. Montenegro called mangroves and seagrass “puzzle pieces that make our community whole.”.

The study covers two sites in one national park, and the elevation rates carry wide error margins, plus or minus 2.6 millimeters a year at BISC-1 and 3.2 at BISC-2.

Seasonal wrack volume tracked surface elevation change at both sites, but it revealed no clear relationship with the soil accumulation measured above marker layers.

The authors wrote that wrack volume alone was not driving those patterns, and proposed slower decay and added peat as the route.

Their model also used only two sources, red mangrove and Thalassia testudinum, so it did not separate the macroalgae found on the plots.

Water depth has climbed sharply at both sites since 2019, and the authors warned that longer flooding could eventually starve roots of oxygen.

The National Park Service funded the work under agreement P21AC11346-00, and two of the four authors, Kevin Whelan and Michelle Prats, work for its South Florida and Caribbean monitoring network.

Recent findings suggest that montenegro finished his Ph.D. earlier this year and now works in the Department of Environmental Resources Management for Miami-Dade County, on the same coast where he once loaded a canoe before sunrise.

The full study was published in the journal Estuaries and Coasts.
