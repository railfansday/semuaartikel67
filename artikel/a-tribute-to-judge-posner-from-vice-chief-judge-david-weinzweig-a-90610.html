I thought I'd pass along this tribute, which I thought was moving and apt:.

Dinner with Judge Richard Posner.

Twenty years ago, I was elevated to chair the antitrust section of the State Bar of Arizona, and I needed a headliner to pack the room for my first event. Who in the world of antitrust could draw interest outside that world? The answer was obvious: the closest thing antitrust has to a rock star, Judge Richard Posner.

Sources indicate that so I cold-emailed Judge Posner an invitation to address judges, lawyers and law students in Tempe. I doubted he would reply, but he did, swiftly. He would be happy to come—under one condition. I told him to name it.

Recent findings suggest that no antitrust law. Anything but antitrust law.

I agreed, of course. He could have lectured on gardening for all I cared.

We all have our favorite jurists, past and present. Until last week, Judge Posner was my favorite living one. He died at 87. His writing is the reason I became an antitrust lawyer.

In a recent development, thirty years in the law, and I still turn to one trusted source when an problem is opaque or abstract. I run a quick Westlaw search for Judge Posner's take, and the fog lifts.

It has been revealed that judge Posner wrote more than 3,300 judicial opinions, and he wrote them to be understood. He embodied what Judge Learned Hand preached nearly a century ago: "The language of law must not be foreign to the ears of those who are to obey it." Posner wrote for the people.

He turned abstract ideas into concrete images. Consider his advice to lawyers who ignored the law that cut against them: "The ostrich is a noble animal, but not a proper model for an appellate advocate." I'm not endorsing the tone, but the point slugs you in the jaw.

Posner made hard things look easy, and he worked hard at it. He confessed to going through up to 20 drafts to give his opinions "a casual, colloquial, and spontaneous appearance." As he put it, "I admit to hypocrisy in working so hard to give the appearance of effortlessness!".

Judge Posner kept his promise and came to Arizona to speak on judicial independence, not antitrust. That night I hosted him for dinner at El Chorro, nestled at the base of Camelback and Mummy mountains.

It has been revealed that I was scared. What would I say to Richard Posner for two hours? Convinced I could not carry the conversation alone, I recruited reinforcements, including the Chief Justice of the Arizona Supreme Court and a neurosurgeon.

Sources indicate that I opened with a safe question. Had anyone seen any good movies lately?

Posner went first. The last movie he had seen in a theater was Superman. In 1978. Of course. Of course that was the last movie he saw. He did not have time for those trifling pursuits.

According to recent reports, but then he leaned in. He and his wife had recently bought the first three seasons of 24 on DVD. Each episode, he marveled, captured one hour in a single day in the life of the protagonist, a man named Jack Bauer. A clock even appeared in the lower left-hand corner of the screen so viewers could watch the minutes tick by in real time. Posner narrated his favorite scenes as Bauer landed in one jam after another and always found a way out.

I have told that story to my law clerks for years. Posner described 24 with the passion and precision he brought to his opinions.

Industry observers note that posner was an independent thinker who questioned what everyone else assumed, including his own views. He was a founder of the law and economics movement, building much of it with his longtime friend and collaborator, the economist William Landes.

Posner moved antitrust from the boardroom to the checkout line. He asked whether the alleged anticompetitive conduct created efficiencies and raised or lowered prices for consumers. Courts had not always asked. The Chicago School has largely guided courts and regulators since the Reagan years.

I wrote one book. Posner wrote more than 50, ranging from Law and Literature to Reflections on Judging, all while carrying a full caseload. A 2000 study by Fred Shapiro ranked him the most cited legal scholar of the second half of the twentieth century.

Posner's opinions will lift the fog for generations of advocates. A reporter once asked him how law schools could improve legal writing. He remarked he would have first-year students read Holmes, Learned Hand and Robert Jackson, then try to write with their clarity. Posner is on that list. Law schools should teach his writing.
