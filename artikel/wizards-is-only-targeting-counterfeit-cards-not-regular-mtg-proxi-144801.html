Wizards Is Only Targeting Counterfeit Cards, Not Regular MTG Proxies

Recent findings suggest that A wave of controversy hit Magic: The Gathering this week after Wizards of the Coast revealed it is taking legal action against a group of proxy card businesses, collectively referred to as 'PrintMTG Enterprise,' that produce and sell unofficial MTG cards directly to players on several marketplaces. The lawsuit is worth $28.5 million, and it includes strict conditions if Wizards succeeds, such as handing over all card-printing equipment used in the business.

It has been revealed that the community was immediately up in arms over the announcement, as it felt like Wizards was taking a huge step forward in a war against proxy cards, but the reality is that your average proxy card is safe, and this is actually more about counterfeit cards. Is there a difference? Yes, and it could be a costly one for 'PrintMTG Enterprises.'.

The difference between a counterfeit Magic card and a proxy is that a proxy card is clearly not a real Magic card, typically because it has a plain back or doesn't use official Magic artwork on the front. In most cases, proxy cards have a plain white back, so they can't be confused with an official Magic card.

Counterfeit cards are carbon copies of the original Magic design and are intended to deceive players who aren't all that familiar with the minor details that make a Magic card legit. This can also include using the same paper stock as official Magic cards to make them feel authentic in hand, as well as any details meant to fool the eye.

It's entirely fair for Wizards of the Coast to target any seller looking to reproduce Magic cards and sell them in a condition that could be used to defraud others, and this lawsuit shouldn't be confused with Wizards taking a stance against proxy makers or people.

One of the major debates that arose from this lawsuit is why now? It's fair to say that counterfeit Magic cards have been around for a long time, so why is now the time for Wizards to take a stance and look to get them off the market?

Sources indicate that the reality is that we'll never know why, but it's a positive move for the secondary market regardless. Proxy cards, when advertised and produced correctly, aren't mistaken for regular Magic cards, and those who buy them are 100% okay with that.

Counterfeit cards become a problem when people look on typically trusted marketplaces for an official card but end up buying a counterfeit version without knowing it, because the seller is trying to make a quick buck.

This isn't to say that the companies producing these counterfeit cards do so with the intent of deceiving players in online transactions, but that's what is happening, and it needs to stop.

It has been revealed that I've only ever purchased one counterfeit card in my time playing Magic, and it was a copy of Tibalt's Trickery. It wasn't a very expensive card, but the copy I received was clearly fake, not to the eye, but to the touch. I reported the seller, who had ghosted me after the transaction, and I got my money back, but I wonder how many players that seller managed to fool.

According to recent reports, on the proxy front, I only own one set of proxy cards: the Goblin Storm Secret Lair Commander deck. Due to this deck selling out incredibly fast and being scalped for insane prices online, I decided to proxy it instead, but did so from a seller who clearly stated that the backs of the cards would arrive plain white, and that the paper stock used was quite thin (as a warning for when I sleeved the deck). This was perfect, because I planned to use the deck still at my local store, and with my Commander pod, I wanted to be able to say "hey, this is a proxy, does anyone have a problem with that?" as the code of honor among most groups is that, as long as you admit to what it is, there's typically no issue playing with it.

Magic is a very expensive game to get into in most formats, so proxies let you avoid extortionate secondary market prices (especially on land for Commander right now) and still play with limited-availability stuff like Goblin Storm.

Industry observers note that proxies are great, and they should continue to be allowed as long as certain rules are followed. I have no issue with Wizards setting a clear boundary for what a counterfeit card is and why it isn't good for the game.
