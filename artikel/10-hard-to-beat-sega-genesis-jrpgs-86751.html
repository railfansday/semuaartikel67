10 Hard to Beat Sega Genesis JRPGs

Industry observers note that while many of us played our first JRPGs on PS1 or PS2, they existed way before then. The catch was, many of the JRPGs on older systems were incredibly experimental games. They were less clearly defined, but still worthwhile. That especially applies for the Sega Genesis, which featured many hard to beat JRPGs.

According to recent reports, there's a good mix even with these JRPG adventures, which feature sword and sorcery fantasy, far flung future and others besides. No matter what, though, they all featured some capacity to upgrade your characters, and many were turn-based as well.

As for how they're ranked, we dug deep to find critical reception, and did our best to sort these JRPGs accordingly. So join me in my time machine as we head back to the glory days of the Sega Genesis with these hard to beat JRPGs!

According to recent reports, while the Dungeons & Dragons series has come a long way, it's fascinating to see where it started, at least in videogames. And though Dungeons & Dragons: Warriors of the Eternal Sun was mechanically a pretty weird JRPG, it featured a pretty engaging plot.

Industry observers note that it starts with typical warfare against lowly goblins, then has the human army warped to a mysterious land of the Eternal Sun. Meanwhile, something called the Burrower is driving all your allies insane. It's surprisingly Lovecraftian, and quite neat, especially from back in the Sega Genesis era.

Recent findings suggest that this JRPG has a lot of understandable dungeon crawler energy, with players mustering a party of four different characters whose race and class determines everything. And like any good JRPG, as you level up, you'll learn powerful newly introduced skills, though much is decided by the RNG roll of a dice.

Even though I'm not a huge fan of the whole Mystery Dungeon craze, I can see the general appeal. I discovered them with the Pokemon offshoots, but they started way sooner. One of the first examples is simply called Fatal Labyrinth.

It's about chasing a vicious dragon that guards a Holy Goblet, and safely navigating 30 floors of dangers to get there. Enemies don't move or act until you do, so there's inherent strategy in Fatal Labyrinth. More unique, you'll do well to discover whether scrolls and potions hurt or harm you, and using them accordingly.

Perhaps the most charmingly old school aspect of the game is that gold only serves one purpose – blinging out your funeral when you die! So make sure to stock up on vital food and powerful gear, and maybe your journey won't have an ignominious end.

Industry observers note that in a way, The Immortal makes a better novel than a videogame. It's the tale of a call from deep within a labyrinth from the wizard Mordamir. You're not the person he called, named Dunric, but you'll do! Along the way, you'll navigate through several levels, ride magic carpets, and discover the true nightmare of what happened.

Like any good JRPG, there's NPCs here, and you can even cast magic spells to roast your enemies. Those include the likes of goblins, flesh-eating slimes, shades and even a diabolical dragon. Also surprising for the era, The Immortal was surprisingly graphic and violent at times.

While the game features lots of fun fantasy elements, it's also painfully linear, which made some feel it didn't do enough for the genre. Still, a good early attempt, and one that experimented in some meaningful ways.

Sources indicate that I think it's fair to say that The Lord of the Rings inspired Sword of Vermilion on the Sega Genesis. The game features King Tsarkon, who is warped by a magical ring, and twisted to evil ends. A valiant knight named Blade saves the good king's child, and then 18 years later, counsels the prince on his deathbed. It's up to him to honor his dead father and his valiant protector.

As for how Sword of Vermilion actually plays, it has a lot of ARPG mechanics. Furthermore, the gameplay shifts depending on a variety of circumstances, and alternates between top down and side views for the exploration and boss battles.

In a recent development, while the game has a lot of fun arcade energy, the graphics weren't seen as a high point, even back when it first released. On the plus side, it was also a console exclusive, meaning only those who owned a Sega Genesis experienced this classic JRPG.

I'm usually a fan of when developers try something latest. But with Phantasy Star III: Generations of Doom, it was actually a brand new team brought in who hadn't worked on the previous two games. On the plus side, they tried new features like branching narrative paths and multiple endings. On the negative, in some ways this didn't feel like a Phantasy Star adventure.

Phantasy Star III: Generations of Doom actually spans multiple generations, meaning you'll play as the descendants of your starting main character, which honestly feels a bit like Fire Emblem. Depending on who you marry, you'll continue the game with different abilities as various children.

Industry observers note that this adventure downplays the Technique system, but it makes up for it with auto-battle and an icon-based menu. Either way, this is traditional turn-based fare that, at least for me, makes me feel right at home in any JRPG adventure.

In a recent development, unlike the last game, Ys III: Wanderers From Ys is very much a direct follow up to the previous Ys adventures. It features Adol Christin, mysterious statues and the advent of a brutal demon named Galbalan. The game also handles stats, equipment and experience the same as the other games.

Where Ys III: Wanderers From Ys stands apart is that it utilizes a side-scrolling view instead of the more traditional top-down style. Also, you can't just auto attack anymore, and instead need to pres a button to have Adol swing his blade or cast spells.

While the game can be a bit grindy and repetitive, those who were already fans of the Ys series were very happy with this adventure. It could have done more to branch out, perhaps, but sometimes if it's not broken, you don't need to fix anything.

Whenever you hear that Treasure developed a game, you have to pay attention. They make some of the most creative games out there, though usually it's more platformers than JRPGs. In fairness, Light Crusader is a bit of an odd duck, combining the JRPG genre with isometric graphics and action adventure mechanics.

It has been revealed that you play Sir David, who is trying to save the kidnapped citizens of Green Row. He can run and jump, swing his blade and generally beat up monsters. But he's also capable of casting elemental magic. They can be cast individually or combined, buffing David or healing him, amongst other uses.

Industry observers note that along the way, you'll discover the entire game is about the slighted advances of a diabolical wizard. He wanted desperately to marry the Queen of Green Row, and in his petulance, eventually summons a demon. Definitely a quirky adventure, but a memorable one, even years and years later.

In a recent development, it took me a long while to get to Langrisser, a tactical series as popular in some spheres as Fire Emblem or Tactics Ogre. It didn't start with that name, though. Originally, it was called Warsong, and it was a pretty massive deal on the Sega Genesis. It's a fun but pretty brutal tactical JRPG adventure.

Industry observers note that like many brutal games, Warsong features permadeath, meaning if you're not careful, you could lose some of your best characters forever. Your commanders determine what units you can bring into battle, such as powerful Griffons.

Industry observers note that ultimately, careful strategy rewards you with powerful upgrades to your commanders, including changing their classes. If all that wasn't enough, Warsong also features great music composed by Noriyuki Iwadare. A great game if you're strong enough to get through it relatively unscathed.

It has been revealed that there aren't all that many times I get really jealous about console exclusivity these days. But one of those classics I wish I had played back in the day was Shining Force II. It wasn't just good, but it was considered one of the best tactical JRPGs of all time.

In a recent development, some of the reasons people felt that way was due to how open-ended the game was, as well as the great visual flair. It alternated between top-down while exploring, and side scrolling during combat. Either way, the game was challenging, and forced you to lead Bowie and friends carefully.

As for the plot, Shining Force II takes place 55 years after Shining Force Gaiden: Final Conflict, and is about Zeon's mayhem. But even if you didn't play the previous games, this is one tactical adventure that holds up, though it's also far from an easy time.

According to recent reports, one of the more iconic games I played back in the day was Alundra. It was sort of like Zelda, but not really, and it really captivated me. One game that came before it was called Landstalker, a game about a treasure hunter named Nigel.

Recent findings suggest that while Landstalker may not seem like a JRPG at first, there are a couple of reasons it qualifies as one. Even though you don't level up by traditional means, you can take on sidequests to improve your stats. There's also ways to improve your health, and even equip more powerful gear. A lot of it just requires a desire to explore as much as possible.

Recent findings suggest that much of the game's overall challenge comes from devious death traps and gnarly puzzles, not to mention challenging boss battles. Definitely a strange old-school JRPG, but one that managed to delight fans by taking plenty of creative risks.
