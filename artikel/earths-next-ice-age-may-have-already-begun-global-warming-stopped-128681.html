Earth's next ice age may have already begun. Global warming stopped it

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that about 2,000 years ago, the remnants of a massive ice sheet in the Canadian Arctic began expanding once again. According to upcoming research, this slow buildup of ice may have marked the beginning of Earth's next glacial period, commonly called an ice age. But something unexpected happened. By the 1960s, rising summer temperatures had reversed the trend, causing the ice to retreat instead of grow.

The findings suggest that global warming may have interrupted a newly introduced ice age that was already getting underway, fundamentally altering a natural climate cycle that has shaped Earth for millions of years.

Recent findings suggest that "It was just getting started, everything was normal, the next glaciation was about to happen -- and yet it stopped," states Nicolás Young, a geochemist at Columbia Climate School's Lamont-Doherty Earth Observatory. "It's a very powerful example of how we have jolted the system.".

Ancient Arctic Ice Reveals a Surprising Climate History.

Recent findings suggest that in a study published in Science, Young and fellow Lamont geochemist Joerg Schaefer, along with their colleagues, investigated the history of the Laurentide Ice Sheet. This enormous mass of ice covered much of Canada and the northern United States between approximately 95,000 and 20,000 years ago.

At its greatest extent, the Laurentide stretched from north of the Arctic Circle all the way south to Illinois. In some locations, its ice was more than two miles thick. Although most of this enormous ice sheet disappeared as the climate warmed, one surviving piece remains.

Known as the Barnes Ice Cap, this ancient remnant sits on central Baffin Island in Nunavut, Canada, and covers an area roughly the size of Delaware.

"People always say that it disappeared, but that's not true," says Young.

Scientists already knew that the Laurentide Ice Sheet had repeatedly grown and retreated during the roughly 2.6 million years since extensive continental glaciation began in North America. What remained uncertain was whether the ice sheet had ever become smaller than it is today before expanding again.

Answering that question could reveal whether a new cycle of glaciation had begun much more recently than scientists previously believed.

Industry observers note that (A note on terminology: Technically, we are still living in an ice age-the Late Cenozoic Ice Age-which started about 34 million years ago. Within it, colder glacial periods alternate with warmer interglacial ones, including the one we live in now. What people casually call an "ice age" is usually a glacial period. The last glacial period ended about 11,700 years ago.).

Ancient Plants Preserved Beneath Ice Hold the Clues.

Piecing together the history of an ice sheet is particularly challenging when researchers are looking for evidence of its smallest extent and earliest stages of regrowth. As glaciers advance, they frequently scrape across the land, destroying the geological traces that could reveal where and when the ice first began spreading.

The Barnes Ice Cap, however, offers an unusual opportunity to investigate this history.

Unlike most glaciers, it is frozen directly to the bedrock beneath it and does not slide across the landscape. As a result, plants that once grew in areas later covered by expanding ice can remain preserved in place for thousands of years.

Sources indicate that since the Barnes Ice Cap began retreating in 1960, previously buried vegetation has gradually emerged from beneath the melting ice. Researchers can collect these ancient plant remains and determine when they were alive using radiocarbon dating.

This technique measures carbon-14, a harmlessly radioactive form of carbon that living organisms absorb from the atmosphere. Once an organism dies, its carbon-14 begins breaking down at a predictable rate. By measuring how much remains, scientists can estimate when the plant stopped growing and became buried beneath the ice.

These dates provide a record of when the advancing glacier covered different parts of the landscape.

"If you do this enough times, you can actually develop a timeline of that ice margin systematically advancing over plants. You're actually tracking the expansion of that ice margin," says Young.

Earth's Next Ice Age May Have Begun 2,000 Years Ago.

The research team examined ancient vegetation uncovered by the retreating Barnes Ice Cap, along with plant remains exposed beneath smaller ice caps that have recently melted in the surrounding region.

Together, these samples allowed the scientists to reconstruct thousands of years of glacial change.

Their results indicate that the Laurentide Ice Sheet reached its smallest extent approximately 3,000 years ago. Then, around 2,000 years ago, temperatures became cold enough for the remaining ice to begin expanding again.

Young and his colleagues argue that this renewed growth should be recognized as the start of Earth's next glacial period.

That interpretation differs from previous research based on climate models, which has generally suggested that the next glaciation would not begin for thousands or even tens of thousands of years.

In a recent development, the disagreement partly comes down to how scientists define the beginning of a glacial period. Rather than waiting until enormous ice sheets have spread across continents, Young's team proposes that the first sustained expansion of an existing ice sheet should mark the starting point.

Under that definition, the next glaciation had already begun long before modern scientists recognized it.

The discovery also casts newly introduced light on a controversial scientific debate from the 1970s, when some climate researchers suggested that another ice age might be approaching.

According to recent reports, "It actually was happening," Young says. "They couldn't quite prove it, but now we sort of have the evidence.".

Recent findings suggest that however, this early expansion would not have produced an immediate or dramatic transformation of the planet. According to Young, it would have taken thousands of years for the cooling to become noticeable and roughly 30,000 years for the Laurentide Ice Sheet to regain its enormous continental dimensions.

The process was only in its earliest stages when the warming climate brought it to a halt.

Global Warming Reversed the Growth of the Ice Sheet.

By the 1960s, conditions had changed enough that the Barnes Ice Cap was losing more ice during the summer than it could replace through winter snowfall.

Instead of continuing to expand, the ancient ice began shrinking.

According to the researchers' interpretation, this reversal effectively ended the developing glacial period before it could progress into a full-scale ice age.

In a recent development, the consequences may extend far beyond the present day. Models projecting the future of the Barnes Ice Cap indicate that it could disappear entirely within the next few centuries.

"That will be the true very last remains of the Laurentide, disappearing for good," Young claims.

The Loss of an Ancient Ice Cap Could Change Future Ice Ages.

The disappearance of the Barnes Ice Cap could also have implications for how future glacial periods develop.

According to recent reports, in the past, the Laurentide Ice Sheet provided a starting point for renewed glaciation whenever Earth's climate cooled sufficiently. Because a large existing mass of ice helps create conditions favorable for further ice accumulation, it can trigger reinforcing processes that allow glaciers to expand.

Scientists describe this kind of process as a positive feedback. In this case, existing ice helps promote the growth of additional ice, allowing an ice sheet to become increasingly extensive as temperatures remain cold.

Without the Laurentide's last surviving remnant, a future glacial period might unfold differently.

Researchers cannot yet determine when another glaciation could begin under atmospheric conditions influenced by elevated greenhouse gas concentrations. If enormous continental ice sheets eventually start forming again, they may have to develop without the ancient ice that previously helped initiate their growth.

Recent findings suggest that the findings suggest that global warming has done more than melt glaciers and shrink existing ice sheets. It may have interrupted a glacial cycle that was already beginning, while also removing a feature that helped sustain ice age cycles across North America for millions of years.

"What has been an extremely natural and predictable phenomenon is now gone," notes Young.
