Industry observers note that more U.S. women are taking menopause hormone therapy, latest prescription data suggest.

Monthly dispensing of hormone therapy for women aged 40 to 74 more than doubled since 2023, growing from 2 million to 4.9 million, researchers report November 19 in JAMA. The analysis of U.S. pharmacy dispensing data revealed that this increase began in 2024. The rise continued in 2025, before and after the Food and Drug Administration’s November removal of the black box warning from packaging, and into 2026.

Although the data in the latest study are aggregate, the researchers used population data to estimate the dispensing rate for this age group, which jumped from 28 prescriptions per 1,000 women to 67 prescriptions per 1,000 over the period studied.

In a recent development, the new study wasn’t designed to determine what’s behind the increase. It’s possible that the evidence regarding hormone therapy’s safety and effectiveness for the treatment of menopause symptoms, such as hot flashes, is reaching more women. Medical groups such as the Menopause Society have recommended menopause hormone therapy for women with symptoms who are younger than 60 or within 10 years of the start of menopause, absent certain risk factors including prior breast cancer and stroke.

“Greater attention to women’s health, updated medical guidance supporting individualized menopause hormone therapy and social media may all be contributing to increased interest,” states Dana Sarnak, who studies sexual and reproductive health at Johns Hopkins School of Medicine. Anecdotally, her clinician colleagues report more patients seeking menopause care, she says.

According to recent reports, many women and physicians turned away from hormone therapy in the early 2000s after a large study called the Women’s Health Initiative raised safety concerns, which were later considered overblown. Research since then has provided a more nuanced understanding of the benefits and risks of menopause hormone therapy at different ages.

It has been revealed that there are safer formulations, including patches and gels, of estrogen today then at the time of the Women’s Health Initiative, for which participants took a pill. The new study found that product use increased for transdermal and vaginal estrogen and for progesterone products — women who have their uterus need to take progesterone along with estrogen. The use of oral formulations remained stable. There has been a shortage of estrogen patches in the United States this year, which the U.S. Food and Drug Administration attributes to increasing demand.
