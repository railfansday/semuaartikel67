Latest Update: How Can Energy Improve Lives? Why Counting Connections Isn’t Enough

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Global tracking of energy access should expand beyond connections to include affordability, reliability, adequacy, and development outcomes.

Access to energy is a cornerstone of development—it can improve health, education, incomes, and overall quality of life.

In a recent development, but access is only the first step. A health clinic can’t perform surgery if frequent power cuts make its equipment unreliable. An electric irrigation pump is unlikely to raise a farmer’s income if crops can’t reach buyers. And a small business may gain little from electricity if it lacks financing to buy the equipment it needs to expand.

For energy to improve people’s lives, it must be affordable, sufficient, and reliable—and people and businesses must be able to put it to productive use.

Industry observers note that the United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 7 (SDG 7) calls for “universal access to affordable, reliable, and modern energy services” by 2030. But the two indicators used to track its universal-access target focus only on whether people have access to electricity and whether they primarily rely on clean fuels and technologies. They do not address reliability, affordability, or economic impacts.

According to recent reports, other approaches already offer a fuller picture. The World Bank’s Energy Sector Management Assistance Program developed the Multi-Tier Framework to measure electricity access beyond connections, including whether electricity is available when needed, adequate for the equipment people want to use, reliable and affordable. While the framework is used by some countries to assess these broader dimensions of energy access, such measures are not the default, as many countries rely on the U.N.’s official global indicators.

In a recent development, as governments consider what should follow the SDGs after 2030, they should make multidimensional energy-access measures a regular part of national surveys and use the results to track progress globally. Since access alone does not necessarily deliver better livelihoods, improved measures of access could also be paired with indicators of whether energy is delivering the development outcomes it is intended to support. These could include whether clinics and schools can function as intended, farmers can increase yields and incomes and businesses can grow revenues and employment.

Together, these measures could shift energy planning from the largely supply-side question of how many people have access toward the demand-side questions of what people and institutions need energy for and whether the energy system enables them to meet these needs.

The examples below show why looking beyond connections matters in practice.

According to recent reports, national electrification rates can mask large differences in where electricity is available within a country. Addressing those gaps requires looking beyond national averages to identify the households, businesses, and public facilities that lack access.

Industry observers note that in 2023, WRI, working closely with Uganda’s Ministry of Health, used WRI’s Energy Access Explorer (EAE)—an online mapping tool that combines energy data with information on infrastructure, public services, and economic activity—to assess the electricity needs of health facilities. The analysis found that 675 facilities serving more than 3 million people had no electricity, either grid or off-grid, yet all were in areas with significant solar energy potential. The analysis helped identify places where solar power could fill the energy gap.

In Ethiopia, the Ministry of Water and Energy and the World Bank used EAE to help design a financing program for off-grid electricity. The analysis identified 145 priority woredas, or districts, where investments could reach more than 19 million people. Having this location-based data helped translate national energy-access goals into decisions about where public financing should be directed.

Ethiopia also illustrates why simple access figures can overstate the level of service people receive. A 2025 national energy-access survey found that 65% of households had access to at least one source of electricity. However, just 44% of the population received at least a basic level of electricity service, enough for uses such as lighting and charging a phone for at least four hours a day.

Simply having an electricity connection is not enough if the supply lacks the capacity or voltage stability to operate the equipment people need.

In the Gumla district in Jharkhand, India, villages that had gained household grid connections still lacked enough power for many businesses to function. WRI found that the single-phase electricity supplied was insufficient for productive uses such as milling and oil pressing. Beginning in 2015, Mlinda Charitable Trust, a nonprofit, installed solar mini-grids that supplied three-phase electricity, allowing local enterprises to run equipment, including rice and wheat hullers, oil expellers, flour mills, and pulverizers.

Governments should assess not only whether communities are connected but also whether the electricity supplied can meet the capacity and power-quality requirements of the services and equipment people need—and blueprint grid upgrades or complementary systems accordingly.

A connection provides limited benefit if households, businesses, or public facilities cannot pay for enough energy to meet their needs. Measuring meaningful access therefore also means asking whether the energy is affordable for the people and institutions expected to use it.

In Ethiopia’s Berbere district, farmers initially used petrol-powered pumps to irrigate their fields, but high fuel and maintenance costs meant these were too expensive for many to keep using. Petrol cost $91 to $203 per hectare a year, while repairs and the cost of transporting fuel added further expenses.

In a recent development, WRI and the Danish nonprofit DanChurchAid later installed two solar-powered pumps serving 48 farming households. The solar systems have no fuel costs, while maintenance costs are less than $10 a year, compared with more than $1,300 for petrol pumps. But the two pumps’ combined $19,200 upfront cost was beyond what most farmers could afford out of pocket. Governments and funders should therefore assess both the ongoing cost of using energy and the upfront cost of the technologies needed to use it, and provide financing or targeted support where high initial costs remain a barrier.

It has been revealed that being connected to the grid does not mean electricity will be available whenever it is needed. WRI researchers documented power cuts lasting three to 12 hours a day at two health facilities in Jharkhand, with outages worsening during the monsoon season. The facilities relied on diesel generators and small battery backups during outages.

In 2017, after solar photovoltaic systems and larger batteries were installed, the facilities were able to keep wards and offices running and power other essential equipment during grid outages, reducing their reliance on grid electricity.

Tracking the frequency and duration of outages would give a clearer picture of whether electricity is dependable enough to support essential services.

According to recent reports, even when energy is available, adequate, affordable, and reliable, it may not translate into better livelihoods. People also need equipment, financing, and access to markets to put energy to productive use.

Industry observers note that in Uganda, WRI Africa and partners used EAE to identify $15 million in potential investments in renewable energy technologies, including solar-powered irrigation systems and equipment for cooling and milling, that could benefit more than 28,000 farmers. Some organizations identified through that work have since secured funding for related projects. Masaka District Farmers Association, for example, received an IKI Small Grants award for an irrigation project that equipped 150 hectares of coffee-growing land with drip irrigation and worked with 1,500 smallholder farmers.

In a recent development, in Berbere, solar-powered irrigation has enabled farmers to harvest three to four times a year and grow higher-value crops. In their first season, many farmers earned four to 10 times as much from onion, pepper, and cabbage sales as they had previously earned in a year from staple crops.

Tracking these outcomes alongside measures of energy access can help show whether investments are translating into higher productivity and incomes.

Making greater use of existing multidimensional measures, alongside complementary indicators of development outcomes, could change not just how governments report progress but also how they plan and direct investment.

Industry observers note that rather than starting with how many new connections a project can deliver, governments and funders would ask what people and institutions need energy for, what prevents them from using it effectively, and which investments could remove those barriers. In some places, that may mean extending electricity supply. In others, it could mean improving reliability, lowering costs, or helping farmers and businesses obtain the equipment and financing needed to use energy productively.

Energy is essential to improving human well-being, but it is only one part of what drives development. Like water, health care or education and energy is necessary but not sufficient. Treating access as a starting point rather than an end goal—and measuring whether people can use, afford, and rely on the energy they receive—would give governments a clearer picture of whether energy investments are improving people’s lives.
