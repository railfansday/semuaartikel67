Is A24 Finally Big Enough to Be Considered a Major Studio?

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a recent development, earlier this year when IndieWire reported that A24 had settled a dispute with Marcus Theaters over theatrical terms, a back and forth that kept some of A24‘s summer titles like “The Invite,” “Tony,” and “Onslaught” out of the fourth-largest chain in the country, the reason was because of a debate over A24’s size. When you have blockbusters as substantial as “Backrooms” and “Marty Supreme,” why would you still want theatrical terms that lump you in with the specialized, little guys?

This is the question that Hollywood — and even perhaps A24 internally — should start asking: Should A24 now be considered a major studio? Should A24 be in the same conversation as Disney, Universal, Sony, Paramount, Warner Bros., Netflix, Amazon MGM, Apple, and Lionsgate? In a recent development, in some regards, this is a semantic discussion. Whether A24 is in that made-up tier of six to nine corporate conglomerates that the trades call “majors” doesn’t mean a lick to the general public. Audiences know they care about the A24 brand and logo in the same way they pay attention to the brands of Marvel, Pixar, Blumhouse, IMAX, and a select few others making movies today, and the rest is mostly gravy. Postrak found that on average, 70 percent of people who see A24 movies on opening weekend go to theaters more often than others, at least monthly, so the brand has an impact. But whether it has theatrical terms on par with Paramount or with Bleecker Street doesn’t matter in the slightest.

Industry observers note that in another sense, whether A24 is a major studio and not some cool upstart indie with a lot of scale matters a whole lot. It matters to investors, it matters to people who care about the A24 brand, it matters to theater owners, and it should matter to A24 itself as it defines what it wants to be moving forward. Netflix wasn’t always considered a major, legacy studio, but their size and success got so undeniable that you had to put them in the same conversation as the other majors who drove trends, who influenced deal making for stars and creators, and who eventually had to reach the same labor terms as everyone else.

It has been revealed that A24 might soon be in the same boat, if it isn’t already. Led by the success of “Backrooms,” the tech giant has entered a newly introduced echelon in 2026, one that has allowed it to rival studios that were once much larger than A24 in every facet. Now those lines have blurred, A24’s budgets and the grosses those films are able to generate have grown, it’s earning sizable $75 million investments from places like Google and is in the mix to buy properties like Letterboxd, arguably more than others have speculated its time to sell.

According to recent reports, answering whether A24 is a “major” isn’t a simple question with one answer. There’s several criteria we could look at to make a case in either direction.

In a recent development, A24 through November 15 in 2026 has earned $526.6 million at the domestic box office, according to data from Rentrak. It was led by “Backrooms,” which made $197 million domestic, but that’s not even half its overall haul. This year, that’s roughly $175 million above the next-highest specialized distributor in Focus Features ($351.8 million), which released the even more successful “Obsession” ($263.4 million) this year, and it’s well above the likes of other indie rivals like Neon, Fathom, GKIDS, Angel Studios, Black Bear, and Searchlight.

Recent findings suggest that it has also more than doubled what it brought in across a full year in 2025, a 124 percent increase so far (it still has its major awards play “The Debut” opening in December and “Club Kid” going wider), and it’s nearly four times where it was in 2023 when A24’s total domestic box office was $136.7 million and its biggest release of the year was “Talk to Me,” which made $44.2 million domestic. Four of A24’s movies this year (“Backrooms,” “You Can See Everything,” “The Drama,” and “Primetime”) surpassed that figure.

Sources indicate that it’s safe to say it puts A24 in a tier of its own above the other indies. But does it rise to the level of a major? As recently as last year, the answer was squarely no. But A24 in 2026, per Rentrak, commands a 6.0 percent market share on the year so far. That puts it fifth on the year so far, ahead of Warner Bros., Paramount, and Lionsgate (those numbers will change as some other tentpoles emerge). It would not have even cracked the top 10 last year when its market share was just 2.7 percent and was largely driven by the late-year success of “Marty Supreme.”.

According to recent reports, A24 doesn’t have and probably won’t for some time field a movie that can realistically sniff $1 billion worldwide the way many major studios manage to pump out each and every year. But receipts don’t lie, and what makes this realistically sustainable for A24 to repeat year after year is how much it has grown.

It has been revealed that A24 is releasing in the vicinity of 16 to 18 movies annually, and that puts it easily on par with legacy studios like Disney, Sony, Warner Bros., and Paramount. Skydance, which now owns Paramount and WB, will release 30+ movies a year, but between the two studios. Universal tends to get into the 20s, but only when you combine its output with that of Focus. Netflix puts out way, way more than the whole bunch, but they’re naturally streaming movies that include low-budget genre movies and documentaries.

Some of A24’s films each year are still acquisitions out of festivals, which was the case this year with both “The Invite” (Sundance) and “Club Kid” (Cannes). But like the other majors, A24 produces and finances a large majority of its movies independently, with a handful of co-productions. Importantly, it works with newly introduced filmmakers and forges relationships so that they stay in their pipeline, whether they’re newcomers like Lance Oppenheim and Kane Parsons or veterans like Celine Song, Alex Garland, and Ari Aster who have returned to A24 time and again.

A24’s international distribution has it partnering with other overseas distributors, which is structurally different from major studios that have robust international distribution arms, but it has still released some of its movies in English-language territories like the UK. You can imagine that as A24 continues to grow, that could be one area of natural expansion.

Recent findings suggest that where A24 still lags significantly behind other majors though is in its overall content spend, a number that A24 doesn’t publicly disclose. Netflix had a content spend this year of about $20 billion. Disney was around $24 billion and recently projected to increase to $26 billion in its next fiscal year. Maybe if A24 starts picking up sports rights to the NBA it can start getting into that ballpark.

It has been revealed that but even when focusing just on film, something like “Backrooms” was still produced for a modest $10 million, whereas other studios have multiple titles that well exceed $100 million in production budget alone. We do know though that its budgets have increased dramatically as A24 pursues more tentpole properties. “Marty Supreme” was previously its most expensive title, but its adaptation of the video game “Elden Ring” will blow that out of the water, with a budget in the vicinity of $170 million or above. That kind of investment carries risks. There are Marvel movies that don’t cost that much.

A24 hasn’t yet put its stamp on a four-quadrant, animated Dr. Seuss movie or something similar that would easily make you think it has ambitions of becoming a major studio, but the types of movies it has released has grown more varied over the years. It has horror movies, adult comedies, biopics, thrillers, and even, of course, documentaries like “You Can See Everything,” an area that it looked like A24 was scaling back on not too long ago. When “Elden Ring” opens it will squarely be an action fantasy, and the studio has other genre films, comedies, thrillers, and more in the works for next year.

According to recent reports, you can also see A24’s diversity in how it’s approaching awards plays versus commercial titles or specialized fare. Something like “The Drama” this year opened wide right out of the gate given its star power, whereas others like “The Invite” and “Tony” have stuck to platform releases before going wider. And those smaller, art house titles alongside its genre movies and its tentpoles are still a key part of its business model and identity. Movies like “Pillion,” “Mother Mary,” and “The Moment” have done business on a much smaller scale, but are still key in helping the tech giant get to that 6 percent share (they lost money on some, though).

Sources indicate that the definition of major studio today has evolved to the point that the movie division is just a component of a massive media conglomerate, one that also includes television, streaming, gaming, sports, news, theme parks, and other ancillary businesses. If you’re Apple or Amazon, your movie studio is a small cog, practically a loss leader, in the larger tech empire. It gives these companies valuations of tens of billions of dollars, and it’s why A24 could still be a target for M&A if it chooses to sell.

Sources indicate that A24 does have its own record label for music and original movie score releases and vinyl pressing, it has been producing a podcast since 2018, it owns the Cherry Lane Theater in Fresh York for stage productions and concerts, it sells plenty of kooky merchandise, and it even has a membership program and campus ambassador program that have seen growth in recent years. They’re all things that suggest the company has more on its mind than movies.

Industry observers note that and as we wrote earlier this week, its TV unit has quietly had as strong a year as its film team. A24 has spread series across virtually every major streaming service, all of them again varying in genre, pedigree, and budget. It’s not just the number of reveals that has expanded but the executive team to support them.

It may be a strength that A24 doesn’t have its own SVOD service to support all these reveals and movies, or a broadcast network or cable channel that could further complicate things. Those businesses are very costly to operate and some are in the decline phase of their trajectories. Yet, it inherently keeps A24 in a lower tier compared to other major studios that do have their own distribution apparatus for television and can license content from other studios like A24. While Gen Z might get excited about the idea of an A24 streaming service, that’s an area of growth that’s less likely to occur in the short term. Well, unless A24 wants to raise a lot more capital.

This is the qualitative assessment of whether A24 should be considered a “major” studio, the one that doesn’t lean on numbers and instead just goes on feel. A24 might argue that it is one of one, that it doesn’t belong to the world of major studios, and that the baggage that comes with that status isn’t worth it. But its leaders probably believe A24 is a step above the other indies that are almost solely distributors. Do we really think the same firm that is taking a chance on movies as weird as “You Can See Everything” or “Primetime” is equivalent to a studio that might make the odd “Digger,” but is mostly interested in producing “A Minecraft Movie” and “Superman?” Is it fair to lump in A24 with Focus, Searchlight, and Sony Pictures Classics, which are quite literally just specialized labels within larger studios?

All that could be true, but it doesn’t mean we shouldn’t reassess how we view A24 as a company or how we handicap its hits and misses in the same way we would with Disney or Netflix. Even if we’re not ready to call A24 a “major,” there’s certainly a major shift in where A24 sits in this industry. With great success comes an even greater spotlight.
