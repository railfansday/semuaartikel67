Brain scans and donated tissue show that premature birth sets the cerebellum on its own track, leaving it out of step at the due date.

Premature birth interrupts a critical period of brain development.

Now, researchers have found that even by their original due dates, babies born prematurely show signs of altered development in a key region of the brain.

The cerebellum of a baby born early matures on a different track. At the due date, its cells are in a different state from a full-term baby’s, according to a new study of brain scans, developmental checkups and donated tissue.

Georgios Sanidas and Gabriele Simonti of Children’s National Hospital in Washington, D.C., led the work.

The study’s senior authors were Panagiotis Kratimenos of Children’s National and the George Washington University School of Medicine and Vittorio Gallo of Seattle Children’s Research Institute.

The tissue came from infants who had died and whose families consented to autopsy, and the authors thank those families in the paper.

In an interview with Earth.com, Kratimenos noted that the work offers parents of preterm babies relief as well as hope.

“Our work reveals that premature birth does not break the brain, nor does it simply pause it,” Kratimenos said.

Between 24 and 40 weeks of pregnancy, the cerebellum grows about fivefold in volume, faster than any other part of the brain.

It handles movement and balance, and it is wired into thinking and language as well.

For a baby born at 26 weeks, much of that growth takes place in a neonatal intensive care unit.

Doctors have known that early birth changes how the cerebellum grows and how a child develops.

What they didn’t know was what happens to the cells inside it, because that can only be studied in human tissue.

The team followed 176 infants born early, none of whom had major brain injury, through scans and checkups.

At the due date, each extra week of pregnancy before birth was worth about 230 cubic millimeters of cerebellum, in proportion to the rest of the brain.

Ultrasound revealed the size gap already in place at 28 weeks. The babies born earliest then grew at the same rate as the rest, so they had not closed it by the due date.

Later checkups showed the same pattern. Between nine months and three years of age, the babies born at the lowest gestational ages were more likely to have motor, thinking and language delays.

A smaller cerebellum went with motor and thinking delays in particular. Lung disease and the other complications of intensive care shaped the numbers, but they didn’t explain them.

To see the cells, the team turned to cerebellar tissue from 83 infants who had died, born anywhere from very early to full term. In 11 of them, the researchers mapped which genes were switched on where.

Three patterns of gene activity tracked how early a baby had been born.

The cerebellum’s most numerous neurons, the small granule cells, were still running an immature program in the babies born earliest.

In babies born closer to term, that program had given way to one built for forming and firing connections.

Under a standard microscope, the granule cells in the earliest-born babies look finished, because they have already reached their adult position.

“Even though those granule cells had reached the right neighborhood, they were still carrying the genetic features of immature cells,” Gallo stated.

The large neurons that carry the cerebellum’s output, the Purkinje cells, were different again.

In the earliest-born infants their genes were tuned to building cell material, with signs of stress, rather than to settling into a mature circuit.

“Being born early forces those same cells to prioritize immediate survival over wiring, leaving them in an unfinished state even months later,” Kratimenos told Earth.com.

Industry observers note that under the microscope, in 77 of the brains, the differences were structural too. A temporary outer layer of brain cell precursors that normally disappears after birth lingered longer in preterm infants.

In full-term infants the density of Purkinje cells fell with age, the normal trimming that refines a circuit. In preterm infants it stayed flat.

The layer that holds the Purkinje cells’ branches grew at the same rate in both groups.

The preterm cerebellum continued to grow without showing the same signs of cellular maturation.

It kept its overall architecture while its development went a different way, which is why a scan that measures size alone may miss it.

Recent findings suggest that preterm babies are assessed, monitored and enrolled in trials by postmenstrual age, the weeks since the mother’s last period. The authors argue that this number measures time, not biological maturity.

Two babies at the same postmenstrual age can be at different developmental stages.

Treatments given around a premature birth, such as magnesium sulfate before delivery and caffeine after it, may therefore act on tissue that differs from one baby to the next.

“To time treatments properly, we have to match our therapies to the biological clock of a baby’s brain cells, rather than their age on the calendar,” Gallo said.

Gallo suggests that therapies designed to promote brain wiring may be less effective before the cells reach the appropriate stage of development.

In a commentary on the paper, Molly Kelly-Goss and Elizabeth Crouch wrote that a preterm infant may follow two calendars.

One is the cerebellum’s physical growth, and the other is the maturation of its cells and molecules.

In their words, “neither captures the true complexity of brain health at the time, nor what it means for the child years later.”.

Sources indicate that the study can’t separate the timing of birth from everything that comes with it. Inflammation, low oxygen and the stress of life outside the womb all arrive together.

The tissue and the scans also came from different infants, since the cerebellum can’t be sampled in a living baby’s brain, and each infant had one MRI scan.

Whether the gap persists, shrinks or is made up later in childhood is not known.

The authors wrote that the same slow maturation that leaves the preterm cerebellum vulnerable may also leave its development open to change for longer.

Gallo mentioned the team’s gene signatures for each stage could guide treatment in two steps.

The first would protect a baby from inflammation and stress in intensive care, and the second would bring in therapies that boost brain wiring once the cells are ready.

Testing that will take studies that time treatments to a baby’s biological stage.

The full study was published in the journal Science.
