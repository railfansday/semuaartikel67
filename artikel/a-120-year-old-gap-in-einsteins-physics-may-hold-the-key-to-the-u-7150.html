A 120-Year-Old Gap in Einstein’s Physics May Hold the Key to the Universe’s Earliest Matter

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A missing piece in Einstein’s century-old theory could help scientists understand how matter behaved in the universe’s earliest moments, revealing more about a time we can barely recreate.

Industry observers note that in particle accelerators, scientists smashed ions together at nearly the speed of light to recreate the fluid that existed just after the Big Bang. The resulting droplets were roughly the size of an atomic nucleus and survived for only tens of septillionths of a second. One way to investigate their interior was to track heavy quarks, particles that traveled through the droplets without being part of the surrounding fluid.

That fluid, called quark-gluon plasma, offered a glimpse of the universe’s earliest matter. In the first fraction of a second after the Substantial Bang, matter existed as a trillion-degree mixture of quarks and gluons, subatomic particles moving at close to the speed of light. This state lasted just a few microseconds before the particles came together to form more stable protons and neutrons.

According to recent reports, reading the clues carried by heavy quarks required an accurate description of how the fluid pushed and pulled them. Albert Einstein established a relationship between a particle’s motion and a fluid’s microscopic structure in 1905. At speeds approaching that of light, however, the familiar description missed some of the interactions that shaped a particle’s journey.

It has been revealed that jean Du Plessis, a graduate student in MIT’s Center for Theoretical Physics, a Leinweber Institute, and Bruno Scheihing-Hitschfeld of the Kavli Institute for Theoretical Physics at the University of California at Santa Barbara (UCSB) developed a theory to address that gap. It predicted how a particle moving at relativistic speeds, meaning close to the speed of light, was likely to be pushed and pulled as it passed through a fluid. They reported their findings in Physical Review Letters.

Sources indicate that the researchers began with a relativistic heavy quark traveling through a gas of weakly interacting quarks and gluons. Those weak interactions made the calculations manageable. They calculated the forces acting on the heavy quark through the strong nuclear force, the fundamental force that held quarks and gluons together, to determine how its momentum was likely to change.

Their calculation produced a probability distribution, a curve showing how likely different changes in momentum were. It also described the likelihood of kicks of different strengths, since stronger kicks produced larger changes in momentum.

Industry observers note that “Our result reveals a latest source of information carried by heavy quarks in rare, unusually large changes in their motion,” Du Plessis said. “How often those large changes occur depends on the microscopic nature of the plasma, raising the possibility that they could provide a new way to learn what the quark-gluon plasma is actually like inside.”.

According to recent reports, the idea of learning about a fluid by watching particles move through it had roots in a much more familiar setting. In 1827, botanist Robert Brown observed grains of pollen jiggling and swirling in seemingly still water. That erratic movement became known as Brownian motion and later inspired Einstein’s work.

Recent findings suggest that einstein’s 1905 theory explained how invisible activity within a fluid could keep a larger particle moving. Atoms and molecules in the fluid exerted random forces that combined to kick and jostle the particle, while drag from the fluid slowed it down. The relationship between these random kicks and drag determined how the particle eventually reached the same temperature as its surroundings.

According to recent reports, scientists subsequently used Einstein’s explanation to study many fluids, including quark-gluon plasma. They assumed that a particle passing through the plasma would experience a balance of microscopic kicks similar to that experienced by pollen in water. In this familiar picture, the kicks followed a bell-shaped probability distribution. Typical kicks clustered around the peak, while extreme kicks occupied the tails and were equally unlikely in opposite directions.

According to recent reports, “Einstein highlighted that the drag and the random kicks must balance in just the right way for a particle eventually to come to the same temperature as its surroundings,” Du Plessis explained. “For the types of fluids he was thinking about, he was correct. But for particles that are moving at relativistic velocities, this relationship breaks down. And we’ve known for 20 years that something is missing. So we have to go beyond the work that Einstein did.”.

The new calculation resembled Einstein’s bell-shaped curve only for small kicks.

“The usual Brownian picture assumes that large kicks become exceedingly rare,” Du Plessis stated. “For heavy quarks, however, these rare kicks are more common than the bell curve predicts, and are not equally likely in opposite directions: A large kick that slows the quark down more than expected from drag is more likely than an equally large kick that leaves it moving faster than expected.”.

According to recent reports, when the researchers repeated their calculations for plasmas made up of different particles, they found the same tilted pattern, with uneven tails at either end of the distribution. They also recognized the same structure in an earlier calculation for a strongly coupled plasma, where particles interacted strongly, despite the differences in the underlying microscopic physics.

“This is the first time that this seemingly universal structure was realized and really formalized,” Du Plessis noted. “This gives a concrete path forward for how to better understand the quark-gluon plasma, using heavy quarks.”.

The researchers envisioned applying the theory to predict how heavy quarks moved through quark-gluon plasma, then comparing those predictions with experiments. That comparison could help characterize the plasma’s microscopic structure and establish what kind of fluid existed in the universe’s first moments.

Du Plessis mentioned the primordial plasma was likely “more strongly interacting than this Brownian motion way of modeling it would suggest. It’s more like a liquid and less like a gas.”.

It has been revealed that reference: “Heavy Quark Transport Is Non-Gaussian beyond Leading Logarithm” by Jean F. Du Plessis and Bruno Scheihing-Hitschfeld, 5 November 2026, Physical Review Letters. DOI: 10.1103/7fd5-1kq5.
