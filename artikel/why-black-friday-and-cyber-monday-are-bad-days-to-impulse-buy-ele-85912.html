Not every Black Friday or Cyber Monday deal is as sweet as it seems. With electronics and tech in particular, you could be buying last year's model that has actually been on sale since the summer—and was maybe a better price a few months ago. Here’s how to avoid impulse buying a misleading Black Friday “deal” and make sure you’re getting the best bang for your buck.

When you see products like food, clothes, or home goods with a big "Black Friday" or "Cyber Monday" tag slapped on them, it's probably safe to take advantage of that deal. Chances are it's the same exact product you'd find on other days of the year. The same can’t be said for a TV, laptop, and other tech deals, where the product itself is constantly being upgraded.

Because tech is always evolving, and retailers want to move inventory before newly introduced models come out, electronics are especially susceptible to a practice known as "price anchoring." That's when retailers mark up prices in the weeks leading up to Black Friday, before "slashing" those prices back down, giving the appearance of a deal that isn't really a deal at all. Still, when you see that marked-up number, aka the "price anchor," you might get tricked into thinking you're saving money when you're not. There's also the false sense of urgency that gets created. It's second nature to assume that anything labeled "deal" won’t last long—even when this so-called deal price has actually been going on since the summer.

Price anchoring isn't even unique to Black Friday. The nonprofit Consumers’ Checkbook tracked prices at 25 major retailers over 33 weeks in 2022 and found that more than half the items tracked were offered at false discounts every week or almost every week. Less common, but also worth noting, is a known practice of "derivative" or retailer-exclusive models produced specifically for the holiday shopping season. These versions often feature cheaper components, fewer features, or lower specifications than their regular counterparts. A 55-inch TV from a reputable brand might look identical to the year-round model, but it could have a lower refresh rate, inferior panel technology, or fewer HDMI ports.

Recent findings suggest that in the same vein, that "amazing deal" on a laptop might come with last year's processor, or a smartphone that's about to be replaced. The problem isn't that the tech is an older model, but again, it's that it probably has been at this low a price, or lower, since the summer. Alternatively, some deeply discounted electronics are refurbished units or open-box returns that have been repackaged. Again, while this isn't necessarily a bad thing, impulse buyers may not fully realize what they're buying.

Industry observers note that the key phrasing here is impulse buying. Events like Black Friday are psychologically engineered to trigger impulse purchases. Limited-time offers, countdown clocks, and "while supplies last" warnings create artificial urgency. When you're caught up in the moment, it's easy to convince yourself you need a latest tablet, smartwatch, or gaming console, even if you hadn't considered buying one before.

It has been revealed that to avoid a low-key scam, do your research. The easiest way to tell that you’re getting the correct product is by checking the model or serial number. And with online shopping, finding and double-checking those numbers is quick and easy. In addition, don't just compare prices—compare specs. Look at processor speeds, RAM, storage capacity, screen resolution, refresh rates, and connectivity options. A $300 laptop isn't a deal if a slightly more expensive model offers significantly better performance that will last longer.

In a recent development, if you do see a deal that catches your eye, it could pay to wait. Cyber Monday, post-Christmas sales, and even January clearances often feature electronics deals that rival or beat Black Friday prices, all without the famous chaos and pressure. Then again, you risk more “out-of-stock” results if you wait, so the risk is up to you, depending on the popularity of the item you’re eyeing (and how badly you want it).

The bottom line: Don’t fall victim to a falsely advertised lower-quality product, and do a little research to find actually good deals ahead of time.
