Industry observers note that pose detection in the browser is a solved problem. You can load a model, point it at a webcam, and get a skeleton tracking a human body in real time, on a laptop, with no server involved.

And then you have a skeleton that follows someone around the screen, which is a toy.

In a recent development, the gap between that toy and a feature someone would pay for is smaller than most developers expect, and it isn't made of machine learning. It's made of trigonometry you already know. This article builds a working bicep-curl rep counter to show exactly where that line falls — and more importantly, which side of it your bugs live on.

Recent findings suggest that the model has no concept of exercise. It doesn't know what an arm is for. It has never heard of a bicep curl. It reports where it thinks your joints are, thirty-odd times a second, and stops.

Industry observers note that fifteen lines, and that's the feature. The counter increments exactly once per cycle, on the edge, because the guard on phase makes every other frame in that cycle a no-op.

Recent findings suggest that if you used a single threshold — say, 100 degrees — you'd get a counter that fires repeatedly whenever someone holds their arm near 100 degrees and the estimate jitters by a degree either side. Every tiny fluctuation across the line is a transition, and every transition is a rep.

Sources indicate that two widely separated thresholds give you hysteresis. Once the arm is "up," it has to travel all the way past 150 degrees before the counter will consider another rep. Noise of a few degrees can't produce a count because no amount of jitter near 55 crosses 150.

According to recent reports, this is the same trick a thermostat uses to avoid switching the heating on and off every few seconds, and it's the single most useful pattern in this whole category of work. Any time you turn a continuous signal into discrete events, you want two thresholds, not one.

visibility is the field people skip, and skipping it is how you get phantom reps.

When a joint goes out of frame or gets occluded, the model still returns a coordinate for it. It is obliged to return 33 points. What it does is return a guess, with a low visibility score attached — and a guessed wrist position can swing wildly between frames, which looks exactly like a very fast curl.

Returning null and having update bail out means an occluded frame holds the current state rather than corrupting it. The arm stays "up" until the model can actually see it come down.

Industry observers note that if you want to be stricter, smooth the angle over the last three or four frames with a rolling median before it reaches the state machine. A median rejects single-frame outliers in a way a mean doesn't.

It has been revealed that when my rep counter miscounts, I don't retrain anything. I don't collect a dataset, I don't label examples, I don't spin up a training job. I look at two things: my thresholds, and my state machine. One of them is wrong.

That's a normal bug in normal code, and you already know how to find it. You can log the angle. You can set a breakpoint on the transition. You can write a unit test that feeds a synthetic sequence of angles through update and asserts the count — no camera, no model, no fixtures:.
