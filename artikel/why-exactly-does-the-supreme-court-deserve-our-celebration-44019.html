Why Exactly Does The Supreme Court "Deserve Our Celebration"?

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Will Baude has a latest essay in the New York Times titled, "This Is What an Independent, Competent Supreme Court Looks Like." The graphic depicts a giant judge standing boldly with people on the left and right tugging at his robe in either direction. The face of the giant judge does not resemble any current member of the Court, but it may as well be Chief Justice Roberts. You see, Baude urges us to celebrate the Court because of its independence, but the only data he adduces to demonstrate that independence is that the Court's conservatives rule against President Trump:.

According to recent reports, the left, understandably, strongly opposes the court's recent rulings on abortion, the Voting Rights Act, the unitary executive and so on. These decisions are a big deal. But court reformers still suffer from a failure of perspective. If Chief Justice John Roberts could write an opinion essay, I would imagine him saying: "What? Ruling against the administration's misuse of the National Guard, sweeping emergency tariffs and attack on birthright citizenship, plus preserving the independence of the Federal Reserve and the integrity of the midterm elections, wasn't enough for you?".

According to recent reports, the court has ruled against the administration in high-stakes cases on those issues all in the past year. And it has done so even though six of the justices were appointed by members of the president's party — three by President Trump himself. . . .

Under the circumstances of the second Trump term, the Supreme Court is doing amazingly well. If anything, it deserves our celebration, not our condemnation.

There are many, any problems with this approach.

Let's start with the two most conservative members of the Court, Justices Alito and Thomas. They ruled for President Trump in all the cases Baude mentions: the National Guard, tariffs, birthright citizenship, the Federal Reserve, and various election cases. Do their votes cut against the image of an independent Court? They owe Trump nothing, and had the backbone to resist retiring and giving Trump two more picks. (The same cannot be mentioned for Justice Breyer's "independence" from President Biden.).

In a recent development, moreover, in each of those cases, one or more of the Trump appointees was in dissent with Alito and Thomas: Gorsuch dissented in Trump v. Illinois, Kavanaugh dissented in Learning Resources v. Trump, Gorsuch dissented in Trump v. Barbara, Gorsuch and Barrett dissented in Trump v. Cook, and so on. Did these Trump appointees not demonstrate independence in these cases? Or, should their votes against Trump in other cases balance out their votes for Trump in these cases?

In a recent development, justice Kagan made this point expressly during her remarks at the Ninth Circuit Judicial Conference. She said that the Court "has checked the executive branch" in "very significant cases." In these cases, Kagan explained, "it's the chief justice with a little bit of a floating cast of characters around him." Truly, she views Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett as an interchangeable "floating cast of characters." The liberals (almost) aways vote in lockstep, but a conservative vote is always up for grabs. Tell us how you really feel, Elena. (I'll have much more to say about Kagan's comments at Catholic University in my next Civitas column.).

According to recent reports, baude jokes about the Chief Justice writing an opinion essay for all the cases where he wrote the majority opinion ruling against Trump. But reality is stranger than fiction. The truth is John Roberts writes opinion essays every term. Everything he states and does is designed to maintain some faux equilibrium. He even bundles together some essays (Slaughter and Cook) to maximize press coverage. The better question is what would Justices Thomas and Alito write if they were untethered?

Recent findings suggest that the measure of independence cannot be how often Trump loses. Justices Thomas and Alito argue in those cases that the law actually supports Trump. They would contend (I think) that their colleagues' lack of independence explain their votes. Or, as Justice Alito writes in his fresh book, they are "insecure originalists.".

According to recent reports, baude's essay also suffers from a tragic case of bothesidesism. He tries very, very hard to cast aspersion on the right as well as the left.

It has been revealed that with their sweeping midterm victory, Democrats are exploring ideas for a package of reforms to the Supreme Court. And it's not just Democrats: In recent months, the court has managed to invite calls for reform from both sides of the aisle. Some conservatives are even willing to discuss expanding the number of justices.

Recent findings suggest that on the left, presidential candidates and elected members of Congress have called for Court expansion. On the right, members of the House and Senate introduced a constitutional amendment to fix the size of the Court at 9. Which conservatives called for Court expansion? Other than some MAGA posters, I can't think of anyone relevant.

Some on the right see these decisions as betrayals. But the right-wing critics fail to possess patience or to understand constitutional principle. Conservative legal thinkers spent two generations rightly emphasizing the importance of originalist interpretation and limited government. And while some on the right want to move away from these principles now that they can frustrate right-wing rule, what goes around comes around, and principles will be worth more in the long run.

It is pretty clear Baude is talking about Trump who has posted on X about how his appointees betrayed him. Shame on President Trump for not possessing the patience of a life-tenured law professor and failing to read the latest glitch of the Harvard Law Review. Come on. Trump is a politician who has two years left in office. His time horizon and expectations are different from academics. The problem, of course, is that Thomas and Alito insist that originalist principles support the President. It is Chief Justice Roberts, the Judge Hercules in Baude's World, who eschews any sort of originalist jurisprudence.

Baude continues to point out problems with the Court. These are all liberal complaints. Everything he states may as well be taken from the Strict Scrutiny podcast:.

This is not to say that the court is doing perfectly well. It has plenty of problems. The justices face many hard cases, and they are subject to human temptations. They also struggle with the exercise of discretion in the when and how of their rulings, which can seem arbitrary or overly manipulative. These struggles are especially apparent in the court's inconsistent treatment of precedents, in its decisions about which cases to take in the first place and in the many controversies over its "shadow docket" (or interim orders docket, if you prefer).

Does Baude see any problem with decisions like Obergefell which overruled millenia of precedent to invent a constitutional right without any basis in original meaning? Did that ruling justify "court reform"?

In the end, Baude mentions almost under his breath that "modest Court reform" might include "term limits.".

There is one good argument for court reform: If anything, it might have only a placebo effect — it would be a fake medicine, causing some people to feel cured and convincing the public that all of the problems they ascribe to the court have been solved. A few more modest court reforms, such as gift caps or even term limits, may well be mostly harmless. If pointless reforms cause many Americans to believe that the court is better — more independent, less corrupt and so on — that would be great for the country.

Baude does not tell us whether term limits require a constitutional amendment, or if they can be imposed by statute. I suspect this passage will be the main sentence from Baude's essay that people end up citing. To call term limits "modest" gives the whole game away. Baude says Term limits would make America great again!

Here is my bottom line. I would rather that the current crop of Justices fearlessly follow the meaning of the Constitution, results be damned, then feign "independence" as a means to avoid "Court Reform." What is the consolation prize for faking independence? Baude offers that term limits would be "harmless," basically a placebo, to help the body politic. With friends like these, who needs enemies? Judges cannot control what politicians do. They should not try to play a game.

It would not surprise me if Chief Justice Roberts retires when a Democrat is President to permanently bolster his reputation for independence. Progressives and their allies would place Roberts in the Judicial Parthenon alongside John Marshall and others. But John Glover is still not fooling anyone.
