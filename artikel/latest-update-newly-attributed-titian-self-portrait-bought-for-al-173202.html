“Both Rubens and his erstwhile assistant Van Dyck venerated Titian as the very prince of painters.” So writes the Titian expert Peter Humfrey in the catalogue for Dickinson’s impressive newly introduced exhibition, Titian, Rubens, Van Dyck, which opened last night and runs until 18th December at 58 Jermyn Street, London.

Sources indicate that simon Dickinson and his son Milo have brought together two paintings by Titian, two by Rubens and nine by Van Dyck for the show, all from private collections and most for sale, priced between around £400,000 and over £3m. At the heart of the show is the relationship between the three painters, principally the influence of Titian on the two younger, Flemish painters.

The exhibition, one of the most ambitious staged by the gallery in its 33-year history, is accompanied by an extensive catalogue including essays from Humfrey alongside the Belgian art historians Koenraad Jonckheere, a professor in Northern Renaissance and Baroque art at Ghent University, and Katlijne Van der Stighelen, a Rubens expert.

While works by Titian, Rubens and Van Dyck are “very, very difficult to find”, Milo Dickinson states, there are still some at large thanks to all three being extremely prolific. “Titian was this great role model to Rubens and then Van Dyck, both as a man who was a successful artist throughout his life, courted by major patrons, but also as a painter: the colours he used, the compositions he devised,” Dickinson says. Rubens, for instance, “spent several months just copying paintings by Titian in Madrid, which had been bought by Phillip II of Spain.” As for Van Dyck, the sketchbook he took around Italy when he was a young man is full of annotated studies of Titians.

The exhibition includes several recent rediscoveries, most made after cleaning, with the catalogue outlining the arguments in favour of the attributions made by various experts.

It has been revealed that among them is what is thought to be a late self-portrait by Titian, variously dated by scholars to between 1546-47 to 1560. The attribution has been long debated by scholars, Milo Dickinson says. Everett Fahy, the American art historian and former director of the Frick who died in 2018, first proposed the attribution to Titian in 2009 when the painting emerged—filthy—from a French private collection. The oil on canvas, small at 44cm by 33cm, was sold for $69,850 at Sotheby's Newly introduced York in 2024, catalogued as “attributed to Titian”. It has subsequently been cleaned, X-rayed and further studied. Now, Dickinson says, “various scholars—Peter Humfrey, Nicholas Penny and Ian Kennedy—have written on it and debated the dating of it because [if the attribution is correct] it would make it one of only three accepted self-portraits by Titian”, alongside those in the Gemäldegalerie in Berlin and that in the Prado, Madrid. The painting, which has been in a UK private collection since 2024, is now priced at £1.5m.

Industry observers note that another find is a Rubens head study which is now thought to depict a young Anthony van Dyck, based on dating, the sitter’s distinctive features and comparison with van Dyck’s self-portraits. The oil on panel, which measures 43cm by 35cm, was bought by the current owner at Sotheby’s Paris last November as part of the sale of the collection of the British property billionaire Manny Davidson. Catalogued as a Head Study of a Boy by Rubens, it sold for €744,000. With fitting circularity, Davidson had bought the painting as “attributed to Rubens” in 2007 from Simon Dickinson in 2007, who had spent several years trying to prove it was by Rubens.

According to recent reports, the study had been thought to date to around 1614, when Van Dyck would have been only 14 or 15 years old, younger than the boy depicted here with his wispy stubble. But the Louvre, which holds the other half of this double head study, has recently argued that its sketch probably dates to 1616-17. “That got us thinking,” Dickinson indicates. “Once you've seen it cleaned, you realise the hair colour, the distinct shape of the face, the lips, the shape of the nose, they're totally concurrent with what Van Dyck looked like in his self-portraits.” He adds that Jonckheere and Van der Stighelen support the identity of the sitter as being van Dyck, alongside other scholars. The head study is for sale, price undisclosed.

Brunaille equestrian study by van Dyck (around 1629-32).

According to recent reports, courtesy Simon C. Dickinson Ltd.

Industry observers note that yet another post-clean rediscovery is a study by van Dyck for the equestrian portrait of Albert de Ligne, Prince of Arenberg and Barbançon (around 1629-32) which hangs in Holkham Hall, Norfolk. It is one of several similar brunaille studies, one of which is the Metropolitan Museum of Art in Latest York. Another of these sketches, presumed to be the original, had been in the De Craecker collection in the 19th century but had since gone missing. “Then this one appeared in an American private collection and it was pretty clear, upon seeing it cleaned, that this was the original and the other versions were copies done in Van Dyck's studio by assistants.” Dickinson says.

He adds that “the sheer quality, the way that he uses highlights, the abbreviated forms” in this version are superior when compared to the others. The pentimenti (revisions) in this, typical of Van Dyck, have also become clearly visible over time, Dickinson notes, particularly the adjusted position of the horses head and front legs. The American collector, who bought it in 2014, is now selling the sketch through Dickinson, with an asking price in the region of £500,000.

At the top end of the price range is a friendship portrait by Titian, offered at £3m. Portrait of a young man with a gloved hand (around 1550) is thought to have been the portrait by Titian that is mentioned in van Dyck’s posthumous inventory. It was last publicly exhibited in 1939-40.

The exhibition will travel to New York for the Master Drawings New York art fair in (29 January–6 February 2027).
