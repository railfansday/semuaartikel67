6 ways to make homework less of a battle when your kid has ADHD

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that tonight’s homework is a single worksheet. It’s been 50 minutes. Your kid has sharpened every pencil in the house, visited the bathroom twice, and is now face-down on the floor insisting it’s impossible. You can feel your patience slipping by the second.

In a recent development, if homework battles turn your evenings into a standoff, you’re far from alone, especially if your child has ADHD. About 1 in 9 US kids have been diagnosed with the condition , and the American Academy of Pediatrics indicates it can profoundly affect a child’s academic achievement. For families managing ADHD, the after-school stretch is often the hardest part of the day.

It has been revealed that there’s a reason those hours are so trying. By the time a child with ADHD gets home,  they’ve spent “six or seven hours working harder than their classmates to sit still and focus,” says Michael Morse, MD, a board-certified child and adolescent psychiatrist who directs the ADHD & Anxiety Center at Alvord Baker and is an associate professor of psychiatry at Georgetown University. That effort is used up, and for many kids the medication has worn off too, so “the after-school brain is often a tired brain.”.

The resistance usually isn’t a matter of defiance. Executive function, the set of skills needed for getting started, planning and keeping track of steps, develops more slowly in kids with ADHD. That’s why a child can freeze in front of an assignment they’re fully capable of completing, and why the thing that helps is making the first step smaller rather than pushing harder.

Sources indicate that the worst time to start homework is the moment your child walks through the door. The school day has already drained the same systems homework demands, so Morse suggests framing the immediate after-school window as a time to refuel. “A snack with some protein comes first, because after-school hunger makes both focus and mood worse,” he states. Movement comes next, whether that’s riding a bike or walking the dog, which burns off the day’s tension and helps many kids with ADHD settle. Make the break predictable with a clear end, and pick a consistent homework time that comes after the break and well before your child is too fried to think.

Industry observers note that most importantly, hold off on screens. “The transition from screens to homework can be particularly painful,” Morse indicates, so it’s usually easier not to turn them on after school at all.

Industry observers note that “A task feels too big when a child can’t see where it starts,” Morse says. Many kids with ADHD freeze in front of a big assignment because their brain can’t break it into steps on its own, so the task is to shrink the first step until it feels easy.

According to recent reports, say a book report is due in two weeks. Sit down together and have your child break it into pieces: choose the book, read one chapter, jot notes on the main character. Then put each piece on a calendar so the deadline isn’t, in Morse’s words, “a cliff at the end.” For a single night’s work, 10 math problems can become two sets of five with a break in between. And when your child finishes a piece, say so out loud, since that small win makes the next one easier to start. (Younger kids need the pieces smaller and the check-ins more frequent.).

“Players usually appreciate coaches who run laps with them,” Morse says. He encourages parents to be “a coach and a model, not an enforcer.” In practice that can look like sitting at the kitchen table with your own work, while your child does theirs. A calm adult nearby helps many kids with ADHD get started and keep going. What a coach doesn’t do is finish the work so it comes back perfect, “because then the teacher never sees what the child can really do,” Morse says. The job is to help strategy the night and praise the effort while leaving the outcome up to your child.

Sources indicate that when homework turns into a power struggle, handing a little control back to your child can often defuse it. Offer two options you’re happy with either way, like “Math or reading first?” or “Before dinner or after, but before screens.” “A choice gives a child some control,” Morse states, which can take the fight out of the room before it starts. (If homework is still a daily battle no matter what you try, Morse notes a tutor can often take the coaching role with less friction than a parent.).

According to recent reports, some nights, the right move is to stop. “A parent should call it when the homework is no longer teaching anything or when substantial conflict emerges,” Morse notes. If your child is in tears, exhausted or stuck on the same problem despite your help, more pushing won’t produce learning. “It mostly produces a worse night and a child who dreads the next one,” he says. When you stop, have your child’s back by sending the teacher a short note about how long they worked and where they got stuck. Most teachers would rather know.

“Your relationship with your child is more vital than any assignment,” Morse says, and that relationship is what carries a kid through years of school. Kids with ADHD already hear a lot of criticism, and “if homework turns every evening into a battle, home stops feeling like the place where they’re accepted.”.

Some homework problems are too massive to solve at the kitchen table. “I’d start advocating when the problem looks bigger than effort,” Morse says. Watch for homework that routinely takes far longer than the teacher intended, work your child understands but can’t get written down or turned in, or nights that keep ending in tears. Those point to a gap between what the assignment asks and what your child can do right now, and pushing harder at home won’t close it.

Start by telling the teacher how long the work is actually taking. Some kids need more formal support through a 504 plan or an individualized education program, the school plans that put accommodations in writing. This matters especially for girls, Morse indicates, “whose late-night effort can keep their grades up and hide how much they’re struggling.”.

Industry observers note that as kids get older, Morse’s advice is to hand over the wheel gradually, with middle school as the moment to watch, when additional teachers and long-term projects arrive just as parents tend to step back. Teach your child to use a planner, check in briefly each evening, and let them take on more as they show they can. The long-term goal is teaching a kid which tasks will always need a system or a helper, because “knowing when to ask for help is one of the most valuable skills a teenager can carry into adult life.”.

In a recent development, that need goes well beyond ADHD. Accountability partners and body doubles, “people whose quiet company helps you start and stay on task,” work for nearly everyone. “Not everyone knows to ask,” Morse notes.
