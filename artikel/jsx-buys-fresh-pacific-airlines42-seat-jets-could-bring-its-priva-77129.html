JSX Buys Fresh Pacific Airlines—42 Seat Jets Could Bring Its Private Terminal Flights To More Travelers

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that JSX has acquired Latest Pacific Airlines, and intends to bring the failed airline back with 42 seat Embraer regional jets. That’s 12 more seats than JSX sells on its planes today, and it gives them a way to grow beyond the limits of their current operation. I think this fits a tech giant that’s been unusually creative about finding markets and figuring out how to serve them. They brought turboprops to Santa Monica’s shortened runway and used a free membership program to gain access to Teterboro in order to serve New York. JSX isn’t laying out the New Pacific strategy yet. Asked about the deal, they tell me: JSX is continually evaluating opportunities to support its long-term growth and bring its distinctive flying experience to more Customers and more communities. As part of that work, we are pursuing additional pathways that could provide even more ways to deliver our award-winning service in the future. Our focus remains on the exceptional experience, convenience, and hospitality our Customers have come to expect from JSX. New Pacific has filed its intent to resume flying and requested a waiver of the usual 45 day advance filing requirement. An operating projection calls for: Embraer ERJ145-LR aircraft with 42 seats. Two aircraft initially, growing to six by the fifth month. 10,106 flights and roughly 309,000 passenger boardings in the first year. Flights averaging about 1.2 hours gate to gate, with staffing growing to 104 employees. New Pacific used to be Northern Pacific, the startup that wanted to connect the mainland U.S. and Asia through Anchorage using Boeing 757s. It seemed unlikely to work anyway but the closure of Russian airspace to U.S. carriers kept them from even trying. It pivoted to short domestic routes and then sports and corporate charters. I reported its shutdown last November, just two weeks after it unveiled an all-business class venture with beOnd. The bankruptcy process disposed of its 757s. What it has instead is Part 121 certification allowing operations beyond the 30 passenger limit of JSX’s current Part 135 public charter model. I first flew JSX home from Southwest Airlines Media Day in late 2022. JSX’s 4 p.m. departure got me home for dinner, an hour ahead of the Southwest flight I would have taken. I reached the terminal 19 minutes before departure. In Austin, I was in a Lyft less than ten minutes after touchdown. The plane was comfortable, with a seat on each side of the aisle and a side table next to mine. But getting my time back was what made it worth paying more (but honestly, not much more). I didn’t need a bigger airport lounge. I needed to spend less time at the airport. By my March 2023 visit to JSX’s headquarters, they’d fixed my one real complaint: no internet. JSX was the first carrier with Starlink, and the first to finish equipping its full fleet. Notably United Airlines owns a stake in JSX. The early JSX adoption is likely related to United’s head start with Starlink among major airlines. JSX’s business depended on putting several things together: inexpensive used aircraft, a comfortable cabin, friendly service and airports that let customers avoid wasting time. At the time, airport real estate was their biggest constraint. Finding another city with people willing to buy a ticket wasn’t enough. They needed somewhere to put the operation. Look at Santa Monica. The city is literally closing the airport and shortened its runway to 3,500 feet. JSX brought in ATR 42-600 turboprops configured with 30 seats. They found an aircraft suited to an airport that’s convenient for customers on the west side of Los Angeles, and obtained permission to operate despite the local politics. At Teterboro, the solution involved how they sold tickets. The airport prohibits publicly advertised scheduled passenger service. JSX put the flights behind a Club JSX login, limited bookings to members, and marketed the destination publicly as New York City. Joining the program is free, but joining is how they gain access to the airport. Those are different solutions to different problems. With New Pacific, JSX gains the ability to operate with more than 30 seats. My guess is that they see markets where a different version of their product could work, with more passengers sharing the cost of each departure. There is a regulatory insurance benefit. American Airlines, Southwest and the big pilots union have pushed to restrict JSX’s business model. I anticipated that sort of campaign in my first review. Having a Part 121 operation would be useful if the rules change. But the Federal Aviation Administration’s current agenda lists its public charter rulemaking as a long-term action with the next step undetermined. They never closed the rulemaking effort. But I think it’s possible to overread the regulatory risk insurance strategy here. They could still use private terminals with a part 121 operation and 42 seats. Federal security rules provide for scheduled and public charter flights with 31 to 60 seats outside the usual secure terminal area. This wouldn’t work at every airport JSX presently serves due to airport certification requirements. And part 121 means different pilot requirements, including mandatory age 65 retirement (they couldn’t use their ex-American and Southwest senior captains). If they do plan 42 seats that a different product – still more generous than a 50-seat sardine can but not nearly as spacious as the current 30-seat setup JSX appears to have a plan to add 40% more seats to some of the regional jets they own, and operate them under a scheduled airline. They haven’t mentioned what they’re actually planning, but it may be a different niche than their current model rather than the assumed ‘buy a scheduled airline in case the next administration capitulates to lobbyists and puts them out of business’ the way ALPA, Southwest and American sought to do under the last administration.
