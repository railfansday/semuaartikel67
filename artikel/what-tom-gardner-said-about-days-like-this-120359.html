It has been revealed that if a glance at your portfolio this morning made you wince, the broad market shares the blame. The S&P 500 fell about 2.5% and the Nasdaq dropped more than 3.5%. The CBOE Volatility Index, often called Wall Street's fear gauge, jumped about 20%. If you own a lot of growth stocks, your account probably fell harder than the broad market.

Back in September, Tom Gardner sat down to record his sixth Quarterly Call, and he spent a good chunk of it on days like this one. He didn't predict today. Tom doesn't make market calls, and he gently scolds people who make spectacular ones. Instead, he explained why a drop like this shouldn't catch a Fool off guard, and what to do when one comes. Five ideas from his call are worth rereading before you touch the sell button.

It has been revealed that tom reminded us that the S&P 500 has historically fallen 10% about once a year. He called a pullback of 10% to 15% normal. Think of it as the toll you pay for decades of stock market returns.

He also argued the odds of a pullback were higher than usual when he recorded the call. The S&P 500 and Nasdaq were trading 10% to 30% above their typical historical valuation ranges. When stocks are priced for good news, it takes less bad news to knock them down.

In a recent development, and he warned that when the index falls, the high-growth, high-beta stocks many Fools own tend to fall further. High beta means a stock swings harder than the market in both directions. This morning followed his script, with the tech-heavy Nasdaq down about a percentage point more than the S&P 500. If your portfolio is down more than the index today, your stocks are behaving the way Tom said they would.

Recent findings suggest that "Emotional reactivity is the enemy of wealth creation," Tom remarked. A red day tempts you to make a dramatic move, like selling everything or backing up the truck.

According to recent reports, tom's alternative is incrementalism. Buy and sell in stages, and recheck your thesis at every step. His example came from Hidden Gems, which sold 15% of its CrowdStrike (CRWD -5.87%) position in the weeks before the call. Tom said the team didn't expect calamity for the business. The stock looked richly priced, so HG took some cash off the table and kept the rest of its position.

Recent findings suggest that tom argued that moving in steps builds conviction. Each partial sale makes you rethink your reasons, and the rethinking can get you out of a bad position sooner than an all-or-nothing standoff in your head. If you feel the urge to do something before the holiday, do a small something.

Tom's first recommendation for this market was to pair higher-quality companies with lower return expectations. He used Costco (COST -0.15%) to show what he means.

He thinks Costco could deliver about 10% a year plus a small dividend. Over a decade, such a pace turns $10,000 into roughly $27,000. Tom admitted a return like Costco's sounds dull to anyone who's seen a stock do 2.7 times its money in six months. So he asked members to picture Costco handing them about 10% a year for the next decade, and to decide honestly whether those returns would satisfy them. Your answer, he stated, sets the frame for how you invest going forward.

Industry observers note that lower-quality companies carrying high expectations pose the opposite danger. Tom warned they can fall 50%, 70%, or 90% and never recover. If one of those becomes one of your largest positions, it can be "quite damaging to your pursuit of financial freedom," he said. He recommended keeping steady ballast in your portfolio. Ballast is the heavy weight low in a ship's hull that keeps the ship steady in rough water.

Industry observers note that tom reaffirmed a core Foolish principle. Own 50 or more companies, and keep each for more than five years on average.

Sources indicate that his reminder of why came from a stock he owns. Rocket Lab (RKLB -7.87%) had run up to $150 a share and then been cut in half by the time of the call. Tom still believes in the business, but he called a swing of that size a reminder of "the importance of having a diversified portfolio.".

Sources indicate that even this morning, not everything fell. The small-cap Russell 2000 was up about half a percent while the Nasdaq slid.

Industry observers note that tom's final action step was about your household, and it may matter most on a day like today. He suggested a family exercise. Figure out how cheaply you could live for a month and still enjoy your life. Then ask how big your emergency fund should be. Would six months of expenses be enough, or would you sleep better with nine or 12? If your family gathers for Thanksgiving tomorrow, you'll have everyone at one table to talk it through.

It has been revealed that his goal is to simulate a recession, a market decline, or a job change before any of them shows up. "Taking the step to improve the sustainability of a high-quality life is vastly more significant and notable than squeezing out extra percentage points of returns, particularly in a richly priced market," he said.

A household with its bills covered and cash in reserve can treat a falling market as a sale. Tom described investors who get through a 20% or even 40% decline comfortably. They keep a cash position on the side and put it to work in their favorite companies at lower prices. In a reply to members in the call's comments section, Tom mentioned his own cash position sits in the 10% to 15% range.

According to recent reports, tom closed the first section of his call with a line from his father, who created the Potential Growth Indicator you'll find on our site. After a long, happy day, when the Gardner kids asked to stay up late, their dad would say, "Tomorrow's another day." Tom offered his dad's advice to investors who try to cram every win into today. It works just as well for losses.

It has been revealed that this week, tomorrow is literally another day. U.S. stock markets are closed for Thanksgiving, and Fools get a break from the ticker. A bad morning is one morning, and Fools invest for decades.
