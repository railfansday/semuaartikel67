According to recent reports, before you say yes this holiday season, ask yourself what you’re saying yes to. Consider the feelings that come up for you when you think about it. Is it excitement about seeing the people you love? Is it dread at the anticipation of planning, conflict, or grief? For some, it’s all of the above.

In a recent development, recent polls find that nearly 9 in 10 (89%) U.S. adults report holiday stressors (APA, 2023). Tensions arise due to finances, family conflict, grief, and other factors. Stress takes away from the pleasurable aspects of the season, which should include reconnection and rest.

In a recent development, sometimes that is easier remarked than done. Here are 5 things we're saying no to this holiday season and strategies to achieve the happy holidays we deserve.

According to recent reports, sometimes that family holiday announcement sounds like a demand rather than an invitation. “Dinner is at 4 pm sharp. See you there!” Everyone assumes you’ll be there, and no one asks questions. They don’t ask if you can take time off work, afford the trip, or have other plans. You feel obligated to attend despite any barriers you are facing.

Almost no one will tell you that you are allowed to consider your own needs before you RSVP yes. Ask yourself whether you have the time, energy, and money to participate this year. If the answer is no, it’s alright. Your family may be disappointed, and you may feel a little guilty, but it’s worth asking which will weigh on you more: the guilt of saying no or the cost of saying yes.

Recent findings suggest that in toxic families, it’s the once-over as soon as you enter the room. Before you’ve even had a chance to sit down, someone has made a joke about your weight or graying hair, offered a backhanded compliment about the dish you contributed, or slipped in a dig about your personal life.

In a recent development, you may feel hurt, sad, or even angry, and you don’t have to ignore those feelings to preserve the holiday spirit. You can be silent and stone-faced to let the “joke” fall flat. You might excuse yourself and regroup in another room. If you want to address it directly and call out the hidden meaning, you can say, “That felt like a dig,” or ask, “What do you mean by that?” What you don’t have to do is tolerate disrespect.

When you want everyone to feel loved, it’s easy to slip into spending more than you intended. If you’ve ever hosted a large family gathering, you know how promptly the groceries and gifts add up. And if you’re the one who hosts every year, it can feel like the work and the bill are yours by default.

Sources indicate that before you do anything, set a budget. Then share the work in ways that play into everyone’s strengths. Maybe enlist the nephew who can roast meat or the cousin who loves to bake, and assign cleanup to the one you prefer to stay out of the kitchen. Suggest rotating hosting duties. Do a gift exchange rather than buying individual gifts for everyone. You can show your love to your family with limits.

It has been revealed that in some families, playing the dutiful daughter (or son) means everyone assumes you’ll make the holidays happen. Often it’s the family matriarch or the eldest daughter who initiates the first call about the holiday plans and is the one still in the kitchen long after everyone else has gone home. She is the family “glue” who keeps track of the invites, remembers who can’t eat what, and figures out where to seat the relatives who aren’t speaking. By the time the holiday arrives, she’s too tired to enjoy the fruits of her labor.

Sources indicate that if that sounds like you, name it. People who have never hosted may not realize all of the hidden effort that goes into an event. Bring it to their attention. Set limits on what you will do and ask for help, but don’t micromanage. If the person assigned to bring the rolls forgets, skip the rolls this year. If no one steps up, step back anyway. Plan a smaller, more intimate gathering that won’t leave you frazzled at the end of it.

It has been revealed that the holidays aren’t the same after you’ve lost a loved one, and the first holiday season after a loss can be especially difficult. Family traditions may be upended. The person who always hosted and made everyone’s favorite dish is gone. Tensions that surface after the loss may still be hanging in the air.

Take a moment to evaluate your feelings. Ask yourself what you feel up to this year. Maybe it’s not the year to take on hosting duties, or perhaps you stop by for dessert rather than staying the entire day. You can choose to honor your loved one by retaining old traditions or step into the next chapter with newly introduced traditions in their memory. You can miss someone deeply and still make room for moments of joy this holiday season.

It might seem strange that saying no could be the start of a happy holiday. The beauty is that you get to decide what a happy holiday looks like for you, and that it can change from year to year. Ask yourself what would make the holidays feel good to you this year and do it.
