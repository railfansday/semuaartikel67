Why the 2021 heat dome killed some mussels and spared others

During the 2021 Pacific Northwest heat dome, mussels just a few feet apart on the same rocky shore faced very different odds of survival.

During the 2021 Pacific Northwest heat dome, a mussel’s survival came down to two things: its size and its location on the shoreline.

The heatwave brought 100-degree weather to a region used to highs in the mid-70s. Many marine organisms withered.

According to recent reports, but the mussels at Cattle Point on San Juan Island fared relatively well. Researchers built a physics-based model to predict survival from mass and heat transfer data.

“We found this survival pattern that favored small mussels high on the shore and large mussels lower on the shore,” said lead author Jack Litle from the University of Washington (UW).

Although the experts looked only at mussels, the model can apply to other shellfish elsewhere.

Sources indicate that the carpet of mussels covering the rocky promontory varies in size. Smaller mussels cluster higher up the shore, while the beefier ones sit 10 to 15 feet lower.

Before the heatwave, researchers had been collecting temperature data using biomimetic “robomussels” for decades.

Former UW graduate student Brian Helmuth developed the approach. The study’s senior author, Emily Carrington, has managed the robomussels for the past 10 years.

A small thermometer embedded in black epoxy continuously records the robomussel’s temperature.

It has been revealed that litle approached Carrington, a professor of biology at UW and his advisor, about studying the effect of extreme heat on mussels. She suggested Cattle Point, where the robomussels were already spread out.

Carrington also suggested they look at size as well as survival since she thought older, often larger mussels might tolerate heat better than young ones.

The team returned to the site one, two, and three weeks after the heatwave began and counted the dead mussels.

When the researchers analyzed the data, the results seemed underwhelming.

Overall, mortality was low and the size pattern was the opposite of what they expected.

The researchers expected most of the dead mussels high on the shore to be small and most of those lower down to be large.

The mussels that died up high were bigger than average, while those that died down low were smaller.

“I can just so clearly remember where Jack and I were standing when the first data graphs popped on the screen – it was one of those rare ‘aha moments,'” Carrington said.

Sources indicate that “We immediately began thinking about how rates of heating depend on body size. Jack got to work developing the heat budget model to show how the temperature effects of body size change with habitat.”.

Sources indicate that the explanation comes down to tides and cooling. Mussels higher on the shore get sun for nearly twice as long as those lower down.

Mussels can cool in two ways. They can “sweat” out seawater or they can rely on the wind.

In a recent development, larger mussels hold more seawater, which stretches out the window for evaporative cooling. Smaller mussels have more surface area relative to volume, which makes them better wind coolers.

The mussels that died high on the shore likely ran out of water for evaporative cooling. They were also too major for efficient wind cooling.

Those that died lower down were too small to hold enough water for extended evaporative cooling.

The model could be applied to other coastal areas with tides.

Oyster growers keep oysters low on the shore to fatten up, then they move them higher to strengthen their shells. This study could tell them how big the shells can get before heat mortality risk rises.

In the future, a similar approach could shed light on other evaporative coolers too.

“Evaporative cooling is not just a shellfish thing – amphibians, birds, and mammals do something very similar,” Litle stated.

“The biology is different, but the same physical principles apply. This same sort of thinking could be extended to different taxonomic groups.”.

This study shows that predicting who survives a heatwave from traits alone can mislead.

Recent findings suggest that body size interacts with microhabitat at the meter scale. That interaction shapes which cooling strategy is available. And it flips the direction of selection from one spot on the shore to the next.

Small mussels fared better high on the shore, while large mussels fared better lower down.

The study is published in the journal Proceedings of the Royal Society B Biological Sciences.
