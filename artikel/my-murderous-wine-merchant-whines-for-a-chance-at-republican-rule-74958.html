My murderous wine merchant whines for a chance at republican rule in Crusader Kings 3’s Silk and Silver expansion, which isn't only about the money

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, the tentacles of my trade empire snake up towards France in the west, down towards Africa in the south, and off into Hungary in the east. Yet, I still can’t reach out and grasp the throne that sits right in front of me. I’m Marco Sanudo, and the throne of the serene republic of Venice eludes me.

I’ve shed blood in its name, the blood of someone else named Marco no less. I’ve managed to avoid punishment for that crime, despite the fact it earned me a fiefdom to rule while I’ve tried to up my patrician score enough to be the lucky party the next time a ruling Doge dies in office (or runs out their 10 year term). Sadly, Doge Leonardo, the mysterious masked lord who’s had an iron grip on power since the first couple of old blokes to hold the position in this playthrough popped their clogs, is proving incredibly tough to oust.

Recent findings suggest that it should, in theory, help that he’s my best mate, thanks to a well-timed feast. It certainly did in being granted a pardon for offing that other Marco, but not even that and a plague which infected nearly everyone in the realm aside from me have paved the way to Dogedom. In the meantime, I’ve striven to gain wealth and influence. I’ve worked up to having four trade routes running at once, with goods like wine, iron, and textiles flowing from market to market across southern Europe and bringing a steady supply of coin back to my Venetian estate. I’ve built trade posts at the extremities of my routes, giving me the option of starting from further afield and reaching new lands if the cash flow dries up closer to home.

It has been revealed that this is where Crusader Kings 3’s Silk and Silver expansion has blended a bit with the game’s traditional feudal moneymaking to supercharge my coffers. The land holding I killed a dude for, the Proveditorate of Zara, means I’ve also had tax money from modern day Croatia to add to both my trade profits and the income my domicile generates on its own. I am, as the medieval kids say, minted.

According to recent reports, getting to the point of serious pocket clinkage is, as you might imagine given Silk and Silver’s focus on filling the game’s world with travelling merchant companies and wheeler-dealing republican patrician families, the beating heart of the expansion. A near-constant stream of notifications about prices of different goods I’m trying to hawk in different locations encourage me to regularly prune and re-work my routes so that max profits are being earned and no carrying capacity’s being wasted. The bubbling up of wars and plagues impacts prices in affected cities, making it a never-ending battle if you’re desperate to optimise everything within an inch of its life. I’ve found that degree of micromanagement unnecessary if the goal is just to keep a decent profit margin ticking over. Just remember to replace the trader assigned to a route if they die, recall them every few years so they don’t stress themselves to death, and check in every so often to re-evaluate whether you’re running goods that are suitably in demand.

If you stick at it for a decent amount of time, with a certain type of good - alcohol or jewellery, for example - your expertise in dealing in them increases, making it easier to sell for better prices. Trade routes left running between the same markets also gain boons from being in place for a long time, offering a counterpoint to rejigging them every five seconds to wring out a few more coins. Most of my routes have run along a path that includes stops at four markets, with the occasional location boasting a monopoly I’ve bargained for with the local ruler, so I don’t have to worry about competition at every stop.

Recent findings suggest that when you’re playing as a merchant who isn’t locked into the ruling inner circle of a republic, making your routes as lengthy as possible is one of the three moneymaking areas your company can specialise in. That first one’s caravaneering, and is the one I leant into in a non-Marco Sanudo playthrough. Beyond that, you can become an expert loan shark, lending cash to rulers or paying their ransoms in order to recoup interest and collect hooks you can use for favours. Manufacturing expensive artifacts that can be sold individually to collectors for a profit is the third option. The latter two I’ve only dabbled in a little bit as of writing, having earned enough gold mostly from caravanneering to buy the land containing the city with my home market and be anointed a Sheikh. So, beginning as a landless merchant is a nice alternative to Crusader Kings 3’s long-established wandering adventurer starting point, with both allowing you to work your way up from lowly serf to landed lordly dynasty in your location of choice.

If you’re after a more specific historical scenario, Silk and Silver has Guelphs and Ghibellines. This is where we find Marco Sanudo, as well as the other patrician family head I’ve played as thus far, Speronella Dalesmanni. The setup is that the Pope and the Holy Roman Emperor have had a bit of a tiff, essentially rooted in who should have certain powers, with different cities and powerful families in northern Italy taking either the Guelph (Pope-likers) or Ghibelline (Holy Roman Empire-enjoyers) side depending on what suits their goals or principles. I’ll be honest, aside from being another way to easily earn friends or enemies in the ruthless crucible of merchant republican politics, where votes and reputation-bolstering aid from other nobles are key, the situation’s not had a hugely obvious bearing on my playthroughs thus far.

In a recent development, I think the closest it’s come is during my Dalesmanni run, if largely due to the existence of the By God Alone expansion allowing a fresh branch of Catholicism, dubbed Italian Christianity, to be created. The initial conversion push from the bishop responsible led a number of my fellow northern Italian nobles away from the Roman Rite headed by his Popeliness, making the Guelph/Ghibelline split more clearly defined.

In the early hours of both my Sanudo and Dalesmanni playthroughs, I’ve been far more focused on simply trying to push the respective patrician families up the hill from posh toffs with naught but a vote and a manor to elected rulers of their great cities, with land holdings to match. Speronella’s situation in Padua is made incredibly tough by the fact she’s a woman in a world where every succession rule is designed to put blokes first, so I’ve had more success with Marco and his quest to become the Doge of Venice.

According to recent reports, as the head of a patrician family, one of select few elite clans who steer the oligarchical republic, I was faced with earning Marco enough respect among his fellow family heads that they’d consider voting him to the top of the succession list, thus securing his power whenever the current Doge got the boot. Pulling that off, while also not kowtowing to the requests and schemes of those same peers, isn’t easy. A large cocktail of factors go into determining whether another noble likes you enough to vote in support of your succession. There’s your patrician rating, which is upped by doing things which specifically show you to be good at this Italian noble house lark, such as establishing trade monopolies for the republic, gaining the Pope’s favour, or having close relatives who also hold key positions in the republic. However, how close a friendship you have with the target and how their character traits align with you also play a huge rule.

Sources indicate that thus it was that, at one point, Marco was best mates with the current Doge and had a decent patrician rating, but had only amassed 68 of the 72 points needed for noted Doge to vote for him because the Doge was generous, and therefore didn’t care for Marco’s ambitiousness. That same Doge either liked or respected Marco enough to grant him a council position and the status of regent, but he would not back Marco to replace him permanently for love nor money. This is the same man who liked Marco so much that when I pulled off that not-so-sneaky murder on another noble house head in order to inherit the keys to the Proveditorate of Zara, the Doge was instantly willing to pardon me and prevent Marco suffering any punishment for the crime.

Industry observers note that not even a couple of serious plagues hitting Venice, which at one point had all three people ahead of Marco in the queue suffering from smallpox, could get him on that throne. Eventually, it took a bit of lucky timing when his Doge mate died of old age - a few years after beating smallpox - to finally install Marco as Doge. Given that delayed promotion, as of writing I’ve not yet had enough time in the big chair to give a proper verdict on how it differs from regular feudal rulerdom in the long run.

Recent findings suggest that what I can do instead is tell you a bit about the fun of council votes. The same pool of voters who in a republic like Venice, get a say in who succeeds the ruler, also vote on the passage of laws and decrees. One proposal you can put forward is a vote to change who exactly is allowed a seat in this voting pool. It defaults to just being heads of patrician families, but can be extended to include all vassals, or tightened up so only the Doge’s councillors or the ruler themselves have a say, that being the way despotism lies. As you can imagine, passing a change to who can vote can drastically change the state of play, either tightening up the number of folks who might oppose or support you, or bringing new folks into play in a fashion that dilutes individual power, but gives you more pieces on the board to mind or manipulate to your advantage.

I’ve found it suitably tough to orchestrate successful votes to change laws and customs which medieval Italian noble families, expectedly, have firmly entrenched stances on. Getting rid of checks and balances, such as the requirement to vote on banishments or wars - so the Doge can rule as a despot - has proven one such challenge, as has changing laws governing men being prioritised over women. I tried to pass equal succession as a substitute for men being put first while playing as Speronella, but met an insurmountable wall of resistance due to the region’s culture and faith. So, all I could do was try to put my young male heir in the best position possible to make the sorts of gains I couldn’t.

Industry observers note that much easier to weasel into place, either through some canny swaying or the offering of hooks, are laws giving state backing or other priority to trading in certain types of goods. Alcohol, for instance, which certainly hasn’t hurt the profits of Marco’s two wine trade routes.

Recent findings suggest that thus, cash continues to flow through the tentacles of my trade empire in sizable globs. As he’s now Doge, I’ve got Marco using the ample funds of Venice’s treasury to build a fresh piazza and have him focused on making the city’s already-strong trade league even more formidable, by attracting new members from the few other northern Italian blocs.

Industry observers note that while I’m fairly sure there are folks who’ll find Silk and Silver's constant barrage of trade route notifications annoying, or that their power-grabbing skills and sheer multitude of money-making means combine to make playthroughs less challenging than they could be, so far I've found it another enjoyably sonorous string to Crusader Kings 3’s bow. Above all else, playing as a merchant gives more reason to invest in stewardship skills beyond just maximising your tax yields, while the republican politics of the DLC's main region fleshes out rewarding intrigue-focused builds. Diplomacy and martial skills are less key, but still have their place at times.

If you’re an absolute stickler for historical accuracy, you may well find other things to gripe about, but aside from the Guelph and Ghibelline conflict fading into the background, I’ve found plenty to like in my quest to become top Doge.
