A wave of 'pent-up quits' could boost turnover in the job market, expert says: 'The labor market is a bit like a coiled spring'

Frustrated workers could trigger a wave of turnover, creating a shift in the sluggish job market, according to experts at the job boards Indeed and Glassdoor.

Industry observers note that that sluggishness, or what labor economists call a low-hire, low-fire economy, means that fewer people are getting hired, and fewer workers are leaving their jobs — even as a growing share of job holders report frustration with their career opportunities. In 2026, 21% of workers report feeling dissatisfied with their career prospects, according to Glassdoor reviewer data. Dissatisfied workers rated their jobs at a one or two out of five stars.

It has been revealed that A stagnant job market can discourage even the unhappiest of workers from quitting, but hiring could soon ramp up. Indeed's Job Posting Index, or JPI, which tracks the level of job postings on Indeed relative to a pre-pandemic baseline, saw year-over-year growth for the first time in September 2026 since 2022. The upward trend suggests that the job market is "heating up," Glassdoor chief economist Daniel Zhao tells CNBC Make It.

As more opportunities open up, job holders might regain the confidence to exit, turning into "a wave of quits and turnover," Zhao notes. Turnover could also help those who are out of a job secure employment as roles that were once filled become vacant.

In a recent development, "The labor market is a bit like a coiled spring right now," Zhao remarked at a press event Tuesday in New York City. "There's a lot of pent-up potential energy, and it's unclear exactly which way that that spring is going to release that energy.".

In a healthy job market, employee dissatisfaction can forecast turnover rates. When employee dissatisfaction reached 17% in April 2022, per Glassdoor reviews, quit rates also reached their highest levels at 3%, seasonally adjusted, according to data from Bureau of Labor Statistics Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey data published by the St. Louis Federal Reserve.

According to recent reports, however, when workers feel anxious about their future job prospects, turnover can stall. Though workers feel dissatisfied with their jobs at higher rates in September 2026 than they did in April 2022, only 2.1% of workers quit their job in the month, according to the BLS JOLTS.

According to recent reports, "There aren't those opportunities to climb the career ranks internally or externally," Zhao states of the job market. "There's no outlet for the frustration or dissatisfaction that [workers] might be feeling.".

Sources indicate that employee turnover was one focus of Indeed and Glassdoor's 2027 Hiring and Worklife Trends Report, published Wednesday. Their research, which assumed a consistent relationship between worker dissatisfaction and resignations based on historical data, found that the quit rate is 0.22% lower than what employee sentiment would typically suggest. While the figure seems small, it is equivalent to an additional 349,000 quits per month.

"Workers are very hesitant to quit, especially without something lined up, because hiring is very sluggish, and long-term unemployment is looming over them," Zhao tells CNBC Make It.

Recent findings suggest that while unemployment levels are stable, people are remaining jobless for longer, according to data from the BLS. Those who face long-term unemployment, job seekers who have been out of work for 27 weeks or more, accounted for 27.6% of all unemployed people in October 2026.

Even if more jobs open, turnover across sectors could vary. Industries that are highly exposed to artificial intelligence like software development, engineering and banking previously dragged Indeed's JPI down, meaning employers in these sectors posted fewer jobs. Indeed's data shows that postings in these sectors have since seen growth, and could result in what experts call "pent-up quits," which occur when dissatisfied workers leave their jobs once market conditions improve. Job posts on Indeed for employers in industries with less AI exposure like healthcare have softened, according to experts.

However, business and professional services, which includes banking, shed 27,000 jobs in October 2026, according to data from ADP Research.

"As we look ahead to 2027, that increase in demand might not necessarily translate into more hires, and it also might not be enough hires to give workers the confidence that they can actually quit their jobs and move on to something better," Zhao says.

Even as the job market appears to be trending in a positive direction, further economic pressure could continue to place a strain on employers and their hiring plans.

"Nobody was really expecting 2026 to come with the geopolitical challenges that have happened and the the impact that has had on energy prices and inflation," Zhao indicates. "And so there's always the outside chance that something happens in 2027 that derails the job market from the path that it's on.".
