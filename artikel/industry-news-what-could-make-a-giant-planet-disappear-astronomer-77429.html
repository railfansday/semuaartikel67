Industry News: What could make a giant planet disappear? Astronomers may have the answer

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A giant planet stopped crossing its star as seen from Earth, and astronomers suspect an unseen neighbor tipped its orbit.

It has been revealed that astronomers had been watching a giant planet pass in front of its star for years. Then, in March 2026, TESS looked again and saw no sign of a crossing.

Recent findings suggest that the planet, TOI-2535 b, hasn’t gone anywhere. Its orbit has tipped, most likely from the pull of a second planet in the same system, one that astronomers haven’t detected directly.

TOI-2535 b, more than 1,000 light-years away, is the first confirmed planet around a single star that astronomers have seen stop crossing.

Samuel Yee, a postdoctoral fellow at UCLA, led the study with astrophysicist Erik Petigura.

Each of those crossings is called a transit. “The planet’s orbit is tilting so quickly that its transit disappeared while we were watching,” Yee mentioned.

When a planet passes between its star and Earth, it blocks a little of the starlight.

The star dims for a few hours, and that small dip is how NASA’s Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS) finds fresh worlds.

A transit only shows up if the star, the planet and Earth line up almost perfectly.

If the orbit tips by just a few degrees, the planet passes above or below the star as seen from Earth, and the star never dims.

TESS checked on the star, called TOI-2535, five times between April 2019 and March 2026.

In a recent development, the planet orbiting it is a hot Jupiter, a gas giant that orbits very close to its star. It goes around once every 8.24 days. Mercury takes 88 days, more than 10 times as long.

To confirm the planet, the team used an instrument on the 11.5-foot (3.5-meter) WIYN telescope at Kitt Peak National Observatory in Arizona.

The experts tracked how the star shifted back and forth, and the measurements ruled out a small star.

Industry observers note that most planets that cross their stars keep a steady schedule. TOI-2535 b’s crossings came as much as six hours early or late. No hot Jupiter that circles its star in under 10 days has a bigger timing swing on record.

Over the years, the crossings grew shorter and blocked less starlight.

A planet blocks less light, for less time, as its path moves from the middle of a star toward its edge.

Over seven years, the path likely moved across more than half of the star’s face. It passed the middle, grazed the edge, then missed the star entirely.

From the ground, the team tried 15 times and caught a crossing only six times, because the planet kept arriving when it wasn’t expected.

When TESS looked again in March 2026, it found no crossing anywhere in the window where the planet could have shown up.

Industry observers note that so what’s pulling on it? Planets in the same system tug on each other. If two orbits are tilted relative to one another, those tugs slowly swing the direction each orbit faces.

Recent findings suggest that the team’s computer models suggest the neighbor is at least as massive as Saturn, so it’s probably a second giant planet. The two likely move in a simple rhythm, such as one circling twice for every lap of the other.

By the models, the two orbits must be tilted at least 5 degrees apart to turn this fast.

In most tightly packed systems, orbits stay within 1 to 2 degrees of each other. If this tilt holds up, the system will be one of only a handful so far out of line.

Recent findings suggest that “We think of the universe as resembling clockwork, where everything is regular, but that’s not true. If there are multiple planets in a system, they can affect each other’s orbits,” Yee remarked.

“We know of a few systems that are like this. This is the most extreme example we’ve seen because the two planets are interacting so strongly.”.

Petigura used Earth’s moon to explain it.

“If the moon’s orbit around the Earth was perfectly aligned with the Earth’s orbit around the sun, we would see an eclipse every month,” Petigura mentioned.

It has been revealed that “But because the moon’s orbit is tilted, eclipses are less common. The tilt of the moon’s orbit is a relic of its violent formation history, which resulted from a collision with the early Earth.”.

The same geometry decides when TOI-2535 b will be back. The team calculated that each orbit goes through a full cycle every 30 to 50 years.

Recent findings suggest that the researchers expect the planet to start crossing its star again within that span. Its hidden neighbor could start crossing even sooner.

Early planet formation models suggested giant planets need lots of ice to form. So hot Jupiters probably formed far from their stars and moved closer later.

Industry observers note that how? Yee and Petigura see two ways. One is violent. A near-collision knocks the planet onto a stretched-out orbit, and the star pulls it in.

Industry observers note that the other is slow. The planet drifts inward with other planets through the gas and dust around a young star. This way should leave the orbits flat.

If the suspected giant neighbor is there, the two planets likely drifted inward together through the gas disk around their young star.

In a recent development, “Because TOI-2535 b has a close neighbor, it probably migrated by slowly spiraling in instead of getting flung in,” Yee said. “We may be seeing a formation channel that doesn’t happen very often but is very interesting.”.

Recent findings suggest that the tilt is still a loose end. The researchers think parts of the system may have tugged on each other later, tipping the orbits over time.

Recent findings suggest that hundreds of hot Jupiters are known around other stars. Few have a planet close enough to change their orbits. When one does, the neighbor is almost always much smaller. The only other known exception is WASP-148.

The team can’t say how massive the neighbor is or where it orbits. The crossing times also scattered more than the models predicted.

According to recent reports, the star’s back-and-forth motion was jumpier than expected for an old, calm star, too. Both clues could mean there’s more than one unseen planet.

TESS’s first four years of data alone couldn’t have clearly shown the shrinking crossings.

According to recent reports, with no crossings left to time, the researchers wrote that the best way forward is to measure the star’s back-and-forth motion often. A neighbor as heavy as the models suggest should show up in that data on its own.

The full study was published in the journal The Astrophysical Journal Letters.
