Most Airports Cap Concession Prices… But The System Is A Total Joke

Travelers are conditioned to expect high prices at airport retailers and restaurants, so over $5 for a bottle of water at an airport shop, or $25 for a burger at a mediocre airport restaurant, might not catch anyone off guard.

It has been revealed that however, did you know there are caps on how much retailers at airports are supposed to be able to charge, and they’re largely just not enforced? Well, or perhaps more accurately, they are enforced, but using very dishonest math. The backstory of this is interesting.

Airports generate revenue in a variety of ways, and one of the major ways is through the retailers they have in the terminal. An airport is like a shopping mall with a very captive audience, and as you’d expect, rent for businesses in airports is absolutely through the roof, as a result of that.

But in order to balance things out and prevent retailers from exploiting consumers, over 80% of airports in the United States specifically stipulate the maximum prices that airports can charge consumers. These terms are typically baked into the contracts that airport retailers sign, and failure to adhere to the rules can lead to consequences, at least in theory.

If there are supposed to be rules in place capping airport pricing, then why is this such a widespread issue? More Perfect Union recently published a great video investigating this issue, and it’s a fascinating watch. First they actually analyze airport pricing, and then they confront airport authorities about the lack of enforcement.

It has been revealed that here’s what’s interesting — airport retailers do have to submit comparisons for benchmark pricing, to prove that they’re not violating rules and exploiting customers. The issue is what kind of businesses are considered comparable for the purposes of “street pricing.”.

For example, 9NEWS recently investigated this at Denver Airport, finding that a basic Starbucks coffee cost $4.55 in the airport, compared to $2.95 outside the airport (a 54% markup), and a bag of cashews cost $9.99 in the airport, compared to $4.74 outside the airport (a 110% markup).

The airport’s VP of Concessions agreed to sit down with the reporter to discuss this, and she said the results of his investigation were great news — she suggested that 93% of the prices were compliant, and said “that’s pretty darn good.” How could that be, given the high markups compared to nearby businesses?

This is how we take care of our passengers, by ensuring that they are paying fair and transparent pricing, while supporting our business partners.”.

Back to the original story, the More Perfect Union reporter confronted the Port Authority of Upcoming York and New Jersey about pricing like $6.89 for one liter of Smart Water, compared to closer to $3.50 at nearby bodegas.

The response was that “comparing a Hudson to a bodega isn’t appropriate,” because “Hudson is a specialty market.” The approved price comparison was reportedly based on the price of water at Tablet Works, a “wellness & concierge pharmacy” in a high-end mall.

The other thing to keep in mind is that a large percentage of airport retailers and restaurants are run by the same few companies, like HMSHost, which is a subsidiary of Avolta. So clearly they have a centralized approach to submitting pricing comparisons, and know exactly what they’re doing. Interestingly, the few independent businesses in some airports were found to have the most honest pricing.

So to sum it up, the system is designed to be rigged. Airport authorities can technically pretend they’re looking out for consumers by setting a cap. Retailers can submit pricing comparisons to the highest prices they can find anywhere. Then airport authorities verify those prices to pretend they’re doing their jobs.

In a recent development, airports are obviously complicit in all of this. They make money by charging sky high rent, and the reason they can do that is because retailers can charge hugely inflated prices. The more profits retailers make, the more airports can charge in rent. And they know that no consumer is going to avoid an airport because of high concession costs.

Around 80% of airports in the United States cap the prices that retailers can charge, so in most cases, you’re not supposed to be paying more than 15% above “street pricing.” The glitch is the liberties that businesses can take with what constitutes “street pricing.”.

Recent findings suggest that the price of the bottle of water or smoothie you’re buying isn’t compared to what you’d pay at a nearby gas station and smoothie shop, but instead, is being compared to what you’d pay at Erewhon. Talk about a very broken system, with no incentives for any party to actually be honest….
