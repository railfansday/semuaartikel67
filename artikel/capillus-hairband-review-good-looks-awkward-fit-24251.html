Capillus HairBand Review: Good Looks, Awkward Fit

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Excepting the few models that look like regular hats, most red-light therapy for hair growth devices look … well, pretty weird. From stormtrooper helmets to headpieces that look like they were designed for Geordi La Forge, these aren’t exactly accessories you’d want to wear in public. Which is why it was nice to unbox the Capillus HairBand and find, at long last, a normal-looking headband.

Discreet black, fabric-covered, and sized like a normal hair accessory, the HairBand’s only indication it might do something more than hold back hair is a little 2 x 1-inch battery pack that sits jauntily cocked to one side. It’s refreshing to finally see a red-light device that doesn’t embarrass the user or frighten animals and young children, but after testing it for over a month, I found the HairBand has its share of shortcomings for the price.

Recent findings suggest that interestingly, Capillus makes a series of full-coverage devices with laser diodes built into what looks like a normal black baseball cap. However, the cheapest of these hats runs $1,699, and the most expensive “triple-wavelength” model, with 312 laser diodes, runs $3,299. The 2-inch-wide Capillus HairBand provides a similar laser diode treatment for less, at $599 with only 82 diodes (650 nm). (Capillus says the headband is designed to prioritize areas where hair loss is most noticeable, such as the hairline and temples.).

Unlike straight LED lights, laser diodes provide collimated beams of light that travel in a straight line, allowing for more focused and intense treatment of hair follicles. I actually saw some success with this treatment when I tested the HairBand’s closest competitor, Hairmax’s LaserBand 82 ComfortFlex ($849). It couldn’t help follicles that were scarred or had already died, but studies have shown that red-light therapy can energize follicles that have more recently become dormant.

I did see some growth in the middle of my part with the LaserBand, and while I stopped using it to switch to the HairBand exclusively for the past five or so weeks, I admit this growth did make it difficult to isolate the HairBand’s contribution, especially since the headband’s coverage area is so small. Thus, the following observations are mainly on the wearing experience of the HairBand, not hair growth.

It has been revealed that at only 2 inches wide and five minutes per section, the HairBand took at least 15 minutes to cover my entire part, compared with the 3-inch-wide LaserBand, which also only needs to be used three times per week at 30 seconds per section for a total of 90 seconds. I’m a Type-A full-time working parent in my mid-forties, and let me tell you—15 minutes of doing nothing is a long time in my world.

It has been revealed that the HairBand has teeth and could sit on my head as long as it was in a normal headband position, right in the middle, but the weight of the battery pack pulls it forward ever so slightly, so if I was leaning over or doing anything that didn’t hold my head exactly straight, it would start to slip off. This meant I had to hold it with my hand, which meant no multitasking.

Capillus’ marketing materials use words like “ritual” and feature pictures of twentysomethings admiring themselves in the mirror, but I can’t imagine these women are suffering from the perimenopausal hair loss that would necessitate use of a red-light device in the first place.

According to recent reports, I don’t consider myself to have an overly large head and have never had trouble with hats or other hair accessories, but this headband, which weighs only 6 ounces, felt quite small and somewhat precarious on my head overall. I should note it is also rather overtly feminine in style—my husband and I were able to share “custody” of the LaserBand, but he declined to wear this one, and I didn’t blame him.

There were two other concerns during my testing. One, the back of the box said it contained quick-start instructions, but my box only had the headband and USB-C charging cord—no physical user guide to be found (except online, but I had to do some googling). Not that one is needed, since it’s pretty obvious how it works—push the start button on the battery pack, and it will beep and shut off when the five minutes are up—but it’s an odd omission at this price point.

In a recent development, two, this device is not presently FDA-cleared for treating hair loss or promoting regrowth. This isn’t ideal, but FDA-cleared is much different from FDA-approved; it simply requires the manufacturer to prove its product is “substantially equivalent” to an already FDA-cleared product. (And it doesn’t ensure results, just that it’s safe to use as directed.) Capillus’ rep tells me the brand is currently working on the FDA filing for the HairBand, which was released in September of this year, and intends to have it cleared in 2027. (The brand’s cap-style devices are FDA-cleared.) Even so, the HairBand is a unique device in this space, but if I’m spending that much on a red-light device, I would prefer a little more versatility in the design.
