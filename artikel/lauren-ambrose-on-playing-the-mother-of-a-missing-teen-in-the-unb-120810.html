Industry observers note that lauren Ambrose sounds almost embarrassed to admit that she was puzzled the first time she read the script for Nick Payne’s “The Unbelievers.” “I was like, ‘Oh my God, what is this? I don’t understand this at all,'” Ambrose told IndieWire, scrunching up her face in a mask of bewilderment. “What’s he getting at?”.

The drama, which is wrapping up its limited run at the Manhattan Theatre Club this month, is elliptical, moving backward and forward in time as it follows Miriam’s search for her missing son, Oscar. That description sounds like a thousand thrillers, but “The Unbelievers” resists easy classification — it’s a narratively daring story, overflowing with ideas about grief and faith and family. Sources indicate that it was only after doing a workshop for the play that Ambrose discovered that what she initially found so alienating about “The Unbelievers” was actually its greatest strength. “There’s a lot to talk about, and a lot to parse, and a lot to think about, and it’s highly interpretable and analyzable, and I think that’s actually a marker of good art is that it can be interpreted in many ways,” Ambrose said over Zoom, hours before she was set to take the stage again.

The role of Miriam is almost Everest-like in its difficulty. As the years pass without any sign of Oscar — not knowing if he’s been kidnapped or murdered or ran away — Miriam becomes more obsessed, more unmoored by the lack of resolution. That strains her relationship with her two daughters and Oliver’s father, who are processing their grief in different ways and worry that Miriam is growing fanatical in her insistence that he may still be out there.

Sources indicate that “It’s fun in a perverse way to go on this crazy journey every night,” Ambrose remarked. “There are all these wild turns. It’s tiring vocally. My body hurts, my neck is messed up. But it’s amazing to get to play a part like this. You just have to throw yourself off a cliff.”.

Ambrose made a name for herself in films like “Can’t Hardly Wait” and on “Six Feet Under,” where she played Claire Fisher, a rebellious dreamer growing up in her family-run funeral home. The show debuted in 2001 on HBO, where it aired alongside “The Sopranos” and “Sex and the City” at the peak of the cable channel’s cultural supremacy. Decades later, “Six Feet Under” has been rediscovered on streaming by Gen Z, giving it an unexpected surge in popularity.

Sources indicate that “It was a golden age,” Ambrose remarked. “We were making these hour-long films each week. And HBO was so open and supportive of creative people. The attitude was, if you want to try something, try it. There weren’t algorithms determining everything. That freedom is palpable with the series, so I think that’s why it’s resonating and why it’s being embraced by this new generation.”.

Ambrose remains in demand on the big and small screen, recently appearing on another water-cooler hit, Showtime’s “Yellowjackets.” But she’s made a point of returning to the stage throughout her career, appearing on Broadway in “Exit the King” and “Awake and Sing!,” as well as earning a Tony Award nomination playing Eliza Doolittle in a revival of “My Fair Lady.”.

In a recent development, “I love the stage — I love it so much,” Ambrose said. “I love a rehearsal room. It’s my favorite place in the world. It’s where you’re figuring it out and making connections. But a play is always a living thing. We’ve opened ‘The Unbelievers,’ but I haven’t stopped my work of trying to scavenge around and figure out this mysterious character.”.
