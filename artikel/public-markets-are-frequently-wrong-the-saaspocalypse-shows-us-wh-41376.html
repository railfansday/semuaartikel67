Public Markets Are Frequently Wrong. The SaaSpocalypse Shows Us Why

🌐 Source: Crunchbase • 📅 19 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 5 min read (993 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways
  • Panics and pile-ons are a familiar pattern in the public markets, with a herd mentality wiping value from companies in an instant.
  • We have seen this play out with AI’s impact on software companies and the supposed end of the Software as a Service business model.
  • The SaaSpocalypse didn’t reveal the end of software.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Panics and pile-ons are a familiar pattern in the public markets, with a herd mentality wiping value from companies in an instant. Trading can swiftly become a barometer of emotions, rather than a well-informed gauge of value.

We have seen this play out with AI’s impact on software companies and the supposed end of the Software as a Service business model. The doom-laden talk of the SaaSpocalypse was noise, with little signal, and most traders were initially unable to distinguish between a firm that will thrive in the AI age and one that will be decimated by it.

The SaaSpocalypse didn’t reveal the end of software. It revealed the limits of public markets in judging its value.

This pattern erodes trust in the public markets. For companies, being publicly owned may no longer be appealing, and founders considering an exit may not view an initial public offering as the ultimate goal.

According to recent reports, the markets have struggled to keep up with the tech advances of the AI era and judge their impact on incumbent companies. Sometimes they underreacted and ignored these developments, and other times they overreacted and panicked.

It has been revealed that when OpenAI debuted ChatGPT to the public in November 2022, Alphabet stock was punished even though later Google emerged as a clear winner with generative AI. And with SaaS, the markets were slow to respond to the advent of generative and agentic AI through to the end of 2025.

According to recent reports, the market capitalization of enterprise software companies continued to rise through this period, until January 2026 when all hell broke loose. The launch of Claude Cowork triggered a panic, especially when additional plug-ins were announced later that month that targeted specific workflows for enterprise support functions such as HR and legal.

Sources indicate that from there, valuations went into a tailspin, with traders treating software companies indiscriminately. Over two days, an estimated $285 billion of market capitalization was wiped from software, data analytics and financial tech companies, and by April the benchmark cloud software index — the BVP Nasdaq Emerging Cloud Index —  had dropped by 31% since the start of the year.

It has been revealed that as an investor in software over many decades, I have seen this pattern many times before. AI does not mean the end of software companies. Rather, AI has driven a shift in how software is built, where user growth will come from — primarily agents rather than humans — and in the sources of defensible competitive advantage. This was always going to benefit some companies, like those deeply embedded into their customers’ core business processes, and harm others.

According to recent reports, there were predictions of the death of SaaS, but this is the hype cycle pattern that always plays out when upcoming technology emerges: a surge of excitement, then a correction, then productivity. In the short term, the markets overestimated the harm AI would inflict on the software sector as a whole.

That is now being corrected as the markets start to realize which companies benefit from AI and which are threatened by it. At the end of October, the cloud software index was up by 26% since the start of the year and by a massive 83% since its nadir in April.

In a recent development, at my firm, Oxx, we never believed in the SaaSpocalypse, and the markets have recovered from this turmoil. The recovery is bifurcated with some sub sectors, like cybersecurity and data and analytics, faring well while others, like workflow management, have fallen back.

In cybersecurity, a great example is CrowdStrike, whose share price dropped by 27% in early 2026 but was up 114% between the start of the year and the end of October.

Industry observers note that the knee-jerk reactions to the latest developments with AI, however, are not just temporary market sentiment. They are part of a broader shift that has occurred since the late 1990s and accelerated in 2002 when the Sarbanes-Oxley Act was introduced to improve investor protections in the wake of the dot com crash.

It produced the unintended consequence of driving manufacturer ownership from public to private markets. While the number of public companies in the U.S. has halved, private markets have grown dramatically in recent years. This is where much of the smart money can be found, along with a more accurate, and patient, assessment of software companies’ value.

The panic over the SaaSpocalypse has shown that the public markets aren’t an accurate reflection of the intrinsic value of many software companies. This disconnect may be causing a systematic mispricing of private tech giant transactions — multiple transactions which had been in process early this year fell away in the wake of the false signals which came from public markets. This needs to be addressed. At the moment, few people recognize there’s a problem with the pricing function of public markets, and the first step to solving this problem is to recognize there is actually a problem.

Richard Anton is the co-founder and general partner of Oxx, a specialist software investor backing European founders on a mission to transform their industries with AI at the moment they’re readying to scale.
