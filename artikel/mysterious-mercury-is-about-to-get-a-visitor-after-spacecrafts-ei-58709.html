Mysterious Mercury is about to get a visitor - after spacecraft's eight-year epic voyage

Why is a spacecraft flying to Mercury?

After an eight-year journey travelling almost six billion miles (10bn km), a spacecraft called BepiColombo is about to reach its final destination and get into orbit around Mercury.

It will be entering an extremely hostile environment: Mercury is the closest planet to the Sun and temperatures can reach a fierce 430C and then plunge to -180C, and it is constantly blasted with intense solar radiation.

Until now, the planet has been little explored but the hope is this mission will solve some of Mercury's major mysteries.

These include uncovering why there is ice in its craters despite its proximity to the Sun, and understanding the mysterious hollows that are dotted all over its surface.

"I think it's a little bit of an under-loved planet in the inner Solar System, but I think that's one of the reasons why it is so compelling," noted Prof Emma Bunce, from Leicester University, who is a principal investigator on the mission.

"There are lots of reasons, scientifically, why we really want to understand Mercury, how it formed, how it evolved, what it looks like today, but also how it interacts with the Sun and the extreme space weather that it experiences.".

The spacecraft - just seen here - has taken selfies as it has closed in on Mercury.

The BepiColombo mission, which is a collaboration between the European Space Agency (ESA) and Japanese Space Agency (JAXA), blasted off in 2018, launching from French Guiana.

With the spacecraft heading deep into the inner Solar System, it has had to battle the Sun's immense gravitational pull.

So it has had its brakes on for the entire journey, and has also taken a very convoluted path - looping once around the Earth, twice around Venus and six times around Mercury - to slow it down enough to get into orbit.

This journey should have taken seven years, but a problem with BepiColombo's propulsion system meant the already epic voyage was extended by a year.

The final manoeuvre to get into orbit is a perilous one: it needs to perform a 37-minute handbrake turn to be grabbed by the planet's gravity.

If anything goes wrong, the spacecraft could hurtle past Mercury.

A close up view of the scarred surface of Mercury.

Only two spacecraft have visited Mercury until now - both Nasa missions.

Mariner-10 flew past the planet three times in the 1970s, and then the Messenger spacecraft arrived at Mercury in 2011 and spent four years in orbit.

But there is still much to discover about this tiny world.

Mercury is only a bit bigger than our Moon, and is covered in ancient volcanoes, towering cliffs and colossal craters.

And time on Mercury passes in a mind-bending way.

Recent findings suggest that the planet zips around the Sun so quickly, a year on Mercury lasts just 88 Earth days. But it turns incredibly slowly on its own axis, so a single day there lasts 59 Earth days.

These strange hollows have not been seen anywhere else in the Universe.

As well as investigating why Mercury has ice in its craters in the polar regions, the mission will also spend time studying strange dents in the planet's surface.

There are thousands of hollows all over it. They are shallow but with steep sides and often clustered together, and in planetary terms, they are young: about 100,000 years old.

Scientists know they are not impact craters - larger pockmarks on the surface caused by colliding space rocks - but they are not really sure what the hollows are or how they formed.

BepiColombo will also help to study another aspect of Mercury that has been puzzling researchers. Despite the planet's tiny size, it has a huge metal core.

Prof Emma Bunce mentioned that the planet could once have been much bigger, but was potentially the victim of a "hit and run" early in its history.

"One of the explanations is that the mantle was stripped away in a large collision," she explained.

"Or it's a combination of collisions over a longer period of time that then strips away its outer outer regions, its mantle and its crust, and leads to the planet that we see today.".

Or she remarked, it could have just formed that way in a metal-rich part of the early Solar System.

A life-size model of BepiColombo is on display at the Science Museum in London.

Unusually, this is a sort of "buy one get one free" mission - there are two spacecraft onboard.

A few weeks after arriving at Mercury they will separate.

An ESA craft will study the surface close-up, the other, which belongs to JAXA, will view the planet from a distance.

Jessica Marshall, a systems engineer at Airbus, who built the spacecraft, showed us Bepi's key features - and said the moment of separation would be "nerve-wracking" for the team.

The two spacecraft had to be very securely attached together for their launch and long journey, but now, eight years later, they have to delicately disconnect.

"We have designed a special mechanism that we have thought very carefully about," said Marshall.

With two orbiters at Mercury, both packed with instruments, the BepiColombo team hopes to find answers to Mercury's mysteries.

Of course, they may uncover many more once they arrive.
