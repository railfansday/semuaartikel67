Orgs that leak data 'prioritize corporate interests over customers' says Have I Been Pwned’s Troy Hunt

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Leakers move through denial, bargaining, delay, legal threats, then claiming Hunt is the real villain.

Troy Hunt, the founder of data breach reporting site Have I Been Pwned (HIBP), has revealed how organizations respond when he informs them they’ve leaked data – if they respond at all.

Hunt gave Anthropic’s Claude access to an email inbox containing over eight years of his email correspondence and then asked the AI to write a post summarising them, a method he mentioned “was the best way to highlight the behaviors I keep seeing across many breaches and subsequent disclosure efforts.”.

Writing in person, Hunt described those behaviors as “standoffish behavior, deliberate delays, heavy involvement from lawyers, and a constant theme: prioritizing corporate interests over those of their customers.”.

Once Claude takes over writing duties, it opens with the observation that Hunt sent his standard notification email to 73 organizations between March 2018 and November 2026 and also used website contact forms.

Claude/Hunt focuses on the breach notifications that don’t go well, for example when first contact “often goes nowhere” because “many organisations have no obvious way to report a security incident.”.

Some organizations Hunt contacted logged his notifications as customer support requests. “In one case, Troy received three automated surveys asking him to rate the support he'd received,” Claude wrote, before observing “A message that an organization’s customer data is being traded among criminals ended up in the same queue as a question about a late delivery.”.

In a recent development, once someone at victim organization gets the message, a common response is to say they can find no evidence of the breach. Others try to play down the incident, saying it was only a website defacement or that Hunt is re-reporting a previous breach the victim has already disclosed.

If an organization accepts Hunt is probably onto something, it typically says it needs to investigate the matter.

Claude’s take on Hunt’s mails suggests he responds by pointing out that this is an irresponsible approach, because while the investigation takes place, “people whose data has been taken don't know they need to change a password or watch for phishing.”.

Next, victims send in the lawyers who ask if their employer or client is legally required to reveal the breach.

Hunt typically replies by saying it is up to the victim to decide if the incident meets the threshold that triggers mandatory disclosure requirements under local laws, but that he intends to notify HIBP users regardless.

Victims don’t like hearing that and respond in three ways:.

Calling to discuss disclosure, rather than committing to it;.

Asking to see the wording HIBP will use in its disclosure, a request that sees Hunt refer the victim to the HIBP site for examples of the thousand-plus breach reports already on the site;.

Arguing that disclosure, not the breach, is the real harm.

Hunt/Claude suggests that each argument victims use to avoid disclosure “sounds reasonable on its own,” but argues “Together, they mean the people most affected by the breach are the last to hear about it.”.

The post also reports on some better interactions, such as a small booking platform that was initially skeptical about Hunt’s report, then admitted he was right and acted swiftly to inform customers.

A European travel app hardened its systems, rotated its keys, and published a disclosure. “Troy held off loading the data so he could link to their statement, and thanked them for their transparency,” according to the Claude-generated text.

The bot’s analysis also offers examples of when breach notifications go well.

According to recent reports, “Someone with authority over security answered rapidly,” Claude concluded. “They treated Troy's data as help rather than as a threat. They told their customers what they knew, even when they didn't know everything yet, and updated the details later. None of them needed lawyers to manage the conversation.”.

In a recent development, hunt appears to have asked Claude to offer its opinion of his email trove. “I don't think the main obstacle to disclosure is technical, or even legal,” the post states. “It's about incentives. For the organization, every day without a public statement is a day without bad headlines.”.

In a recent development, hunt also said he won’t make a habit of having AI write his content, but said Claude “absolutely nails it!” Unlike many of the orgs Hunt contacted. ®.
