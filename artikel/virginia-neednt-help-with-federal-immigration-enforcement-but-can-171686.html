📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

From today's decision in U.S. v. Virginia, by Judge Toby Heytens, joined by Judge DeAndrea Gist Benjamin:.

It has been revealed that [W]e vacate a preliminary injunction preventing the Commonwealth of Virginia from rendering unenforceable existing agreements between the Federal Government and the Commonwealth's state or local agencies "to perform [the] function[s] of a federal immigration officer" unless certain conditions are met. The Federal Government may not "command" the Commonwealth's "officers, or those of [its] political subdivisions" (Printz v. U.S. (1997)) to assist with federal immigration enforcement efforts, and the Commonwealth's supreme lawmaking body has imposed upcoming limits on when its agents may agree to do so. That choice does not violate the intergovernmental immunity doctrine because it neither requires the Federal Government to do anything nor limits any private party's ability to contract with the Federal Government.

It also does not violate the Federal Constitution's Contracts Clause for two separate reasons: (1) because the existing agreements all expressly permit the non-federal party to unilaterally terminate without cause; and (2) even if they did not, the Commonwealth would have constitutionally valid justifications for imposing fresh terms for future cooperation in any event….

Sources indicate that [But] we affirm a preliminary injunction forbidding enforcement of a fresh state law that regulates when federal employees may wear facial coverings and must display visible identification while performing their federal duties…. [J]ust as Congress may not "issue orders directly to the States," States may not issue orders directly to the Federal Government. We thus conclude the law at issue violates the intergovernmental immunity doctrine….

In a recent development, our holding is limited. We reiterate that "an employee of the United States does not secure a general immunity from state law while acting in the course of his employment," and we make no effort to provide a full list of situations in which federal officers may or may not be prosecuted for violating state law.

Recent findings suggest that we also emphasize that neither the Federal Government's broad authority in immigration matters, nor "the history and context for the enactment of" the Mask/Identity Law play any role in our analysis. Whether the Mask/Identity Law directly regulates the Federal Government does not depend on what motivated the Commonwealth's decision to enact the law or whether its various provisions (or the practices that prompted them) represent wise policy.

Finally, we do not address whether other legal requirements—including the Fourth Amendment—limit the permissible use of masked and otherwise-unidentified law enforcement officers….

In a recent development, judge Robert King agreed with the majority as to the likely validity of Virginia's refusal to assist with immigration enforcement, but dissented as to the mask/identity statute. For the full (long) opinions, see here.
