Sources indicate that the round closes. The announcement goes out. For about a week, everyone is happy. Then the pressure doubles.

The number that used to be a hope is now a promise. The board that used to be patient now has a timeline. And the founder who just finished the hardest sales job of their life is expected to make the best decisions of their life, immediately, on less sleep than they’ve had in months.

In a recent development, I’ve spent 17 years coaching about 1,500 leaders, including venture-backed founders. The pattern repeats often enough that I treat the first 90 days after a raise as a danger window.

Founders don’t get complacent in that window. They get flooded, and most of them won’t say so out loud.

Neuroscientist Amy Arnsten’s review in Nature Reviews Neuroscience puts it plainly: “even quite mild acute uncontrollable stress can cause a rapid and dramatic loss of prefrontal cognitive abilities.” The prefrontal cortex weighs options and holds a plan steady.

It has been revealed that notice the word uncontrollable. After a raise, a founder controls less than the cap table suggests: the hiring market, customer timelines, the board’s expectations. The founder hasn’t become less intelligent. The judgment that built the manufacturer is just harder to reach.

Porcelli and Delgado, writing in Psychological Science, stressed volunteers with a hand in ice water then offered them financial gambles. They grew more cautious about potential gains and more willing to gamble on potential losses. It was a lab study, but I see the same shape in a founder six weeks after a Series B: hesitant about the bets that justified the round, and suddenly willing to roll the dice on anything that feels like a threat.

In a recent development, the decisions that go wrong in this window rarely feel reckless. They feel rational, because the nervous system is treating uncertainty like an emergency.

It has been revealed that senior hiring. An investor who advises 20 fast-growing technology startups told me one of the biggest sources of leadership friction he sees is a CEO hiring a head of sales because they know the person. The relationship feels safe, so the offer is generous. Then the results don’t come. Under pressure, familiarity feels like certainty. But knowing someone isn’t the same as knowing they can lead sales at the company’s next stage.

In a recent development, expansion. Pressure condenses time. The new market planned for month 12 moves to month three. The data didn’t change. Waiting became unbearable. One founder I coached pulled a 12-month expansion into three, bragged about how little sleep he was getting, and did everything himself. The second-market launch was a sloppy, drama-filled disaster. The board brought me in to put him back together and triage the launch. Ask whether the evidence changed or your tolerance for uncertainty did.

Recent findings suggest that co-founder relationships. Harvard Business School professor Noam Wasserman, who studied 10,000 founders for “The Founder’s Dilemmas,” has put the share of high-potential startups that fail because of co-founder conflict at 65%. One founder withdraws because they’re overwhelmed. The other interprets the silence as distrust. A trauma-informed lens doesn’t treat every disagreement as trauma. It builds enough trust that a disagreement doesn’t become one more emergency.

Recent findings suggest that if you sit on the board, the week the wire clears is when you start paying attention. Composure can mean steadiness. It can also hide exhaustion.

Sources indicate that the founders who come through this window well tend to have one person outside the cap table, the org chart and the family they can think out loud with before a decision hardens. Investor Matt Cohen has written here that founders often have no one inside their company to confide in. Founders, who is that person for you right now? If you can’t name one, that’s useful information too.

According to recent reports, the capital is in the account. The open question is whether the person deciding how to spend it can still reach the judgment that raised it. Find that out before the first substantial decision, not after.
