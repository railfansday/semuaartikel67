China’s AI-powered smart-city model is going global

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

To understand the Chinese smart city, it helps to know that twenty-first-century infrastructure, technology and policy increasingly makes the whole of China a single, interconnected economic unit. Trains, planes and autonomous vehicles shuttle about, with the centre of megacities such as Shanghai and Shenzhen merely being in the middle of ‘one-hour circles’ for commuters that allow them to reach anywhere in the wider urban area with ease. Previous barriers to the internal movement of workers, such the hukou system, which restricted access to public services based on a person’s birthplace, are being removed, making it easier for people to compete for jobs across the country. And all the while, the Internet supercharges these shifts to a nationwide marketplace, backed by an ever-expanding supply of data centres. Sichuanese farmers of organic lemons now livestream from rural villages on apps such as Xiaohongshu or Douyin as they compete for city restaurateurs to buy their products.

So, if the goal of a ‘smart city’ is to use substantial data and AI to outsource governance functions, keeping everything running smoothly, then maybe we should think of China as a single city, with its different provinces and megalopolises as neighbourhoods with their own special roles. For instance, three of China’s megacities each have their own crucial function: Beijing regulates and invests in fundamental research; Shanghai is home to multinational corporations, frontier research in AI and biotech, and finance; and the Greater Bay Area, including Shenzhen and Guangzhou, is home to innovative manufacturers, such as electric vehicle producer BYD, tech giant Huawei and consumer electronics firm DJI.

China’s 15th five-year strategy unveiled earlier this year sees the country in this way, too, by entrusting certain functions to different cities and regions, and with rural areas also playing an essential role. In the northeast, farming is increasingly carried out with the use of drones and significant data by state-owned companies such as Beidahuang. In Inner Mongolia and Xinjiang, data centres are powered by abundant solar and wind energy, where they calculate how the self-driving cars of China’s eastern seaboard should navigate roads or process the AI that helps to keep the country’s state-of-the-art high-speed train network running on time.

This approach on data is embodied by 东数西算 (roughly translated as ‘east data, west compute’), a national-level strategic project to channel data from developed eastern regions to resource-rich western regions for processing and storage using eight data-centre ‘hubs’ located across the country. Eastern megacities generate the vast majority of China’s data and AI requests, but they bring skyrocketing land costs, power shortages and cooling difficulties that make hosting data centres unattractive. Instead, the solar farms of Xinjiang’s deserts and the wind farms of the Mongolian steppe are used to provide renewable, cheap energy to data centres.

Sources indicate that one key example is Ulanqab in Inner Mongolia, which has been transformed from a traditional farming region into a prominent data powerhouse, earning the name ‘Grassland Cloud Valley’. The energy costs in Ulanqab are low, because the government has been installing massive renewable capacity, relying on the area’s abundant wind and solar resources. Mongolia is cold for most of the year, so data centres use standard air cooling rather than massive air-conditioning set-ups — effectively, they just cover the servers with plastic and leave the door open for wintry winds to circulate. Major firms such as Huawei, Alibaba, Kuaishou and Apple have constructed large-scale cloud bases in Ulanqab. The region houses China’s largest intelligent computing centre for autonomous driving (Fuyao), built by Xpeng Motors and Alibaba Cloud, which has helped make it 170 times faster to train self-driving models. It means an autonomous taxi driving around Beijing is effectively being controlled by servers in the far-away desert of Inner Mongolia.
