Raheem Sterling wore black to his sentencing hearing today, just as he had when he arrived at Basingstoke Magistrates Court two months ago and admitted to 90 minutes of terrifying rush hour driving, with canisters of nitrous oxide on the passenger seat beside him.

He was visibly more subdued this time, wearing the look of a man who’d spent two months contemplating the prospect of a jail stretch, whilst the contemporaries he once stood with in the England squad, Harry Kane, Jordan Henderson and Jordan Pickford, were continuing to hit the heights.

According to recent reports, imprisonment was only ever a narrow possibility under the sentencing guidelines for dangerous driving, given the miracle that Sterling did not cause serious injury or worse as he zig-zagged between lanes on the M3 and M4 that morning last May. Critically, he was not found to be under the influence of nitrous oxide. A motorist who drove in equally erratic fashion and initially ignored police on Cheshire’s M56, in a BMW which had only three tyres, was handed a suspended sentence earlier this year.

Sources indicate that but while the mitigation preceding the magistrate’s decision that Sterling would walk free - his 12-month prison sentence is suspended for 18 months - garlanded him for his charity work and promotion of racial equality, don’t let it be said that his conduct that day was anything less than a disgrace. The tetchy hyper sensitivity Sterling displayed with the police officers who arrested him revealed a shocking lack of contrition and shame.

Recent findings suggest that sterling has had his challenges, of course. The early death of his father and a nomadic London childhood spent helping his mum on cleaning jobs. But then he disappeared into the cocooned existence of the football academy world, full of the little princes who have people fawning over them, telling them how good they are and throwing obscene amounts of money at them.

Raheem Sterling's conduct the day of his car crash was nothing short of a disgrace.

Sterling's meteoric rise has meant he does not have the tools to deal with rejection.

Kane has flourished in a way that Sterling hasn’t because he was forced to learn the hard way, deal with rejection and develop a sense of perspective. The princes are at the mercy of the hangers on and leeches, around them for a cut of their next major pay cheque. It was Sterling’s misfortune to select Aidy Ward as his agent – the man who conducted ugly, public contract negotiations with Liverpool which included declaring that Sterling wouldn’t be staying at Anfield even if he were offered nearly £1m a week. The reputational damage that caused Sterling should have seen him sack Ward on the spot.

According to recent reports, the problem for those whose adolescence takes place in football’s gilded cage is that they combust and can’t hack it when the going gets remotely tough. For Sterling, read Dele Alli, Kalvin Phillips and Aaron Ramsdale.

But what makes Sterling’s personal disintegration so needless is that he really has had positive influences around him in the background. Friends who were there when he wanted to develop a voice to speak against racial discrimination in football. There when he wanted to establish a Foundation to help the kind of London teenagers that he used to be.

He lost those influences during the chaotic descent into the insanity of that spring morning when he went berserk in his car. Some of them have described to me how he flatly refused to respond to calls and went into ‘oblivion mode’ in the hours after his arrest. It seemed like he was seeking escape from any vestige of the game which is all he has known.

England captain and Sterling's former team-mate Harry Kane has thrived due to the early setbacks in his career.

The tragedy is Sterling has a strong support network who want the best for him.

The bleak reality for Sterling is that his career in football is surely over now. Yes, he could pick up the thread in some place below the Premier League, though his unhappy experience at Feyenoord – where there was a prurient fascination with his struggles – suggests that he will face the same relentless scrutiny wherever he goes. In the modern world, more gratuitous pleasure than ever is taken in the fall of those who were once great. It will be a circus if he pitches up somewhere upcoming.

But the evidence of that work with boys in London suggests that he has far more of a hinterland to turn to than Alli and his ilk, if only he can re-establish those positive influences and seek help.

According to recent reports, A moment in time, precisely two years ago, proves what his future might look like. At a meeting room in King's Cross, Sterling sat with a group of seven young men, all of whom were deemed at risk of drifting into knife crime and who had been sent along to his foundation by Brent's youth offending service.

It has been revealed that after an initial 15 or 20 minutes when the boys seemed blinded by the superstar in their midst, they became more comfortable, seeming to feel he understood the likes of them. There were smiles as he described the various school social groups he knew. 'The cool group, the middle of the road group and the geeks,' as he called them. The 'geeks' always seemed to be a bigger part of his world when exams approached, he remarked with a grin. The room was wreathed with laughter, then.

Industry observers note that the group session evolved into several one-to-one conversations between him and the boys who'd been more reticent. It was then that he pointed out the value of allowing outside mentors to help you, if your home life is challenging. 'Having someone else involved changed the course of my life,' Sterling told them.

Recent findings suggest that now is the moment when Sterling needs others to help change the course of his own life and if he accepts that, he could play a part in public life again. First comes the challenge of rousing himself from any lingering self-pity, emerging from the celebrity bubble and appreciating that he is supremely lucky to remain a free man after those abominable, mindless few hours in his souped-up car.
