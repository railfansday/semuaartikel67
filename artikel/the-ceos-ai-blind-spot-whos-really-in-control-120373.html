The CEO's AI Blind Spot: Who's Really In Control?

Travis McGregor is CEO and Co-Founder of SLC Digital, a hardware-rooted transaction assurance platform helping enterprises combat AI fraud.

According to recent reports, ​Most CEOs have treated AI governance as a technology matter, something to delegate to the CIO, the security team or the vendor. That made sense when AI was primarily drafting emails and summarizing reports. But as AI systems take on greater responsibility—from approving payments to accessing sensitive records and acting on behalf of employees and customers—organizations need to do more than understand what these systems can do. They need to know which actions are authorized, who approved them and under what conditions.

Recent findings suggest that once a system acts, someone has to answer for what it does. That responsibility sits with executive leadership, and many organizations are unprepared. According to Grant Thornton’s April 2026 AI Impact Survey, 78% of senior business leaders lacked strong confidence that their organization could pass an independent AI governance audit within 90 days. Three in four boards have approved major AI investments, but only 52% have set clear governance expectations.

Throughout my career in the mobile industry, I’ve watched newly introduced technologies advance faster than the infrastructure needed to support and secure them. AI is following that same pattern, but the stakes are considerably higher.

I think of AI sovereignty as an organization’s ability to keep meaningful control over its AI systems, including where data lives, how models and agents behave, how information moves among vendors and who can authorize what those systems do.

Three gaps tend to erode that control.

Permissions are often granted broadly when a system is deployed, then left untouched as its responsibilities grow. An agent set up to draft vendor correspondence could eventually gain access to payment workflows. Access and authorization are different things, and a high-risk action deserves its own approval.

Agents frequently work through existing credentials and enterprise workflows. When an agent uses an employee’s delegated access, a valid session alone may not establish whether a specific action reflected genuine human intent or exceeded the authority originally granted.

Many companies have AI policies on paper with little visibility into whether those policies are enforced, especially across third-party vendors, cloud infrastructure and the wider AI supply chain.

According to recent reports, CEOs don’t need to design authentication systems or manage individual permissions. They do need to set expectations for control, oversight and accountability. That means deciding what AI systems can access, which decisions they can make alone and which actions require explicit human authorization. Those are judgments about risk tolerance, and they belong at the top.

Recent findings suggest that A governance policy offers little protection if an organization cannot demonstrate that its controls are being enforced. The CEO’s role is to set the limits and demand evidence that they are working.

According to recent reports, as regulatory scrutiny of AI-driven decisions grows, organizations that cannot trace authorization chains may face greater compliance and liability risks. Counterparties are likely to follow. Just as proof of security controls became standard in enterprise vendor reviews, evidence of verifiable AI authorization may become an increasingly important part of due diligence.

According to recent reports, adversaries are already moving. Deepfakes, voice cloning and synthetic identities are live threats today. Cyber-enabled fraud accounted for more than $17.7 billion in losses reported to the FBI in 2025. The bureau also recorded more than 22,000 complaints involving AI, with reported losses exceeding $893 million. Microsoft’s Digital Defense Report 2025 likewise documents how AI has accelerated fraud and impersonation techniques, including automated phishing. Workflows that rely mainly on behavioral inference to confirm who is acting may be particularly vulnerable to manipulation.

Sources indicate that the least visible risk may be operational. An AI system can work exactly as designed and still cause harm if it holds broader permissions than anyone intended, initiating actions no one explicitly approved.

According to recent reports, for each high-stakes AI decision affecting a customer, transaction or regulated asset, leadership should be able to say who authorized it, what permissions were granted and whether the action stayed within bounds. If answering those questions requires a forensic investigation, the organization may lack sufficient visibility into its AI systems.

Identify the workflows where an AI recommendation, an authenticated session or a standing permission shouldn’t be sufficient on its own, and require stronger, verifiable authorization for those actions.

Recent findings suggest that look past price and performance. Ask who controls the data, where processing happens, how information flows through third- and fourth-party relationships and how permissions are enforced. Jurisdiction, recovery procedures and accountability must also be part of vendor oversight.

Industry observers note that activity logs can show what happened, but may not establish whether an action was explicitly permitted. Require systems to preserve evidence of identity, permissions, authorization and execution for high-risk actions, including cryptographically verifiable records where appropriate.

AI is moving from a tool that helps people make decisions to one that can act on their behalf. That shift demands a different kind of oversight, one that goes beyond policies, permissions and vendor agreements to establish whether consequential actions were actually authorized.

The organizations that get this right will be better positioned to adopt AI with confidence, knowing they can enforce boundaries, demonstrate accountability and maintain control as these systems become more autonomous.

For CEOs, the real test of AI leadership is no longer how promptly their organizations can deploy the technology, but whether they can maintain control over what it does next.​.
