According to recent reports, you just hired an entry-level employee. A week later, they miss an important deadline, affecting several other projects like dominoes.

Your follow-up email carries an edge, and, within seconds, their reply hits your mailbox:.

It has been revealed that “Hello, I sincerely appreciate your feedback and understand the importance of timely delivery. I take full accountability and remain committed to improving our processes moving forward.

For a moment, it may feel like a perfect reply. Their acknowledgment of your feedback and promise to do better feels almost like a relief, but at the same time raises a red flag: Why haven't they offered any explanation for what happened, and will their mess-up and apology be a repeat?

Conflicts, even minor ones, can raise our heart rate, especially at work, where professional reputation and job security may be at stake. We may act in a way that leads to regret later, and a reality check against AI may not be wrong. I call it "resolution illusion" when a message displays the signs of "this is what the opponent would most like to read in the reply", such as empathy, acknowledgment, and professionalism. At the same time, the underlying problem remains untouched, and that may even build some resentment. This may eventually lead to major conflict.

Recent findings suggest that in an August 2026 Big Think interview, Terence Tao compared scientific research to hiking toward a waterfall. AI, he suggested, can work like a helicopter that takes you straight there. The hike teaches you the terrain, and a wrong turn might lead to a discovery you never set out to make. This, of course, isn't just about hiking.

In any situation, people seem to learn better through understanding and making mistakes. In conflicts, they have to decide what to say after what is already noted and/or done. They may question their assumptions and build their own future behavioral "protocol" in similar instances, after considering how their actions may affect themselves and others.

According to recent reports, in the workplace, in particular, they may need to revisit where instructions were unclear or ask for help they should have requested sooner; it is all part of getting lost, which leads to new professional discovery. That learning process may be uncomfortable, but it gives them practice in building their professional role, critical thinking, exercising judgment, speaking up, and establishing personal limits around work. When AI-polished responses are sent within a second, it becomes easier to skip the uncomfortable but necessary path for professional development, from A — the missed deadline — to B — figuring out what went wrong and what needs to change in this case.

In a 2025 survey, knowledge workers reported thinking less critically about tasks they were more confident AI could handle (Lee et al., 2025). In a separate experiment, readers perceived AI-assisted messages as less reflective of the sender’s own values than messages described as entirely human-written.

Philosophers Lai and Archer (2026) stated that relying on AI for emotional communication can deprive people of opportunities to discover what they truly value and who they really are. In the workplace, this is especially key, since it is where most people spend the majority of their time. An apology may sound like the product of reflection even if the sender skipped the necessary steps (remorse, responsibility, impact, and the desire to repair). This is only one part of the glitch. The other is: If the reply makes the issue look settled, who absorbs the relational fallout?

Research on unrewarded work suggests an answer. In a study published in the American Economic Review, Babcock and colleagues examined tasks everyone wants done, but no one wants to do, such as writing a report or serving on a committee. Women were more likely than men to volunteer for these tasks, to be asked to do them, and to say yes when asked. The reason was not that women preferred this work. In mixed groups, people expected women to say yes, and in all-female or all-male groups, the difference disappeared (Babcock et al., 2017). Cleaning up — making sure everyone understood what went wrong and what not to do in the future — after a polished AI reply is this same kind of work that may fall to women.

Sources indicate that A woman asked to fix what a polished reply left unresolved can use the following three questions to hand the thinking back to the person who made the mistake: What happened? What will you do differently in the future? How will you know it started working? The questions give the employee a simple checklist and a chance to reflect, explain their side of the story, propose a fix, and take responsibility for following through. For high-achieving women, the relevant boundary may no longer be knowing when to say no; it may be knowing when not to perform another person's unfinished thinking.

Imagine the employee had said what they were actually thinking:.

"Hello, I thought someone remarked it's due Friday.

Now the manager has something to work with – the scheduling matter, miscommunication, role clarification, and how the process works.

It has been revealed that the manager learns how the employee understood the process, who they depend on, who depends on their work and responsibilities, and who to speak with. Exchanges like this can serve as a reference point in case of similar problems.

A polished message that solves conflict skips all that, allowing the same or more errors in the future, which may eventually lead to job loss.

According to recent reports, it's not to say we shouldn't use AI for conflicts. Some workplace conflicts can be hostile, and for an entry-level employee, it can take time to spot; AI can help organize a timeline, clarify thoughts, brainstorm ideas, or find words for a boundary they already understand. This allows them to express themselves to the best of their knowledge and genuinely mean every word of an AI-assisted reply. The end product is what matters — the next deadlines should be handled based on the lessons learned from the first mistakes.

Conflicts may raise your heart rate, and AI can save a heartbeat, but it should not become another person’s unresolved conflict tomorrow.
