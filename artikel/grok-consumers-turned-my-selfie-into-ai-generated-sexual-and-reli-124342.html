I snapped a quick selfie in front of the stage with the inauguration banner billowing behind me.

Recent findings suggest that in the selfie, I’m bundled up in a burgundy fur-trimmed coat, my hijab clinging to my head against the cold winter wind. I’m proudly smiling at the camera. “I’ve always wondered what you all whackos look like without all that mess.”.

I founded Muslim Girl, a digital media platform, as a teenager to reclaim our narratives and identities online. I wanted Muslim women to speak for ourselves, on our own terms, instead of allowing others to define us. I’ve dedicated my career to empowering women to take control of their own choices. But in a matter of moments, Grok robbed me of mine.

The person I was most afraid to tell was my dad. After my mom passed away a few years ago, he’s become way more protective of his only daughter, and for an old-school Arab Muslim immigrant, I didn’t know how he would react. He was furious. At first, I was afraid he was going to be mad at me for being in this situation since I have always been a vocally public woman online. I wear the hijab not out of heightened strictness or chastity, but rather as a guard against the male gaze in a hypersexualized society that commodifies women’s bodies and pressures us into how we should look or present ourselves.

They took a police report, noted there was nothing they could do, and suggested that I make my account private. I refuse to diminish myself from the public at risk of having my basic rights and dignity violated online. None of us should have to.

That’s why I’m suing Elon Musk’s xAI. This isn’t just about my hijab or even about me. It’s about what we will and will not allow AI to do to us. I hope this becomes a landmark case that draws a line for the rights we are entitled to online. Even if it means standing up to the richest man on Earth.
