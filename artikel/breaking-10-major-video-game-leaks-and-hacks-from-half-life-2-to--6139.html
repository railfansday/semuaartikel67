Breaking: 10 major video game leaks and hacks, from Half-Life 2 to Grand Theft Auto VI

🌐 Source: Qz • 📅 16 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 12 min read (2316 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways
  • These video game leaks and hacks exposed source code, unreleased footage and player data, from Valve's Half-Life 2 theft to Rockstar's Grand Theft Auto VI breach.
  • A video game studio's most valuable assets rarely sit in a vault.
  • The nature of the threat has shifted over two decades.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

These video game leaks and hacks exposed source code, unreleased footage and player data, from Valve's Half-Life 2 theft to Rockstar's Grand Theft Auto VI breach.

A video game studio's most valuable assets rarely sit in a vault. They live on servers as source code, proprietary engines, unfinished builds and plans for games the public has not yet seen. That concentration of unreleased work makes studios and platform operators targets for intruders. When the files escape, the damage can reach release dates, employees and players at once.

The nature of the threat has shifted over two decades. In 2003, source code for Half-Life 2 was stolen from Valve's network, and the stolen build leaked online that September. Valve's Gabe Newell publicly asked for help finding the person responsible. A man named Axel Gembe was later convicted in Germany. More recent incidents follow a different script. Extortion groups such as Ragnar Locker and Lapsus$ break into corporate networks, copy data and press companies for payment. When a tech giant refuses, the stolen files can end up online anyway.

In a recent development, the costs take several forms. Some leaks push back a launch. Half-Life 2's release slipped to November 2004. Others spoil a story before release, as happened when cutscene footage from The Last of Us Part II surfaced in April 2020. Ransomware attacks on Capcom, CD Projekt Red and Insomniac Games exposed source code, personal information or plans for future projects. The 2011 PlayStation Network breach exposed data from roughly 77 million accounts, according to Sony $SONY's own reporting at the time. Sony kept the network offline for about three weeks.

According to recent reports, in September 2022, a user called teapotuberhacker posted around 90 videos of in-development Grand Theft Auto VI footage taken from Rockstar Games. In June 2021, attackers claimed to have taken FIFA 21 source code and the Frostbite engine from Electronic Arts $EA. EA confirmed that game source code was taken and stated no player data was involved. Hardware makers are exposed too. Nvidia $NVDA, whose graphics cards are sold to PC gamers, confirmed an intrusion in 2022.

Recent findings suggest that what follows is a selection of 10 major video game leaks and hacks, not a ranking or a complete record. Each slide covers what was taken, who was responsible where that is known, and what happened next. The order does not reflect severity.

It has been revealed that half-Life 2 source code was stolen from Valve's network, and the stolen build of the game leaked online in September 2003. The game had not yet been released.

Recent findings suggest that the leaked material was an in-development version, not a finished product. Unfinished work from inside the studio reached the public before Valve chose to show it.

Valve did not handle the theft quietly. Gabe Newell publicly appealed for help in finding the person responsible.

According to recent reports, half-Life 2 reached players in November 2004. By then, the stolen build had already circulated online.

A German man, Axel Gembe, was later convicted in Germany over the theft.

Recent findings suggest that in September 2022, around 90 videos of in-development Grand Theft Auto VI footage appeared online after a hack of Rockstar Games. A user called teapotuberhacker posted the clips.

The leak was made up of dozens of separate videos rather than a single screenshot or rumor. Together they gave the public a wide look at a game still in production. None of the clips came from Rockstar's own channels.

The volume of the leak mattered. A single clip can be dismissed as a fake or an outlier. A set of around 90 is much harder to dispute, and it highlights a project from many angles at once.

Recent findings suggest that in-development footage differs from a finished trailer. It shows a project before its developers have decided how to present it. Clips captured during production can show work that is still incomplete. A leak of this kind takes the first public look at a game out of the studio's hands.

Recent findings suggest that the attacker behind the teapotuberhacker account was also linked to a breach of Uber $UBER. That link tied the Rockstar leak to an intrusion at a organization outside the games industry. It demonstrated that the studio was one target among several.

Arion Kurtaj, a member of the Lapsus$ hacking group, was later found to have carried out the Rockstar hack. That finding attached a name to what had begun as an anonymous online handle. It also tied the leak to a group whose other targets included Nvidia $NVDA, the graphics card maker.

In December 2023, a U.K. court ordered Kurtaj held indefinitely in a secure hospital. The order came about 15 months after the footage first appeared online. The court set no fixed end date for that period of detention.

Sources indicate that the case connects one studio's unreleased footage, a separate attack on Uber and a U.K. court order through a single attacker.

The Nintendo Gigaleak was a series of leaks, beginning in 2018, that put internal Nintendo source code, design documents and prototype material online. Files tied to the Pokémon and Super Mario series were among the material exposed.

In a recent development, the files did not come out in a single dump. They surfaced in batches from 2018 onward. Each batch added material that Nintendo had kept internal.

It has been revealed that the leaked files covered several eras of Nintendo hardware. Material from the Super Nintendo era appeared alongside files from the Game Boy Advance, the GameCube and the Wii. That range sets the Gigaleak apart from a leak centered on one unreleased game.

In a recent development, three kinds of material were involved. Source code is the human-readable programming behind a game. Developers write it before it is compiled into the form a console runs. Design documents record how a game was planned, from its mechanics to its levels. Prototype material captures a game in an earlier, unfinished state.

The Pokémon and Super Mario files tied the leak to two long-running Nintendo series. Material of this kind shows how games in those series looked while they were still in development. Final retail releases do not carry that record.

According to recent reports, attackers broke into Sony $SONY's PlayStation Network in April 2011, and Sony took the service offline for about three weeks. Data from roughly 77 million accounts was exposed, according to Sony's own reporting at the time.

The PlayStation Network is Sony's online service for its PlayStation consoles. It handles player accounts, online multiplayer and the console's digital store. While the network was down, those services were unavailable to its customers.

It has been revealed that this breach differs from most game industry leaks. Many high-profile cases involve a studio's unreleased work, such as source code, in-development footage or design files. The 2011 attack hit the customer side of the business instead. The exposed data belonged to the people who play the games, not to a product still in development.

Story details and cutscene footage from Naughty Dog's The Last of Us Part II leaked online in April 2020. The game was released in June 2020, about two months later.

It has been revealed that the leaked material came in two forms. One was written information about the game's plot. The other was cutscene footage. Cutscenes are the scripted sequences that carry a game's story between stretches of play.

In a recent development, the April leak was not an isolated event. It came during a broader run of leaks around the title before its release.

The result was that parts of the game's story were public before players could reach them by playing it.

According to recent reports, in December 2023, the Rhysida ransomware group published about 1.67 terabytes of data stolen from Insomniac Games. The files went online after a ransom demand went unmet.

In a recent development, the leaked material spanned three areas. It included content from Wolverine, information about Insomniac employees and looks for the studio's future projects.

According to recent reports, the Wolverine content came from a game that had not been released. Unfinished work reached the public on the attackers' timetable rather than the studio's.

The employee information meant the breach reached Insomniac's staff as well as its games. Personal records sat alongside game assets in the published data.

Sources indicate that the future project plans were a separate category. Their release put the studio's internal planning in public view.

The 1.67-terabyte figure describes data the group posted after the ransom went unpaid. It is a measure of what was published, not a volume the attackers only claimed to hold.

Attackers breached Electronic Arts $EA in June 2021 and claimed to have taken 780 GB of data. They said the haul included source code for FIFA 21 and the firm's Frostbite engine.

EA confirmed that game source code was taken. The firm mentioned no player data was involved.

Industry observers note that source code is the human-readable set of instructions behind a game. It indicates how features are built and how systems connect. A finished game, by contrast, reaches players in compiled form.

According to recent reports, frostbite is a different kind of asset from a single title. A game engine is the underlying software toolkit a studio uses to build games. It handles core tasks such as rendering graphics and simulating physics.

In a recent development, engine code can matter beyond one release. A publisher can build several games on the same engine. That makes the engine's code a shared foundation rather than part of one product.

Recent findings suggest that the 780 GB figure came from the attackers, not from EA. The company's confirmation covered the theft of game source code. It did not confirm the attackers' account of the full volume taken.

According to recent reports, EA's statement separated this incident from breaches that expose customer records. The material at issue was the company's own intellectual property, not its players' accounts.

Sources indicate that in February 2021, attackers behind a ransomware attack on CD Projekt Red claimed to have stolen source code for Cyberpunk 2077, The Witcher 3 and Gwent. The company refused to pay, and the data later appeared online.

The claim did not center on one unreleased title. It reached across three separate products in the studio's catalog at once.

It has been revealed that source code is the human-readable set of instructions programmers write before a game is compiled into the version players install. It shows how a game's systems were built. For a studio, it is the working blueprint behind a finished product.

A ransom demand gives attackers leverage. The organization pays, and the attackers offer to keep the files private. CD Projekt Red declined that bargain.

Sources indicate that the refusal did not keep the code out of view. Once the files surfaced online, the studio no longer controlled who could read them.

The Ragnar Locker ransomware group breached Capcom, the Japanese game publisher, in November 2020. Capcom said up to about 390,000 people may have been affected.

That number was not Capcom's first estimate. The company revised it upward from an earlier, lower figure. The scale of the breach that Capcom reported grew after its first disclosure.

Ragnar Locker made its own claim about the theft. The group stated it had taken about one terabyte of data from Capcom. That volume rests on the attackers' word.

The two figures come from opposite sides of the incident and measure different things. The terabyte claim describes how much data the group remarked it removed. Capcom's number counts the people whose information may have been exposed.

Neither figure answers the other's question. A data volume does not show whose records were inside it. A count of people does not show how many files left the network.

Read together, they give two separate accounts of one breach. One is the attackers' description of what they took. The other is the manufacturer's assessment of who was exposed.

In February 2022, the Lapsus$ hacking group claimed it had stolen about one terabyte of data from Nvidia $NVDA. Nvidia confirmed that an intrusion had taken place.

In a recent development, nvidia designs graphics cards, the components that render games on PCs. It is a hardware manufacturer, not a game studio. Its place among video game security incidents comes through those cards and the PC gamers who buy them.

According to recent reports, the one-terabyte total comes from Lapsus$, not from Nvidia. The firm's confirmation covered the intrusion itself. The size of the stolen data set remains the group's own claim.

According to recent reports, that split leaves two separate questions with two different answers. Whether someone got into Nvidia's systems is settled by the company's own statement. How much they took rests on the word of the people who took it.

The group also made a demand that went beyond the stolen files. Lapsus$ called on Nvidia to remove the crypto-mining limiter on its graphics cards. That demand targeted how Nvidia's products work, not only the data the group said it held.

Sources indicate that the limiter demand is where the breach reached the gaming market directly. It concerned the same graphics cards that PC gamers buy. A data theft at a chip designer turned into a demand about a feature of consumer hardware.

Lapsus$ later turned up in a games case. Arion Kurtaj, a member of the group, was found to have carried out the September 2022 hack of Rockstar Games. That breach led to the leak of in-development Grand Theft Auto VI footage.

In December 2023, a U.K. court ordered Kurtaj held indefinitely in a secure hospital. The Rockstar case tied the same group to intrusions at a chipmaker and a game publisher within the same year.
