Since OpenAI debuted ChatGPT to the public in late November 2022, the stock market has enjoyed a pretty epic rally fueled by enthusiasm surrounding artificial intelligence (AI). Over the last four years, the S&P 500 (^GSPC -0.04%) has gained 85%, while the Nasdaq Composite (^IXIC +0.01%) has surged 119%. Even the Dow Jones Industrial Average (^DJI -0.35%) has advanced by 51%, illustrating how the bull market has extended beyond technology stocks.

In a recent development, the AI revolution has propelled stock market valuations to historically elevated levels, raising concerns that investors may be witnessing another bubble. One useful valuation tool suggests investors have good reason to be cautious right now, although its history also demonstrates why attempting to time the market is often a costly mistake.

It has been revealed that developed by economist Robert Shiller, the cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings (CAPE) ratio compares the S&P 500's price with its average inflation-adjusted earnings over the last 10 years. Unlike the price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio, which measures a company using one year of historical or projected profits, the CAPE ratio smooths out fluctuations in earnings across economic cycles. This makes it useful for evaluating whether the broader stock market is expensive or reasonably valued relative to historical levels.

Recent findings suggest that historically, the CAPE ratio has averaged approximately 18. However, recent readings have sustained above 40. Such elevated valuations can make stocks vulnerable to disappointing earnings, rising interest rates, or shifts in investor sentiment.

Shiller CAPE Ratio data by YCharts.

An elevated CAPE ratio doesn't automatically mean a stock market crash is imminent. To understand why investors shouldn't use the CAPE ratio as a market-timing tool, it's worth examining three historical periods when valuations approached or exceeded 40.

Sources indicate that these three episodes illustrate why the CAPE ratio is more useful for assessing long-term investment risk than predicting short-term market fluctuations. Investors who sold simply because valuations became historically expensive in early 1999 would have actually missed additional gains before the dot-com bubble eventually burst. Similarly, today's elevated valuations don't necessarily establish when the next downturn will inevitably begin or how severe it could become.

Although the historical evidence suggests the market could be headed for disappointing returns, it also offers an encouraging lesson for investors with a long-term time horizon.

Consider someone who invested $10,000 in an S&P 500 index fund on March 24, 2000, right before the dot-com collapse. Despite experiencing multiple bear markets and recessions over the following decades, that investment would have grown to approximately $68,400 by Nov. 13.

Industry observers note that someone who invested $10,000 at the S&P 500's peak on Jan. 3, 2022 would have first endured a 25% decline over the following nine months. However, the investment would eventually have recovered and grown to about $15,800 if they had held through today.

Although the CAPE ratio has approached or exceeded 40 during periods of stock market euphoria, it has never reliably provided a timetable for the next correction or bear market. Investors who maintain diversified portfolios, avoid chasing momentum, and remain invested through volatility over the long term have always benefited from the stock market's resiliency.
