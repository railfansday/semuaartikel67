Breaking: Eight Crucial Router Settings You Should Check (or Change)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that your router is a key piece of your home network, as it connects all of your other devices to the internet. This also makes it a highly valuable target for attackers and can leave your data vulnerable—especially if you simply plug your router in with its default settings unchanged. Whether your router is brand new or it's been a while since you audited your existing setup, these are the key settings to check and change right now.

Your router's security protocol determines how encryption and authentication work to protect data that's transmitted over your network. WPA3 Personal is the newest standard and the most secure, so if it's available on your router, enable it. It's compatible with devices that support Wi-Fi 6. Some older devices may not work with WPA3, in which case the WPA2/WPA3 Transitional mixed mode is a good alternative. At minimum, set your router to WPA2 Personal (AES).

It has been revealed that some routers ship with factory default login credentials, which are the username and password you need to access your device's admin dashboard. (This is not the same as your network name or wifi password, which you set up later.) Default router passwords are searchable online, which leaves your router's admin settings open to anyone. Once you get into your dashboard for the first time, change the default credentials and enable two-factor authentication for your admin account if available.

Industry observers note that while some newer routers do have unique, device-specific defaults, it's a good idea to check this setting and ensure it's secure. If you're prompted to create custom admin credentials during the setup process, do it.

Recent findings suggest that your network name, or SSID, is how your network appears when you search for available wifi. By default, this may be the name of the router brand or a short string of characters, but you should choose something unique to your network. Make sure there's no identifying personal information, such as your name or house number. While you may be tempted to hide your network and obscure your SSID, this may actually make your information less secure, so keep this setting disabled. You can, however, add "_nomap" to the end of your network name, which prevents your router's physical coordinates from being mapped by location databases.

The password to access your network should also be unique and secure and should not include obvious personal information. One option to keep it memorable and easy to type is to create a passphrase using five to seven unrelated words that, combined, have at least 16 characters.

Industry observers note that two additional settings that leave your network vulnerable are Wi-Fi Protected Setup (WPS) and Universal Plug and Play (UPnP). The former lets users connect to your network with a PIN, which can be vulnerable to brute-force attacks. The latter allows smart devices to easily connect to your network—but this also allows malware to spread more readily. You may need to have UPnP on in order to connect devices initially but should disable it afterward.

Any easy win for your network security is segmenting traffic to different networks, so if one is compromised, the data on the others remain unaffected. Many wifi routers have a "guest mode," which creates a separate SSID and password that you can share with visitors instead of giving them your primary network credentials. You can also set up a separate network for all of your smart home and IoT devices so exploitable vulnerabilities in these devices don't put your computers, phones, and other connected devices at risk too.

According to recent reports, routers run a type of software—called firmware—just like any other device. Firmware controls the router's core functions and is not immune to vulnerabilities, which can be exploited by bad actors. And, just like other types of software, manufacturers occasionally push updates to patch these flaws. Check your router's dashboard to see if automatic updates are toggled on. Some devices require an opt-in for auto updates during setup, so you should enable them if they are off. Some routers may even require manual updates—in this case, set a monthly or quarterly (at minimum) calendar reminder to check for patches.
