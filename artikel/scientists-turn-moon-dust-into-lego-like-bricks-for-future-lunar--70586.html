Scientists turn Moon dust into LEGO-like bricks for future lunar bases

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, building permanent bases on the Moon could become much easier if astronauts can turn the material beneath their feet into construction supplies. Researchers at the University of Arkansas have demonstrated a method for 3D printing simulated lunar dust into interlocking, LEGO-like bricks. The approach could eventually allow NASA to produce building materials directly on the Moon, reducing the amount of equipment and supplies that must be transported from Earth.

The study, published in NPJ Advanced Manufacturing, was led by first author Cole McCallum, who was an undergraduate student at the University of Arkansas when the research was conducted. Wan Shou, an assistant professor of mechanical engineering at the university, served as senior author.

To manufacture the bricks, the team used a 3D printing technique known as laser powder bed fusion. The process begins by spreading a thin layer of powder across a flat surface. A concentrated light source heats the particles until they fuse together, a process called sintering. The printing platform then moves downward, allowing another layer of powder to be added and fused. Repeating these steps gradually produces a solid three-dimensional object.

Unlike Earth's soil, lunar regolith contains no organic material. Instead, it is made up of sharp, irregular fragments produced by billions of years of impacts from asteroids, micrometeorites, and other debris striking the Moon. Because actual lunar material is difficult to obtain, the researchers worked with a commercially available substitute called Lunar Highlands Regolith Simulant (LHS-1). This material is designed to resemble samples brought back by NASA's Apollo missions during the 1960s and early 1970s.

Turning Moon Dust Into Bricks Without Added Binders.

Previous efforts to 3D print structures from lunar regolith have relied on additional substances called binders to hold the particles together. Although these materials can improve the strength of printed objects, they introduce a major logistical challenge: the binders would need to be delivered from Earth.

Recent findings suggest that the University of Arkansas team demonstrated a different approach. Using a relatively low-power light source, they became the first researchers to directly fuse simulated lunar regolith into interlocking, reconfigurable construction blocks without adding a binding agent. Instead of relying on another material to hold the structure together, the lunar dust particles bond directly with one another.

"There's nothing else it's binding to. The regolith powder is binding to itself," McCallum said.

Industry observers note that the team produced small bricks measuring a few centimeters across, including pieces shaped like crosses and the letter H. Their interlocking designs allow the blocks to fit together in a manner similar to LEGO pieces, potentially making it possible to assemble larger structures from individual printed components.

According to recent reports, however, the experimental bricks are not yet ready for demanding construction applications. Compared with objects manufactured using binders, the blocks made entirely from simulated lunar regolith contain more internal pores and have lower structural strength.

According to recent reports, "These structures we're making can be self-supported," McCallum noted. "But I would be hesitant to say our process could be used for something that's supporting other loads at this moment yet.".

Challenges Ahead for Building Lunar Bases.

Sources indicate that before this technology can be considered for actual construction on the Moon, several key questions must be answered. Shou explained that scientists still need to determine how the printing process would behave under the Moon's low-gravity conditions and in a vacuum. The strength and durability of the finished bricks must also be improved to make them suitable for practical applications.

Recent findings suggest that despite these challenges, Shou believes the technique could eventually offer an efficient way to manufacture construction components and larger structures for permanent lunar settlements. The ability to produce interlocking bricks from locally available material could be especially valuable as NASA works toward establishing a sustained human presence on the Moon.

How Undergraduate Research Helped Drive the Discovery.

Recent findings suggest that for McCallum, the lunar construction project was also an opportunity to gain valuable scientific experience early in his education. Originally from Little Rock, Arkansas, he joined Shou's laboratory during the early stages of his undergraduate studies at the University of Arkansas.

"He had this really strong belief that undergrads should get involved more with research," McCallum mentioned.

Sources indicate that mcCallum graduated in May with two degrees, one in mechanical engineering and another in physics. His academic achievements also earned him a place in the inaugural class of Lafayette Fellows, a competitive program that selected just 30 students from a pool of more than 3,400 applicants. Established to commemorate the 250th anniversary of the United States, the fellowship supports students pursuing master's degrees in France.

He is now studying aerospace engineering in Toulouse at the Institut Supérieur de l'Aéronautique et de l'Espace, also known as ISAE-SUPAERO, which ranks among France's leading aerospace engineering institutions.

According to recent reports, although his current studies have taken him in a different direction from the additive manufacturing research he conducted in Shou's laboratory, McCallum credits the experience with helping him develop essential research skills. Working through experimental difficulties and learning from unsuccessful attempts gave him a valuable foundation for future scientific work.

"It helps you refine and overcome all the problems you have as a researcher. There are a lot of mistakes that I made the first time through," he mentioned. "It was really nice to have a head start on everyone else and make those mistakes early.".
