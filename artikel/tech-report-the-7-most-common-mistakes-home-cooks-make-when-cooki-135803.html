It has been revealed that in a traditional Thanksgiving meal, mashed potatoes are a seemingly simple side dish that does a lot of heavy lifting. They're the perfect vessel for a puddle of gravy, and they’re a creamy complement to everything else on your plate—yes, even your aunt's questionable green bean casserole.

Mashed potatoes prove you can transform even the humblest ingredients into something magical. And yet, we've all somehow still eaten bad ones. With a recipe this basic, even the smallest mistakes have nowhere to hide.

Recent findings suggest that thankfully, those mistakes are 100% avoidable. We rounded up the most common ways to mess up mashed potatoes so that yours turn out as delicious as possible this holiday season.

It has been revealed that it doesn’t matter if you cut your potatoes into a fine dice or rough chunks. Whatever size you pick, stick with it. Making sure your potato pieces are roughly the same size will help them all cook at the same rate. If your knife work is haphazard, you may end up with some pieces that are perfectly cooked, others that are waterlogged and mushy, and some that are still firm in the center.

According to recent reports, like pasta, potatoes need a good amount of seasoning to really shine—which is why salting the cooking water is so key. You can be generous with the salt, since much of it stays in the water and ends up going down the drain. When you start seasoning early in the cooking process, it’s easier to build flavor and adjust as you go.

In a recent development, in the hustle of cooking all of your Thanksgiving dishes, you may be tempted to boil your potatoes ahead of time and mash them later in the day. But it’s always better to work with potatoes while they’re still hot and easier to mash. Waiting until they’re cool will likely require far more elbow grease—and you’ll still end up with uneven chunks.

A smooth, creamy mash is the height of decadence. To make sure the potatoes are lump-free, some people may reach for a blender—but this is one of the most consequential mistakes you can make. Potatoes are naturally starchy, and the more you work them, the more starch gets released from their cell walls. Breaking out the blender may be well-intentioned, but it can leave you with potatoes that are gummy and gluey. Instead, opt for the right tool, like a hand masher, food mill, or potato ricer.

It has been revealed that another vital thing to consider is that potato starches continue absorbing moisture as mashed potatoes sit. They can soak up the milk you’ve added, leaving your potatoes dry and dense. Don’t be afraid to add more liquid—or at least keep some extra on hand to loosen up your mash before serving.

Anthony Bourdain famously pointed to generous amounts of salt and butter as part of what makes restaurant food taste so good. That’s especially true for mashed potatoes. You may be scared to add more than a light sprinkle of salt, but don’t forget to taste and adjust the seasoning before serving. A little extra salt can make all the difference for your Thanksgiving dinner.

Whether you’re feeding your immediate family or a small army, you should always make a big batch of mashed potatoes. This side is one of the most popular components of a Thanksgiving menu, so it never hurts to make extra. It’s also one of the most versatile dishes for repurposing leftovers. You can transform them into gnocchi, shepherd’s pie, or crispy fried croquettes. It may require more mashing in the moment, but your future self will thank you.
