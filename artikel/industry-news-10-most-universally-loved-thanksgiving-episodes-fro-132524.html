Thanksgiving has inspired some of the most beloved episodes in sitcom history. Between the pressure to prepare the perfect turkey, the complicated dynamics of extended families, and the inevitable dinner table disagreements, the holiday is a natural breeding ground for comedy.

Of course, the best Thanksgiving television episodes also capture the holiday's warmth and sentimentality. Some episodes have become iconic thanks to a particularly memorable visual gag or quotable line, while others are beloved for their emotional resonance. The episode's overall quality and the amount of Thanksgiving spirit packed in are equally notable.

According to recent reports, some sitcoms have practically claimed Thanksgiving as their own, making their annual holiday installments an event to remember. While shows like Friends and Bob’s Burgers could easily fill an entire list of the best Thanksgiving episodes on their own, this list includes just one holiday episode per sitcom.

From bona fide television classics to more modern instant hits, these episodes represent several decades of sitcom history. Whether they embrace the holiday's most vital theme, revel in the chaos, or explore the joy of new traditions with a chosen family, these universally loved Thanksgiving episodes are well worth watching each year.

It has been revealed that one of the underrated sitcoms of the 2010s, Fresh Off the Boat’s “Huangsgiving” is a charming addition that offers an immigrant family’s spin on a quintessentially American holiday. For the first time, Jessica and Louis host Jessica’s family for Thanksgiving at their Orlando home, with predictably chaotic results.

Recent findings suggest that jessica suspects her sister is manufacturing family drama to steal her thunder, while Louis overindulges with his brother-in-law instead of preparing dinner. Louis’ proposal to serve Cornish hens, which he considers the “personal pizza” of poultry, is a hilarious bit that snowballs into a scramble to procure a frozen turkey and an unfortunate mix-up involving the oven light.

Sources indicate that “Huangsgiving” is more of a screwball family comedy than an exploration of Thanksgiving traditions. However, the pressure to pull off the perfect holiday gathering is universally relatable, regardless of cultural background.

Seinfeld’s “The Mom & Pop Store” is a decidedly unconventional Thanksgiving episode, avoiding the traditional family dinner in favor of another American holiday institution: the Macy’s Thanksgiving Day Parade. Elaine helps her boss, Mr. Pitt, realize his lifelong dream of participating in the parade, while George becomes convinced he’s purchased a car previously owned by Jon Voight.

Recent findings suggest that meanwhile, Kramer inadvertently puts a neighborhood shoe repair shop out of business, leaving Jerry without most of his shoes. The seemingly disparate storylines converge in spectacularly absurd fashion, demonstrating Seinfeld’s mastery of interconnected comedy.

While “The Mom & Pop Store” doesn’t embrace the sentimental trappings of Thanksgiving, that’s precisely what makes it such a classic Seinfeld holiday episode. From George’s celebrity obsession to Jerry’s accidental puncturing of the Woody Woodpecker balloon, the episode finds humor in the holiday's absurdities rather than its more wholesome traditions.

Full House’s “The Miracle of Thanksgiving” is one of the more sentimental sitcom Thanksgiving episodes, but its emotional sincerity is precisely what makes it so beloved. The Tanner family prepares to celebrate its first Thanksgiving since Pam’s death, with Danny, Jesse, and Joey attempting to recreate the holiday traditions she established.

When D.J. takes it upon herself to prepare the turkey, and Stephanie accidentally ruins the pumpkin pie, their efforts to replicate their mother’s Thanksgiving dinner prove disastrous. However, beneath the kitchen mishaps is a poignant story about a family navigating grief during a holiday that emphasizes togetherness.

Sources indicate that full House’s signature sentimentality is particularly effective for a holiday episode. “The Miracle of Thanksgiving” acknowledges how difficult the holidays can be after losing a loved one, showing that a meaningful family celebration doesn’t require a picture-perfect dinner.

Latest Girl established its tradition of chaotic Thanksgiving episodes early in its run. In the first season’s “Thanksgiving,” Jess decides to host a last-minute dinner at the loft as an excuse to invite a fellow teacher she has a crush on.

Much to her roommates’ chagrin, Jess’ enthusiasm for the gathering far exceeds her preparedness. From attempting to defrost a turkey in the dryer to discovering a dead body in a neighbor’s apartment, the disastrous dinner showcases the ensemble’s comedic chemistry.

It has been revealed that while New Girl doesn’t integrate the holiday into its narrative particularly elegantly, the episode’s conclusion, with the gang waiting in line for Black Friday (or, as Winston calls it, Friday), is a hilarious touch. The episode also captures the appeal of celebrating Thanksgiving with a dysfunctional found family.

It has been revealed that modern Family’s “Three Turkeys” is a masterclass in escalating holiday chaos. Phil takes charge of Thanksgiving dinner for the first time, but Claire, unconvinced of her husband’s culinary abilities, secretly prepares a backup turkey.

Industry observers note that meanwhile, Jay and Gloria pretend to be away for the holiday to enjoy some peace and quiet, while Mitchell insists that Cameron take a turn being the strict parent with Lily. There are, in fact, three turkeys and plenty of Three’s Firm-style hijinks afoot as the extended family’s little white lies snowball into an elaborate farce.

It has been revealed that thanksgiving led to some of the best Modern Family episodes because the holiday allowed characters’ individual neuroses to collide with hilarious consequences. While “Three Turkeys” uses Thanksgiving as a fairly generic holiday setting for its more emotional beats, the comedy surrounding the pressures of hosting and navigating competing family expectations makes it a particularly relatable holiday installment.

It may not be a traditional sitcom, but Master of None's season 2 Emmy-winning “Thanksgiving” is too exceptional to leave off this list. The gorgeous standalone episode chronicles Denise (Lena Waithe) and Dev’s (Aziz Ansari) friendship across decades of Thanksgiving celebrations, following Denise’s journey of coming to terms with her sexuality and coming out to her family.

According to recent reports, co-written by Waithe and Ansari and inspired by Waithe’s own experiences, “Thanksgiving” is an honest portrayal of why holidays can be so emotionally complicated. Revisiting the same family dinner table over the years is an inspired narrative device, illustrating how familial relationships evolve even when traditions remain the same.

It has been revealed that while other beloved Thanksgiving episodes mine family dysfunction for laughs, Master of None finds humor and humanity in the messiness of unconditional love. It’s an episode that will make viewers cry and want to call their moms.

Sources indicate that few sitcom characters take Thanksgiving as seriously as Bob Belcher, whose enthusiasm for the holiday has made Bob’s Burgers’ annual Thanksgiving episodes a beloved tradition in their own right. In “Turkey in a Can,” Bob’s meticulous preparations are repeatedly sabotaged when his carefully brined turkeys mysteriously wind up in the toilet.

It has been revealed that what begins as an absurd whodunit culminates in a surprisingly touching revelation about Bob’s anxiety over Tina growing up. Meanwhile, Bob’s increasingly awkward interactions with a grocery store butcher as he repeatedly purchases replacement turkeys provide some of the episode’s funniest moments.

Recent findings suggest that “Turkey in a Can” is the best episode of Bob's Burgers because it finds genuine emotional resonance in utterly ridiculous circumstances. Bob’s unwavering commitment to Thanksgiving dinner, despite his mounting frustrations, is a testament to how preparing food is his love language.

How I Met Your Mother’s “Slapsgiving” combines classic sitcom antics, HIMYM lore, and holiday sentimentality into 22 glorious minutes. As Marshall and Lily prepare to host their first Thanksgiving as a married couple, Marshall takes sadistic pleasure in psychologically tormenting Barney, counting down to the third slap of their infamous slap bet.

In a recent development, meanwhile, Ted and Robin struggle to navigate their friendship following their breakup, threatening to disrupt the gang’s holiday festivities. The episode strikes a delightful balance between the emotional growing pains of adulthood and the wonderfully juvenile antics that define the group’s dynamic.

Recent findings suggest that marshall’s musical performance of “You Just Got Slapped” is one of the most memorable payoffs to a running gag in How I Met Your Mother. By establishing Slapsgiving as one of How I Met Your Mother's unique holiday traditions, the episode also celebrates the joy of creating upcoming memories with a chosen family.

Cheers’ “Thanksgiving Orphans” is one of the most iconic Thanksgiving episodes in sitcom history, perfectly encapsulating the classic NBC comedy's enduring appeal. With the gang’s respective holiday intends falling through, Carla reluctantly hosts Thanksgiving dinner at her house.

However, mounting frustration over an interminably slow-cooking turkey culminates in a spectacular food fight, complete with a memorable glimpse of Norm’s notoriously unseen wife, Vera. The episode showcases the ensemble’s impeccable comedic timing, but its premise also captures the essence of Cheers.

If someone has nowhere else to go, they'll always have a place among the bar’s regulars. For all its slapstick comedy, “Thanksgiving Orphans” is a surprisingly poignant celebration of chosen family. The gang may drive one another crazy, but it's comforting knowing they’ll never have to spend the holidays alone.

Industry observers note that when it comes to sitcoms, Friends owns Thanksgiving. Several of the show's Thanksgiving installments could have comfortably topped this list, particularly “The One Where Ross Got High” and “The One With the Football.”.

Recent findings suggest that however, “The One With All the Thanksgivings” packs arguably the most Thanksgiving per minute of any sitcom episode. As the gang reminisces about their worst Thanksgivings, a series of hilarious flashbacks expands the characters’ shared history, from Chandler’s childhood holiday trauma to Monica and Chandler’s disastrous encounters in the '80s.

Recent findings suggest that the episode also delivers one of Friends’ most iconic romantic moments, with Chandler inadvertently professing his love for Monica while she dances with a turkey on her head. Between its ridiculous wigs, quotable dialogue, romantic developments, and genuine holiday sentimentality, “The One With All the Thanksgivings” is everything audiences could want from a sitcom Thanksgiving special.
