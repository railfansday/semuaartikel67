Ford Designed Its Fathom EV Battery Pack to Bend in a Crash, for Safety

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Ford’s upcoming LFP battery pack isn’t a fragile electric car component that needs protection. During my visit this week to the automaker’s newly operational battery plant, engineers proudly showed me a video of the battery being crash-tested, the shell and the prismatic cells within bending violently under the force. My jaw practically hit the floor.

According to recent reports, conventional wisdom views an EV battery as a potentially explosive component that, if damaged, can cause catastrophic failure and hours-long fires that are difficult to extinguish. Years of lithium-ion batteries in phones, tablets and laptops taught us that and it’s largely held true for electric cars as well.

So the anxiety is well founded, even if, statistically, EVs are generally as safe as (if not safer than) internal combustion engine vehicles in crash testing and far less likely to catch fire.

It has been revealed that ford dropped that bombshell of a video on me at the end of my tour of its Blue Oval Battery Park outside Marshall, Michigan. However, I was barred from recording or photographing the footage.

The design team’s mantra was “don’t assume a cell must never deform.” For the upcoming Fathom and Ford’s Universal EV platform, or UEV, the pack is designed to carry up to 40% of side-impact forces and is treated as part of the vehicle’s crash structure. The company’s leadership and engineers are confident that the EV won’t catch fire or explode despite the bent cells.

In a recent development, “Traditionally the battery is thought of as payload, a fragile jewel you must protect,” remarked Vladimir Bogachuk, chief engineer for advanced vehicle structure architecture at Ford. That approach, he said, means heavy bracing and protective structures around the pack. “The battery is not a jewel you must protect,” he said.

Bogachuk (along with every other Ford engineer and executive I spoke with that day) takes a less-is-more stance on the new EV platform. “The best part is no part,” he told me, “and if you have to have a part, it’s pretty much got to serve multiple functions.” And so the engineers set out to remove unnecessary armor and build a battery that serves double duty as an energy reservoir and crash structure.

Sources indicate that without that extra protection, the resulting EV can be lighter and more efficient, with improved driving dynamics. The UEV pack is deeply integrated into the vehicle’s structure: Its top lid is the cabin floor, and the seats bolt directly onto it. The bottom of the pack is the EV’s underbody. A service door beneath the Fathom’s rear seats will provide access to the charging and power electronics for service without the need to drop the pack.

It has been revealed that the crash videos showed deformation that would scare me on an older EV, but when I stopped to think, it wasn’t much different from what I’ve seen in conventional car side-impact crash tests. Ford states an impact strong enough to deform the battery would also deform the chassis of a conventional vehicle or traditional EV, so the vehicle would likely be totaled anyway. More importantly, the engineers say the driver would be safe from thermal runaway and fire.

When asked, Ford’s engineers said they don’t believe in a “right chemistry” for every electric vehicle. The UEV platform’s LFP — short for lithium iron phosphate — chemistry, prismatic cells and “jelly roll” construction were chosen for low manufacturing cost, resistance to temperature extremes, suitability for bidirectional energy use and the ability to deform while remaining inert under designed loads and forces.

Other automakers have floated solid-state batteries as a potential solution to battery safety and energy density, but the technology isn’t ready for use in production cars — at least not at the scale and price that Ford needs for the reasonably priced Fathom.

Ford hasn’t stated the LFP pack’s energy capacity, the cell capacity or the number of cells the Fathom’s pack will comprise. Based on a cutaway displayed during my tour, I estimate 90 to 108 individual prismatic cells. The aluminum prismatic cans that enclose the cathode-anode “jelly roll” battery provide structural support and aid thermal dissipation throughout the larger pack.

Wes Thibault, chief engineer for global energy storage systems, described the rigorous validation that the cells and packs underwent to reach the point where they can be trusted to bend like a taco. He told me that Ford has “a dedicated abuse-testing team” integrated with battery design and engineering at every level and stage of the manufacturing process, searching for edge cases and probing the limits of what the chemistry, the cells and full production-spec battery packs can take, from withstanding over 150,000 miles of simulated daily driving to remaining safe and inert in a crash. Along the way, CT scans of the cells offered an inside look at the jelly roll and how it was responding.

Ford’s testing covers structural integrity and load-bearing capability, heat soak from minus 40 to 60 degrees Celsius, IP67 water resistance and humidity cycling, and high-charge and high-discharge tests. For multiaxis vibration, the battery, seats and brackets are strapped to what I can describe only as a giant mechanical bull that shakes the hardware through roll, pitch, yaw and multiple harmonic frequencies. Ford can combine multiple factors in a single test, such as a high-humidity heat soak under vibration during high discharge.

Once the first batch of UEV vehicles are on the road, Ford says it’ll be able to capture real-world data from the fleet to further fine-tune its validation methods for future generations of EVs.

Given how well the first model year of Ford’s Mustang Mach-E‘s batteries have held up after six years and thousands of real-world miles, it’s clear the automaker knows a thing or two about building a reliable battery.

That mentioned, the UEV platform’s LFP chemistry and construction are very different from those of the earlier generation, and the automaker is asking much, much more of the pack than just holding and discharging energy. It’s asking the pack to actively protect the souls onboard in the event of a crash. Testing to failure early and learning from those mistakes swiftly is something the engineers I spoke to seem to take very seriously.

This bold, out-of-the-box approach to how the automaker thinks about battery fragility — protecting the souls onboard rather than the “jewel” that is the battery from a potential crash at the end of the vehicle’s life — comes with the promise of more range and efficiency, less weight and more driving fun over the vehicle’s lifetime.

I’m ready to believe. I want EV batteries to be safer and less scary. That promise depends heavily on whether the Fathom’s UEV battery pack is as safe as Ford says it is when it reaches driveways and dealerships in late 2027.
