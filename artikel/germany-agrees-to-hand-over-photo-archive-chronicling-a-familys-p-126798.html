Germany Agrees to Hand Over Photo Archive Chronicling a Family’s Persecution by Nazis

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The German government has agreed to hand over an extensive photo archive to the Jehovah’s Witnesses that documents the persecution of a pacifist Christian family under the Nazi regime.

Jehovah’s Witnesses were persecuted by the Nazis for their religious beliefs, particularly their refusal to serve in the military, pledge allegiance to Adolf Hitler, or participate in Nazi organizations. An estimated 4,500 Jehovah’s Witnesses were sent to concentration camps.

According to a report by The Latest York Times, among those persecuted were Franz and Hilda Kusserow and their 11 children, who lived in Bad Lippspringe, a town in the German state of North Rhine-Westphalia. Two of the couple’s sons were executed after refusing military service because of their religious beliefs. The three youngest children were taken from their family and placed in a Nazi training school, where they were prohibited from contacting their relatives. Both parents and their remaining children were sentenced to prison terms.

Industry observers note that one of their daughters Annemarie Kusserow was arrested by the Gestapo in October 1944 and remained a prisoner until May 25, 1945, after the war in Europe had ended. Over the following decades, Annemarie gathered and preserved a collection of more than 1,000 photographs, documents, drawings, letters, and reports documenting her family’s experiences.

Sources indicate that in her 2005 will, Annemarie bequeathed the entire photo archive to the German headquarters of the Jehovah’s Witnesses. However, her brother Hans Werner Kusserow, to whom she had loaned the collection for research purposes, sold it to the Bundeswehr Military History Museum in Dresden, Germany, in 2009. The archive has remained in the German museum’s possession ever since.

According to the Jehovah’s Witnesses’ official website, the sale of the photo collection to a military museum was “especially troubling” given that two of Annemarie’s brothers had lost their lives because of their pacifist beliefs. The organization notes the sale of the archive “resulted in years of legal battles.”.

Industry observers note that the Jehovah’s Witnesses filed a lawsuit to recover the archive from the Bundeswehr Military History Museum, which is operated by the German armed forces. On November 19, the museum and the religious organization reached an “amicable agreement” under which the collection will be handed over to the Jehovah’s Witnesses. Both parties have agreed to keep the terms of the settlement confidential.

According to The New York Times, Sebastian Stock, a spokesman for the religious organization in Germany, notes that once the transfer is complete, the “Jehovah’s Witnesses will develop and present a concept for making the archive accessible for further academic research.”.

In addition to digitizing the archive and making it available to scholars, the Jehovah’s Witnesses plan to display some of the documents in a fresh exhibition examining the history of the religious group in Central Europe.
