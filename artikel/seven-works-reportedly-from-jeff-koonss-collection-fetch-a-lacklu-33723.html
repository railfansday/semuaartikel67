Seven Works Reportedly from Jeff Koons’s Collection Fetch a Lackluster $104 M. at Christie’s, Led by a $31.2 M. Picasso

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that jeff Koons has long been an art collector. As far back as 2010, when the Latest York Times visited “one of the world’s most famous living artists,” his Upper East Side home held works by artists ranging from the French Realist Gustave Courbet and the Rococo painter Jean-Honoré Fragonard to the Impressionist Édouard Manet and the 16th-century Antwerp painter Quentin Massys. He had generally avoided discussing his collection, the Times wrote, “out of discretion and privacy.”.

Recent findings suggest that collectors and the public are getting another look at that collection this week. Nine works consigned to Christie’s Fresh York came from the artist’s holdings, according to two unnamed industry sources who spoke to the Times this fall. The sources stated the house had guaranteed Koons a minimum price and that he planned to plow the proceeds toward his famously expensive art production. Sources indicate that seven of the works, grouped under the title “Heroes and Lovers,” were offered at the house’s 20th-century art sale on Tuesday. Together they were estimated at $104 million to $153.5 million. The low end alone is slightly more than the $91.1 million Christie’s got in 2019 for Koons’s own 1986 sculpture Rabbit. The other two works will be offered during the house’s Classic Week next year.

Sources indicate that the group did not perform particularly well in the end, hammering for a total of just $87.8 million in total, well shy of the low estimate; with the house’s fees, the group sold for $104 million. No lot exceeded its estimate; four fell short of their low estimates. Four of the works sold at an inflation-adjusted loss.

Recent findings suggest that leading the pack with four works, including the top-estimated piece, was Pablo Picasso; the sale also included examples by Fragonard, Cornelis Cornelisz. van Haarlem, François Le Moyne, Édouard Manet, and Tilman Riemenschneider.

In a recent development, accounting for up to 60 percent of the total estimate for the sale was Picasso’s Le Baiser (1969), showing two lovers entwined in a kiss and estimated at $40 million–$60 million. Koons bought it at Sotheby’s in 2008 for $17.4 million ($27.1 million, adjusted for inflation). It has hung on the walls of institutions from the Musée d’Art Moderne de Paris to London’s National Gallery and Latest York’s Guggenheim Museum. But the splashy provenance and exhibition history couldn’t get it to its low estimate. It hammered for just $26.5 million to a man bidding in the room, to sell for $31.2 million with the house’s fees.

Sources indicate that picasso’s 1941 canvas Tête de femme (Dora Maar), showing the photographer who was then his lover, was estimated at $25 million–$35 million. Koons acquired it in 2007 at Christie’s New York for $16.3 million ($26.1 million, adjusted for inflation). It barely squeaked by its low estimate to hammer at $25.5 million to the house’s Katharine Arnold, vice-chairman and head of post-war and contemporary art, selling for $30 million million with fees.

He purchased the Spaniard’s Couple (1969), showing a duo in an embrace, at Sotheby’s London in 2011, paying $10.5 million ($15.8 million, adjusted for inflation); it was estimated at $12 million–$18 million. It had not been exhibited since a 1970 show at the Palais des Papes in Avignon, France. It hammered for $13 million to Christie’s global president Alex Rotter, or $15.6 million with fees.

In a recent development, the Spanish artist’s Tête de femme (1939–40) was estimated at $10 million–$15 million. The piece had passed through the hands of the legendary American collectors Burton and Emily Tremaine in the 1940s and the dealer Larry Gagosian in the ’80s. Koons acquired it at Sotheby’s Paris in 2012 for $7.9 million ($11.5 million, adjusted for inflation). It hammered to deputy chairman Olivier Camu for just $6 million, to sell for $7.4 million with fees.

In a recent development, bound to set a record was the Dutch painter Cornelis Cornelisz. van Haarlem’s 8-foot-wide canvas Hercules and Achelous (1590), which was estimated at $12 million–$18 million. The artist’s previous high was $8.1 million, achieved by the same painting at the same auction house in 2008, when Tuesday’s seller acquired it. It was on loan to Upcoming York’s Metropolitan Museum of Art from then until 2018. That $8.1 million far exceeds the artist’s second-highest price, $1.7 million, set with The Raising of Lazarus (1602) at Christie’s London in 2023. It hammered to Andrew Fletcher, global head of the old masters department, for $11.5 million, just shy of its low estimate, to sell for $13.9 million with fees.

Industry observers note that A wooden sculpture by the German artist Tilman Riemenschneider, Saint Catherine (ca. 1505), bore a $4 million–$6 million estimate. Koons bought it at Sotheby’s New York in 2008 for $6.3 million ($10 million, adjusted for inflation), setting the artist’s still-standing record, and then lent it to the Met, where it remained on view until 2019. (That price is double his second-highest, which is, in turn, nearly six times his third-highest.) It hammered within estimate for $4.5 million to François de Poortere, head of old masters for New York, or $5.6 million with fees, shy of what Tuesday night’s seller paid for it.

It has been revealed that the cheapest piece on offer from the Koons trove was a Manet pastel, Femme au tub (ca. 1878–79), estimated at $1 million–$1.5 million. Koons paid $2.3 million for it at Sotheby’s London in 2014 ($3.3 million, adjusted for inflation). He lent it to a show that appeared at the Art Institute of Chicago and the Getty and to another at the Met. It hammered shy of the low estimate at $800,000, or $1 million with fees, less than half what the seller paid for it.
