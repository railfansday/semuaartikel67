A Caltech robot just became the first to build a structure in space without any help from astronauts

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A Caltech robot released on March 30, 2026 assembled one bay of a truss in orbit with no astronauts, in what Caltech calls a first.

Early on March 30, 2026, a SpaceX rocket lifted off from Vandenberg Space Force Base. On board was a Caltech robot packed into a box about 35 cubic feet (1 cubic meter) in size.

On the ground, Jaden Suh, then a Caltech postdoctoral scholar on the team, watched the launch with tears in his eyes.

Industry observers note that months later, that box built a structure in orbit without help from astronauts, a first according to Caltech. Its robotic arm picked up struts and joints from nearby dispensers. It then assembled a fresh section of a truss in orbit.

Sources indicate that the demonstration, called ASTRA, was meant to build an entire reflector antenna. The team ran short of time and built only the initial section. The mission ends in December, according to Caltech.

ASTRA, short for Autonomous In-Space Tensioned Reflector Assembly, flew as one of 10 payloads on Vigoride 7. That orbital vehicle was built by Momentus Inc. Vigoride 7 itself rode to low Earth orbit on SpaceX’s Transporter-16 mission.

It has been revealed that inside the payload were two truss sections, or bays, that had been built on the ground, plus an armlike manipulator. Motorized dispensers held the rest of the parts needed to finish the structure.

Recent findings suggest that the team leader is Sergio Pellegrino, a professor of aerospace and civil engineering at Caltech. He is also a senior research scientist at NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory. He called it “the first time that a structure has been autonomously constructed in space.”.

The payload was completed, tested and handed over to Momentus in less than 12 months. Pellegrino said that schedule was an enormous undertaking for a small team.

Once ASTRA reached orbit, the team sent commands to its computer through the Vigoride vehicle. First, the restraints that held the parts in place during launch were released.

Industry observers note that next, the robot pushed the two prebuilt bays out of the builder. Then it assembled the third bay on an assembly plate.

Three team members sent the commands. They were Charles Sommer, a former Caltech systems engineer, Alan Truong, a Caltech research engineer, and graduate student Tara Venkatadri.

Sources indicate that after each step they checked the result against photos downloaded from orbit. The payload carries 11 cameras.

Contact with the payload was limited to about seven or eight windows a day, each lasting roughly 15 minutes. Operations were repeated to get around unforeseen problems, of which Caltech said there were many.

To fix them, the engineers kept a twin of the payload, a flight spare, in Pellegrino’s Caltech lab. They tested possible fixes on the spare and then sent the working version up to orbit. When an matter is confirmed in space, Sommer remarked, the team tries “to mimic the problem on the ground.”.

In a recent development, ASTRA was meant to build a reflector antenna about 4.6 feet (1.4 meters) across from composite fiber tubes, aluminum joints and Kapton, a thin, heat-resistant plastic film. DARPA calls the robot a relatively simple gantry, able to move along three straight axes.

In a recent development, on a normal cycle the arm builds one bay on an assembly plate, attaches the reflective film and pushes the bay out into space. Springs keep the two film nets under tension. Then it starts on the next bay, until all 11 are done.

In a recent development, igor Bargatin’s group at the University of Pennsylvania co-invented the perforated film with the Caltech team. Per Caltech, it needs less built-in tension than the knitted metal mesh normally used in foldable reflectors, so engineers can design lighter structures.

Andrew Detor, the DARPA program manager for the project, compared the approach to building with Tinkertoys or K’Nex construction toys in the agency’s statement.

It has been revealed that ASTRA belongs to DARPA‘s Novel Orbital and Moon Manufacturing, Materials, and Mass-efficient Design program, known as NOM4D. DARPA started it in 2022.

Sources indicate that until now there have been two ways to put a large structure in space. One is to fold it for launch and unfurl it in orbit. The other is to piece it together by hand from sections sent up over several releases.

Recent findings suggest that caltech describes building the structure in orbit as a third option. Detor said NOM4D is aiming at structures “hundreds of meters or kilometers in scale.”.

NOM4D was not first planned around a flight test. After good results from teams in phase 2, Detor changed the last phase to a space demo. He noted the program went from the idea of an on-orbit demo to launching a working prototype in 15 months.

Sources indicate that ASTRA is one of two projects that reached phase 3. The other comes from the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign. Later this year it will test a new resin for composite structures on the International Space Station.

It has been revealed that the team considers it unlikely that ASTRA will finish the full truss before December. Two bays were built on Earth and one in orbit, so eight of the 11 remain unbuilt.

Recent findings suggest that caltech mentioned the engineers learned enough to show that the assembly approach is viable. They also learned that the design needs to be simplified and refined.

Recent findings suggest that suh is now an assistant professor at the University of Texas at Austin. He noted orbit differs from the ground in many ways, and that is the reason for proof-of-concept flights.

Even complete, the ASTRA antenna would span only 4.6 feet (1.4 meters). DARPA’s target is hundreds of meters or more, and Detor remarked nothing approaching that scale can be achieved today.

Pellegrino said the method fits structures too large to be built on the ground and unfolded. Large reflectors could serve as mirrors for space telescopes, like the big optics planned for NASA’s Habitable Worlds Observatory, or as wide radio antennas.

In a recent development, pellegrino also co-directs Caltech’s Space Solar Power Project, which aims to collect solar power in orbit and send it to Earth without wires. He remarked the same approach could help build very large solar arrays for that work.

Industry observers note that detor listed large space depots, habitats, solar arrays and radio antennas as things a future space economy will need. Robots also appear in prepares for building on the Moon, such as NASA’s plan to use 3D printing and robots for a lunar base. Any such economy would share orbit with the millions of pieces of debris that already circle the planet.

It has been revealed that suh joined ASTRA at the start, as a postdoctoral scholar at Caltech. He stated many of his original ideas are in the system, and that “sending your stuff to space is what I’ve been dreaming of my entire life.” Part of that system is now a truss bay in orbit, photographed by the payload that built it.

The information was obtained from Caltech and DARPA.
