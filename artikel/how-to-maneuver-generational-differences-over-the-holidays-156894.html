How to Maneuver Generational Differences Over the Holidays

With the holidays yet again upon us, people will be setting their holiday gathering plans and preparing for an onslaught of multi-generational togetherness. When coming together for the holidays, the goal isn’t necessarily for everyone to think or celebrate alike; it’s to celebrate harmony and happiness while making room for the differences that you know may come with getting so many people of varying ages together in one room.

Here, we discuss how to make room at one’s table for generational differences while protecting the deep thread of connection that makes everyone family.

Industry observers note that we’ve all experienced it. The sideways glances at the child who won’t eat their turkey. The sighs and stories about “Back in my day…” The admonishment to the teenager to “put your phone away.” The mom in the middle of it all who just wants a group photo of everyone before they dig in.

In a recent development, these are the sights and sounds of most holiday family gatherings. The younger generation has taken over the planning and organization of events, while the older generation sits in the wake of it all, wondering why anything had to change.

In a recent development, even though the holidays bring together people who love one another, they can also bring friction. According to a 2023 American Psychological Association poll, 89% of U.S. adults reported holiday-related stress, and 22% identified the experience or anticipation of family conflict as a source of that stress.

However, generational differences don’t have to derail a holiday gathering. Once we learn to stop anticipating conflict and start remembering the reason we get together in the first place, we can begin looking forward to the fun stories, skill sharing, and memory-making that make the holidays so special.

It has been revealed that it can be easy to get immediately annoyed when Grandpa starts talking about how they used to discipline kids or Great Aunt Margaret claims that your stuffing was made incorrectly. Try to reframe statements before irritation or misunderstandings get out of control.

For example, instead of getting bothered when an older member of the family admonishes a child for being on their device, think about why they may be mentioning it. Perhaps they want to spend more time talking with the child, or maybe there are some cultural expectations around the holidays at play.

Researchers found that interactions and education can reduce or even eliminate ageism, which can be at play during multi-generational events. By teaching the younger generation about aging and helping them connect with older people, you can eliminate miscommunication, fear, and negative feelings.

Laying out expectations and boundaries can also be an effective way to help the holiday get-together go more smoothly between the generations. Prior to the event, invite the older generations into a conversation about prepares. This way, there will be less chance of surprise or disappointment, and the older generation family members will feel like they are contributing.

Recent findings suggest that take care to ask about meaningful traditions in the family and choose a few to carry on while leaving room for something latest. Discuss notable topics like gift budgets, transportation, locations, and food options before the holidays so that there is less room for conflict.

In a recent development, boundaries can sometimes sound like a dirty word to older generations, but they can still be key for intergenerational harmony during the holidays. First, distinguish between what you are willing to compromise on to keep the peace and a personal boundary that absolutely must be respected.

For example, letting Grandma tell stories about her childhood on the farm may not necessarily hurt anyone, but comments about your body, children declining a hug from a relative, or politics may be in need of some boundary-setting intervention. If boundaries that are crucial to you are repeatedly ignored, have a place you can go to take a break or a code word with your partner to signal that you need some help getting out of a conversation.

In a recent development, when you get different generations of family together for the holiday, there is bound to be some friction. We live in increasingly divided times, and people may feel more comfortable around their family letting conflicting opinions fly. Holiday hosts should aim to connect the generations instead of steeling themselves for inevitable discourse.

Sources indicate that the ultimate goal isn’t to resolve all differences and agree every step of the way (that’s likely impossible). Instead, families should make room for stories, laughter, and the kind of connection that’s only possible between people who know one another as well as family members do.
