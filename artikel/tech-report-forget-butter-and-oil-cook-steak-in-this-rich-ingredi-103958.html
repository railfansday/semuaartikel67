Tech Report: Forget Butter And Oil: Cook Steak In This Rich Ingredient For A Better Crust & Beefier Flavor

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

When preparing steak, cooks often focus on selecting the best cut, then preparing it with a specific method to an ideal doneness. Less emphasis is placed on the fat employed for the task — although what you pour into the pan also impacts both the flavor and texture of your meal. So skip standard pantry staples like butter and oil, and try a version with beef tallow instead.

Industry observers note that so — what is beef tallow? It's essentially rendered beef fat, an ingredient that lends numerous advantages to steak night. Foremost is the ultra-savory profile: Tallow is made by slowly cooking down beef trimmings, resulting in a rich, meaty taste. This staple works as a flavor enhancer in addition to a cooking medium, complementing the deep, savory character of steak. And if you buy a higher-quality grass-fed variety, you may impart even more complexity, introducing earthy and grassy notes to your dining experience.

In a recent development, in parallel, beef tallow helps create a mouthwatering crust. The ingredient boasts a higher smoke point than butter and some other fats. So whether you're searing on high heat or finishing the steak in the oven, the fat can withstand high temperatures, enabling browning and Maillard reactions that create a terrific crust. That adds up to an elevated steak experience, all from swapping out the fat that goes into the pan.

Preparing a steak with beef tallow doesn't require any special accommodations. The fat is solid at room temperature and can be shelf-stable when properly stored, making it an easy pantry addition. You'll only need about a tablespoon per portion: Simply spoon the tallow into the skillet, where it'll melt and cover the cooking surface. It's a cooking medium that's especially effective on cast iron — whether a griddle or a pan — as it can also be used to season the cookware. You can also use tallow when grilling by coating both the cut itself and the grill grates with a thin layer of beef fat.

Recent findings suggest that any steak cut can be prepared in tallow; just note that it lends a rich flavor. So if you're making a mild-tasting filet mignon, for example, the addition could be overpowering. It's better to pair tallow with robust cuts like a ribeye, porterhouse, or Upcoming York strip, thereby producing an especially beef-forward meal. Otherwise, there's no need to worry about the details. Simply add a spoonful to your pan and sear away; this staple makes for a dependably delicious steak experience.
