OPINION — Once again, the next Congress is going to be filled with losers.

Sources indicate that this is not a personal judgment about their character or personality, but a simple recognition that many members have lost races before getting elected. And the incoming class is no different.

I’ve written many versions of this column for more than a decade, and yet the lesson always seems to get lost, no pun intended. Parties routinely attack candidates who enter a race with losses on their record. And, of course, it happened this cycle as well.

“We beat him once. We’ll beat him again,” National Republican Senatorial Committee Chairman Tim Scott declared when Democratic former Sen. Sherrod Brown unveiled his challenge to appointed GOP Sen. Jon Husted in the summer of 2025.

“Ohioans gladly sent him packing last year, and they’ll not hesitate to toss him aside again,” stated a Republican strategist.

Brown lost reelection to Republican Bernie Moreno in 2024 but came back to defeat Husted for the state’s other Senate seat. And Brown is far from the only candidate to avenge a loss with an electoral victory last week.

According to recent reports, the fact of the matter is that losing a race doesn’t have to be the end of a political career. Losses can lay the foundation for future success by building name ID or a fundraising network. Or sometimes it takes a change in the political environment or change in circumstances, such as running for an open seat instead of challenging an incumbent.

In some instances, though, the losses are piling up.

According to recent reports, republican Rep. John James of Michigan now has three statewide losses on his record. He lost Senate contests in 2018 (taking 46 percent of the vote) and 2020 (48 percent) and came up short in his bid for governor this year (44 percent).

Republican former Sen. John E. Sununu of New Hampshire, who lost reelection in 2008 to Democrat Jeanne Shaheen, fell in his comeback attempt for the open seat last week.

But they could take heart from another repeat loser. Charlie Crist, a former Florida governor and congressman, has had a handful of high-profile losses over the years, including for Senate and two gubernatorial comeback bids. He was just elected mayor of St. Petersburg.

So even though I know it’s a futile request, let’s have a bipartisan agreement not to attack candidates for past losses, because losing doesn’t mean they can’t later pull off a win.
