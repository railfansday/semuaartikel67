Football Practice and Red Dirt Music: On the Field With Oklahoma Band Keyland

In a recent development, there is arguably no better day for football — watching it or playing it — than the autumnal crispness of Thanksgiving. But on this particular late summer afternoon, despite triple-digit temperatures and the Oklahoma sun making it feel even hotter, football is the sweat-drenched task at hand: Coach Kyle Ross is putting me through practice. It’s not lost on either of us that Ross’s primary job is coaching high-energy junior-high athletes in the state’s flagship football program, at Bixby, and I’m an out-of-shape journalist in his late 40s. If I finish Ross’s drills upright and healthy, we’ll declare it a success.

“What are you? Three-for-three or four-for-four catching passes here?” Ross — who is also the founder and frontman of the band Keyland — asks me. My football career ended in high school, 30 years ago, but I was a slot receiver with a reputation for catching anything thrown my way. What would my parents say if I started dropping passes now? “I will throw my body on this turf to not miss a pass,” I promise him.

Recent findings suggest that over the next few months, Ross will attempt to strike a rather unique balance as coach and touring artist. He’ll headline and sell out the Mercury Lounge in Tulsa, 30 minutes north of Bixby, open an East Coast run for Morgan Wade, and announce a series of 2027 dates supporting Kaitlin Butts. And while he’s ostensibly promoting Knuckle Sandwich, Keyland’s third studio album, Ross will always have football season in the back of his mind.

“I haven’t found anything better to do,” Ross claims of why he continues to coach. “Except for maybe, get paid to play guitar and drink beer.”.

Few pastimes are as all-encompassing in Oklahoma as football. Its two largest college programs — Oklahoma and Oklahoma State — play their home games in modern stadiums to sold-out crowds on most Saturdays in the fall, but it’s the high-school version of the sport that unites the state. Students, fans, and parents fill stadiums in the state’s urban cores and rural farm towns every Friday night, and for the past decade, no program has dominated this landscape like Bixby’s. The varsity Spartans are eight-time defending champions in Oklahoma’s largest class and this season were ranked No. 1 again by the state’s largest media outlet. Recent findings suggest that this is Ross’s program. He’s an assistant coach for Bixby’s eighth-grade team and a junior-high English teacher. He grew up in rural Missouri, playing football through high school. Shortly after graduating college, Ross befriended Tyler Schneider — Bixby’s longtime offensive coordinator. The two hit it off, and within weeks, Ross joined the Spartan’s staff. Seven years later, Ross is throwing passes to me on the same turf that those Spartans play their home games. We’re here to talk about music, but I am here to experience what it’s like being one of Ross’s players.

“Let’s go!” he shouts as I haul in another pass. “And he scores!”.

In a recent development, as it happens, that particular end zone is where I scored my last touchdown as a player. Before Bixby became the state’s juggernaut program, it was a mid-sized school in the same conference as my hometown team, the Okmulgee Bulldogs.

Sources indicate that when he’s playing as Keyland — Ross uses the moniker interchangeably for his solo and full-band demonstrates, named for a duck club he and his father used to hunt near his Chillicothe, Missouri, hometown — he wears an infectious smile that’s integral to his show. He describes the irreverent Knuckle Sandwich, which Keyland recorded at the Sonic Ranch studio outside of El Paso, as a product of pals hanging around for several days and finding music at the end of it. “This album is about taking what you do seriously, but not taking yourself seriously,” Ross states. “That kind of embodies the whole creative process in recording it. It’s more of a hang than anything, and we get music at the end of it.”.

According to recent reports, it’s a microcosm for his entire music career. He had a guitar growing up, but played it sparingly. It was not until he was in college and caught a Chris Stapleton gig that he decided to give music a try. “I fell in love with Chris Stapleton, and that was the entry way to the long rabbit hole I fell into,” he says.

According to recent reports, landing a full-time job right in the heart of Tulsa’s music community was a bout of good fortune. Ross befriended Brandon Clark, frontman of the Red Dirt outfit BC & the Major Rig and highly respected in Red Dirt circles. Clark has a weekly gig at Mercury Lounge, and in 2022, he suggested that Keyland get booked at the bar.

Keyland’s first Mercury Lounge show was opening for the Big Rig. Shortly afterward, venue general manager Ashley Kirkley booked Keyland as a headliner. The show sold out, and Keyland have been a regular since.

“Keyland is a great band, no doubt, but the energy and charisma that emanates from Kyle is palpable at every show,” Kirkley says. “Beyond being an incredible performer with a distinctive voice that is unlike anything I’ve heard, he is also one of the most down to earth, kindest people I have ever met. Kyle is always striving for greatness in everything he does. He’s an incredible human, and we are incredibly lucky to have him as a part of our music family.”.

Recent findings suggest that keyland have since gone on to, at least, regional notoriety. Ross has been booked multiple times at nearby Cain’s Ballroom, playing alongside the likes of Johnny Mullenax and opening a sold-out show for Josh Abbott earlier this year.

“Everyone’s at different levels of what they’re doing,” Ross claims of the Tulsa music community. “I have friends who have sold out a place like Cain’s. I’m friends with people who are doing house indicates for ten people. It almost seems like everyone has a very similar mindset of, ‘We’re almost there.’”.

Ross knows better than anyone that Keyland is growing at a fast clip. He is likely to come to a crossroads between coaching and making music before too many more gigs or summer practices pass. But until then, he’s happy to pull double duty. And even at Bixby, where the focus is annually on another state championship, he has found support. He had a fan from the start in Schneider, but he’s brought most of the Spartans’ coaching staff onto his bandwagon — and even a few players.

“There’s a huge Venn diagram of coaches’ interests outside of football, and one of them is music,” Ross says. “All the coaches are super supportive. We have over 20 coaches, and a huge number of them have been to more than one concert. It’s just a community-oriented place to work. The kids, as they get older, they start to appreciate it.”.

And on that note, practice is over. Ross’s actual players are starting to trickle in to Bixby’s behemoth of a training facility. Ross gives me a Bixby Spartans shirt for surviving his drills.
