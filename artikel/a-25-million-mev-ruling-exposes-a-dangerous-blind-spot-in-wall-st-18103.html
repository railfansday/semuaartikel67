A $25 million MEV ruling exposes a dangerous blind spot in Wall Street’s tokenization push

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A federal judge on Nov. 16 acquitted Anton and James Peraire-Bueno in a $25 million Ethereum maximum extractable value (MEV) exploit case because prosecutors had failed to prove a material misrepresentation.

The decision leaves a separate question for tokenized US stock markets about what protects investors when transaction ordering works against them.

The SEC had already opened a conditional route for those markets in September on its framework, which preserves applicable securities antifraud and manipulation laws, but requires venues to disclose their policies for MEV even when they have none.

MEV concerns the value captured through transaction ordering. Four operators' notices show how much can differ within that framework, from transaction privacy and planned sequencing controls to the prospect that investors bear their own losses.

Recent findings suggest that in her 46-page opinion, US District Judge Jessica Clarke found insufficient evidence to sustain wire fraud and the related wire-fraud and money-laundering conspiracy charges. The missing element was a material misrepresentation in the brothers' Omakase operation.

The opinion describes a 2023 operation that used lure transactions to attract sandwich-trading bots and exploited a relay-code vulnerability, producing about $25 million in 12 seconds. The targeted bots themselves sought to profit by placing transactions around someone else's trade, a practice that can leave that trader with a worse execution price.

The case was more specific than a simple claim that someone profited from transaction ordering, since prosecutors had to establish the charged fraud.

Clarke found that the trial evidence did not establish a material misrepresentation in the Omakase strategy, and frustrating MEV-Boost's intended design could not supply that missing element.

The court also distinguished precedents involving spoofing, where orders create a misleading impression before being canceled. Here, the lure transactions executed and also were not placed on a regulated market with rules prohibiting the conduct relied upon in those precedents.

That reasoning does not establish a universal requirement that wire fraud occur on a regulated market, nor does it determine whether all forms of MEV extraction are lawful.

Recent findings suggest that in a March 30 letter, the Securities Industry and Financial Markets Association (SIFMA) argued that exposing pending trades to sandwiching and front-running can conflict with securities-market expectations. It called for technical protections or fair-sequencing rules when automated market makers handle securities.

An automated market maker (AMM) lets participants trade against assets committed to a pool using software-defined pricing. The ordering of those trades can affect who receives a favorable price and who captures the resulting profit.

Keeping an investor's pending instruction private and deciding who processes it first are economically significant choices.

Recent findings suggest that the SEC's Sept. 17 order created temporary, conditional relief from the definition of an exchange for qualifying tokenized National Market System stock venues using permissioned AMM pools. Certain liquidity providers trading proprietary capital received separate relief from the dealer definition until Sept. 17, 2031.

According to recent reports, the order retains applicable securities antifraud and antimanipulation provisions, including Exchange Act Section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5. Its conditions include permissioned access, public notices, records, and specified trading stoppages.

A venue must describe policies and procedures designed to address it, or state that it has none. It must disclose known material risks, explain any mitigation and compensation arrangements, and describe trading-abuse monitoring or acknowledge its absence.

A venue's published account of its protections can be detailed while leaving investors exposed to risks the operator expressly acknowledges.

Sources indicate that the SEC's public list, reviewed Nov. 16, contains four notice filers. Their submission dates range from TSV LLC's Sept. 23 notice to Formation Securities' Nov. 9 filing.

TSV LLC's current notice says Canton's architecture generally avoids broadcasting readable pending transaction contents to unrelated participants, aiming to reduce opportunities for conventional public-mempool front-running and sandwich attacks.

The operator still acknowledges risks of information leakage, sequencing, compromised infrastructure, and collusion.

According to recent reports, OKXICE's Nov. 5 notice disclosed that it has no policies and procedures designed to address MEV. That sits alongside general protections, including permissioned participation, participant minimum-output settings, surveillance and the ability to revoke access for manipulative trading. OKXICE says those features mitigate, rather than eliminate, MEV risk.

Its planned pools use Uniswap v4 on XLayer, where the notice claims the sequencer generally orders transactions according to offered network fees.

In a recent development, perpetuals.com's notice proposes generally first-in, first-out sequencing, with priority for trading-halt instructions and signed arrival receipts intended to make ordering verifiable. Yet its affiliated sequencer would receive nonpublic transaction information.

A private pending-transaction route can reduce exposure to outsiders while leaving a concentrated information advantage to manage.

Its October notice describes safeguards that were not fully implemented and surveillance intended to precede trading. Formation's notice likewise describes planned safeguards for its Solana venue.

Preventing an abusive trade, detecting it afterward, and compensating its victim are different functions.

TSV indicates participants generally should not expect compensation for market or impermanent losses, compromised participant systems or losses caused by unaffiliated third parties, unless it expressly agrees otherwise or the law requires it.

Recent findings suggest that perpetuals.com reports no established compensation policy for the risks described in its notice. Formation specifically excludes compensation for lost or compromised private keys or wallets, which does not establish a general recovery guarantee for other losses.

Criminal proof, securities-venue duties and compensation terms address different parts of investor protection.

A disclosed safeguard is not a guarantee of prevention or repayment.

Recent findings suggest that the SEC order recognizes that sequencing can support price-stabilizing activity as well as abuse. TSV says it does not intend to prevent lawful ordinary post-execution arbitrage or price alignment that avoids misuse of confidential information and other prohibited conduct.

Industry observers note that the dividing line cannot simply be whether someone profits after another trade. Relevant distinctions include whether pending information was misused, how the venue controls ordering, what conduct it addresses, and which legal obligations apply.

For institutions considering these markets, the notices expose concrete choices about privacy, sequencing, surveillance and loss allocation. The execution model determines that a private route still needs controls over privileged information, while a public route needs a response to abusive ordering.

Industry observers note that the next disputed on-chain securities trade would raise questions about what representations were made, what rules applied, who controlled the information and ordering, and what recourse the investor had. Bringing stocks onto a blockchain makes those choices visible.
