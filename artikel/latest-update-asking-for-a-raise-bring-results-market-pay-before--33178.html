Latest Update: Asking for a Raise – Bring Results & Market Pay Before the Budget Is Set

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that ask for a raise in a scheduled meeting, a few months before next year’s raise budget is set or right after a substantial win. Bring your results and what the market pays for your role, and ask for a specific number. If the answer is no, ask what it would take and when you can revisit it.

It has been revealed that the reason you give decides how easy your request is to turn down. Rising rent tells your boss what you need, and a coworker’s salary invites a list of ways that job differs from yours.

Raises come out of a budget the company sets in advance. The Conference Board projects U.S. employers’ median raise budget for 2027 at 3.5% of pay, with 3.1% for merit raises and the difference for actions like promotions and raises meant to keep people from leaving. With most of it going to merit raises, your boss needs a business reason to give you more than the usual raise. Your results show what the company gets for the money, and your market rate, what employers in your city pay for your role, highlights what it would cost to replace you.

It has been revealed that ask your boss when next year’s raise budget gets set, and make your case a few months ahead. In Mercer’s mid-July survey of U.S. employers, 87% noted their 2027 raise budgets were still preliminary, so midsummer is early enough at most of them. Right after a major win is the other good time to ask, since 64% of them said they’ve given raises outside the yearly cycle or will in 2027.

Book a meeting about your pay rather than bringing it up in passing, so your boss comes ready to talk numbers. Bring two or three results with numbers on them and the pay data for your role, then ask for one figure or a narrow range backed by that data.

Sources indicate that say you’re a marketing coordinator earning $58,000. You bring two campaigns that beat their targets and pay data putting the role near $64,000 in your city, and you ask for $63,000. You leave with a $5,000 raise, about $3,000 more than the $2,030 a 3.5% raise would have added.

If the answer is no, ask what you’d need to deliver to earn the raise and when you can bring it up again. Then email your boss a short recap of both that day, so the next conversation starts from what you agreed on.
