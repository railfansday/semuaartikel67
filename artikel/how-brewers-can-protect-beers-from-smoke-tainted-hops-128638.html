How brewers can protect beers from smoke-tainted hops

The 2020 Oregon wildfires burned over 1 million acres, destroyed thousands of structures, and killed 11 people. Smoke from the fires spread across the entire region; this “smoke taint” adversely affected not only local wine production, but also hops grown for beer brewing. Since the Pacific Northwest produces more than 95 percent of US hops and about 40 percent of the global supply, the potential economic impact is significant.

That’s why Oregon State University (OSU) scientists have been studying how brewers could offset the negative impacts of smoke taint while still incorporating affected hops in their brews. Most recently, they found that it all depends on the quantity of tainted hops that are included and when hops are added during the brewing process, according to a upcoming paper published in the Journal of the American Society of Brewing Chemists.

As previously reported, the chemistry of brewing beer is a very active area of research. For instance, in 2018, OSU chemist Thomas Shellhammer—who also co-authored this latest paper—identified the growing popularity of “dry hopping” (adding hops after fermentation, common for pale ales) among craft brewers as the likely culprit of the exploding beer bottle phenomenon.

Industry observers note that all beer contains hops, a key flavoring agent that also imparts useful antimicrobial properties. (Beer spoils quickly without them.) Brewers mash and steep grain in hot water, which converts all that starch into sugars. This is traditionally the stage where hops are added to the liquid extract (wort) and boiled to give the beer that hint of bitterness. During the boiling process, a portion of the resins (alpha acids) in hops isomerize into iso-alpha acids. That chemical rearrangement of the molecules is what produces bitterness. Yeast is then added to trigger fermentation, turning the sugars into alcohol.

Sources indicate that far from being the chemically inert ingredient brewers have long assumed, hops contain starch enzymes that, under certain conditions, can boost their activity to such a great extent that excessive re-fermentation occurs (“hop-creep”) after bottling. Dry-hopped beers produce significantly higher CO2 levels and packaging pressures, because there is much more re-fermentation going on than usual. And sometimes that pressure builds until the bottle explodes.

In the wake of the 2020 wildfires, Shellhammer turned his attention to smoke-tainted hops. The primary culprit is volatile phenols (VP), which are produced when the lignin in plant cells is exposed to the high heat of a wildfire. Earlier this year, the OSU team discovered that smoke exposure while the hops are drying increased VP uptake and substantially altered the hops’ aroma; those with higher levels took on meaty, smoky, and artificial BBQ notes; medicinal flavors have also been reported. Those notes were so strong that they overpowered more desirable beery qualities, like citrus notes or aroma intensity.

For this latest project, Shellhammer worked with graduate student Cade Jobe to harvest Idaho-grown Cascade hops. Then they “smoked” the hops in kilns using wood pellets injected into the air intake systems, then dried them. They also harvested and dried non-smoked hops as a control.

According to recent reports, next, Shellhammer and Jobe brewed several batches of beer, blending smoke-tainted hops with untainted hops at different levels (25,  50, and 75 percent), along with beer brewed using only tainted hops and beer brewed with no tainted hops. They did this using both a dry-hopping process and one where they added hops during wort boiling. They measured the VP concentrations of each batch of beer with gas chromatography. Finally, a panel of 24 blind tasters evaluated each beer.

Industry observers note that shellhammer and Jobe found that dry-hopping with smoke-tainted hops resulted in increased VP concentrations; the more tainted hops were included, the higher those concentrations. The tasters reported more intense smoky aromas in those beers as well as meaty, smoky, medicinal, and artificial BBQ flavors.

Sources indicate that by contrast, when tainted hops were added during the boil, the resulting beer was pretty much indistinguishable from beers brewed with no smoke-tainted hops, even though the former still showed the same increases in VP concentrations. There was no discernible difference when hops were added during the wort boiling stage.

In a recent development, the authors suggest brewers could blend smoke-tainted hops with untainted batches when dry-hopping, keeping the smoke-tainted hops to less than 50 percent. This would still allow brewers to use tainted batches of hops, reducing the economic impact of wildfires on beer production.

“Hop growers and brewers are looking for guidance on whether and how to use smoke-affected hops,” mentioned Jobe. “This research begins to provide some practical information to help hop growers and brewers understand how smoky hops impact beer so that they can make the best decisions.”.

In a recent development, there’s still more research to be done to confirm the precise ratios and to replicate these initial findings. Future research should also explore the impact of hops exposed to smoke under field conditions, when the plant is still metabolically active, along with consumer studies to evaluate whether beer drinkers more generally will find the flavors of beers made with blended hops (tainted and untainted) acceptable. The findings may not extend to wine production, however, since the fermentation processes are so different, per the authors. And grapes comprise almost all the raw materials in wine, while hops are a smaller fraction of beer brewing ingredients.
