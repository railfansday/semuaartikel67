Sources indicate that shares of Annaly Capital Management (NLY +0.54%) have tumbled more than 25% from their 52-week high. That has the mortgage-focused real estate investment trust (REIT) trading at a dividend yield of more than 16.5%.

That seems like an enticing yield for income-seeking investors. Here's a look at whether the high-dividend REIT is a buy, sell, or hold at its currently lower price point.

The main factor weighing on Annaly's stock is rising interest rates. The 10-year Treasury rate has risen from a low of 4% to around 5% over the past few months. When rates rise, the value of fixed-income investments like the mortgage-backed securities Annaly holds falls. That's evident in the decline in Annaly's book value, which fell from $20.15 per share in the second quarter to $17.86 per share in the third quarter.

NYSE · As of 11:19 AM ET 11/25/26.

An investment in Annaly at this point is a bet on two things. First, that it can generate enough earnings available for distribution (EAD) to continue paying its high-yielding dividend, which the REIT did during the third quarter, as EAD of $0.80 per share comfortably covered its $0.75 per share dividend. The improvement in EAD in recent years has enabled Annaly to increase its dividend twice. However, that followed a string of cuts in prior years.

Sources indicate that the other factor you're betting on is a relatively stable interest rate environment. If rates surge, Annaly's MBS portfolio would lose value (and its borrowing costs would rise). However, while falling rates would boost the value of its holdings (and lower costs), they would also reduce the yield on latest MBS investments, which could affect returns.

Industry observers note that while Annaly could deliver a strong total return from its current price point, I believe the long-term return will likely be much lower than its current yield implies. As its history shows, the firm has delivered a rather pedestrian total return since its IPO, even with dividends reinvested. That's why I sold my shares a few years ago and wouldn't buy more now, even though they're 25% below the 52-week high.
