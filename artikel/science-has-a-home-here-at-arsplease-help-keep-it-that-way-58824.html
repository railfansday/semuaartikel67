Science has a home here at Ars—please help keep it that way

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, it’s now been 21 years since I first published an article at Ars Technica. It has been the only home I’ve known as a writer, and it has been essential to my development from scientist to blogger to journalist. It’s no exaggeration to say that Ars has helped make me who I am today.

Recent findings suggest that so, asking you to support Ars via a subscription is very personal (and a bit selfish) for me—I’d like to stay here! Jobs that give you this sort of independence, that let you both edit and write, are very rare in journalism, and I’m unlikely to find another. Helping secure Ars’ future through your support will mean I don’t have to.

Recent findings suggest that but I’d rather make a case for why you’ll benefit from supporting Ars. In fact, I’ll make three: the writing, the people, and the community.

Since my start here, the central mission of Ars has been the same: cover the news in a way that helps our readers understand the facts and why they’re important. The landscape has shifted dramatically over that time—technology, science, and policy are very different from 21 years ago. But understanding those changes and why they happened matters. It matters for everything from what you should buy today to what the world you’re going to be living in 10 years from now will look like.

I’ve had the privilege of helping to decide how to translate that mission to science coverage. That decision took place against an important backdrop: While most people value things like evidence and research, they often struggle to recognize how those are used to build a body of reliable scientific information. That struggle kind of made the decision for me. Putting people in the know means helping them understand how science works as a process, and why that can produce information about the world we can trust.

It has been revealed that focusing on that has made our science coverage different from most of what you can get elsewhere. We don’t just tell you what’s fresh in the world of science; we help you understand the history of the massive questions, the breakthroughs that allow us to address old problems in new ways, and the clever ideas people have for how to extract reliable information from a noisy and chaotic Universe. I’d like to think our readers get more out of that sort of writing, and it’s worth supporting.

But I’d be giving you a very incomplete picture of things if I only talked about science. Everyone at Ars has had to think about what sort of stories and presentation fit best with our mission. While our writing is essential now, it’s the people behind it that will ensure that it remains vital no matter what other changes come our way in the coming decades. And I can say with more than a little confidence that Ars is full of people you can trust to keep it that way.

One reason you can trust that is that the people at Ars now are, in many cases, the same ones who were here when you first started reading the site. We have remarkably low turnover, especially at the higher levels in our organization. There are two reasons for that: Ars is incredibly well-managed, and the people here believe in our mission.

Recent findings suggest that the former is notable because it should assure you that the money we get from your subscription won’t be wasted on some ill-advised pivot to video, or whatever the next craze is. The latter matters because it’s the people here who make Ars what it is. I work with some of the most thoughtful people I have ever encountered, and they all care deeply about making our work informative and compelling. I have also been lucky enough to work with a large group of freelancers over the years, and I have nothing but good things to say about them. We work hard to make sure that, even when the name on the byline is unfamiliar to you, you can be confident that you’re getting the same quality of reporting.

All of them—the full-time staff and the freelancers—are smart, great writers and know their areas of expertise better than most people in this business. Like I’m sure you do, I learn a lot from what they write (unlike you, I also get the bonus of learning from them in our Slack channels).

Recent findings suggest that but it’s not just my fellow writers whom I’ve learned from. We’ve got one of the smartest communities on the Internet, with experts from pretty much every area we cover regularly contributing to the comments. In the discussions attached to my articles, I have regularly seen them fill in details I didn’t have space to mention, clarify things in response to other readers’ questions, and talk about related topics that didn’t really fit into the article. I’ve learned a lot from this, and I’m confident the rest of the readers have, too.

Recent findings suggest that it’s strange to say the opposite of one of the central dogmas of the Internet: Read the comments. But it’s something I’ve told our freelance writers pretty regularly.

It has been revealed that we’ve kept things that way even as most other sites’ comments turned toxic and were shut off. That’s taken both an editorial commitment and lots of staff time spent moderating and engaging in community management. Those don’t come for free, so your subscriptions will help support that, too.

According to recent reports, I hope you’ll consider signing up if you’re able. Beyond the financials, I know we all appreciate it as a sign we’re on the right track.
