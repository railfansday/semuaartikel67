It has been revealed that over 3,000,000 sold! Call it the McCamry. Along with the Ford Taurus and the Honda Accord, this Toyota is right at the top of the list when Amer­ican families go latest-car shopping, and right at the top of the ratings in our last family-sedan comparison test (November 1995). Since its introduction here in 1983, Toyota has diligently smoothed up, slicked up, and suaved up the Camry till the only remaining awkwardness is the unlikely spelling of its name.

Industry observers note that that won’t change as Toyota introduces its fourth-­generation Camry in Sep­tember, and neither will the mix of virtues that make this model a favorite. We’re talking refinement of the Camry theme, not reinvention. In fact, the basic platform and powertrain carry on, though engineering improvements seem to touch every part of the car. It’s slightly bigger in each exterior dimension—0.7 inch longer, 0.4 inch wider, 2.1 inches longer in wheelbase; the same four­-cylinder and V-6 engines are modestly more powerful (up 8 hp in the four, up 6 hp in the V-6); and the interior has been upgraded to meet all the safety standards scheduled in the next few years, including the 1997 side-impact and 1999 head-­impact requirements. The aerodynamic drag coefficient has been improved to 0.30, from 0.33.

Toyota is, however, tightening the Camry’s focus. It’s available only as a four-door now in three trim levels: CE, LE, and XLE. Gone is the coupe, which won over just five percent of Camry buyers. Gone, too, is the station wagon, a shape that convinced only three per­cent of Camry buyers in its losing battle against the tide of minivans. (Production of a Camry-based minivan begins next spring.).

Sources indicate that in the past, we’ve joked that the Camry’s personality is one of aggressive bland­ness. Unlike the frisky feeling of the Accord and the athletic tautness of the Dodge Intrepid, both of which come through loud and clear before you’ve driven to the first corner, the Camry’s subtlety grows on you over time. It’s smoother, softer, quieter than the others, less com­municative, less mechanical. Instead of talking to you in the very pleasant way of those others, it’s the silent type that, you soon notice, goes expertly about its job.

In a recent development, in this new Camry, Toyota sharpened the expertise without changing the per­sonality. It’s still a car that’s light to the touch: easy to steer, easy to brake. The ride is commendably free of harshness, thanks in part to mounting the suspension on isolated crossmembers front and rear. The four­-cylinder version, with its 195/70SR-14 tires, rides very smoothly and ignores almost completely the road textures that create so much droning in sporting cars. The V-6s come with 205/65HR-15s on six­-inch-wide wheels. These tires are noticeably harsher and noisier than those on the four-cylinder cars, though road nose is still quite reasonable when compared with other cars in this class. Road grip and handling predictability are well above the minimums we’d set for family cars.

Industry observers note that the Camry V-6 has been one of the top performers in this class. The 1997 model gains six horsepower (now 194 hp at the same 5200 rpm as the previous peak), gets shorter final-drive gearing (3.93 versus 3.43), and weighs about the same as last year’s smaller car. Another performance plus: A manual transmission has returned to the V-6 lineup, available only on CE models. How­ever, our LE V-6 auto­matic was no quicker than the last LE V-6 we tested, managing 0 to 60 mph in 8.3 seconds. Expect the five-speed to trim that by about four-tenths.

It has been revealed that on the road, the automatic’s eagerness to change down a gear or two when you press the pedal, even slightly, and the seamless smoothness of those changes give this car quick reflexes in traffic. The engine revs with the hushed moan of a Lexus. This year, traction control is optional on LE and XLE V-6 models. When it comes to powertrain pleasures, the Camry is world-class.

Still, it’s the more subtle details of pas­senger accommodation that make this Toyota a family favorite. In the previous­-generation Camry, our comparison tests show two adults fit in the back seat more comfortably than in some other brands up to 10 inches longer. We expect this Camry advantage to widen fractionally in this upcoming model because both legroom and head­room in back are increased by a half-inch. Although the Camry is wider on the out­side, energy-absorbing padding to protect in side impacts takes up the extra space and, in certain spots, a little more. The Camry still has pockets in the front doors, unlike the Taurus, which sacrificed them for crash-protection equipment.

Those who wish to maximize rear-seat space should think twice about opting for power front seats (standard equipment on the top-of-the-line XLE). The manual­adjust buckets have excellent toeroom under them, which the power adjuster interrupts with a transverse bar. Tall folks won’t like that.

According to recent reports, as in most modern sedans, the Camry has fold-forward rear seatbacks split one­-third and two-thirds, allowing the cargo compartment to be extended into the pas­senger area. Here’s another think-twice: Camrys equipped with the optional child seat—it’s built into the two-thirds section of the rear seat—have that section fixed in place, ruling out all but a narrow passage into the trunk.

At the rear, the trunklid opens wider and lower than before. Space inside, however, is a bit less in the new body—14.1 cubic feet versus 14.8 before. And the new car still has the conventional trunk hinges with the sickle arms that swing down into your luggage when the lid is closed.

Conventional is the right description for the upcoming instrument panel, certainly more conventional than the old one, which tried to visually compartmentalize the cockpit by emphasizing a huge black field in front of the driver. That was the good part. The bad part was the deemphasized and totally boring padding it left for the passenger’s amusement. Now the all-new panel stretches its padding across the inte­rior in an equal-opportunity gesture that makes the interior seem brighter and wider. The glove box is 29 percent larger. The audio controls were raised to a mid­dash height, making them easy to see and reach. The climate controls have changed over to the increasingly common turn-to-adjust knobs. They’re large enough, and so obvious in their workings, that they are easy to operate in their less prominent location. The Camry continues to position its ignition switch in the dash where you can readily see it, instead of hiding it on the side of the steering column.

Toyota has proven itself to be the master of those little conveniences that add up to an exceptionally pleasing car. In this fresh Camry we see more clever trade-offs as the engineers cut costs, reduce weight, and usually end up serving the customer better, too. For example, the new front bumper cuts the part count nearly in half and saves weight, yet it increases protec­tion to 5 mph from 2.5. Inside, the seats recline in stepless increments now, and the buckets’ height adjusters use a single knob instead of two. Both of these changes cut costs, Toyota says. We say they make the car better, too. Models that previously would have had retracting antennas now have the antenna screened on the rear glass, saving weight and cost. The new Bosch anti-lock brake system weighs half as much as the old one.

Still, not every cost saver is a benefit. The LCD odometer is simply gray num­bers on a gray field, making it less visible than the glowing digits in competitors like the Chrysler Cirrus and the Dodge Stratus. And the new one-piece front-bumper cover now incorporates the grille into its overtly ordinary Rubbermaid-looking façade.

Industry observers note that there’s been much speculation in the automotive-trade press about a possible price cut. Don’t hold your breath. Sales of the old Camry hit records earlier this year with no apparent price cutting. Were there sweeteners for the dealer? Sure, but other brands had them, too. Toyota knows all the tricks for selling cars without collapsing its price structure-tricks, we think, that mostly won’t be needed to move this shrewdly updated Camry.
