IBM Has Paid a Dividend Every Year Since 1916. Here's What the Stock Yields After Its 26% Drop.

It has been revealed that international Business Machines (IBM +1.89%) has paid shareholders a dividend every year since 1916. And in April, it lifted its quarterly payout for the 31st straight year.

The stock's recent history is rockier. Shares finished June 2 at $329.23, a record close. The steepest drop since then was on July 14, when shares fell around 25% in one day after IBM reported preliminary second-quarter results that missed its own expectations. At about $243 as of this writing, the stock is still 26% under the record.

Industry observers note that the drop has lifted the yield for anyone buying today. IBM pays $1.69 a share every quarter, or $6.76 a year, which works out to a dividend yield of around 2.8% at the stock's current price. At the June record, the same payout yielded about 2.1%.

IBM's free cash flow (the company's measure of the cash its core business produces after capital spending) rose to $14.7 billion in 2025 from $12.7 billion in 2024. Its dividends cost about $6.3 billion last year, or roughly 42% of that cash. And with its third-quarter report on Oct. 21, management upped its forecast and now expects free cash flow to rise by over $1 billion in 2026, which would push this year's total above $15.7 billion. With the dividend on track to cost about $6.4 billion this year, the payout would take roughly 40% of that cash.

The nine-month numbers look tighter at first glance. Through Sept. 30, IBM generated $7.8 billion of free cash flow, up $0.6 billion from a year earlier, and paid $4.8 billion in dividends.

Industry observers note that but IBM takes in much of its cash late in the year. Its fourth quarter alone produced $7.6 billion of free cash flow in 2025, around half the year's total. And free cash flow improved in the third quarter, growing $0.7 billion year over year to $3.0 billion after a $0.3 billion fall in the second quarter.

Sources indicate that IBM's CFO, Jim Kavanaugh, said on the earnings call that the company's free cash flow margin for the first nine months was the highest in its reported history. The dividend itself looks safe.

But the raises are small. April's hike lifted the quarterly payout by a penny, to $1.69 from $1.68. Two years before, it was $1.67.

Instead, IBM's spare cash has gone to acquisitions. It spent $11.4 billion to buy businesses from January through September, most of it on data-streaming software company Confluent. That's almost four times the $3 billion or so of free cash flow left after dividends in those months.

According to recent reports, IBM drew down cash and added debt along the way. Its cash, restricted cash and marketable securities dropped $3.4 billion from the end of 2025 to $11.1 billion, and its debt grew $2.6 billion to $63.8 billion.

In a recent development, last year followed a similar pattern. IBM spent $8.3 billion on acquisitions in 2025, almost all the $8.5 billion or so of free cash flow left after dividends. And even if this year's free cash flow clears $15.7 billion, what remains after dividends would still be less than the $11.4 billion IBM has already spent on deals.

The deals are adding to IBM's growth. In the third quarter, its data software revenue climbed 20% on a constant-currency basis, helped by Confluent, which IBM didn't own a year earlier.

But the debt has a cost. Management stated higher net interest expense, plus capital spending and cash taxes, ate into this year's free cash flow gains.

For dividend investors, that means the payout will probably keep growing slowly while acquisitions come first.

By itself, I don't think it is. With raises of a penny a year, a buyer now is mainly locking in a 2.8% yield that grows slowly.

In a recent development, most of the return needs to come from the business, and its price looks fair to me. At about $243 a share, IBM's market value is roughly $230 billion. Over $15.7 billion of free cash flow this year would be almost 7% of that, compared to about 5% at the June record.

Overall, the dividend looks safe, and a buyer now gets more of it per dollar invested than in June. At about $243, I think IBM stock is worth considering, mostly for the growth that deals like Confluent might add to its software business. The 2.8% yield is a steady bonus as that plays out.
