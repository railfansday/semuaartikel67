Industry News: Mazama Energy has a solution for geothermal’s biggest drilling problem

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that success in geothermal energy is guided by two basic goals — drill fast and drill deep. Hit both targets, and geothermal startups have a good chance at being cheaper than nearly every other source on the electrical grid.

It has been revealed that but while the goals might be straightforward, they’re anything but easy. Drilling fast generates heat, which damages equipment, and so does drilling deep.

Those two foes are what led Mazama Energy to develop a fresh technology, called Carbon Chill, that uses carbon dioxide to cool the drill bit and bring cuttings to the surface. The technology will not only let the company drill deeper, but also faster. The startup gave TechCrunch an exclusive look at Carbon Chill.

“Our rate of penetration increased 20% over the already very high rates we were achieving,” Sriram Vasantharajan, co-founder and CEO of Mazama Energy, told TechCrunch.

According to recent reports, speed has other advantages in geothermal. Drilling represents about 25% to 30% of the total cost of building a power plant, so reducing drill time can bring costs down significantly.

If geothermal startups succeed in drilling faster and cheaper, they stand to help reshape the grid in the 2030s. Hyperscalers are already planning the facilities that will enter operation at the end of the decade.

Sources indicate that mazama has been working on the problem for years now. In October 2025, the Khosla-backed startup stated it had drilled a well in Oregon that reached 629˚ F (331˚ C), and a more recent well has surpassed 700˚ F, Vasantharajan said. In September, the startup raised $135 million in a round led by Centaurus Capital and Doerr Capital.

Mazama hopes that its new carbon dioxide-based cooling technology will help it prove that geothermal energy from super-hot rock is a viable contender in the electricity market.

In a recent development, already companies like Google and Meta have started to explore the use of geothermal energy, in part because the potential is enormous: A report from the National Laboratory of the Rockies said that geothermal could generate between 26.5 to 57 terawatts of electricity in the U.S. alone. If just 10% of the conservative estimate were developed, it would more than double the U.S.’s peak generating capacity.

Geothermal experts have identified super-hot rock — in which temperatures exceed 752˚ F (400˚ C) — as a promising way to improve the performance of individual wells. At those temperatures, water turns “supercritical,” a gaseous-like liquid state which carries five to 10 times more energy than water from traditional geothermal wells. By harvesting more energy from each well, developers have to drill fewer of them, bringing costs down.

It has been revealed that accessing that heat can be challenging for traditional drilling methods. Most geothermal drillers use “mud” — a combination of water and other compounds to keep the bit cool and clear cuttings from the borehole. But mud can only bring temperatures down so much. Eventually, they become so hot that electronics in the drill bit, which help keep it on course, start to fail.

High temperature-rated electronics exist, but they’d be cost prohibitive, Vasantharajan remarked.

According to recent reports, instead of mud, Mazama injects liquid carbon dioxide down the center of its drill string. As it travels deeper, it gets heated by the rock around it, which turns it supercritical, too. Then, just before it reaches the drill head, Mazama has it flow through a valve that looks a lot like a rocket nozzle. As the gas expands, it escapes through the cone-shaped nozzle and rapidly cools, absorbing heat from the electronics and the drill bit.

“I look at [carbon dioxide cooling] as a binary,” he remarked. “Without such technologies, I am, today, not able to drill these horizontal wells at super-hot rock temperatures.”.

It has been revealed that that capital injection will help Mazama drill more wells deeper. By next year, Vasantharajan said his company hopes to complete a single well that’s capable of generating up to 15 megawatts of electricity.

It has been revealed that “What was just a dream or a quest, I can tell you with confidence is now a promise,” he noted. “We see a path towards this commercial super-hot rock geothermal.”.
