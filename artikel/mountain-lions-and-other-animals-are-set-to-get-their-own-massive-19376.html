Mountain lions and other animals are set to get their own massive land bridge that crosses over 10 lanes of the 101 Freeway near Los Angeles

California's newly introduced wildlife crossing over the 101 Freeway is set to open in December 2026, reconnecting habitats for mountain lions and other animals.

The Wallis Annenberg Wildlife Crossing is scheduled to officially open to wildlife on December 2, 2026. The planted bridge for animals spans the 101 Freeway in Agoura Hills, California.

According to recent reports, it will reconnect wildlife habitat in the Santa Monica Mountains with the Simi Hills to the north. The freeway below carries more than 300,000 vehicles a day.

The late P-22, a mountain lion that lived in Los Angeles, helped inspire the project.

According to Caltrans, the state agency managing the project, the bridge over the freeway is about 210 feet (64 meters) long and roughly 174 feet (53 meters) wide.

It spans ten lanes of pavement: eight lanes of traffic and two breakdown lanes. A second structure carries the crossing over Agoura Road, a local road.

The crossing broke ground on Earth Day, April 22, 2022. The first stage, over the freeway, was completed in June 2025.

Caltrans costs for all stages are now estimated at $114 million, pending a final contractor estimate for parts of stage two. Public funds cover $77.7 million, or 68 percent, and private donations $37.2 million, or 32 percent.

Costs outside that budget, including the native plant nursery and wildlife research, are covered by additional private donations, according to the project’s official FAQ.

In a recent development, the top of the structure will be covered in nearly 1 acre (0.4 hectares) of native vegetation. The project will also restore more than 10 additional acres (4 hectares) of nearby habitat as coastal sage scrub and oak savannah.

In a recent development, the first stage of construction called for about 5,000 plants, the National Wildlife Federation mentioned in 2023. Key species include coast live oak, toyon, ceanothus, sages, buckwheat and penstemon.

According to recent reports, the plants are grown from seeds collected within 5 miles (8 kilometers) of the site. More than one million native seeds had been gathered by mid-2023.

According to recent reports, unlike most wildlife crossings, this one must deal with heavy traffic noise and light. The design tackles both with a large vegetated barrier and other design features.

Exclusionary fencing and vegetation cues are meant to steer animals toward the bridge. The site is already a natural funnel between open space to the north and south.

According to recent reports, the National Park Service has studied mountain lions in and around the Santa Monica Mountains since 2002. It calls the 101 Freeway “the biggest barrier between the Santa Monica Mountains and other large natural areas.”.

Biologists have documented at least 32 mountain lions killed by vehicles in the study area, part of a wider toll of roadkill on highways.

The isolated lions have the lowest genetic diversity ever documented in the West. The only population with lower levels was in south Florida in the mid-1990s.

In a 2022 study led by UCLA, sperm samples from five male lions that had died were on average 93 percent abnormal. The team also identified nine adult males from the Santa Monica and Santa Ana mountains with physical signs of inbreeding.

A 2016 modeling study in Proceedings of the Royal Society B put the local population’s chance of extinction within 50 years at 15 percent. When inbreeding depression was included, that probability rose to 99.7 percent.

In the simulations, at least one new lion arriving per generation was enough to largely maintain genetic diversity.

Park biologists first captured and radio-collared P-22 in March 2012, when he was estimated to be two years old.

In a recent development, to reach Griffith Park, he had crossed both the 101 and the 405, two of the busiest freeways in the world. He then spent more than ten years there, in the smallest home range ever recorded for an adult male mountain lion.

In a recent development, after scientists noticed a change in his behavior, he was captured on December 12, 2022. He was euthanized under general anesthesia five days later.

“P-22 was a fascinating animal to study,” Jeff Sikich, lead field biologist for the park service’s mountain lion study, noted in 2023.

On October 28, scientists captured the first photo of an animal on the unfinished crossing, a coyote climbing its southern slope.

Bobcats, coyotes, gray foxes, mule deer and mountain lions already regularly use a trail next to the site.

Recent findings suggest that biologists from Santa Monica Mountains National Recreation Area planned to install remote wildlife cameras in early November. Their monitoring framework includes 14 objectives, from documenting use by mountain lions and mule deer to tracking genetic diversity, vegetation, light and sound.

“This is more than just about whether animals use the crossing,” remarked Seth Riley, wildlife branch lead at Santa Monica Mountains National Recreation Area. “We want to understand how effective it is in reconnecting an ecosystem that has been divided by the freeway for decades.”.

“That is a much bigger scientific question and answering it will take years,” Riley added.

Images of animals using the crossing will be made public, but not in real time, for security reasons.

The information was obtained from the National Park Service.
