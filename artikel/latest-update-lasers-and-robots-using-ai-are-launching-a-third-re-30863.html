Lasers, robots and spot sprayers cut herbicide use, but two weed scientists explain why herbicide resistance will keep evolving around them.

In theory, a machine that rolls through a farm to zap or dig up individual weeds should be the perfect way to eliminate harmful chemicals and stop chemical-resistant weeds in their tracks.

However, two scientists recently warned that this high-tech farming dream is not yet the absolute cure everyone hopes it will be.

Recent findings suggest that resistance comes from the weeds that are hit and survive, not from how much herbicide the field gets. So a machine that treats only the weeds can cut herbicide use sharply and still favor resistant plants just as strongly.

The warning comes in a commentary by Lynn Sosnoskie of Cornell University and Stephen Duke of the University of Mississippi.

It’s an assessment of what the upcoming machines have done so far, with no new field experiments of its own. It weighs the benefits against the trouble that the two scientists expect will follow.

“In the last 100 years, there have been two revolutions in weed management: synthetic herbicides and herbicide-resistant crops,” said Duke, a research professor at the University’s National Center for Natural Products Research.

“It looks to me like AI-driven precision weed management will be the next revolution.”.

The machines come in three kinds, and all start with a camera. Software picks weeds out from crop plants and bare soil as the machine moves. Most carry their own lights under a hood, so they can work at night.

A sprayer then hits each weed with a nozzle, a laser burns its growing point, or a cultivator pulls it out.

The savings can be real. In U.S. trials of the one spot-spraying system, herbicide savings averaged about $50 an acre ($123 a hectare) in soybeans and $17 an acre ($43 a hectare) in corn.

In vegetable trials that Sosnoskie ran, laser weeding caused almost no crop injury, left the crop plants at least 30 percent heavier, and matched the yields of weed-free plots.

Sosnoskie, an associate professor in Cornell’s School of Integrative Plant Science, works on fruit and vegetable crops, and she remarked herbicides can damage or stunt them.

“But what we’re finding is that with precision technology, we can keep some, and sometimes even all, of the herbicide off the crop,” she added.

Some commercial robotic weeders run about $75,000 or more, and laser systems can cost over $1 million.

According to recent reports, one U.S. analysis estimated that a spot-sprayer pays for itself in saved herbicide only after treating about 53,000 to 116,000 acres (21,500 to 47,000 hectares). Where the area falls, within that range, depends on weed density and herbicide prices.

Duke described an organic farmer in the Netherlands who bought a laser weeder for more than a million dollars. It saved the farmer so much hassle that he bought a second one, Duke noted, but that won’t be true for everyone.

“Some of these farmers who are working with high-value crops can spend that much, but many can’t.”.

Sources indicate that organic growers have a particular reason, Duke stated. They can’t use synthetic herbicides, so they depend on hand weeding, and that labor is getting harder to find.

Prices are expected to fall as production scales up, the authors wrote, and some systems may be retrofitted onto existing equipment.

Farmers worldwide spend an estimated $35 billion to $47 billion a year on herbicides, and the authors wrote that wide adoption of these machines could shrink that market.

Fewer sales could mean less money for finding latest herbicides, which are already scarce. Few substances with a new mode of action have reached the market in four decades, even as public pressure builds against chemicals such as glyphosate, the most widely used of them.

It has been revealed that the weed resistance problem is subtler. A weed becomes resistant when a treated plant survives and reproduces, so what matters is what happens to each sprayed plant, not how much herbicide the field gets.

Industry observers note that A camera that misses a weed leaves it untreated, and a nozzle that only partly covers one gives it a low dose. The authors wrote that both could speed up resistance rather than slow it down.

Fast growers such as Palmer amaranth and waterhemp can quickly outgrow the size a sprayer handles after a missed pass, and each plant can drop hundreds of thousands of seeds.

“That doesn’t mean every weed is resistant to every herbicide, but there are a lot of herbicide-resistant weeds out there,” Duke said. “It’s become a really major problem.”.

Industry observers note that the second warning is about the camera itself. A weed that sprouts under the crop canopy, grows in an unusual shape or looks enough like the crop stands a better chance of being left alone. Over many seasons, that could select for weeds that are harder to spot.

According to recent reports, weeds have done this before. Under generations of hand weeding, a barnyard grass that grows in rice paddies evolved a more upright shape and a white central vein on its leaf.

Recent findings suggest that both are features of cultivated rice, so farmers pulled it less often. The authors called a repeat under machine vision speculative but plausible.

Recent findings suggest that lasers and cultivators favor a different set of weeds, because both attack only the part of the plant above ground. Perennials that regrow from roots, tubers or underground stems could come to dominate fields where those machines do most of the work.

Recent findings suggest that “What I’ve been arguing is just because we have this technology, that doesn’t free us from weeds,” Sosnoskie noted. “With weed control, we always have to think about how the weed is going to respond or try to ‘win.'”.

According to recent reports, the software is only as good as the pictures it learned from. Dust, crop residue, odd lighting and a stressed crop that looks like a weed can all throw off the system.

“I worked with a manufacturer whose original algorithm kept missing one specific weed species: common ragweed,” Sosnoskie said. “Well, it turns out they don’t have the same ragweed species in California.”.

The authors’ own conclusion is blunt. Weeds evolved around herbicides, and they expect weeds to evolve around the new machines too. Which traits, and how fast, is still speculation.

The systems will also need better sensors and more varied training images to hold their accuracy from one region to the next, they wrote.

Sources indicate that furthermore, the old, soil-applied herbicides that stop weeds before they sprout are likely to stay in use alongside the farm AI. Fewer weeds coming up means fewer for a camera to miss.

The full study was published in the journal Engineering.
