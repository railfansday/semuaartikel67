Meta Refused to Share Muse for Testing, UK Financial Watchdog Complains

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Meta declined a suggestion from the UK’s financial regulator last week that they joint-test Muse using their sandbox, according to the watchdog’s head of innovation Colin Payne. It comes as regulators across the UK, including the competition and data watchdogs, warned Meta it needs to ensure its AI products comply with UK consumer protection laws.

“It’s another force of attack, agents hitting the streets, most of them coming from the US,” Payne, of the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA), said Tuesday in a panel hosted by AI firm Whitespace. “We’ve got a number of entrants into the market. We want to see what they’re going to be doing in the market. I think it’s just reasonable,” he later explained to WIRED. Payne told WIRED the FCA had been hoping the organizations could work together to investigate what interactions might look like between the tool, marketed as a personal assistant, and a normal customer.

His organization has been experimenting with other firms as many, including Amazon, roll out AI shopping assistants, and other agentic tools in the UK.

Recent findings suggest that the FCA especially wants to understand how payments will be made by agents—as multiple British retailers, including grocers Sainsbury’s, Marks & Spencer, and Ocado, department store Debenhams Group, and electronics seller Currys said they’d been working with Meta to smooth Muse’s access to their sites. Payne is concerned about potential unintended consequences of prompts, especially when a user has a low level of understanding of how the AI product works. “If an agent is acting autonomously, we want to make sure it’s in the interest of the consumer,” he says.

Sources indicate that payne did note the testing would have taken time to set up. “Normally you have a long period of time speaking to a firm and then you plan it over a period of weeks or months,” he explained, but Meta only made the FCA aware of its prepares last month.

“We’ve engaged constructively with the appropriate regulatory authorities and will continue to do so,” Meta Spokesperson Matthew Pollard tells WIRED.

It has been revealed that more than 10 million users have set up Muse in North America since it was launched in September. British watchdogs say they will be monitoring Muse’s rollout closely as they are bracing for significant uptake on their side of the pond, too, given that many Brits already use Meta’s other products, like Instagram, Facebook, and WhatsApp. “If it’s going to be equally popular in the UK, we need to be able to see what it’s going to do,” Payne says.

Industry observers note that the ICO stressed the importance of users understanding and being able to control how Muse uses their personal data. The Competition and Markets Authority has already made Meta adapt its terms and conditions for UK consumers to say Meta must exercise “reasonable skill and care/professional diligence” when providing Muse, the EU and UK terms say, with the document acknowledging that outputs may still be inaccurate and actions may not be completed as intended.

“If we have concerns, we will not hesitate to act to protect UK consumers,” the CMA mentioned in a statement.
