AI adoption could disproportionately harm Black and Latino workers

🌐 Source: Fastcompany • 📅 20 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 6 min read (1111 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has become increasingly clear that the AI revolution is not just poised to upend white-collar jobs, despite what public discourse might suggest. While entry-level roles are, in fact, on the decline in tech and finance—and employers continue to use AI as a rationale for layoffs—there is ample evidence that rapid AI adoption could interrupt pathways for workers without college degrees. The effects of this upheaval will be uneven, which means certain demographic groups are more likely to be impacted. Some of the jobs that have been deemed “highly exposed” to AI, including clerical and administrative roles, are largely dominated by women, and a newly introduced report from the McKinsey Institute for Economic Mobility posits that Black and Latino workers could also face major consequences. The report finds that Black and Latino workers are overrepresented in the sectors where automation and AI adoption will supplant work altogether, rather than augment work. While the overall rate of AI adoption is relatively stable across different demographics, Black and Latino workers will be affected more acutely by job displacement. Across working hours that are impacted by AI adoption, 40% of hours performed by Black workers and 41% by Latino workers will be displaced (as compared to 37% among white workers). That may not seem like a huge gap. But as the report points out, many of the jobs that are most exposed to AI have historically been pathways to the middle class, making them crucial drivers of social mobility across communities that have faced systemic barriers to entry. Black workers, in particular, dominate six jobs that are likely to see the biggest decline in labor demand, according to McKinsey, including postal mail carriers and customer service representatives; Latino workers are also especially likely to hold some of those jobs, namely dishwashers and hand packers. Black and Latino workers—along with workers in rural regions—are also much more likely to be in jobs that involve physical labor, which are more vulnerable to being automated through robotics and other AI-enabled systems. On the whole, lower-wage workers will face the greatest impact, and are 7.6 times as likely to need to transition into a new job when compared to higher-wage workers; people without a college degree are 1.8 times more likely to need to do the same. Meanwhile, white workers are better positioned to both avoid this level of AI disruption and actually benefit from AI adoption in a number of ways. It’s not just that they’re more likely to hold jobs where AI will enhance their work, boosting productivity rather than replacing employees outright. When compared to their Black and Latino counterparts, white workers are also 30% to 50% more likely to work in the industries where private capital will be deployed, yielding more jobs and wealth creation, the McKinsey report states. That means white workers already have more of a foothold in jobs involving robotics or semiconductors or biotech, setting them up for success as hundreds of billions of dollars pour into those industries. (Look no further than the Anthropic IPO this week, which has created thousands of millionaires.)  In other words: Without broader policy changes or reskilling initiatives, many Black and Latino workers may be forced out of their jobs and locked out of opportunities to cash in on the AI boom.
