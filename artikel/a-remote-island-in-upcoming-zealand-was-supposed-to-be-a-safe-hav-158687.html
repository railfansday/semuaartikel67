A remote island off New Zealand’s coast reveals the challenges of protecting native birds from invasive predators.

Today’s Image of the Day from NASA Earth Observatory features Resolution Island, a remote expanse of wilderness off the southwestern coast of Latest Zealand.

Surrounded by deep blue waters and covered in dense vegetation, the island appears to be an ideal sanctuary for wildlife.

Its isolation, however, has not always offered the protection that conservationists envisioned.

For more than a century, Resolution Island has been at the center of an ambitious effort to preserve some of Latest Zealand’s most extraordinary native birds.

Its history reveals both the potential and the limitations of using islands as natural barriers against invasive species.

The image was captured on December 12, 2025, by the Operational Land Imager aboard the Landsat 9 satellite.

Resolution Island lies within Fiordland National Park, a spectacular region of mountains, forests, waterfalls, and narrow waterways that extend deep into the southwestern coast of New Zealand’s South Island.

During past ice ages, enormous glaciers moved through the region, gradually carving deep valleys into the landscape.

As the glaciers retreated and sea levels rose, ocean water flooded many of these valleys, creating the dramatic fjords visible today.

It has been revealed that resolution Island is the largest island within the national park. Its mountainous terrain reaches an elevation of 3,507 feet at the summit of Mount Clerke.

The island’s varied landscape supports wetlands, ancient evergreen forests, and high-elevation grasslands. These distinct environments provide a range of habitats for native wildlife.

New Zealand’s long geographical isolation allowed wildlife to evolve in remarkable ways.

Before humans arrived, the country had virtually no native land mammals other than bats. Birds occupied many ecological niches that mammals filled elsewhere in the world.

Some species gradually lost the ability to fly, while others developed behaviors that left them particularly vulnerable to ground-dwelling predators.

Among them were kiwi, which forage along the forest floor, and kākāpō, the world’s only flightless parrot.

For millions of years, these birds survived without facing the kinds of mammalian predators found on other continents.

That evolutionary advantage became a liability when people introduced unfamiliar animals into Latest Zealand’s ecosystems.

Rats, cats, and members of the weasel family brought newly introduced pressures that native birds were poorly equipped to withstand.

By the nineteenth century, the decline of Upcoming Zealand’s native wildlife was becoming increasingly apparent.

Industry observers note that european settlers had introduced stoats and other predators in an attempt to control rabbit populations. Instead, these animals created another ecological problem by preying on vulnerable native species.

In 1872, conservationist Thomas Henry Potts suggested that Resolution Island could provide a refuge for birds threatened by introduced predators.

The surrounding ocean appeared to offer a natural protective barrier, allowing native species to survive without the dangers they faced on the mainland.

Resolution Island became a protected reserve in 1891, making it part of a pioneering chapter in Latest Zealand’s conservation history.

Long before modern wildlife restoration programs existed, naturalists were experimenting with ideas that would eventually become central to endangered species management.

One of the most dedicated figures in this undertaking was Richard Henry, who became the island’s caretaker.

Henry recognized the urgency of protecting birds that were rapidly disappearing from other parts of the country.

Working in an isolated and physically demanding environment, he transported hundreds of native birds across the waters of Fiordland.

More than 700 birds, primarily kiwi and kākāpō, were relocated to Resolution Island and neighboring islands during his conservation efforts.

Much of the work involved traveling by rowboat through a region known for challenging weather and rugged terrain.

Henry’s efforts represented an early attempt to prevent extinction by establishing wildlife populations in geographically isolated locations.

Unfortunately, the assumption that the surrounding waters would keep predators away proved incorrect.

In a recent development, by 1900, invasive predators had been detected on Resolution Island. Stoats were capable of swimming across the channels that separated the island from nearby land.

The discovery undermined years of painstaking conservation work and exposed a weakness in the island’s natural defenses.

Sources indicate that stoats are highly adaptable hunters. Their ability to exploit different environments makes them particularly problematic in Fresh Zealand, where many native birds nest on or near the ground.

Henry attempted to control the invading predators through trapping but eventually left the island to pursue conservation work elsewhere.

His experience demonstrated that geographical isolation alone was not necessarily enough to protect vulnerable species.

More than a century later, conservationists continue to build on Henry’s original vision.

In 2008, Upcoming Zealand’s Department of Conservation launched a predator management program on Resolution Island to improve the prospects of native wildlife.

Maintaining such a program across a large, mountainous island presents considerable challenges.

Dense forests, steep slopes, and remote locations complicate efforts to monitor wildlife and control invasive animals.

Conservation also extends into the surrounding waters, where a marine reserve protects coastal ecosystems near the island’s distinctive Five Fingers Peninsula.

Viewed from space, Resolution Island offers a striking portrait of an apparently untouched wilderness.

Yet beneath its forest canopy lies a complex ecological history shaped by human intervention, invasive species, and generations of conservation work.

The island remains a powerful reminder that protecting nature sometimes requires far more than simply setting it aside.
