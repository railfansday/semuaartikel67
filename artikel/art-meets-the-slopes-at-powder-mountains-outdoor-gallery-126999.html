Art Meets the Slopes at Powder Mountain’s Outdoor Gallery

Recent findings suggest that most skiers and snowboarders will likely pause at some point during a day on the slopes to take in their surroundings. After all, ski resorts typically offer great mountain views. But at Powder Mountain in Utah, visitors get more than a scenic view. They might also encounter a bright pink pineapple rising among the pines, a classical Greek column twisted into an improbable shape, or poetry carved into a boulder. These three installations are the latest additions to Powder Art Foundation’s skiable outdoor art museum, an evolving collection that challenges how—and where—we experience art.

In a recent development, davina Semo’s Listener (2020–2024), a patinated cast-bronze bell, is part of Powder Art Foundation’s outdoor art collection at Utah’s Powder Mountain. Photography courtesy of Powder Art Foundation.

In a recent development, established in 2024 in partnership with Dia Art Foundation, Powder Art Foundation occupies 8,000 acres of Utah’s Wasatch Mountains. The foundation’s goal is to bring contemporary installations into dialogue with the landscape while honoring the region’s connection to the Land Art movement.

“We’re building a collection that people encounter while spending time outdoors, so I’m interested in work that can surprise you and hold your attention without needing a museum around it,” notes Alex Zhang, chief creative officer of Powder Mountain.

Schemata Architects Designs a Modern Chalet on Powder Mountain.

This year’s newly introduced installations—from artists Jenny Holzer, Rose Wylie, and Xu Zhen—join an existing collection that includes works by Davina Semo, Nobuo Sekine, Nancy Holt, Kayode Ojo, and others. Each takes a different approach to interacting with the landscape. “I like having that range, rather than one consistent approach to what outdoor sculpture should look like,” Zhang notes.

It has been revealed that jenny Holzer’s ‘THE EYE’ (2026), featuring poetry by May Swenson carved into a native quartzite boulder, sits within the forested landscape of Utah’s Powder Mountain. Photography by Kim Raff for Powder Art Foundation.

Recent findings suggest that whether skiing in winter or hiking and biking during warmer months, visitors may encounter THE EYE (2026), a latest installation by Jenny Holzer. The American neo-conceptual artist conceived of an evolving commission for Powder Mountain centered on work by the late Utah-born poet May Swenson.

Holzer selected a native quartzite boulder already embedded in the mountain, carving a phrase from Swenson’s poetry directly into the stone rather than relocating it or disturbing the surrounding vegetation: “The eye also a sun wanders and all that it sees it owns.”.

Industry observers note that A close-up of Jenny Holzer’s ‘THE EYE’ (2026) reveals a line of poetry by May Swenson carved directly into a native quartzite boulder at Powder Mountain. Photography by Kim Raff for Powder Art Foundation.

According to recent reports, blending into its natural surroundings, the work could easily go unnoticed by visitors who aren’t looking carefully. As snow, sunlight, and vegetation transform the landscape throughout the year, the inscription offers a point of continuity, even as the experience of encountering it changes.

THE EYE is the first in what Holzer plans to be an ongoing group of carved stones across the mountain. Like this one, future boulders will be selected on the mountain and the art created in place.

Rose Wylie’s ‘Pale-Pink Pineapple/Bomb’ (2025), a 12-foot-tall painted bronze sculpture inspired by a pineapple she sketched in 2013, stands among towering pine trees at Powder Mountain. Photography by Kim Raff for Powder Art Foundation.

It has been revealed that A decidedly different approach comes from Rose Wylie, whose Pale-Pink Pineapple/Bomb (2025) takes the form of a 12-foot-tall, bright pink painted bronze sculpture. The pineapple-like totem marks Wylie’s first permanent public sculpture in the U.S. The 92-year-old British painter describes the work as “poetic transformation,” which began in 2013 when she purchased a Ghanaian-grown pineapple at a local market. Struck by its distinctive surface and dramatic crown of leaves, she sketched the fruit.

A close-up of Rose Wylie’s ‘Pale-Pink Pineapple/Bomb’ (2025) highlights the exaggerated crown of leaves that gives the bright pink sculpture its distinctive silhouette. Photography by Kim Raff for Powder Art Foundation.

According to recent reports, more than a decade later, those drawings have taken on a new dimension. Standing among 50-foot pine trees, the painted bronze sculpture retains the exaggerated silhouette of Wylie’s original sketches. Its form shifts between recognizable fruit, fantastical plant, and something more explosive, suggesting how scale and material can transform an ordinary subject into something entirely unexpected.

In a recent development, unlike Holzer’s work, which is quite literally part of the scenery, Wylie’s is impossible to miss, its almost cartoonish silhouette creating an unexpected interruption in the natural landscape. Shanghai-based artist Xu Zhen takes yet another approach with Hello (2021–2022), which transforms a classical Corinthian column into a twisting, serpentine form.

Xu Zhen’s ‘Hello’ (2021–2022) transforms a classical Corinthian column into a twisting, serpentine sculpture nestled among the aspen trees at Utah’s Powder Mountain. Photography by Kim Raff for Powder Art Foundation.

To develop the sculpture’s serpentine form, Zhen studied the movements of snakes alongside representations of dragons and serpents from different cultures. He ultimately selected the Corinthian column for its ornate capital, whose leaf-like details suggested the head of a living creature. Using a 3D-scanned model as his starting point, he developed the form digitally before producing a full-scale prototype that informed the final bronze casting.

“My work has always been about discovering alternative forms that an object might take,” the multimedia artist explains. “These forms should feel natural, as though the object was always meant to exist that way, while remaining outside our conventional understanding and experience.”.

Industry observers note that A close-up of Xu Zhen’s Hello (2021–2022) reveals the ornate Corinthian capital, whose leaf-like details give the twisting column an almost creature-like appearance. Photography by Kim Raff for Powder Art Foundation.

In a recent development, installed among the mountain’s aspen trees, Hello takes on what Zhen describes as a “distinctly surreal quality.” Yet the setting also feels surprisingly natural. When he first saw the sculpture at Powder Mountain, the artist recalls feeling “that it had returned to the world where it belongs.”.

It has been revealed that for Zhang, the experience of encountering these works is as important as the artworks themselves. “You’re moving, your heart rate is up, there’s adrenaline, and then you come around a corner and encounter a work without expecting it,” he says. “I think being that engaged in your body changes how you experience the art.”.

Visitors to Powder Art Foundation’s fall open house gather around Nobuo Sekine’s Phase of Nothingness – Stone Stack (1970/2025) at Utah’s Powder Mountain. Photography by McCade Gordon for Powder Art Foundation.

Not every work is intended to have the same relationship to the mountain. “Some should feel like you discovered them; others should stop you in your tracks,” Zhang states. “The mountain connects everything.”.

The Powder Mountain art trail opens for the winter season in mid-December, with lift-accessible installations available from 9 a.m. to 4 p.m. daily and night access to the Sundown area from 4 p.m. to 9 p.m. Access to the installations is included with a lift ticket or season pass.
