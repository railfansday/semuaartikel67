Tech Report: Startups Are Picking Names That Can Work Even If The Original Business Model Doesn’t

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that startup naming tends to be a cyclical endeavor. For a few years, founders might be enamoured of silly monikers, made-up words, and creative misspellings. Then the pendulum swings, and startups defer to simple names that describe what they actually do.

In a recent development, so where are we now? Probably somewhere in the middle.

Recent findings suggest that per a Crunchbase analysis of startups 1 founded in the past three years, companies attracting investor interest are largely opting for names that are both distinctive and vague. It’s a preference that reflects this era of fast-evolving AI, in which founders want room to adapt.

Examples of this stylistic direction abound among the most heavily funded newer startups. There’s Also, which is of course a micromobility spin-out. Perplexity is a genAI organization although you couldn’t tell that by its name. And then there’s Sunday, which makes household robots, in case that wasn’t clear from the name.

Recent findings suggest that “Reality is a lot of these companies, in seven or eight years they’ve done at least one or two major pivots,” Athol Foden, who runs the naming consultancy Brighter Naming, told us. That makes it logical to pick a name that can still work afterward.

When startups do make inventive picks, it’s often out of necessity rather than preference.

Sources indicate that “We’re running out of words. Everything is trademarked multiple times over,” mentioned Bruno Benedini, co-founder of branding consultancy Taillight. “So we have to look for something unexpected.

Given that reality, Benedini is always on the lookout for startups that exemplify the element of surprise. One standout is Hugging Face, the AI model sharing and development platform, which Nvidia agreed to acquire for $12.9 billion in September. It didn’t succeed just because of its strange name, obviously, but it didn’t hurt.

Another more recent upstart that caught his attention is Flapping Airplanes, an AI research lab that raised a $1 billion Series A last month.

“It’s super playful, but you remember it, and you have this visual,” he said. Plus, it’s not the kind of name one’s lawyer finds is already taken by a competitor.

Recent findings suggest that the AI boom is also often characterized as a period in which companies are building the foundational layer of a technology that will fundamentally transform our lives for decades to come. Given this mindset, it’s perhaps predictable that we’re seeing a plethora of names with themes tied to building and industry.

In a recent development, foden points to Databricks as one of the more longstanding representatives of this naming approach. Its brand evokes the notion of digital data as one small component of a massive building endeavor.

But newer upstarts are onto this trend as well. There’s an abundance of recently founded companies with “foundation” in their name, for instance, including Foundation Health, Foundation Industries, Foundational Industries, Foundational and Foundation.

Another recent example with an industrial, maker vibe is Blacksmith, a cloud infrastructure startup founded in 2024 that’s pulled in more than $100 million this year.

Dating app Rivet looks like a more unusual sector match for an industrial name. The two-year-old startup runs a platform in which volunteer matchmakers pick matches for daters.

Nuclear startup Standard Nuclear, meanwhile, reads like a twist on Gilded Age industrial giant Standard Oil.

In a recent development, there’s also a continuing effort to add a humanizing element to AI and robotics technologies. After all, no one wants to patronize a manufacturer called “AI-Enabled Robotics Overlord Who Will Soon Be Taking Your Job.”.

It’s not just newer startups applying this tactic. Startups have a long history of going with popular first names as brands. And of course Anthropic has secured massive market share  of late with a chatbot and coder named Claude.

Newer funded startups with first name-based brands include Theo, Henry, Clara and Mira, among others. Then there’s Humans&, an AI startup that’s taking a different route to attempt a humanizing brand.

It has been revealed that of course, nowadays it’s not even necessary to confer with a human when generating naming ideas. You can just ask AI for suggestions.

Per Benedini, the technology’s not there yet.

“We still use AI to see what it comes up with, and it’s like 99% garbage,” he stated. Moreover, selecting a name is only the first step — one must also determine if it’s available for use.

Sources indicate that still, it’s early days. Perhaps in the future it will be AI naming AI companies to appeal to other AI agents. But hopefully there will still be a role for us to play going forward.

According to recent reports, sample set was limited to startups in English-speaking countries. ↩.
