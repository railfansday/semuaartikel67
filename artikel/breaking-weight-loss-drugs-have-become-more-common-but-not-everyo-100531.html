Breaking: Weight-loss drugs have become more common, but not everyone has access

A study of more than half a million privately insured adults found that where patients received care was linked to whether they filled a prescription for weight-loss drugs.

Semaglutide and tirzepatide are sold as Wegovy and Zepbound, and the two weight-loss drugs can seem to be everywhere.

It has been revealed that only 8.5% of eligible adults in the study filled a prescription for either drug within six months. Patients were more likely to fill one if they received care in areas where more residents had a college degree.

This gap grew as the drugs became more common.

Recent findings suggest that private insurance was held by every one of these patients. Coverage alone was therefore not enough to make access equal.

The study was led by Minh Trung Le, a research data analyst at Boston University School of Public Health.

He was based in the school’s Department of Global Health. Le worked with Andrew Stokes, an associate professor of global health at the school.

Insurance billing records were used for the analysis. Such records can show a pattern in who received a prescription. The cause of that pattern cannot be shown by them.

Stokes stated insurers pay for these drugs far more readily when a patient has diabetes.

He called people with overweight or obesity “a population that has been left behind.”.

Sources indicate that the records were drawn from a national database of insurance claims. More than 175 million people are covered by that database.

From it, Le and Stokes selected adults between the ages of 18 and 64. They first met the study’s eligibility criteria between July 2021 and June 2025.

Industry observers note that clinical guidelines decide who qualifies for the drugs. The bar is a body mass index, or BMI, of at least 30. A BMI of at least 27 is also accepted alongside a weight-related condition.

High blood pressure and sleep apnea are two examples.

Recent findings suggest that people with type 2 diabetes were excluded from the study. People on Medicare or Medicaid were left out as well, along with those without insurance. That left 512,389 patients.

The researchers checked whether patients filled a prescription for semaglutide or tirzepatide within six months of first meeting the study’s eligibility criteria.

Ozempic and Wegovy are the brand names of semaglutide. Tirzepatide goes by Mounjaro and Zepbound.

It has been revealed that versions labeled for diabetes are at times prescribed for obesity. These prescriptions were included in the count.

Industry observers note that the clinics were compared through the ZIP code of each patient’s health care provider. Each ZIP code was looked up in the Census Bureau’s American Community Survey.

The survey reports how many residents hold a bachelor’s degree or higher. On that basis, patients were split into four equal groups.

In a recent development, among patients diagnosed in the summer of 2021, 1.9% were prescribed one of the two drugs within six months. For patients diagnosed in the spring of 2025, that share had risen to 16.6%.

Even then, about five of every six eligible patients had no prescription after six months.

Semaglutide was approved for weight management by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration in June 2021. Tirzepatide got the same approval in November 2023.

Both belong to a class called GLP-1 drugs. In clinical trials, drugs in this class have helped people lose weight. They have also protected the heart.

In a recent development, both drugs were in short supply at times during the study. When semaglutide was scarce, tirzepatide prescriptions rose quickly. The reverse was seen when tirzepatide was scarce.

Recent findings suggest that le and Stokes took that as a sign that doctors swap one drug for the other. By the second half of 2024, tirzepatide had become the more prescribed of the two.

Recent findings suggest that at first, prescription rates were about the same in all four neighborhood groups. The rates began to differ in late 2022.

Recent findings suggest that by spring 2025, 21.7% of patients seen in areas with the highest share of college graduates filled a prescription. In the other three groups, the share ranged from 14.4% to 15.2%.

The results were then adjusted for age, sex, race, BMI and other illnesses. The number of doctor visits was taken into account as well.

Between 2021 and 2023, odds of a prescription were 15% lower in the least educated group. The most educated group served as the benchmark. For 2024 and 2025, that figure reached 35%.

Among men, the neighborhood gap was wider than among women. The overweight range indicated the widest gap of any BMI category. Odds in the least educated group were 40% lower there.

For the most severe obesity category, the difference shrank to 10%.

Recent findings suggest that le and Stokes offered a possible reason. More illnesses tend to be on record for patients with severe obesity. An insurer’s approval rules may be easier for them to meet.

According to recent reports, by the spring of 2025, 20.7% of women were prescribed one of the drugs. Among men, the figure was 11.9%. After the same adjustments, men had less than half the odds that women had.

Age was linked to prescriptions too. Adults ages 35 to 54 had about twice the odds of adults ages 18 to 24.

Black patients and Hispanic patients each had 11% lower odds than white patients. About a third of the records had no race or ethnicity listed, though. Those two estimates may be shakier as a result.

It has been revealed that as BMI rose, so did prescriptions. Even so, most patients in the most severe obesity category went without one. Only about one in five of those diagnosed in the spring of 2025 got a prescription.

Earlier research suggests that early treatment can prevent or ease later complications, Le and Stokes wrote.

Among patients who first qualified in summer 2021, 1.9% filled a prescription for either drug within six months.

Recent findings suggest that by spring 2025, that share had risen to 16.6%. Even then, about five in six eligible patients had no recorded fill within six months.

Industry observers note that the forms for that approval are filled in by the staff of the clinic. Fewer of those forms may be handled by a clinic where staff are few.

Recent findings suggest that cost was named second. Different amounts are charged to patients by different employer health prepares for the same drug.

In a 2026 study, 8,914 privately insured adults who had started semaglutide were followed by other researchers.

After accounting for other factors, researchers found that patients who paid more than $168 per 30-day supply were 9 percentage points more likely to fall short of the study’s refill target than those who paid $21 or less.

Recent findings suggest that A review of approval rules and of patient costs was requested by Le. He also asked for help with approval forms for clinics that are short of staff.

Payment programs that reward clinics for quality targets were mentioned in the paper as well. If prescribing rates were made a target, a short-staffed clinic could be penalized for the shortage, not its care.

The ZIP code of the clinic was held by Le and Stokes for each of the 512,389 patients.

It has been revealed that the income and the schooling of each patient were not held by them. The study therefore cannot assign the gap to the clinics or to the patients.

Semaglutide and tirzepatide are sold to some people by direct-to-consumer sellers and no insurer is involved.

Sales of that kind are not recorded in claims and were not counted by the researchers. Which groups of patients buy the drugs in this way is not known.

A diagnosis had already been given by a doctor to every patient in the study.

Diagnosis codes are applied unevenly and milder obesity is often left unrecorded, Le and Stokes wrote. Some adults who were eligible may have been missed for that reason.

Industry observers note that more than 60 data sources are combined in the database that was used. It was still not described by the authors as a match for all insured Americans.

Industry observers note that the cause of the neighborhood gap has not been found. Insurance rules, prescribing habits and patients’ awareness of the drugs were listed by the authors for future studies.

Records of direct-to-consumer sales were also suggested by them, to show who is missing from the claims.

The full study was published in the journal Health Affairs Scholar.
