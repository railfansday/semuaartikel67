Today’s leaders. Tomorrow’s bad role models?

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a recent development, there is a rising generation of students in high school and college who are forming beliefs about how to lead. The most prominent examples of leaders in politics, business, and education make me concerned about the future. Role models are an important part of the way we learn how a complex task should be handled. We may adopt the behaviors of role models or react against them, but we internalize a lot of the prominent people around us. I’ll highlight a few of the problems I see and explore some potential solutions. The negative role models Politics has been the source of negative role models for future leaders for a long time. For decades, politicians being interviewed on television repeated talking points that were only vaguely related to the questions they were asked. The support of leaders for complications often depended more on party affiliation than on whether the position would lead to a meaningful benefit. And over the past 10 years, there has been a rise in true mean-spirited rhetoric that has devolved into name calling and insults rather than reasoned discussions of the issues of the day. The last decade has had few inspiring business leaders either. Prominent news stories focus on billionaires who are chasing additional profits while currying favor with leaders who have political power. The heads of AI companies forge ahead with the development of models that require resource-intensive data centers without letting concerns about the economic, social, and environmental costs of the work influence the pace of development of fresh models. University leaders have generally been no better. Many universities around the country have become highly politicized. Some university leaders are so paralyzed by the politics around them that they refuse to make any changes at all. Others push forward changes without bringing the community along with them to achieve their ends and alienate their students, staff, and faculty in the process. None of these are examples of healthy leadership. What is healthy leadership? Ultimately, good leadership involves three key components. Leaders must establish a vision for the future that brings people along with them and inspires them to engage to create that future. Leaders must grapple with the complexity of the world and work to develop solutions to significant problems that chart a course through competing priorities. Finally, leaders must meet the moment in a principled way in which they communicate clearly about the factors that drive the path forward. In politics, one of the differences between campaigning and governing is that campaigns can be one based on emphasizing the negatives of an opponent, while governing requires supporting a course of action that addresses key problems. In an era of perpetual campaigning, it can be difficult to find people who actually embrace a position and see it through. In all aspects of life, there are rarely courses of action that are good for everyone all the time. Instead, everything has tradeoffs. Great leaders recognize those tradeoffs, find a reasonable compromise among them, and then help the people they lead to recognize the value of that particular path. They are also committed to continuous improvement of outcomes even after they implement a change. The path forward To ensure that we have great leaders in the future, we have to acknowledge the absence of great role models at present. Then, there are two significant things we must do. First, we must hold our leaders accountable to be better role models. That should start with our university leaders who are all educators at heart. They must take a step back from the local politics of their position and remember that their actions are being watched and may ultimately be emulated by others. We should also remind our business and political leaders that winning is important, but how you win also matters. Encourage them to do things that send the right message to the people learning from their example. And those of us who teach and write about leadership must also provide a counterweight to the existing role models. If there are key skills that leaders should have that are not easily found among role models, then we must use them as examples of what not to do. We must point out ways that the short-term benefits of poor leadership have long-term negative consequences. As complex as the current moment may seem, the world of the future will be more complicated as the influences of climate change, globalization, artificial intelligence (and a host of other issues) affect the world. We will need leaders who unite us around a vision and take a far different approach than the leaders who dominate the news cycle right now.
