📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, it's a joyous day for lawyers representing plucky little game and console makers Sony. A UK class action lawsuit which accused the tech giant of using the PlayStation Store's dominant market position to fuel "excessive and unfair" prices for digital games has been dismissed in its entirety by the Competition Appeal Tribunal in London.

As reported by Reuters and The Game Business, the body ruled that the lawsuit - brought by Alex Neill on behalf of folks who bought digital games on PlayStation between August 2016 and February 2026 - failed to establish that Sony had infringed competition law.

Industry observers note that the lawsuit claimed that PlayStation's dominant position when it comes to selling digital games on its platforms via the PlayStation Store subjected consumers to "excessive and unfair prices", as a result of terms and a 30% commission fee levied against developers. Neill and co argued owning a PlayStation traps game buyers within a competitionless ecosystem, thereby impacting their ability to seek out a better deal.

It's quite similar to the arguments which have been made against Valve in similar lawsuits concerning the Steam overlords' dominance over the PC game buying space, though a key distinction is that there is some competition on PC via the likes of the Epic store and GOG. How effective some of that competition can be in practice is a different kettle of fish, but the options are there.

It has been revealed that in a judgement that could potentially factor into the responses to any similar lawsuits against Valve or other PC storefronts going forwards, the tribunal ruled that Sony compete in a wider ‘systems market’ against Xbox, Nintendo and PC, with PlayStation having no legal duty under competition law to allow third-party stores onto their platform as competition closer to home. The latter the tribunal noted would be expensive, complex and come with potential security ramifications.

As if that wasn't enough, the body ruled that as far as the pricing being unfair went, Neill hadn't proven that Sony’s profit margins were excessive and declared there was no evidence the presence of rival storefronts on PlayStation would've seen prices come down. That's essentially the word NO written in the largest font possible on a sheet of paper the size of a table, then.

In a recent development, every lawsuit's different, especially when filed in different countries, but if I was one of the other class action suits against Sony or another big game seller on the go at the moment, I'd maybe not be feeling so hot right now. On the other hand, perhaps seeing what the tribunal judged Neill not to have provided suffiencient evidence for might aid those other efforts in formulating their arguments ready to face the scrutiny of government bodies and corporate law-talkers.
