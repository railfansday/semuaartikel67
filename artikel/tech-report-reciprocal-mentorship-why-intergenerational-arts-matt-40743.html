When I was in high school, I spent my afternoons in a retirement home across the street from campus visiting with a resident. What started as a weekly visit became two to three visits a week. We would watch old black-and-white films she loved when she was young, make art together, and she’d tell me about her life. High school is tumultuous and isolating. Being 80 in a retirement home, I learned, can be, too. Through spending time together, we each stepped outside the isolation of our separate worlds; as a result, we both felt less lonely. I didn’t understand the mechanics then, but what we both experienced—the quiet power of being needed, of having someone show up week after week, of creating something together—is exactly what community-based knowledge and a growing body of research now show: intergenerational connection works.This scene repeats itself across the United States, though mostly unseen. In Montgomery County, Maryland, high school students and adult artists co-design creative workshops in affordable housing communities through Arts for the Aging’s Empower Project. In the American South, Alternate Roots—a 50-year coalition of artists and community organizers—brings together emerging and veteran creatives in social justice work (Merin, 1977). In Los Angeles, parents and children sit together making altars for Día de los Muertos, co-creating around cultural traditions through programs like Multigenerational Afterschool Arts (Kane & Kunisaki, 2025). These programs reflect the understanding that different ages need each other.Our contemporary world segregates by age in ways that would astound people living just 150 years ago. Schools for the young. Workplaces for the “productive years.” Retirement facilities for the old. This age segregation is remarkably recent; a product of early-20th-century industrialization and its drive for efficiency (Freedman & Stamp, 2021). Graded education, child labor laws, Social Security, and mandatory retirement all contributed to the reshuffling. Sun City, which opened in Phoenix in 1960, pioneered the modern age-segregated retirement community. In contrast, 19th-century America was thoroughly age-integrated: multigenerational households, mixed workplaces, even almshouses that housed the old, young, orphaned, and poor together.

The cost of this segregation runs in both directions and is measurable. Young people may lose consistent relationships with adults who’ve navigated complexity and failure, and a sense of historical continuity that can anchor identity (Freedman & Stamp, 2021). Older adults lose something equally vital: the felt sense of being needed. Research shows that people over 60 who live in age-segregated communities experience higher levels of loneliness (Kim and Choi, 2025). A 2018 survey found that the two loneliest groups in America are younger people and older people—the very populations most separated by design (Freedman & Stamp, 2021). Social isolation and loneliness are linked to higher risks for depression, cognitive decline, heart disease, and early mortality (Hodgson et al., 2020; National Institute on Aging, 2019). Purpose, or the concrete experience of being relied upon, is an exceptionally strong predictor of mental health and longevity (Boyle et al., 2009; O’Súilleabháin et al., 2026). When that sense of purpose is ripped away, the same neurobiological threat response triggered by chronic loneliness is activated (Eisenberger and Cole, 2012). For communities of color, age segregation compounds existing healthcare barriers. Traditional mental health services are often inaccessible due to systemic racism, historical harm, and documented mistrust in the medical system (Alang, 2019). Community-based arts spaces, rooted in cultural knowledge and embedded in neighborhoods, reach people that formal systems do not. Research shows that culturally tailored, community-led arts programs improve mental health outcomes in underserved communities and create what researchers call “essential social infrastructure” for mental wellness (Aguirre et al., 2025; Hand & Golden, 2019; GBHI, 2024).The programs that work best operate on a principle increasingly recognized in intergenerational research: mutual benefit rather than hierarchical mentorship. Traditional models, such as younger people “helping” elders or elders “sharing wisdom,” recreate the hierarchies we’re trying to dismantle. Instead, effective programs support what researchers call “equal group status,” where both ages are genuinely needed to complete the work and knowledge flows bidirectionally (Cheng et al., 2026; Jarrott, 2023).Arts for the Aging’s Empower Project pairs high school artist assistant fellows with adult artists in residence. Both are paid and are equal decision-makers. Young people and professionals learn from each other, and both contribute to designing and leading workshops for their community (Arts for the Aging, 2026). Alternate Roots works similarly. A younger artist reflected: “Older, more experienced people shared resources that helped my personal development. Later, I generated opportunities for others.” (Clark et al., n.d.). This is reciprocal mentorship. The evidence for what this creates is substantial. The Wallace Foundation’s study of 35 community-based arts organizations across eight U.S. cities, which followed youth of color over 18 months, documented measurable outcomes: increased sense of belonging, skill mastery, sense of purpose, leadership development, and community connection (Osai et al., 2025). Such intergenerational programming can have positive rippling effects on mental health and well-being for both generations, and across the lifespan (Arring et al., 2026; Jenkins et al., 2021; Park, 2015).

Furthermore, studies show that arts-based programs reduce mental health stigma (Beato et al., 2024; Gaiha et al., 2021). More recent research examining intersectionality-informed art interventions with Black, Indigenous, and People of Color girls ages 14-16 found that art-based work supported narrative agency, processing of intergenerational trauma, and community connection (Birnkammer et al., 2026). Why does this work? In part, it is due to biological factors. Chronic loneliness activates threat-response systems throughout the nervous system (Eisenberger and Cole, 2012). Being part of a genuine collaboration and feeling needed activates the reward system. Creative work engages the brain differently compared with talk therapy; it is embodied, non-verbal, and grounded in making something material. There’s also something that neuroscience alone may not be able to measure: these programs offer the felt experience of belonging across differences, of mattering to someone. They offer reciprocal vulnerability.These programs also provide something foundational: spaces where genuine connection across ages is built, and where respect is reciprocal. Where in your community could genuinely reciprocal creative work happen, and what would it take to build such a space?
