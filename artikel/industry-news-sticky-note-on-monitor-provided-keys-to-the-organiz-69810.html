📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Don't leave your credentials out in public, where any visitor can see them.

In a recent development, welcome, once again, to PWNED, where we talk smack about organizations that have smacked themselves down by practicing poor security hygiene. This week's story involves everyone's favorite low-tech password manager: the sticky note.

Recent findings suggest that our terrifying tale comes courtesy of Christian Espinosa, a certified penetration tester with over 30 years of experience and the CEO of Blue Goat Cyber, a security firm. Espinosa shared an experience where critical credentials were right out in the open for any person in the office to see.

According to recent reports, during a walkthrough of a client's office, he noticed a password written on a sticky note that was taped to an employee's monitor. While writing down your password and placing it in a physically secure paper notebook might be ok, taping it to the computer is definitely not.

"Before I get my fingers on any system, I first walk the physical area that the system is located in — attackers do that when they're looking at a target too," Espinosa explained. “Physical security vulnerabilities are neglected much more often than technical security vulnerabilities and firms invest in firewalls where a sticky note is placed 3 feet from the front door.".

Recent findings suggest that espinosa took a photo of the sticky note and immediately tried logging into that employee's account. Success. Then, he tried the very same password on the VPN, which apparently had been maintained by that employee.

It has been revealed that once he connected to the VPN, Espinosa had full access to the company network and all of its shared drives. He opened a shared drive that this employee had access to and there was an HR folder with all kinds of personally identifiable information (PII) about employees, including home addresses, driver's licenses, and scanned W-4 forms. There were even spreadsheets that listed everyone's salaries.

Recent findings suggest that the accounting server was a particular treasure trove of client information. On that server, Espinosa found a folder with vendor banking information, invoices, and payment records.

"At that point, a password I had read from a sticky note during a walk-through had taken me from one desk to information that could have been used for payroll fraud, identity theft, or vendor-payment fraud," Espinosa stated.

Recent findings suggest that after getting access to all this information, the pen tester presented his findings to the client. They promptly disabled the rogue account and started reviewing VPN access logs to see who else might have gotten into the network. They then searched the office to see if anyone else was exposing a password on a sticky note. Finally, they tightened permissions on the HR and accounting shares so that only people who really needed access to that data could get in.

The lessons here are pretty obvious. Don't expose your passwords and, perhaps even more importantly, limit who has access to file shares that contain mission-critical data such as employee or client information.

Sources indicate that "The part that really got the client's attention was how easily the attack could have continued after I left the building," Espinosa posited. "Someone could have photographed that sticky note, walked out, connected to the VPN later that night, and started going through employee and financial records from somewhere else." ®.
