As I write this, I cannot stop glancing at my Japanese maple, its entire canopy ablaze with crimson and orange foliage. It's my little jewel of the garden, a sentiment shared by many of my friends with these small trees in their landscapes.

In a recent development, japanese maples (Acer palmatum) are counted among the most beautiful ornamental trees around, with their elegant branching structure, graceful canopy, and spectacular fall foliage. Since these trees are usually hardy down to USDA zone 4 or 5, they may shrug off winter cold. But that doesn’t make them immune to winter damage from the cold weather, harsh winds, and fluctuating soil moisture. Young trees and container trees are especially vulnerable.

So you should have more than a few items on your list for Japanese maple tree care before winter. If not, you’ve come to the right place. This guide explains the six simple but essential tasks to ensure your Japanese maple makes it through winter with flying colors. You can do them at the same time as your other garden winterizing tasks.

It has been revealed that don’t worry too much about watering your Japanese maple during winter. When the leaves fall and the tree enters dormancy, it needs very little water. Water trees only if there has been no precipitation for several weeks and the ground is bone dry.

Sources indicate that however, to help them through the winter, give them one final irrigation before the ground freezes. If the summer has been hot and/or dry, make this a deep watering. A soaker hose is ideal for delivering a slow, even soaking across the tree's root zone without wasting water. The Gilmour Round Soaker Hose from Amazon is a good option, as it distributes water evenly along its length.

According to recent reports, tuck a cozy blanket of mulch around your precious tree in November. Use something organic – wood chips or bark work well. Miracle-Gro Organic All Natural Mulch from Home Depot is a good option, made from natural, shredded wood. Layer up to 4 inches (10 cm) of mulch over the root zone. This protects the roots, holds heat in the soil, and prevents frost heaving.

Don’t make the mistake of piling the mulch up against the trunk in a “volcano” arrangement. The mulch should never touch the trunk since that can cause wood rot.

Sources indicate that is your Japanese maple a young tree you planted within the last three years? You’ll want to give the juvenile tree extra protection. This will also be true if you live in a region with an exceptionally cold winter.

According to recent reports, in these circumstances, help your tree survive with insulation. Wrap vulnerable trunks or branches with burlap, such as this Riare Natural Burlap Plant Protection Wrap from Amazon, to shield them against harsh, drying winter winds. Leave the burlap loose enough to allow the tree to breathe.

If your tree has broken or diseased branches, the best time to take those off is now, whenever you notice. And you should check regularly to spot problems early. But aside from this type of damage, do not give your Japanese maple a haircut in November or at any other point during dormancy. Pruning at this time can leave cuts vulnerable to disease and frost damage.

In a recent development, would you have an energy drink just before bedtime? Of course not. And for all the same reasons, do not fertilize your Japanese maple in November. Fertilizer, especially nitrogen fertilizer, induces newly introduced growth just as the tree should be entering winter dormancy. This new growth is tender and will easily freeze in the first flush of winter weather, leading to dieback or split bark.

In spring, if your tree needs feeding, use a gentle, slow-release fertilizer, such as Espoma Organic Tree-tone (6-3-2) from Amazon. Apply sparingly, following the package instructions, as Japanese maples generally don't need much fertilizer.

It won’t surprise you to hear that container maple trees require more protection than a Japanese maple with its roots deep in the garden soil. Their roots are much closer to the cold air on all sides, and therefore can freeze or dry out faster.

In a recent development, you’ll want to take steps to protect a potted Japanese maple. A good first step is to insulate the containers with bubble wrap or burlap. Wrap the pots well and cluster the containers together near the house to attract radiant heat. It also helps to use pot feet, like Bosmere's Pot Toes, or even wood blocks to raise the containers off the cold ground and support good drainage.

In the most severe winters, you can move your container trees into a shelter, like an unheated garage or a sheltered porch.

Sources indicate that does your tree still have leaves as Thanksgiving arrives? This is normal, not a cause for alarm.

Trees have an abscission layer at the base of their leaf stems that releases the leaves to fall in the cold. But Japanese maples build this layer more slowly than other deciduous trees like red or sugar maples. And if you’ve had a mild autumn, this can delay leaf drop even further, even into December.

According to recent reports, this means additional watering work for you. While your maple still has leaves, you’ll need to keep watering whenever the soil is dry. At the same time, rake up leaves that do fall and remove them. You don’t want soggy or decaying piles of maple leaves since this can encourage the spread of fungal spores.
