Don Bluth on the future of animation and AI: Thats definitely a road I dont want to travel on

Sources indicate that for children of the '80s and '90s, Don Bluth was a major influence on how we saw the world. With movies like The Secret of NIMH, An American Tail, The Land Before Time, All Dogs Go to Heaven, and Anastasia, this American animator offered us realms of adventure, with plucky heroes as well as villains so fantastically frightening that we carry the thrill of it all with us decades later.

In a recent development, social media is evergreen with fans remembering the tender moments of Littlefoot and his tree star, or the terrifying ones, like Fievel or Mrs. Brisby being chased down by monstrous, roaring, ravenous cats. Now, for fans new and old, filmmakers Dave LaMattina and Chad N. Walker offer Don Bluth: Somewhere Out There, a documentary that explores the life and work of the innovative animator through his own words.

Recent findings suggest that in an interview with Mashable, Bluth said watching the finished doc "brought tears to [his] eyes," because it refreshed memories of his colleagues. Together, they pursued animation not for the money but for the art and a deep desire to create something meaningful for future generations. Yet, when asked about his legacy, Bluth is quick to look back on the path that led him to his work.

He began by speaking of Winsor McCay, the father of American animation. In a wistful storyteller tone, Bluth recounted how a little boy witnessed the "magic" of the 1914 short film Gertie the Dinosaur, in which a pencil-sketched brontosaurus comes to life. This little boy was so inspired that he'd grow up to make his own animated short featuring a mouse named Mickey.

According to recent reports, that little boy, of course, was Walt Disney, who would go on to make 1937's Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs, the first animated feature film produced in the United States. That movie would go on to inspire a young Bluth, who'd direct his first animated feature in 1982 with The Secret of NIMH.

This path of inspiration to iconic art wasn't as direct a line as you might think.

In a recent development, yes, Bluth scored a dream job, working at Disney Animation studio for 10 years on films like Sleeping Beauty, Robin Hood, and The Rescuers. But after Disney's death in 1966, Bluth and 17 other Disney animators feared the studio had lost their way, creatively.

He told Mashable he'd invite these artists to his home on weekends, where they'd draw together to develop innovative pitches for their corporate bosses. When they were shot down, Bluth — along with Gary Goldman and John Pomeroy — built their own company, Don Bluth Productions, to "see if we could do better than they were doing." For this, Bluth remembered, "We were called traitors.".

Recent findings suggest that that's not how Bluth saw his team. "[These] wonderful people had the courage to leave their jobs and security and go out there and try and do something great," he stated, "not just to feed their egos, but something they felt helped them grow up. And they wanted to give back.".

Recent findings suggest that it was not an easy journey, as Don Bluth: Somewhere Out There details, revealing the highs and lows of Bluth's most beloved and worst-reviewed productions, as well as the wildly popular video game Dragon's Lair. In our interview, he spoke repeatedly about how the doc filled him with gratitude as he looked back for the filmmakers' interviews.

Sources indicate that to Mashable, he reflected, "What happened after so many movies, All Dogs Go to Heaven, American Tail, working with Steven Spielberg on both of those pictures, and George Lucas [on The Land Before Time], [it] became really, really obvious to us that we were doing the right thing [by] writing the right stories, and trying to make something that would move people's lives for the better — not just to pat our own backs and say, 'Look what we did.' [We were making] something that would help maybe children grow up and have some degree of morality about them. So then, that was our goal in everything.".

In Somewhere Out There, the rise of computer animation — which Bluth refers to as "3D animation" — came as a devastating blow to the animator, who'd spent his life developing the skills of hand-drawn 2D animation. To this day, Bluth still advocates for the artistry of hand-drawn animation.

It has been revealed that "The way I look at this is animation in the 2D form is people, armed with a pencil in their hands, have to be able to draw," he explained, "and they also have to be able to act. And they have to have feelings about story.".

According to recent reports, to Bluth, CGI movies aren't animation. "3D animation is puppetry," he said, adding, "[With 3D animation,] you drop your pencil. You put your hands on a keyboard, and you make a wireframe puppet act.".

In a recent development, bluth shared several of his complaints about the look of 3D animation, saying, "To me, it looks very plastic... The colors are muted, and they're trying to put shading in there, trying to look more like a live-action picture." He asked, "Why are we trying to get to a place which looks more real?" adding, "That isn't the purpose at all of 2D [animation]. The purpose is to take you to a magical world. 2D is trying to make audiences come to our own world.".

According to recent reports, noting the animation industry changed forever with the massive success of 1995's Toy Story, Bluth said, "Toy Story [movies] were quite fun to watch, but they didn't make me feel anything very deep. And animation is intended to do that, particularly with Walt Disney pictures. Snow White, Bambi, [and] Pinocchio — those pictures did something to change the way you thought and help you grow up in a way that was really good. And they did it in such a beautiful way.".

Recent findings suggest that it was when Disney Studios, post-Walt, moved away from these tenets that Bluth and his colleagues set out on their own. He noted, "We left the Disney Studio because it was leaving out the morality statements and leaving out all the beautiful colors and everything.".

In terms of what recent animated films impressed Bluth, he said, "I liked Klaus a lot," commending Sergio Pablos' inventive Christmas tale, which employs hand-drawn animation with a unique visual style.

Sources indicate that bluth also enjoyed Pete Docter's 2009 Pixar hit Up. "That was totally in 3D," he noted, "but it had a great story. For my taste, it went on too long. You know, the story was really about this couple promising each other they would go back to some place in their life, and she died. So, the man, true to the promise, tries to fulfill that [by taking their house on the journey]. So, I love that.".

Overall though, Bluth lamented, "Animation has become an advertisement for sugar cereal and whatever. There are more advertisements on animation than there are actually animated characters. The animated characters are just simply little puppets that jump up and down and tell one-line jokes. So, that's not good. Now, the last laugh is about to hit us like a tsunami; that is AI.".

Through the stories of Russian Jewish immigrant Fievel Mousekewitz, the widowed mother of four Mrs. Brisby, the orphaned brontosaurus Littlefoot, and rogue mutt Charlie B. Barkin, Bluth was striving to create stories that would inspire children to be good and creative, as Disney's movies had done for him. He doesn't see a future where AI can provide such mindful, moral guidance.

According to recent reports, "It seems like AI is able to do wonderful, wonderful things," Bluth mused, "except AI is a machine, and it has no soul. It will borrow or steal, if you will, from everything that it knows, and give you what you ask for. But where's the creativity?".

Recent findings suggest that "AI is a borrower," Bluth explained. "When you enter what you'd like to see, it searches all over and borrows it from other artists, puts it together. It shows you on screen. Now, you can then make corrections. It can do all that, but it is still a machine. It doesn't ask you, 'Well, what is right and wrong? What is evil and good? What do you want me to say?' The machine can't do that.".

In a recent development, he continued, "The only thing you can do is go back to human beings and say, 'Don't replace human beings with machines.' They're the ones who have a heart, who have a soul, and probably have a Creator. So, there's a bigger picture that's going on than just animation on a screen.".

"AI has no soul," Bluth concluded. "It hasn't a clue how to write anything. Everything that you're going to see on your screen has been stolen from other artists.".

He allowed that some AI might be "stunningly beautiful," but "you'll find that it doesn't affect your heart the way you feel… They're not artists that are doing the AI. Many of them don't understand the art of acting or anything. So, what everything looks like in AI animation that I've seen, it all looks like slow motion, and it looks unreal. So, that's definitely a road I don't want to travel on.".

"I read somewhere where someone stated something that stuck in my head," he mused, paraphrasing Oscar Wilde's The Portrait of Dorian Gray. "There are many people who know the price of everything but the value of nothing. And I certainly hope that's not where we're going.".
