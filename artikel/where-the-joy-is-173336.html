📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that one of the most crucial things I have learned about life is that joy is a choice, an orientation, a practice of bending the mind in the direction of gladness — a choice increasingly countercultural in our age of competitive complaint, and yet all the more urgent precisely because our world gives us so many valid reasons for despair. “Experience of conflict and a load of suffering has taught me that what matters above all is to celebrate joy… Life is wasted when we make it more terrifying, precisely because it is so easy to do so,” the artist René Magritte wrote in his timeless manifesto for the courage of joy in the wake of the second World War of his life. “We’ve all had too much sorrow — now is the time for joy,” Nick Cave sang an epoch later as he contemplated the paradox of joy against the backdrop of unimaginable loss.

Joy is not difficult to find, but it can be difficult to trust — partly because the merchants of pseudo-morality have sold us the dangerous myth that, in an unjust world, despair is an emblem of our solidarity with justice, and partly because in this age of grandiosity, we have forgotten that joy is a plurality of smallnesses: a function of what Hermann Hesse called “the little joys” that light up a life with presence.

We need them, these reminders that joy is not a denial of suffering but the fulcrum of our resilience in its midst; that it comes to us not with horns and cymbals but as a distant whisper from the birthplace of the ordinary, not like a bouquet of roses offered with champagne but like the lovely little weed blooming through a crack in the sidewalk of the quotidian. And that is precisely what artist-philosopher Maira Kalman offers in her book Joy (public library) — a love letter to the sweet strangeness of life, to its unboastful wonderland of small miracles, to the way it expands and expands and expands the moment we contract the aperture of attention onto a single unit of gladness.

that suffering and sorrow envelop our planet and our little lives.

And then it goes where we, short-termists of the present, rarely go: into the lives of some of her heroes who lived through times and turmoils far more unimaginable than ours: She paints Alfred Dreyfus sitting in his prison cell on Devil’s Island; she paints the bedroom in which Beethoven, fully deaf, wrote “Ode to Joy”; she paints Virginia Woolf and her “surprisingly colorful” sitting room just before she filled her coat pockets with stones and waded into the River Ouse; she paints Kafka under the black hat he wore as a helmet of despair, his hand rested tenderly on his beloved dog.

As if to remind us that we are always in relationship with those whose lives have touched ours, whether living or dead, whether or not we have ever met them, she paints her imaginary conversations with Gertrude Stein, with James Baldwin, with Vladimir Nabokov.

Dog punctuate this love letter to joy, cradled in arms and curled in corners, peeking from the pages with their searching eyes and their uncomplicated sweetness: “Isn’t that the answer to everything,” she writes with an eye to a friend who tends to her four dogs “with unlimited care and love.”.

And people, of course, so many people with their strange and soulful faces, each a universe of feeling conjured up with just a few brush strokes.

What might happen if you could leave gloom (pervasive) and perils (lurking) for just a little bit?

What solace might you find if you turned your gaze to a thing of beauty.

She reverences trees, “kind and strong and loving,” and a medieval painting of “a saint with a terrific hairdo” at the Met, and a beautiful man with a porkpie hat at the bookstore, and the fig tree in a friend’s backyard, and people singing, and a young man playing Bach on his violin under the cherry tree, and Pablo Casals playing Bach on his cello while smoking a pipe.

And isn’t it true we are lost without music?

Do children not give us our reason?

Page by page of these small reveries — birdsong, a summer breeze, books and libraries, a woman “lost in thought basking in the morning sun with her little dog by her side,” the bowl of fresh berries on a dying friend’s table, the way a garden, any garden, “can fill you with elation,” the way sitting by the ocean, any ocean, is “enough to make your spirits soar,” the way holding a child’s gaze can make you “forget all the sorrows of the world” — you discover that any moment of joy contains “the essence of what it means to be alive.”.

And don’t I know, and don’t you know, how one sublime instant, one sublime, fleeting instant, changes everything and makes the world right?

In a recent development, it isn’t nothing, that instant — this atom of time, this unit of eternity, the only thing we ever have that is ours for the keeping. “Time is a reality confined to the instant and suspended between two voids,” philosopher Gaston Bachelard wrote. Because attention is what fills the instant with reality, how we spend our attention is how we spend our lives. Emerging from these painted pages is a beckoning to train our attention on joy in all its variousness, its unassuming abundance, its sacred smallness.
