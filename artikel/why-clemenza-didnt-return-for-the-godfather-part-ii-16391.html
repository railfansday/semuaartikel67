Why Clemenza Didn't Return For The Godfather Part II

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a recent development, the Godfather Part II continued the story of Michael Corleone (Al Pacino) after becoming the fresh Don of the Corleone family, and while most of the cast from the first movie returned, the character Peter Clemenza was notably absent. Played by Richard S. Castellano, Clemenza was one of Vito’s caporegimes and oldest and most trusted friends, who was instrumental in Michael cementing his power, and his absence was conspicuous.

In a recent development, in 1972, Francis Ford Coppola brought what’s now considered one of the greatest movies of all time and the best in the gangster genre: The Godfather. Based on Mario Puzo’s 1969 novel of the same name, and with a screenplay co-written by Puzo and Coppola, The Godfather was a massive critical and commercial success and made way for one of the greatest movie trilogies of all time.

The Godfather introduced viewers to the Corleone family in New York City, led by Vito Corleone (Marlon Brando). When Vito decided to hand over his empire to his youngest son, Michael, conflict with the rest of the city’s crime families was triggered, putting the lives of Vito’s loved ones in great danger, while Michael was pushed to take the lead despite staying out of the family business his whole life. The Godfather was followed by two sequels, and though most of the cast returned in The Godfather Part II, Castellano’s Clemenza didn’t come back.

Throughout The Godfather, Clemenza was tasked with getting rid of some of the Corleone family’s enemies, most notably Don Victor Stracci during the infamous “baptism killings” and he planted the gun for Michael to use to kill Sollozzo and McCluskey.

In a recent development, the Godfather Part II arrived just two years after The Godfather, and is obviously both a sequel and a prequel, picking up the story of Michael now as the fresh Don of the family while also going back in time to explore the origin story of Vito. The “present” timeline notably didn’t include Clemenza, which was strange given his strong connection to the Corleone family and his place as one of their most trusted capos.

According to recent reports, coppola said in a DVD documentary (via Mental Floss) that Castellano had declined to return as he wanted more money and dialogue approval. The latter demand came after he ad-libbed the now-famous “take the cannoli” line in the first movie at the urging of his wife, Ardell Sheridan. However, Castellano denied this in an interview in 1981 (via New York Post) and explained he didn’t return in The Godfather Part II as he disagreed with how Clemenza had been written.

Recent findings suggest that according to Castellano, the original script had Clemenza testifying against the Corleone family, which he - quite rightly - claimed was completely off-character. Castellano added that the demands on him were “impossible” as he had settled on a price, and he was required to gain weight after losing 194 pounds to play young Clemenza. He also claimed Coppola promised to tell the press he had turned down the role for artistic reasons, but Coppola ended up saying something entirely different.

Sources indicate that despite Castellano not returning to play Clemenza in the present timeline in The Godfather Part II, Coppola found a way to give him closure and fill in that space. Bruno Kirby was cast as young Clemenza for the flashback scenes to Vito’s backstory, and in the present timeline, Clemenza was killed off-screen, with the betrayal of the Corleone family given to someone else. Fredo mentioned that Clemenza died of a heart attack, but Cicci implied he might have been murdered.

In a recent development, ultimately, Clemenza was replaced by Frank Pentangeli (Michael V. Gazzo), who was given the storyline of testifying against the Corleone family. It was a smart move: seeing Clemenza betray the Corleone family would have felt like too much of a swerve, even if it also felt strange not seeing him in The Godfather Part II. Pentangeli was, instead, retroactively established as another long-term Corleone capo who had worked with Vito in his early career in the olive oil business.

In a recent development, having been a key figure under Clemenza's leadership running the Corleone's family operations in New York, Pentangeli inherits the operation, kicking off an almost-immediate set of conflicts as he seeks to consolidate his position, but Michael refuses to allow him to take out his rivals, the Rosato brothers. Because of Michael's keenness to establish strong relations with Hyman Roth (Lee Strasberg), who is aligned with the Rosatos, he refuses, and Pentangeli is incensed by the insult.

Pentangeli promptly becomes the heart of the story as the walls close in on Michael and the Senate hearing investigating organized crime and allegations of his crimes threaten to crumble his empire. Gazzo's performance is great, and earned him a Best Supporting Actor nomination at the Oscars - which he ultimately lost to Robert De Niro for the same movie, understandably. Any other year, he would have had a strong chance of winning.

Even with the Rosato brothers and Roth pulling the strings, it would never have worked for Clemenza to be the one to betray the Corleones: it had to be someone like Pentangeli, who inherited his power, and like Michael, immediately faced a challenge. Those underworld politics were a great foundation for the entire subplot, which ultimately saw Michael using Frank's brother Vincenzo to intimidate him into recanting his Senate statement in fear. It's at this point that Gazzo really earns his plaudits, and his arc subtly reflecting Michael's own precarious position in the first movie only works because of how well he sells it all.

Sources indicate that ultimately, Robert Duvall's Tom Hagen using Frank's knowledge of Roman history and how traitors' families would be spared if they killed themselves is a brilliant, chilling end to his story, and reveals both the dark morality of this world and the complex parallel with Ancient Rome. There's also an incredibly compelling mystery left unanswered, which was only explained in the original script: Frank's children were under Vincenzo's protection in Sicily, and his brother turning up was supposed to be a reminder not to betray his blood oath and the Mafia's code of silence, or retribution would be taken on his kids.

Sources indicate that it's an unfortunate cut, but the fact that Gazzo's performance so thoroughly convinces of the threat without it ever having to be spelled out is the final testimony to how great he was as Clemenza's replacement. Ultimately, Castellano's unwillingness to sign on for the sequel may have led to the loss of a great character, but it worked out better for The Godfather Part II.

The Godfather Part II is a continuation of the Corleone crime family saga. The film explores Vito Corleone's early life in Sicily and 1910s Latest York, while Michael Corleone navigates the 1950s, seeking to expand the family's influence into Las Vegas, Hollywood, and Cuba.
