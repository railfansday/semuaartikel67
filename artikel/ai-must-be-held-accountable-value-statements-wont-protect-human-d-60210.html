AI must be held accountable — value statements won’t protect human dignity

🌐 Source: Nature • 📅 20 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 6 min read (1150 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

  • AI governance requires more than just a declaration.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a recent development, there is a striking gap in debates about artificial intelligence. Policy bodies and frontier AI laboratories are borrowing the vocabulary of legal constitutions to guide model development and oversight. However, these statements currently don’t include the institutional safeguards that would make such value commitments work.

In September, the UK Parliament’s Joint Committee on Human Rights published its report on AI regulation. It uses ‘human rights’ more than 100 times in as many pages, but doesn’t discuss which rights should be protected or how. AI firm Anthropic in San Francisco, California, has written a ‘constitution’ for its model Claude. In it, numerous values are listed as the tool’s priorities, including the preservation of user autonomy. Yet some of these values, such as autonomy and well-being, compete with each other, as Anthropic acknowledges. The document does not provide an independent arbiter to adjudicate the conflict, or monitor violations by the AI system.

AI governance requires more than just a declaration. It requires a system of accountability. Specific institutions should constrain the tools’ influence in accordance with specific fundamental values and hold firms accountable to individuals affected by AI transgressions. Lawyers call this constitutionalism.

As a scholar of constitutional law, I see parallels between the current debate on AI governance and how national constitutions have been crafted, broken, borrowed and fixed.

In a recent development, countries’ constitutions have distinct underlying priorities affecting their design. These fall roughly into two historical approaches (C. Skach Int. J. Const. Law 7, 175–181; 2009). One takes liberty as its priority: describing what people are free to do and what the state should not do to interfere. The other focuses on human dignity: noting not what individuals may do, but what cannot be done to them, regardless of the circumstances or whether they have consented to it.

Germany’s post-war constitutionalism demonstrates the latter. Its founding document, or basic law, was designed in 1949 to put dignity first and foremost — as both the highest guiding value and a constitutional right, the violation of which is actionable in court. It established an independent federal constitutional court, which citizens can approach directly, that became operational two years later. The choice of this value and the legal institutions to protect it were a response to the atrocities of the Nazi regime, in which people became instruments of state objectives.

Recent findings suggest that frontier AI companies and policymakers should seriously consider using these features of German constitutionalism. When dignity as a constitutional right is violated — by an AI-generated deepfake or by a chatbot that treats a distressed user as just another interaction — the people affected could petition the court directly, and the perpetrator could be stopped and held accountable.

Recent findings suggest that given long-standing concerns about AI’s objectification of people and treating individuals as mere data points for prediction, this type of constitutional approach should guide AI governance. From the use of people’s photos for intimate AI deepfakes to AI facial-recognition databases being built without consent, these concerns regarding user objectification are valid.

Human dignity addresses these issues in ways that fairness, safety and human rights cannot: it covers the relationship between individuals and the technology influencing them. It does not replace other values, but explains why they matter. It prohibits turning a person into a mere object or an instrument of the state or of others.

In a recent development, critics might say that dignity is hard to define, or that making it actionable in an independent constitutional AI court will cause delays to model development. Both are partly true. But the current approach — a set of untethered commitments and declarations about user autonomy and human rights without independent enforcement — is close to meaningless. It will break under pressure, which is just when constitutions should step in. When dignity as a constitutional right is violated, the one violating it can be stopped and held accountable.

This concern also presents an opportunity. Courts are often slow, even for human disputes. Perhaps a slow court is exactly what AI needs. Lately, tech leaders Dario Amodei, Sam Altman, Elon Musk and Demis Hassabis have begun asking for AI development to slow down. Why not use this moment of reflection to think beyond the kinds of industry-funded audit commission that have been proposed, which are self-regulatory and funded by their own members.

According to recent reports, historically, such watchdogs examined technical craft. The need to consider human values and rights and for independent institutions that will protect and adjudicate conflicts means that we are now entering the slow-moving and more-protective realm of public law.

Frontier AI firms and public bodies are beginning to design the values and the institutions that underpin AI. But they must do both simultaneously, producing full constitutional orders to control AI’s power. Otherwise, the risk is that vague value statements without the protective institutions — without full AI constitutionalism — will end up being, as eighteenth-century philosopher Jeremy Bentham noted, “nonsense upon stilts” and nothing more.
