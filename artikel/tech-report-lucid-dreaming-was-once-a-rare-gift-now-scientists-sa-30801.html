Tech Report: Lucid Dreaming Was Once a Rare Gift. Now Scientists Say They Can Help You Learn It.

Some people don’t just dream. They often realize they’re dreaming and can even control the dreamworld, from giving themselves powers that defy the laws of physics to escaping a nightmare by waking themselves up. Lucid dreamers can go on adventures most of us can only dream about (pun intended). But is it possible to train yourself to do the same?

Dreams can happen during any stage of sleep, but they usually occur during REM sleep. When dreamers suddenly become aware that they’re dreaming, they’re in a lucid state. However, people who frequently have lucid dreams are rare. Researchers from the University of Trento in Rovereto, Italy, wanted to find out whether standard exercises, meditation, and virtual reality could teach people to have lucid dreams. Participants in the experimental group practiced open monitoring meditation, which is meant to sharpen sensory awareness and perception through nonjudgmental observation. They then did VR training that took them into virtual worlds full of bizarre, dreamlike scenarios. For comparison, a control group used VR that had nothing to do with dreaming, while a third group did only the standard training, without VR.

All participants started with the same baseline training, which they did while awake to practice dream recall and reality checks. They were told to keep a dream diary, noting each morning whether they remembered any dreams and, if so, what the dreams were about. Participants were also told to do reality checks, such as pinching themselves, to test whether they were awake or dreaming. They did these checks whenever they ran into something strange or simply thought of something strange. On top of that, phone alarms went off every hour for 12 hours each day to prompt more checks. The idea was that the habit would eventually carry over into their dreams. On top of this training, the experimental group did open monitoring meditation sessions in the lab to prepare for their VR training. During these sessions, participants observed their breathing, bodily sensations, thoughts, and emotions without reacting to them. Each session also included a guided exploration of an environment designed to look like a scene from a dream.

“Through repetition, [reality checks] are intended to become habitual and to transfer into dreams via ‘dream residue,’ where they may be triggered by dream signs that are unusual, surprising, or incongruent events, and thereby precipitate insight into the dream state,” the researchers wrote in their study, recently published in Frontiers in Psychology.

To build these scenes, the researchers created LucidDreamingVR, a program inspired by Tibetan dream yoga. Unlike the VR control group, whose content had nothing to do with dreaming, the experimental group completed eight sessions that grew steadily more elaborate, taking participants from recognizing a dream to controlling one. The early tasks taught them to spot dream signs: the virtual surroundings would suddenly shift, cueing a reality check, much as an odd detail might inside a dream. Later sessions drew on dream yoga techniques to let participants rehearse what they might do once they became lucid. With a single spoken command, they could fly, leap to incredible heights or, like Alice after tumbling down the rabbit hole, grow and shrink at will.

The researchers counted both lucid and semi-lucid dreams as “lucid-like” dreams. By the end of the experiment, 11 of the 13 participants who had trained with LucidDreamingVR had reported at least one lucid-like dream. By contrast, eight of 13 people in the control group without VR and only four of 12 in the VR control group reported one. Nearly 12% of the dreams the experimental group remembered were lucid-like, compared with about 3% for the control group without VR and about 1% for the VR control group. Surprisingly, the LucidDreamingVR group remembered the fewest dreams overall, so they also reported the fewest ordinary, non-lucid dreams. These results suggest that combining open monitoring meditation with dream-themed VR may increase lucid dreaming. However, because neither control group meditated, the study can’t show which part of the training mattered more.

It has been revealed that “[Our] findings support the use of immersive environments as…cognitive-enhancement tools,” the researchers said. “Future work should combine VR-based rehearsal with objective sleep verification, factorial designs, and waking cognitive measures to clarify mechanism, specificity, and transfer.”.
