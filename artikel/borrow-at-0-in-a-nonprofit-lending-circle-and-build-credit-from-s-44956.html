📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Join a nonprofit’s lending circle when you need a small loan but have little or no credit history. Members pay in each month and take turns collecting the pot, so nobody pays interest, and the nonprofit reports every payment to the credit bureaus. Your payments continue until the last member collects, so pick an amount you can always pay.

A payday loan commonly costs $15 per $100 borrowed, an annual percentage rate of almost 400% on a two-week loan, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau states. A lending circle charges 0% interest, though you get the money only when your turn comes.

Sources indicate that it’s the same arrangement as a tanda, a susu, or a hui. In a 10-person circle paying $100 a month, a different member takes the $1,000 pot each month. Going first gets you an interest-free $1,000 loan, and going last works like saving $1,000.

Sources indicate that in an informal circle among friends, a member who stops paying after collecting leaves the others short, and nobody’s payments count toward their credit. Mission Asset Fund (MAF), a San Francisco nonprofit, has members sign loan documents, collects payments electronically, and reports them to all three major credit bureaus. In MAF’s version of that example, all 10 members would add 10 on-time payments to their credit reports.

In a recent development, MAF’s circles have 6 to 12 members, loans of $300 to $2,400, and typical monthly payments of $50 to $200. Applicants take its online financial education courses, then settle on a loan amount and their place in the order, so ask for an early turn if you need the money soon.

Sources indicate that in that 10-person circle, the first member still owes nine $100 payments after collecting, and a missed one can hurt the credit history you joined to build. Pick a loan amount whose payment you could make every month of the circle, not just until your turn.
