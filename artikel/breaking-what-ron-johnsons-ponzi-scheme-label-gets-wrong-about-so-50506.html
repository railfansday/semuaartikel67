Breaking: What Ron Johnson's 'Ponzi scheme' label gets wrong about Social Security, experts say

A newly introduced Senate budget committee hearing on Wednesday reignited an old Social Security question: Is the program, which provides monthly benefits to millions of Americans, a Ponzi scheme?

Senate Budget Committee Chairman Ron Johnson, a Republican representing Wisconsin, called Social Security "a legal Ponzi scheme" in his opening remarks. But experts say his comments overlook how Social Security works and the real dilemma for lawmakers over its looming funding shortfall.

Sources indicate that A Ponzi scheme is an investment fraud that uses funds from newly introduced investors to pay existing investors, according to the Securities and Exchange Commission, Johnson wrote in his chairman's hearing memo. He mentioned that Social Security fits the description if you remove the word "fraud.".

Johnson's comments followed his Wednesday morning interview on Fox Business, where he said, "At this point, there's really no way to fix Social Security.".

"You can't fix it, you just shut it down and then hope that you can recover some of your money," Johnson said during the Fox Business interview.

"As far as I can tell, this is the first time in decades that a senior senator, chairman of the budget committee, stated he would rather shut down Social Security than fix it," Sen. Ron Wyden, D-Ore., said during the hearing.

At the Senate hearing, Johnson mentioned his comments during the TV interview were "imprecise" and that he was talking broadly about Ponzi schemes, not Social Security, that need to be shut down.

Johnson mentioned he wants lawmakers to pass a law to ensure seniors' Social Security benefits are not reduced, "then roll up our sleeves and fix the real problem, which is $24 trillion of 10-year deficits.".

The Congressional Budget Office has projected a $24.4 trillion cumulative federal budget deficit over the next 10 years.

Johnson proposed a bill on Wednesday that would allow for Social Security retirement benefits to be funded by general revenue once that trust fund reaches its insolvency date.

Critics say that's not a viable solution. Using the general fund to pay for Social Security would "absolutely explode the national debt" and cost 165% of gross domestic product over 75 years, Maya MacGuineas, president of the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget, a nonpartisan organization, noted in a statement.

The way Johnson described the program during the hearing was "out of touch with the way the American public understands and knows Social Security," stated Bill Sweeney, senior vice president of government affairs at AARP, a nonprofit, nonpartisan organization representing Americans age 50 and over.

Recent findings suggest that "The budget committee should know that Social Security is not part of the budget," Sweeney stated. "It runs on its own budget.".

According to recent reports, social Security does affect the unified federal deficit and debt held by the public when outlays exceed dedicated revenues, according to CBO. The Treasury securities held by Social Security's trust funds count as intragovernmental debt, which is included in gross federal debt but not in debt held by the public.

Social Security benefits are mainly funded by payroll tax contributions. When the program's income exceeds its costs, the surplus is invested in special U.S. Treasury securities held in the trust funds.

Social Security is not allowed to borrow money and cannot go into debt, according to Sweeney.

"It's never contributed a penny to the debt," Sweeney said. "It's not part of the national debt because it's in a separate budget.".

In a recent development, social Security's trust funds may be depleted within the next six to eight years, based on current projections. If that happens, Social Security benefits may be cut.

In a recent development, in six years — the fourth quarter of 2032 — the Social Security retirement trust fund may run out, Social Security's trustees projected in June. At that time, 78% of those benefits would be payable.

In eight years — the third quarter of 2034 — Social Security's combined retirement and disability trust funds may run out, when 83% of scheduled benefits would be payable, according to the Social Security trustees' projections.

Had payroll tax revenue kept up with gross domestic product over the past four decades, the program would be able to pay full benefits until 2059 — an additional 25 years versus current projections, according to recent AARP research.

At the hearing, Johnson held up a picture of the trust funds, which he said is a "four-drawer file in Parkersburg, West Virginia.".

Johnson similarly mentioned the trust fund files at a July 2023 hearing. In 2005, then-President George W. Bush was shown paper evidence of Social Security's U.S. bonds during a visit to the Bureau of Public Debt in Parkersburg.

While the politicians' actions seem to suggest the trust funds aren't real, experts say they are legitimate.

In a recent development, "It's all about trying to scare people into thinking that Social Security is not going to be there for you when it's time for you to retire," Sweeney noted. "For 91 years it has been, and there's no reason to believe it won't be.".

Wednesday's Senate hearing was a "missed opportunity" for a more in-depth discussion of the challenges facing Social Security, remarked Jason Fichtner, a witness at the hearing and executive director of the LIMRA Retirement Income Institute, a research initiative within the insurance trade association LIMRA.

Plans such as raising or eliminating the taxable maximum for payroll contributions to Social Security may push the program's funding woes 20 or 30 years later, but would not make the program solvent for the 75-year target, according to Fichtner, a former Social Security Administration official.

Moreover, fixing Social Security would not resolve the government's larger fiscal complications, which would still leave the economy vulnerable to bond market strain, he mentioned.

It has been revealed that "The other concern is fear," Fichtner said. "We do see evidence now of people claiming Social Security earlier because of their fear of the trust funds running out of money or Social Security not being able to pay benefits.".

A June 2025 AARP survey found 49% of respondents who had recently claimed or planned to claim Social Security benefits earlier than planned were mostly motivated by concerns the program may run out of money.

"Social Security is very effective at solving problems, and it's a thorn in the side of the federal government because they have done a horrible job managing so many aspects of our finances," said labor economist Kathryn Anne Edwards, a witness at the hearing and a senior fellow at the National Academy of Social Insurance, a provider of research and policy analysis on social insurance and economic security programs.

"But Americans don't see it that way," she remarked.

More than half of Social Security recipients surveyed say benefits are their main source of income, according to an August AARP poll.

Social Security reform provides an opportunity to expand the program to help meet Americans' needs, Edwards remarked. Adding universal paid family and medical leave to the program could help individuals and families who don't currently have access to those benefits, she said. Moreover, strengthening unemployment insurance could help ensure financial security amid the prospect of AI-related job losses, Edwards said.

In a recent development, senators who were elected on Nov. 3 will be in office when the retirement trust fund depletion date hits in 2032. The next session of Congress may provide an opportunity for "serious" hearings on the future of Social Security, Sweeney noted.

The Senate budget committee's Wednesday hearing followed other recent hearings on the program's future, including a June Senate finance subcommittee hearing and an August Senate finance full committee hearing.
