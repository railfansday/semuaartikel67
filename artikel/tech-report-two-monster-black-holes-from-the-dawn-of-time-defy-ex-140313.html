Tech Report: Two monster black holes from the dawn of time defy explanation

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Astronomers have identified an extraordinary pair of quasars dating back to just 900 million years after the Significant Bang. Each is powered by an enormous black hole that somehow grew to an astonishing size when the Universe was still in its infancy. Scientists have no definitive explanation for how these two cosmic giants became so massive so quickly. The remarkable pair has been given the Hawaiian name Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua.

An international research team has discovered the most distant compact pair of quasars ever observed, offering a rare glimpse into the earliest stages of cosmic history. Officially designated J1602+4228AB and nicknamed Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua, the pair also holds the record for the closest separation between two known quasars from the early period known as cosmic dawn.

It has been revealed that although astronomers believe paired quasars should be relatively common, finding and studying them is exceptionally difficult. Discovering two so close together, especially from such an early period in the Universe's history, makes this finding particularly remarkable.

The discovery was made possible by combining observations from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) and the Gemini North telescope on Maunakea, a dormant volcano in Hawai'i. The research was led by Silvia Onorato, a postdoctoral research associate at NSF NOIRLab, and is described in a paper published in The Astrophysical Journal Letters.

Gemini North is one of the two telescopes that make up the International Gemini Observatory. Operated by NSF NOIRLab, the observatory is an international partnership led by the U.S. National Science Foundation, with participation from Canada, Chile, Brazil, Argentina, and the Republic of Korea.

How Giant Black Holes Formed in the Early Universe.

Industry observers note that during the first billion years after the Big Bang, the Universe underwent a period of extraordinary transformation. Vast concentrations of invisible matter, known as dark matter halos, collapsed under gravity. The earliest galaxies began producing stars, while increasingly massive galaxies developed the first supermassive black holes at their centers.

As galaxies passed near one another, collided, and sometimes merged, enormous quantities of gas and stars became available to feed their central black holes. Some of the material falling toward these black holes released tremendous amounts of energy. This process, known as accretion, could generate radiation so intense that the glowing region surrounding a black hole outshone every star in its host galaxy.

Astronomers call these extraordinarily luminous objects quasars.

Because galaxy encounters and mergers occur frequently throughout the Universe, researchers expect many quasars to exist in pairs separated by distances smaller than the typical diameter of a galaxy. Yet detecting these systems has remained a major challenge.

In a recent development, one reason is that a supermassive black hole spends only a relatively brief period in its bright "quasar phase." Another is the enormous distance separating ancient quasars from Earth, which makes them appear extremely faint to ground-based telescopes. Even when both objects are visible, their close proximity can make it difficult to distinguish one from the other.

Two Monster Black Holes That Grew Too Significant, Too Fast.

Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua stands out because it is only the third known quasar pair from cosmic dawn. More importantly, its two black holes were already so enormous at such an early stage of cosmic history that existing theories cannot definitively explain their growth.

The black holes are separated by approximately 18,100 light-years, equivalent to about one-fifth the diameter of the Milky Way. One has an estimated mass of 1.5 billion Suns, while the other weighs approximately 400 million solar masses.

Astronomers see the pair as it appeared only 900 million years after the Major Bang, when the Universe was still populated by relatively young stars, galaxies, and black holes. Finding two black holes comparable in mass to many present-day supermassive black holes at this early time raises difficult questions about how quickly the first enormous cosmic structures could assemble.

"There is no definitive theoretical explanation for how a single one of these giant quasars exists so early in the age of our Universe, much less two," says Onorato. "We observed Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua at a time when the Universe was only a thirteenth of its age and astonishingly, its supermassive black holes are just as massive as those we see today.".

The pair's Hawaiian name reflects the extraordinary forces involved. Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua translates to "Two celestial bodies of fathomless powerful darkness in the brilliance of heat reincarnating energy into the Universe.".

The name was developed through the A Hua He Inoa program, a collaboration involving the ʻImiloa Astronomy Center of Hawaiʻi and the University of Hawai'i's Ka Haka ʻUla O Keʻelikōlani (Hawaiian Language College).[1].

How James Webb and Gemini North Revealed the Hidden Pair.

Industry observers note that the discovery began with data collected by the Aether survey, a JWST research program designed to identify and characterize objects from the early Universe. The project is led by Emanuele Paolo Farina, an Associate Scientist at NSF NOIRLab/Gemini.

Industry observers note that using JWST's Near-Infrared Spectrograph Integral Field Unit, the survey assembled observations of 200 quasars. That sample represents approximately 43% of all quasars known from this period of cosmic history.

Sources indicate that while visually examining the survey data, researchers noticed something unexpected. A previously identified quasar, J1602+4228A, appeared to have a companion.

Recent findings suggest that to investigate, the team turned to Gemini North's Gemini Near-Infrared Spectrograph (GNIRS). These observations allowed astronomers to distinguish the two quasars individually, providing crucial evidence about the nature of the system.

It has been revealed that obtaining those measurements was an exceptional achievement. The objects are separated by such a small apparent distance, and the companion J1602+4228B is so faint, that resolving them with a telescope on Earth was expected to be nearly impossible.

Fortunately, the researchers benefited from exceptionally favorable observing conditions on Maunakea. Clear skies and unusually sharp atmospheric conditions enabled them to gather the necessary observations in just two nights.

"The sky over Maunakea was so clean and sharp that we managed to collect the data in only two nights," says Onorato.

The team also used Gemini North's Gemini Multi-Object Spectrograph (GMOS) to gather additional information about the system and refine their understanding of the two quasars.[2].

A Latest Clue to the Universe's First Giant Galaxies.

It has been revealed that exactly how Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua formed remains unknown. However, the researchers believe its extraordinary properties could point to an unusually dense concentration of matter in the early Universe.

In such an environment, frequent interactions between young galaxies may have provided enormous supplies of gas, simultaneously accelerating the birth of stars and fueling rapid growth of the black holes at their centers.

If this interpretation is correct, the quasar pair could help scientists understand how the earliest massive galaxies developed alongside their central supermassive black holes.

It has been revealed that "Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua is a really unique environment," says Onorato. "It's a laboratory for astronomers to study how the very first massive galaxies and their central black holes assembled together through direct galaxy interactions during these early times.".
