In Praise of the Charity Ride, One of the Most Underrated Ways to Tackle Your First Century—or Any Substantial Cycling Goal

They say pride comes before the fall. In cycling, I’ve found pride usually comes before the first true descent.

I relearned this lesson earlier this month, when I found myself at the top of Bear Mountain, the only notable multi-mile climb close enough to bike to from New York City. Its peak was situated about halfway into the 105-mile route of the Best Buddies Challenge New York, an event the organization had brought back for a second year to fund its programming for teenagers and young adults with intellectual and developmental disabilities. The riders gawking at the intermittently vermilion canopy who were pedaling alongside me had each raised at least $1,800 to reach this particular vista, plus access to several deliciously well-stocked aid stations, SAG vehicles, and the promise of a raucous finish-line party.

It has been revealed that though I’m relatively new to road cycling, I’ve done some long gravel rides and recently tackled the Maratona dles Dolomites in Italy, where I buried myself to finish a 66-mile route that featured over 10,000 feet of elevation and one 8-mile climb up the Passo Valparolo. By comparison, the Best Buddies Challenge century, with 2,000 fewer feet of climbing stretched over a much longer route, seemed plenty doable when I signed up in September.

The first indication I had erred came before I had pinned my bib number to my jersey. At a pre-ride reception, I sat down with Alberto Contador (yes, that Alberto Contador), who was returning to the event for the second time. He was confident I could handle Bear Mountain, but warned me that the end of the ride reminded him of La Flèche Wallonne, a notoriously difficult Belgian one-day race known for its repeat ascents of the relatively short, brutally steep Mur de Huy. “The last 30 miles is all up and down…save energy,” he remarked.

By the time I slid into the fourth aid station, which marked the 33-miles-to-go point in my own ride, this advice was well out of my mind. I was supremely confident in my ability to finish strong. Sure, I had gone a bit deeper in the tank to maintain contact with random groups throughout the day in valleys and on shallow sections, but there was so little left to go.

How hard could the rest really be?

The Best Buddies Challenge is relatively newly introduced to New York City, but the concept of the charity ride is not. The Five Boro Bike Tour, which raises money for Bike New York’s free education and mechanics program, has given city cyclists the chance to experience streets without car traffic since 1977. On the West Coast, Washington’s Cascade Bicycle Club’s Seattle to Portland Bicycle Classic now generates about $1 million each year to fund the organization’s community programs, cycling education, and statewide cycling advocacy work. Even the ride I did in the Dolomites this summer offers charity entrants, who have, over the years, raised over $2 million for road safety, food security, disease research, and rural education.

Sources indicate that among non-endurance athletes, these rides often draw comparisons to running events like the Latest York City Marathon. There are certainly similarities—sanctioned routes, feed stations, distances that sound absurd to normies—but cyclists don’t tend to give even long charity rides anything close to the same level of credence as runners do to marathons.

That kind of resistance perhaps comes from those strains of cycling tradition that prize grit and ardor and self-reliance. Conquering Mur de Huy during a race takes superhuman levels of tenacity and determination—charity rides, by contrast, even those that span 100 miles, are much more comfortable affairs. Do some riders dismiss these events because they’re too soft? It’s possible.

Industry observers note that beginner-friendliness is often baked into the event programming of charity rides. Breakthrough T1D Rides, held to fund research for type 1 diabetes, are actually four-day retreats. Participants spend only one day on the bike. Anthony Geraci, national director of Ride & Endurance Programs, claims most of these rides feature “out-and-back” courses. Turn around whenever you feel done with your day! “We always say it’s not about the miles, it’s about the mission.”.

Sure, you could cross-stitch that phrase onto a throw pillow. But it’s also the kind of mentality that cyclists might need to achieve some personal goals, whether that’s riding in a new destination or tackling their first century. In this way, charity rides can be catalysts for remarkable personal achievement.

They also create the conditions for extremely impressive fundraising. Best Buddies International CEO Anthony Shriver (yes, that Shriver) told me that its cycling events account for about 10 to 15 percent of the organization’s annual revenue. Similarly, Kris Rauh, vice president, event experience and operations for NMSS, states its 49 Bike MS events generate about $52 million in revenue annually for the organization.

In a recent development, indeed, she continued, many of the people signing up to do their first Bike MS have no personal connection to the disease in its name. “They come to us mostly because they want a challenge on a bike … they don’t come to us as fundraisers,” she said.

According to recent reports, for charity rides like Bike MS and Best Buddies, it’s important to connect participants to their mission and the impact of their work. As I ascended Bear Mountain and pushed myself on the way back, I wasn’t just riding with Contador and Chris Froome (who was also participating this year) but also plenty of folks who volunteer for or participate in Best Buddies programming. In fact, individuals with intellectual and developmental disabilities often held key roles during its rides. At the Challenge Newly introduced York, a “Buddy” gave a speech while we waited in the start pen, a different “Buddy” sang the national anthem, and still others staffed the aid stations. “From the moment you wake up at 4 o’clock in the morning, you should know you’re doing something that’s going to enrich another human being’s life,” Shriver remarked.

Recent findings suggest that the combination makes these kinds of charity rides feel a lot different than a standard criterium or gravel fondo. And it seems to hold enough appeal to keep the organizations hosting them going. Best Buddies, which has substantial fundraising minimums for riders, gets a 30 to 50 percent return rate, Shriver says. According to Rauh, at Bike MS that number is closer to 60 percent. That’s a lot of people returning to charity rides each year.

About two-thirds of the way into my own Fresh York Challenge, I was actively questioning how this could be possible. Though I had been correct that all the long climbs were behind me, the “rolling” terrain I had imagined was actually a freak show of sharp pitches. I cracked on a climb my Garmin did not categorize, attempting to hold serve over a bump with a gradient of 18 percent.

Recent findings suggest that I unclipped and shuffled my cramping legs up the embarrassingly short walk to the summit. I felt ashamed by the level of hubris I had brought into the event. If it had been just a regular ride, or even a race, this might have been my day. With hours left to a finish that I felt confident most of the participants had already crossed, it would have been totally within my prerogative to pull the quick-release lever off my front wheel and stuff my bike in a Lyft bound back to Brooklyn.

But as I munched on some blissfully salty crackers that quieted my screaming hamstrings, I realized that getting to the end would be an accomplishment for me regardless of how fast I was able to do it. And, importantly, my path to the shuttle bus that would take me back to the city included one final rest stop. Resolved never to underestimate a “ride” again, I got back in the saddle and held the limpest zone 1 pace of my life until I had made it to the aforementioned support tent. With less than 10 miles left, I slumped into a folding chair and sucked down a can of Sprite.

It has been revealed that immediately, I was engaged by Matt, who was participating in the ride as a volunteer. He asked if I was okay, and when it was clear that I was mentally wrecked, but physically fine, gently encouraged me to get moving. With Matt, another volunteer named Ian, and a pair of other riders who had also had similarly tough days, we set off on the last stretch to the end.

Chatting in my bunch, really the only bunch I had been able to hang on to the entire day, made the miles go by faster and the remaining climbs feel shorter. And when the sugar from my soda hit, I realized I was actually less dead than the pair of participants I had just encountered. Ian and I forged ahead, buoyed by the knowledge the end was near. I managed to stay upright enough up the finishing hill to attempt a celebratory pose as I crossed over the finish line: ninth to last. It was the longest ride I’ve ever done and it challenged me to push myself harder than I might otherwise have done. That’s worth something.

Does anyone have a cool $2,000 I could use to try again next year?
