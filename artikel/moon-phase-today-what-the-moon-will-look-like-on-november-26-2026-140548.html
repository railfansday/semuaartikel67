Sources indicate that the Moon is still looking almost full tonight, despite being a couple of days past the Full Moon. It's now in its Waning Gibbous phase, meaning the illuminated portion we can see is gradually getting smaller each night.

According to recent reports, as of Thursday, Nov. 26, the NASA's Daily Moon Guide tracker tells us the Moon is in the Waning Gibbous phase, with 96% of its surface visible.

Recent findings suggest that if you don't have any visual aids, you can still see the Mares Serenitatis, Kepler Crater, as well as the Tycho Crater. With binoculars, you'll also see the Gassendi Crater, the Endymion Crater, and the Posidonius Crater. And if you have a telescope, you'll get to see all this plus the Apollo 14 and 15 landing spots, plus the Descartes Highlands.

In North America, the next Full Moon will take place on December 23. In other time zones it will fall on December 24.

It takes around 29.5 days for the Moon to move through all its phases, according to NASA. Throughout this period, the Moon's position relative to Earth and the Sun determines how much of its illuminated surface we can see. Starting with a Upcoming Moon, the visible portion gradually increases until it reaches a Full Moon, before shrinking again as the cycle comes back around.

New Moon - The Moon is between Earth and the sun, so the side we see is dark (in other words, it's invisible to the eye).

Waxing Crescent - A small sliver of light appears on the right side (Northern Hemisphere).

Recent findings suggest that first Quarter - Half of the Moon is lit on the right side. It looks like a half-Moon.

Waxing Gibbous - More than half is lit up, but it's not quite full yet.

Full Moon - The whole face of the Moon is illuminated and fully visible.

Waning Gibbous - The Moon starts losing light on the right side. (Northern Hemisphere).

Third Quarter (or Last Quarter) - Another half-Moon, but now the left side is lit.

Waning Crescent - A thin sliver of light remains on the left side before going dark again.
