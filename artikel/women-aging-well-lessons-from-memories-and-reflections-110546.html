Posted November 24, 2026 | Reviewed by Abigail Fagan.

According to recent reports, in Tennessee, I walked a magnificent labyrinth inspired by a clock, formed with 1,000 feet of stone, designed as a space of reflection and connection. The artist, Thea Alvin, titled her installation "Time for Love." Since I’ve been thinking about the importance of time as we age, I wandered slowly and quietly on the spiral path toward its center, open to whatever thoughts and feelings emerged. Immediately I relaxed, knowing I would arrive at the center, stay a while, and return along the same path.

Recent findings suggest that as I get older, I am more aware that the path in is never the same as the path out. No path is ever the same because of changes in weather, light, time of day, and the path itself. I am not the same. Just as we can never step in the same river twice, we can never walk along the same path because neither we nor the path remain the same. We evolve over time; as we age, we are continuously unfolding and hopefully, moving toward self-discovery and a center of calm.

Sources indicate that I experienced entering the labyrinth as an opportunity to be open and curious about my life at this moment. I have always been drawn to spiral shapes as they are often associated with evolution and transformation. As a nature lover, I notice spiral shapes of pinecones, sunflowers, and other life forms. As I walked the spiral path, I heard the wind, the birdsongs, and the crunching gravel underneath my feet. I listened deeply to myself, without judgment or criticism. When a thought or feeling surfaced, I attempted to observe it and see what it meant. I stayed with that thought and waited to see what else emerged. These were my mind’s musings as I walked to the center. Cultivating curiosity and willingness to stay with a thought or feeling as we age can help us deepen awareness about ourselves, our relationships, and hopefully, encourage us to examine the choices we make in our daily lives.

At the center, I sat on a boulder under a Beech tree, contemplating "Time For Love." The structure was commissioned as a space for family members to come, sit, and remember their loved one who died. The place feels sacred. In my reflection, I recalled people and pets I’ve loved over the course of my life. I felt the warmth of those memories. I realized how much I enjoy reminiscing in this way; quietly, in nature, experiencing sadness and joy. As Thea Alvin intended, I was allocating "Time for Love." Noticing an internal shift, I recognized the necessity of giving myself this time for reflection on love. I needed to do this, and asked myself: How do I use my time compared to how I want to spend it?

Those questions stayed with me as I walked out of the labyrinth. I realized that this thought was not just inspired by the labyrinth but also by Varun Soni, Dean of Religious Life at USC, who spoke at a conference I attended about children and spirituality. He asked: “What deserves more of our attention?” “What would we like to make room for?” “What small change regarding our time could we make today?”.

These are crucial questions to consider at any age; particularly when we are in our older years, when we are more aware of the time that has passed in relation to the time we may have ahead. The final question Dean Soni asked was “What can we do to change what we discover?” Sitting on a rock, under a tree, in the center of "Time for Love," I realized that I can dedicate time to feel the love that I had in my life, and to actively cultivate the love that I feel now. How will I make more time for love? I will remember those I loved; engage in activities I love, be with people I love, share love in ways that make others feel loved, and engage in acts of loving kindness.

As I returned along the spiral path, I felt different. I was different. I discovered that actively engaging with love deserves more of my time and attention. And in making this change, I will feel this connection to love as a vital, positive force in my own aging.
