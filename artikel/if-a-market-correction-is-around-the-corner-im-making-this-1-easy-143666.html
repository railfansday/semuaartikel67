Anyone waiting to buy stocks at better prices got a lesson in just how promptly an opportunity can disappear.

Nvidia (NVDA -3.91%) CEO Jensen Huang's departure sent some U.S. stock prices sharply lower last week. The S&P 500 (^GSPC -0.25%) entered correction territory, and the Nasdaq-100 briefly sank into a bear market. By Monday morning, Huang was reinstated, and the Nasdaq-100 rebounded by nearly 7%.

How should investors handle this volatility, and what portfolio moves, if any, should you make right now?

This week's rebound was astonishingly quick in that a lot of ground was recovered in a single day. But it does follow a recent track record of rapid recoveries.

Recent findings suggest that the Q1 2025 correction that culminated with President Trump's "Liberation Day" tariffs experienced a similar turnaround. After falling 19% by early April, the S&P 500 was back to all-time highs by the end of June.

Sources indicate that of course, there was the COVID pandemic bear market. The S&P 500 fell by more than 30% in one month's time. After a multitrillion-dollar stimulus package, the index was back to a fresh all-time high by August, just five months later.

Recent findings suggest that it's rare to find just the right moment to buy the dip as it's happening. That's why I usually suggest keeping your systematic investment prepares going as scheduled.

Industry observers note that purchasing shares on a set schedule is an ideal way to make at least some purchases at lower prices without trying to call a market bottom. In sharp, single-day rebounds like Monday, it may not work unless you place a trade manually ahead of time. But during corrections where the recovery process takes months, even a couple of buys along the way can reduce your cost basis significantly.

Keeping your automatic investment prepares going throughout the current pullback might be especially crucial this time around.

In a recent development, A CBOE study last year found that sudden volatility shocks in the markets can result in elevated volatility that lasts for months. Volatility, of course, tends to be closely associated with falling stock prices. If that pattern follows the current correction, it could be a fair amount of time before the S&P 500 and other major equity indexes hit newly introduced highs.

In a recent development, but for regular investors, that can be an opportunity. The longer stocks hover below highs, the better your chance to pick up more shares at discounted prices. It may feel counterintuitive as it's happening. But continuing to make systematic monthly investments can pay off significantly in the years ahead.
