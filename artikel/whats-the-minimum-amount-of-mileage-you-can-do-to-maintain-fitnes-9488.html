What’s the Minimum Amount of Mileage You Can Do to Maintain Fitness? Experts Answer

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that for some runners, taking a week or two off from running after a race is the hard part—they can’t wait to ramp back up their volume again. But for others, crossing a finish line (of any distance) might feel like a natural point to take a break from the sport, or at least to significantly scale back.

Still, if you trained for months to build up the fitness, including your aerobic capacity, lactate threshold, and running economy, you probably want to try to maintain at least some of that afterward. That way, you can go into a spring training cycle with a solid base.

Maintenance mode also gives you the flexibility to be able to jump into a 10K or half-marathon on a whim, notes Mike Sheehy, running coach for Team Abbott who has completed more than 100 marathons.

Keeping a steady mileage base also allows you to experiment. “This gives you a chance to try fresh fuels, try new gear, a new pair of shorts, new pair of shoes, stuff you didn't like during your block,” Sheehy says. You could also experiment with pace. “This is a great block to think about moving your easy pace up 15 seconds to let your heart and body start adapting to that new norm.”.

If you want to strike a balance between giving yourself a well-deserved break and completely reversing all of your gains, the question becomes: What’s the minimum running you can do to maintain your fitness? And how long can I do that without falling back?

Here’s what running coaches and an exercise physiologist have to say, plus what other forms of exercise come into play in the maintenance phase.

First, it’s key to note that taking a week or two off after a marathon and at least one week for the half is not only encouraged, it’s probably the best thing you can do for your body—and your mind.

Recent findings suggest that “The thing I encourage all my runners to remember is patience,” claims Sheehy. “Let your body heal and take that time.” He typically advises runners to take a full week off, then slowly add in easy miles without rushing to get back to quality workouts.

Then by the end of week two, you enter the true maintenance phase. “This is really an exciting time of running,” Sheehy claims. “This is where we’re keeping 90 percent of the fitness with about 60 percent of the work.” He recommends taking the average mileage from your peak marathon training weeks rather than the single highest weekly mileage.

This tends to net out to between 20 and 30 miles a week in the maintenance phase for marathon runners and 10 to 15 miles a week for half-marathoners. That might look like three shorter, easier runs during the week and an 8- to 12-miler on the weekend. (Experts agree it’s best to run more frequently at lower volume for each run than do fewer, longer runs.) “That keeps you 90 percent fit, but allows you to enjoy running and not have the pressure of having to run six or five days a week and without having structured workouts,” Sheehy says.

Andrew M. Jones, Ph.D., professor of applied physiology at the University of Exeter in the U.K., puts the minimum effective dose of maintenance running slightly lower—at 50 percent—though after about four to six weeks at half the volume, runners will likely start to see “more significant deterioration,” he claims, specifically of race-specific endurance. (Running economy may be more resilient.).

That’s not necessarily a bad thing (and allows you to focus on other goals; more on this soon), and 50 percent is enough to preserve a foundation to allow you to pick up at the beginning of a 16-week marathon training plan with relative ease.

Greg Laraia, a running coach at Motiv in Latest York City, agrees and provides this example: Say a runner finished Berlin on September 27 and plans to run London on April 24. After time off, he’d have them work back up to 50 percent of their mileage by October 25, then hover in the 50 to 60 percent of mileage range until early January when their London block would start in earnest.

Sources indicate that retaining intensity during the maintenance phase is arguably more crucial than frequency and duration, per Jones. “It’s impossible to give a minimum effective dose [for intensity] but ideally by week two [after a marathon] they’d get back to the pre-marathon training intensity but with lower volume,” he states, which might be lower duration and/or frequency of quality running. “Intensity is the key stimulus.”.

To hit this stimulus, Sheehy and Laraia both like to have runners add in strides (e.g., four to six 30- to 60-second strides as part of an easy run) before doing more intense speed workouts that could make their way back into the weekly schedule about a month after a race.

Industry observers note that “The upcoming race pace is something I know they can achieve that is apparent in previous race results and workouts; not some aspirational wish pace,” Sheehy states. “This will give them the confidence they need or maybe a wake up call, but it makes for a great starting point, and allows us to calibrate their easy paces and use the lower volumes to let their body (and heart) adjust to the new norm.”.

Industry observers note that sheehy cautions against swapping out runs that make up your maintenance mileage for other cardio, as it is notable to maintain the specificity of running if you want to maintain running fitness. But he often adds in an indoor cycling day during his offseason on top of four or so days of running. If you dropped down your running frequency, an aerobic cross-training workout can support your fitness maintenance. Plus, it gives you a mental break, Jones says.

Laraia states one case where swapping maintenance miles for other cardio could make sense would be when runners come off a fall race injured or worn down and simply need a break from running so much. “Running is always going to be the best option because running is the sport, but if an elliptical or a bike is going to make you miss [running] enough to want to do it again, let’s start there,” he says.

Industry observers note that the maintenance phase is also a great time to focus on strength training, Jones claims. Sheehy agrees, suggesting aiming for two to three days a week. You can do more volume (either via an extra day or more sets/reps) during this phase, but it’s still important to include one true rest day in your plan, Sheehy says.

Industry observers note that strength work helps to build neuromuscular capacity, improve power that can translate to speed, and strengthen your tendons and ligaments for the upcoming season, especially if you focus on heavier lifting. “Let’s get comfortable with the extra load and then let’s see if it makes running better and more efficient,” Laraia states.

Laraia says signs that you may have pulled back too much include a higher-than-usual heart rate during runs or paces that feel harder than normal.

In a recent development, still, it’s probably better to lose a little bit more running fitness in the offseason than to push yourself too hard, he indicates. The former is going to put you in a better position to start a newly introduced training block in the spring, compared with the latter, which could lead to burnout and could set your next training cycle back.

Recent findings suggest that “Realistically, you’re going to be better off taking the extra day versus trying to push through something if you’re early in a block or just trying to maintain fitness,” Laraia indicates. “I’d rather you be 100 percent recovered for every run versus in the build,” where it might make sense to go into a run feeling a little depleted.
