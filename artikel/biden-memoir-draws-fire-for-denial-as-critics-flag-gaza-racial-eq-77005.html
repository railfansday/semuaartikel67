Former President Joe Biden’s new memoir has drawn sharp criticism and opened a debate about how Biden’s choices — and his account of them — will shape his legacy.

Released Nov. 17, "Promise Me, America" defends Biden’s presidency and his decision to seek re-election in 2024. The memoir drew a sharp verdict from The Newly introduced Yorker writer Amy Davidson Sorkin, who mentioned Biden remains "stubbornly in denial," while other critics scrutinized the book's account of Gaza and what it left out about racial equity. Sorkin’s criticism centers on Biden’s response to questions about his age and his continued insistence that he could have won.

It has been revealed that "The true test of a book like this is whether its author will own his losses, too. Biden, for his part, does not," added New York Times nonfiction critic Jennifer Szalai. "He is so preoccupied with burnishing his legacy that he gets awkward and defensive when addressing the two issues many readers will be most curious about: his handling of the war in Gaza, and his decision to run for a second term.".

"The news of the memoir, in short, is that there is no real news: Biden is as stubbornly in denial as ever, " Sorkin wrote.

The dispute around Biden's recollection of his tenure centers on two consequential decisions — his choice to seek another term and his administration’s policy toward Israel during the Gaza war.

Industry observers note that in its review of the memoir, the Associated Press reported that Biden claims he believed he was the best person to defeat President Donald Trump and that the Democratic primaries gave him no warning he was in trouble. Meanwhile, Sorkin and Szalai argued in their reviews that Biden gives too little weight to concerns about his age and fitness when recounting that period, despite acknowledging that his debate performance was poor.

Recent findings suggest that "Biden’s voice gets passive; he portrays himself as the helpless victim of events," Szalai wrote. "He gets similarly defensive when recounting the war in Gaza," she added.

Self-described socialist website Jacobin and other reviewers, including Sorkin and Szalai, criticized Biden's accounting of his relationship with Israel and Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, arguing he described it in the book as if it were much more adversarial than in actuality.

Szalai wrote that Biden gets "awkward and defensive" when addressing both Gaza and his decision to seek another term, and faulted him for how he described Israeli victims and suffering versus Palestinian victims and suffering. She specifically noted that Biden used names to identify Israeli victims but "no names" for Palestinian victims, while calling his language about Palestinian suffering "passive.".

It has been revealed that "The only consistent position is inconsistency," Israeli journalist and political commentator Amit Segal said of Biden’s account of Israel policy, calling it "devoid of doctrine or strategy. ".

Recent findings suggest that separately, Semafor politics reporter David Weigel noted that Biden recounts signing 17 executive orders on his first day but does not mention his order establishing a government-wide approach to racial equity that mandated every executive agency to develop a DEI action blueprint. Weigel wrote that the word "equity" does not appear in the memoir once.

"Biden argues instead that 'every one of my legislative accomplishments had justice baked in,' including 'equal access to vaccines,'" Weigel wrote.

According to recent reports, "Gender gets the same treatment," he continued. "Biden, who had called anti-transgender discrimination 'the civil rights issue of our time,' never uses the word 'transgender' in his memoir. He doesn’t mention his Day 1 executive order that banned discrimination on the basis of gender identity.".
