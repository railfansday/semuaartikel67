He found it teeming with life and beauty.

One night earlier this fall, a biologist and YouTuber in Florida named Joseph Ricketts went snorkeling in a retention pond next to his local Home Depot.

He filmed what he saw and posted a video to his channel titled “Night Snorkel In a Home Depot Retention Pond.” It promptly blew up, hitting more than 2 million views as of this writing, and for good reason: It’s not at all what you might expect.

Industry observers note that ricketts found an aquatic world teeming with life on his dive into what is, from the surface, a stagnant pool of water filled with old beer cans. A water snake undulated through the pond, a bulge in its stomach revealing a recent meal. Tadpoles darted about, some of them half on their way to froghood. Mysterious shrimp scuttled along the silty bottom.

Sources indicate that ricketts, who works as a crocodile and alligator research biologist in Florida, went for his first snorkeling trip when he was 16 years old. On that trip, he came face-to-face with a manatee — and was instantly hooked. “I experienced so much joy in that moment, and I just kind of subconsciously knew I’d be chasing that for the rest of my life to some degree or another,” he told me.

Ricketts still takes a lot of joy in snorkeling, in part because of how it allows him to quickly access a totally different world hiding just under the surface of the water; his YouTube bio declares, “I’ll snorkel pretty much anywhere.” And his recent retention pond dive among the suburban detritus bears that out.

I talked with Ricketts recently about urban ecology, snorkeling, and what motivates a person to jump into the pond behind the Home Depot three miles from his house (his shortest-ever commute to a dive yet). Our interview has been edited for length and clarity.

That’s a good question. I’m scuba certified, and I enjoy anything underwater, but snorkeling is just super accessible. I have a camera housing and a wet suit and other gear, but really all you need is just a mask and snorkel. As long as the water’s not freezing cold, you can get in there and take a look around. I really enjoy how, as soon as you immerse your head, it feels like you’re in a totally different world.

It has been revealed that but the beautiful thing is that you’re not. You’re in the same world, it’s just this is the one that you didn’t have a vision into before. It’s kind of a meditative, calming practice. Just taking a look around at what is happening in nature and how things survive in their own environment is pretty awesome. I love it.

Sources indicate that I honestly had the idea for it a couple years ago. I’d see people out there birding with binoculars and telephoto lenses, and I started wondering if I could snorkel in there. We’ve had a drought so I couldn’t do it for a really long time, and when we finally got some rain I decided to go for it.

Tell me about the retention pond. What is it — and why is it in this Home Depot parking lot?

Industry observers note that florida has a lot of low-level, very wet natural ecosystems, but it’s also an extremely developed state. There are strip malls and roads and concrete everywhere! Natural wetland environments can soak up intense stormwater, but when you totally change environments and put in shopping centers and neighborhoods and pave everything with asphalt, you kind of lose that ability. That stormwater needs a place to go and collect — or it’s just going to flood parking lots and stores and houses.

So they have to put these retention ponds in; nowadays, you can’t build in certain areas unless you put in a retention pond. Not far from the Home Depot is a new-build neighborhood, and they have a retention pond that they had to put in, but they set it up with a little walkway for people that live in the neighborhood. That’s nice because there’s some wildlife that’s able to survive and live there, and people can enjoy this sort-of natural area. But it’s also not as good as the original thing that may have been there, you know?

So you were snorkeling in what’s essentially a replacement for a wetland that might once have been there.

I don’t know the history of that area exactly, but I imagine there was some form of wetland.

What was the most surprising thing that you found?

One thing that I found was a tadpole shrimp, which people call triops. It’s a freshwater crustacean that apparently is not native to Florida. When I saw it underwater, I had no context for what it is. As a biologist, people assume you just know wildlife, but people forget how much wildlife there is in the world! I know a lot about alligators and crocodiles, but there’s still a ton I don’t know. I think it’s crucial to sometimes be able to be like: “I don’t know what I’m seeing or why I’m seeing it” — and then hopefully I can learn about it.

It has been revealed that but anyway, I saw this thing, I had no clue what it was, and I asked a bunch of my coworkers about it but they had no clue what it was either. So then I had to do some research online, which is how I finally figured it out.

“I think that was kind of a personal challenge I had for myself: Can I see something beautiful in a retention pond?”.

In a recent development, apparently they are native to the Midwest. They lay their eggs in soil, and then during times of rain, they really rapidly hatch and feed and grow, and then they reproduce, lay more eggs, and then wait for the next cycle while it’s dry. It’s a real quick boom-and-bust life cycle. It’s not a native species, but I think there’s just a couple areas in this state where you can find them — and one just happens to be right there in the retention pond.

Recent findings suggest that I saw that there were also more common animals there, like frogs, snakes, and dragonflies. But how do you think they all got into this parking-lot pond?

According to recent reports, A lot of frog species adapt to urban environments and find any bit of water they can. They were probably used to a forest and wetland, and now they’ve had to adapt to what’s available currently, which is pretty limited.

Recent findings suggest that I think that’s true for most of the animals you find in there. The natural world is pretty resilient, though sometimes I think we give it too much credit for how resilient it can be.

Sources indicate that life survives, but the way things originally were does not necessarily survive, and I think that’s something that’s really worth protecting. The way some people interpreted the video was like: ”Wow, look at all this life persisting even in this place!” And it was amazing and there is beauty there, but you can’t just accept that and then get rid of all our natural wetlands. We need untouched, expansive ecosystems.

It can be cool to be excited about this little retention pond and what lives in here, but coupled with that also needs to be excitement for protecting huge untouched habitat as well. Both are full of worthy things.

Do you think the retention pond, now that it’s there, is worth preserving?

Recent findings suggest that I think so. One thing I didn’t really get to dive into with the video too much is the idea of a nature desert. I don’t know if I’m making that term up, but it’s kind of related like a food desert: Depending on your demographic or where you’re born, that might be the only bit of nature you have access to. It’s a privilege to be able to travel to a national park or a big state park or something, and not everyone has that opportunity. Your only exposure to nature could be a retention pond that’s in the middle of a city somewhere. And so I do think those places are very worth protecting, because I think it’s important that regardless of your background, if you’re a kid, you have a place that you can go to and feel safe and be exposed to wilderness even if it’s very small urban wilderness.

Do you think people should be snorkeling in more retention ponds?

[Laughs] I don’t recommend that people do that! There are some retention ponds where you could encounter some nasty stuff.

Recent findings suggest that it was definitely a little bit of a stunt. But I also want to help people think about ecological ideas that they might not otherwise think of, while still enjoying the watch.

Another thing I want to get at is so many people responded like: “That’s gross, how could you do that?” But my question is why are we just totally okay with having these huge bodies of water that are gross and contaminated in our everyday lives? Clean water should be the norm!

Still, it’s a place that should be valued and thought of differently.

Do you feel like your own perception of urban ecology or urban environments changed after this?

I think so. That’s another reason why I love snorkeling: I feel like somehow each time I do it, it changes me a little bit. I didn’t know that much life was out there, and I didn’t know what a lot of those creatures were when I first got in. It deepens my personal connection with my local Home Depot in a way I hadn’t really thought would happen five years ago, but here we are.

Do you think you’ll do more urban snorkeling? I know you just went to the Dry Tortugas, which is a very different environment from Home Depot.

I think I am going to look into it. I’m not going to become the retention pond snorkeling guy; I hope to diversify my snorkeling more than that. But I definitely think this is an idea that resonates with people, and it gives me an opportunity to explore some interesting ecological and even philosophical concepts.

Sources indicate that I’ve got some other ideas that I’ve been waiting for the right time to film. There’s a place up in the Carolinas that’s a Frisbee golf course with a little creek running through it, and there are these amazing minnow spawning events that happen in the creek, so maybe one day I’ll be out there in the creek filming minnow spawning while people are playing Frisbee golf nearby.

What are you hoping people take away from the video?

One thing that I hope people take away from a lot of my videos is just a sense of wonder at what’s out there in the natural world, and maybe a broader definition of beauty. I think beauty can be found in unexpected places and from unexpected creatures. I think that was kind of a personal challenge I had for myself: Can I see something beautiful in a retention pond? And do I have the skill with my camera to present that so that maybe other people catch a little glimmer of it too? And I think it happened, which is really exciting.

But at the same time, I hope people realize how key it is that significant expansive habitats are protected and conserved too, and that it’s not just about the big animals like polar bears and manatees, which are kind of easy to care about, but also little rare invertebrates or spiders or snakes, all of which I believe have inherent value and are worth protecting.
