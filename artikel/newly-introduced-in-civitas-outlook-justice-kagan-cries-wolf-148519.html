📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

My latest essay in Civitas Outlook combines much of my recent thinking about Justice Kagan. She has shifted to different modalities of persuasion over her career on the bench. The latest front is to preemptively blame the Court for destroying democracy and then complain how the Court's legitimacy is being wounded. She is like the arsonist who seeks mercy for burning down her own house. The bigger problem is her predictions are simply wrong. Kagan has some serious crow to eat after the blue tsunami. An entire jurisprudence based on consequentialism fails if the consequences never happen.

Here is the introduction of Justice Kagan Cries Wolf:.

Justice Elena Kagan recently confessed, "I'm much better in print than I am in person." She's right. Through a series of carefully coordinated public appearances, Justice Kagan has attempted to influence how her colleagues vote and how the public perceives the Court. Worse still, Kagan made dire predictions about how the Court has wounded democracy and its own legitimacy. But in short order, the midterm elections refuted her consequentialism. Elena Kagan is the Justice who cries wolf. Justices should speak through their judicial writings, not try to distort the process from the sidelines. . . .

This sort of consequentialist judging carries a distinct risk: what if the consequences never manifest? In other words, a jurisprudence based on predicting certain outcomes fails if the predictions are wrong. Kagan did not just try to predict the outcome of the midterm election; she proclaimed she could divine the future of our republic. Alas, in short order, Justice Kagan has proven herself to be less Cook Report and more Miss Cleo.

Recent findings suggest that kagan's point seems to be that ruling against the President who appoints you is proof you're a fair justice. That premise holds only if the law does not support the appointing President's policy. If a Justice simply rules against the President who appoints him, then the Justice is no longer a judge. In his upcoming book So Ordered, Justice Alito chides so-called "insecure originalists" who favor outcomes that yield results they dislike to bolster a reputation of independence. Alito, "Twenty-first-century originalists should shed this insecure mindset." Yet Kagan intentionally feeds into this insecurity.

Justice Kagan, and all Justices, cannot predict the consequences of their decisions. Chief Justice Roberts can predict a decade-long "long game" no better than Kagan can predict the outcome of a single midterm election. They should stop trying to see into the future. Accordingly, the Justices should not be cowed into altering their rulings based on doomsday predictions of what might happen. As for Justice Kagan, the parable of the girl who cries wolf is on point. She can keep speaking, but we don't have to listen.

My point here echoes my point about the Chief Justice last summer. The conservatives should stop listening to colleagues who do not put the Constitution's best interest at heart.
