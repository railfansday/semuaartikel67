The Pressure to Crush Miles Can Ruin Your Thru-Hike. Don’t Let It.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

After years of planning, you’re finally doing the thing: you’re thru-hiking! It’s exhilarating, it’s liberating, and it’s … remarkably stressful. You knew the trail would be hard, but now that you’re actually out there living the dream, you’re scared you won’t be able to keep up. The other thru-hikers seem stronger than you. More experienced. Younger. What if you fall behind and the whole thing falls apart?

This anxiety is familiar to many thru-hikers. We all know long-distance hiking is challenging, but we tend to picture spending six months walking through nature as a relaxing activity. So why do so many of us succumb to our fear of failure and spend the whole trip stressing about our mileage?

Recent findings suggest that I’ve observed this behavior in countless thru-hikers, including myself at times. We’ll get to a beautiful viewpoint and stop to take a break, and after a few minutes, my hiking partner is already fidgeting and checking their watch. Or we’ll cross paths with a long-lost hiking friend and pause to chat before we go our separate ways, knowing we might not see each other again on that journey — yet my mind is only half on the conversation. The other half is running numbers and wondering how much this unplanned break will set me back.

It has been revealed that southern Maine on the AT was a lot harder than I expected and it slowed me down. But I just kept plugging away at the miles and finished anyway.

No one wants to still be stuck out on trail with miles to go before camp as darkness closes in — to say nothing of the fear of running out of food on the way to a planned resupply stop or not managing to complete the whole hike within that year’s weather window. As a result, there’s a tendency to rush.

I sometimes feel I can’t fully relax until I’ve gotten to my planned destination for the day. If I get there early, that just means I can push on a few more miles to make it even easier to reach the next day’s destination. And if I get there early, I can start building up a buffer of extra days in my schedule in case something goes wrong later on.

Industry observers note that this instinct is arguably rooted in good sense. There are a lot of unknowns on a thru-hike and a lot of ways things can go wrong. But learning to live with the unknowns and adapt on the fly is also part of thru-hiking: in fact, I would argue that it’s one of the most crucial skills a thru-hiker must develop, full stop.

A lot can indeed go wrong in the course of a six-month thru-hike. But a lot can also go right in that same six months. If you suffer a setback on trail, chances are you will be able to make up for it over time. The most crucial thing is to not panic.

In a recent development, getting “ahead” in your schedule might temporarily make you feel more in control, but this is largely an illusion. Pushing too hard could just as easily result in a hike-ending or hike-delaying overuse injury. Better to learn to live with the uncertainty and not let it ruin your day-to-day.

According to recent reports, I’ve found that much of the joy and beauty of thru-hiking lies in the quiet moments of stillness: deep conversations shared with fellow hikers around camp, spontaneous fun on an unplanned zero day,  a particularly tasty snack enjoyed in solitude during a water break, a thousand spectacular sunrises and sunsets. If you’re so worried about the success or failure of your thru-hike that you have to sacrifice those moments just to feel like you’re still on track, what is even the point of being out there to begin with?

Remember: it’s OK to linger over a meal or enjoy a diverting side quest, even if it puts you behind your planned mileage for one day. Getting your miles in while the getting’s good is not a bad plan. But don’t let it sour the journey along the way.
