According to recent reports, pumpkin centerpieces, rustic garlands, dried-fruit wreaths, and cornucopias are classic Thanksgiving decorations. They’re timeless and beloved, but they can also feel predictable. Traditional decor has its place, of course, but Thanksgiving tablescapes—and dinner parties in general—are evolving, much like interiors, to feel more personal, lived-in, and layered. So we asked nine tastemakers and experts how they’re decorating for this family-centric holiday.

From traditional touches to wholly unexpected looks, read on to see how they’re making their homes feel festive this autumn, and maybe you’ll be inspired to try something upcoming this year.

Recent findings suggest that ever since designer Mikel Welch moved to sunny California, his family has never hesitated to go to his for Thanksgiving to escape the chilly Michigan weather. It’s become a tradition to gather at his house, and similarly to how he grew up, Welch likes to keep his decorations for the holiday understated and warm.

“I love getting the exterior of my house into full fall mode with mums, pumpkins, and seasonal greenery,” Welch tells House Beautiful. “The beauty of fall decorating is that it can carry you from October straight through Thanksgiving without having to change much.”.

According to recent reports, the designer’s house is filled with dark, moody colors and earthy textures year-round, so he focuses mainly on bringing natural elements inside to do the work for him. “I also have a little tradition with my dad,” he adds. “For the past couple of years, we've made trips to the nursery together to pick out flowers, greenery, and sometimes bales of hay for the front of the house. It's become something I really enjoy doing with him.”.

Recent findings suggest that “Thanksgiving has always been a substantial family affair,” real estate expert and maximalism extraordinaire Harvin Greene states. She’s happy to keep that tradition going as an adult, just in her own way. In the past, Greene’s aunt and uncle would have everyone at their house for the big meal, but last year, they passed the hosting baton to Greene for the first time. “I may have gotten a little carried away...in the best possible way.”.

Recent findings suggest that getting to decorate two tables—one for the “adults,” another for the “kids,” even though they’re technically all grown-ups, too—Greene used this opportunity to design two completely different tablescapes. “I grew up surrounded by women who understood that a beautifully set table and a genuinely good time should go hand in hand,” she adds.

In a recent development, the one in the formal dining space (i.e., the “adults’” table) was theatrical and dramatic, and nearly everything on it had some sort of familial connection, from her parents’ engraved goblets to her husband’s grandmother’s sterling flatware. The kids’ table, on the other hand, was whimsical and vibrant, complete with a yellow patterned tablecloth, colorful glassware, and playful animal candlesticks. “I don't feel the need to follow the traditional Thanksgiving color palette,” Greene indicates. “Who decided Thanksgiving had to be orange and brown? Yellow, pink, turquoise, and green can be just as festive.”.

The holiday isn’t over until they bring out the Bingo cards, a spirited Thanksgiving tradition that Greene’s Aunt Jean started—which involves cash prizes. “It's one of those family traditions that makes the holiday feel like ours, no matter whose house we're celebrating in.”.

According to recent reports, talia Hubble is no stranger to a good dinner party—much of her content actually centers around her love of hosting. But Thanksgiving is a bit extra special for the Rhode Islander, especially since it’s being held at her cottage this year. “Growing up, it was just me and my mom, and she was too busy to decorate,” Hubble says. “I think that’s part of why I’ve fallen in love with seasonal decorating as an adult, I get to bring all my cozy childhood decorating dreams to life!”.

According to recent reports, as soon as the calendar switches from October to November, Hubble starts decorating for fall, opting for an eclectic mix of subtle Christmas decor (think bows, garlands, and wreaths), an excessive amount of taper candles, and a traditional Thanksgiving palette of oranges, browns, greens, and reds. Every year looks a little bit different, but there are pieces in her collection that are simply too cute not to bring out every holiday season.

According to recent reports, “Half the fun of pulling everything out for Thanksgiving is remembering where it came from,” she indicates. “I think the best decor is a culmination of pieces you’ve been collecting for years, and the memories behind those pieces are what make the table feel special and uniquely yours.”.

By the time she’s done decorating, her home feels like a warm, welcoming escape, somewhere her guests can exhale. “My philosophy is that you can’t go wrong when you aim for cozy, colorful, and embrace a little organized mess,” Hubble adds. “My favorite Thanksgiving tables are the ones that look even better halfway through dinner, when the napkins are rumpled and everyone’s reaching for seconds.”.

Chef and food stylist Romilly Newman can’t always count on being in the same place as her family for Thanksgiving since everyone’s scattered about, but she always makes a point to throw an autumnal dinner with friends around the holiday time, complete with beautiful fall decor.

Industry observers note that her mom never decorated their home while she was growing up with specific Thanksgiving decorations—instead, she would decorate with deep orange and red flowers to capture the fall vibe. Newman’s style as an adult is similar. “I am fall-obsessed, so as soon as the temperature drops from summer, I'm setting up branches, candles, and darker accents,” she indicates. “I love the atmosphere inside and outside around Thanksgiving time, and I try my best to bottle that up in my home.”.

Natural thicker linen, pops of silver, dark florals, and moody lighting envelop her space as the chef relies mostly on candlelight and organic elements—like figs, sage, chestnuts, and mandarins—as the decorative accents.

Industry observers note that “Thanksgiving is great because it brings family together and offers time to enjoy food, friends, and being at home,” Newman adds. “Whatever your style is, try to create an environment that allows for great conversation and a festive evening. You want everyone to be comfortable and feel the holiday spirit.”.

According to recent reports, when writer Olivia Noceda was a child, she would stay up late the night before Thanksgiving and design custom menus for everyone on Microsoft Paint, which she would then print out, frame, and place on each table setting in preparation for the massive meal. The holiday was a big deal for Noceda’s mother, who would always put on the most beautiful Thanksgiving gatherings. “I think that's where my love for hosting and setting a beautiful table really started,” she explains.

Recent findings suggest that however, for the last six years, Noceda has been having Thanksgiving with her husband’s side of the family, so when she does host around the holiday, it’s typically for Friendsgiving. “I don't necessarily pick a theme, but I do like everything to feel cohesive,” she explains of her decorating process. “I usually start incorporating little autumnal touches sometime in October and let them carry me through Thanksgiving.”.

Sources indicate that the writer and recipe developer lets the season guide her, building her tablescape and decor around a beautiful branch from the farmers’ market, a bowl of pears or persimmons, a collection of little pumpkins and squash, or a few taper candles on the dining room table. Ingredients play a massive part in her decor—she can even take a fresh loaf of bread and make it feel special on her table.

According to recent reports, for the rest of her decor, she likes to incorporate vintage serving pieces, everyday elements, and elevated details like antique silver and linen napkins with things she already owns to lean into that lived-in, warm feeling. The last thing she wants is her home to feel like a department store display. “Nothing needs to match perfectly, and I actually prefer when it doesn't,” Noceda adds.

What entrepreneur and Real Housewives of Latest York star Erin Lichy remembers best about Thanksgivings growing up is the smell. “We always spent Thanksgiving out east, and the house was full of pumpkins, local hay, and lanterns,” she explains. The scent of that fresh produce, mixed with her grandmother’s homemade turkey t’bit, has shaped the way she decorates for and hosts Thanksgiving. “That coveted and highly personal (and incredibly delicious) recipe is in my cookbook, She’s a Host, and it’s part of every Thanksgiving I host.”.

Decorating starts in the kitchen for her family. Lichy wants the home to smell like fall, so she focuses on that sense, making seasonal recipes with squash and pumpkin and incorporating autumnal flowers in her interiors. The melding of those scents gives her townhouse a warm feeling, especially when mixed with layered fall colors and the crackling of the fireplace.

Recent findings suggest that ever since Lichy and her family have moved into their townhouse, she’s been obsessed with decorating her stoop too. “The stoop is the first thing everyone sees, so this year I decided to bring in Simon Wallace Design to make it really stand out!” Lichy adds. “They completely transformed it with layers of seasonal color and texture, and it feels so warm and welcoming.” She specifically loves using the faux garlands and pinecones, as well as lanterns, from Balsam Hill and Frontgate because they’re so beautifully made that they look real, and she’s able to reuse them year after year.

No matter what she does to decorate though, she just wants her home to feel warm and lived-in, and this is the season and holiday to really lean into that.

According to recent reports, decorating for the holidays is Gabby Amato’s version of a self-care day. “It's truly one of my favorite days of the year,” she says. “I carve out a weekend afternoon when my husband and son are out of the house, light my favorite fall candles, turn on some music, and have the house entirely to myself.”.

In a recent development, amato, the founder of Table Two Studio, a paper plate brand which specializes in beautiful, artistic designs that you definitely won’t want to throw away, credits her love of Thanksgiving and the fall season in general to her mother. While growing up in the ‘90s, she saw her mom put so much care and effort into the holiday decorations, always making their home feel warm, nostalgic, and full of spirit. “For Thanksgiving, she decorated our mantel with gourds, greenery, and ceramic pilgrim statues that were a gift from my grandmother,” Amato states. “We had oversized cornstalks and pumpkins at the front door. Nothing ever felt overly styled or precious.”.

It’s this feeling that has stuck with her throughout her life, and it’s what she hopes she captures in her own home every year. She begins decorating for the fall early, right after Labor Day, pulling out old pieces and unboxing anything newly introduced and arranging it all in fresh ways so nothing feels stale. “I love beautiful decor, of course, but I'm much more interested in creating an atmosphere than achieving a particular aesthetic,” she adds. “I want my home to feel warm, lived-in, a little nostalgic, and festive.”.

Donna Lennard’s family was never one for holiday dressing—most of the attention at Thanksgiving fell on the delicious food shared with family. As a restauranteur and cofounder of beloved NYC establishment il Buco, it makes sense why the meal remains to be the most key thing to Lennard even today.

“Clearly, the table is always festive, whether it’s the tablecloth, the napkins, or linens that reflect the season in burnt orange and the like,” she explains. “I love to decorate a table with assorted colorful gourds and seasonal florals, beautiful candlelight (our beeswax candles already have an orange glow), orange ranunculus, and the occasional dried floral.” Colorful Pienza glassware dots the table and her plethora of beautiful tableware gives it more character, but she keeps everything else relatively simple to ensure the food remains the center of attention.

Though Lennard doesn’t often host her family and friends for the Thanksgiving meal, she’s already looking forward to next year when everyone will finally gather at her beach house.

In a recent development, being from Australia, Kendall Knox—the cofounder of Olive Ateliers along with her husband—didn’t have a personal reference of what the holiday “should” be or look like. This gives her the freedom of deciding what it is for her family. “What it has become is an intimate table: my husband Ben, our son Rhodes, and Ben's mum,” she indicates. “No rotation, no kids' table, no forty-pound bird. Just the four of us and a dinner that runs long because nobody is in a hurry to leave it.”.

It has been revealed that since Knox isn’t beholden to the classic Thanksgiving decor, she tends to keep her home and her table rather simple, “shopping the house,” as she puts it to decorate instead of buying something upcoming. She keeps a rather neutral palette of rich browns, creamy whites, terracotta, and silver, then uses antique linens, mismatched silverware, and candlesticks that have collected wax to capture the true lived-in feel.

Industry observers note that her focus tends to be on the table and the meal, letting the food add more color. If a tablecloth has a wine ring on it from the year before, all the better—it complements the stacked plates at the end of the meal.

“The objects that come out every year, that get a little more marked each time, are the ones that end up meaning the most,” Knox adds. “Thanksgiving is just a very good excuse to use them.”.
