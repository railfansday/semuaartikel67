📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Preliminary evidence shows self-reported brain fog decreases and executive function improves in perimenopausal women treated with ADHD medications.

Stimulant and non-stimulant medications used to treat ADHD significantly improve self-reported executive dysfunction in perimenopausal women without ADHD, according to preliminary evidence presented at the annual meeting of The Menopause Society last month in San Diego. (These findings have not yet been published in a peer-reviewed journal.)1.

Sources indicate that during the presentation titled “Brain Fog or ADHD? Executive Dysfunction in Perimenopause: A Scoping Review of the Role of ADHD Medications,” researchers discussed the effectiveness of two ADHD medications: lisdexamphetamine (brand name: Vyvanse), a stimulant, and atomoxetine, a non-stimulant. They studied the impact of these medications on cognition and ADHD symptom severity in women aged 45 to 60 years during the perimenopausal period. The systematic review included three crossover randomized controlled trials involving 60 patients and two cross-sectional studies of 6,048 patients from Iceland and the U.K.

Industry observers note that the study found that both lisdexamphetamine (LDX) and atomoxetine (ATX) significantly improve self-reported executive dysfunction, as measured by the Brown Attention Deficit Disorder Scale (BADDS), a self-report rating scale that measures ADHD symptoms, compared to placebo. The BADDS scores for LDX owners dropped from 35.7 to 21.2, compared to 29.8 with placebo. The BADDS scores for ATX users fell from 38.6 to 25.5, compared to 30.1 with placebo. Neuroimaging data from a subset of women taking LDX further supports these findings.

It has been revealed that during perimenopause, declining or fluctuating estrogen levels affect dopamine and norepinephrine signaling in the prefrontal cortex of the brain. As a result, many perimenopausal women experience “brain fog,” which is an umbrella term for forgetfulness, slow thinking, difficulty finding words, and reduced concentration, which can also be a symptom of ADHD. This overlap often leaves clinicians trying to discern whether a patient’s brain fog stems from hormonal fluctuations or undiagnosed ADHD, or both.

“Many women at perimenopause notice changes in focus, memory, and organization that look a lot like ADHD, and the two can be hard to tell apart,” said lead author Jennifer Tat, M.D. candidate and fourth-year medical student at the University of Tennessee Health Science Center in Memphis.

According to recent reports, in an ADDitude survey of more than 5,000 women with diagnosed and undiagnosed ADHD, 94% of survey respondents noted their ADHD symptoms grew more severe during perimenopause and menopause. During this stage, respondents said the most impactful ADHD symptoms were brain fog or memory problems, and overwhelm, both of which 70% of women said had a “life-altering impact” in their 40s and 50s.

According to recent reports, “Brain fog became unmanageable,” wrote one 45-year-old mother of two who entered perimenopause at age 41. “Distractibility increased and my inability to complete work tasks in an appropriate amount of time decreased. I began spending all night working just to keep up. Things that I used to have unconscious strategies to deal with were no longer manageable.”.

“I never experienced rage or brain fog before perimenopause,” wrote one woman. “Overwhelm has been more frequent in recent years, and depression began in my 40s. I definitely attribute some of this to hormonal changes.”.

Recent findings suggest that “In perimenopause, the brain fog and memory, inattention, distractibility, perfectionism, hyperactivity, and restlessness problems become progressively worse,” wrote a 52-year-old woman diagnosed with ADHD at age 50. “I have always had these symptoms, but I was able to manage them with systems that I created for myself. My ability to manage them has declined significantly, and this makes these symptoms seem like they are the worst they have ever been.”.

While preliminary evidence from the systematic review found ADHD medications improve subjective executive function, the medications had little to no significant impact on objective cognitive measures, according to tests measuring working memory and sustained attention.

Prior research suggests otherwise.

In the ADDitude article “Could ADHD Medications Improve Memory, Focus & Organization in Menopausal Women? The Research Claims Yes,” the late Thomas E. Brown, Ph.D., described how his research with C. Neill Epperson, M.D., found ADHD medications alleviated cognitive problems resembling ADHD symptoms in menopausal women without ADHD.

In a recent development, the series of three studies measured symptoms with BADDS, which Brown developed. The first study found that ATX treatment significantly improved BADDS scores for working memory; focus scores improved in BADDS scores for women treated with ATX.2 In the second study, executive functions improved, including an objective measure of short-term working memory, in 41% of menopausal women taking 20 to 60 mg LDX daily, compared to 17% for the women on the placebo.3 The third study examined the effects of LDX on the brain functioning of women with no history of ADHD, but with reported cognitive difficulties with working memory, organization, focus, and attention that had begun during menopause.4.

According to recent reports, “As predicted, neuroimaging data showed that LDX activated executive networks in specific areas of the brain,” Brown wrote. “Those data also showed that LDX’s effect on specific brain regions was associated with improved BADDS scores overall, and with BADDS scores for activation and alertness/effort. Imaging data indicated that improvements in brain activation were significantly larger when women were on LDX than when they were on placebo.”.

The studies were published in peer-reviewed medical journals in 2011, 2015, and 2017; however, Brown wrote in 2021, “results of that research have not yet been widely recognized.”.

There is currently no FDA-approved pharmacological intervention for executive dysfunction and brain fog during perimenopause or menopause. Research on the most effective treatments for ADHD during perimenopause is limited as well. That stated, Patricia Quinn, M.D., explained during a 2026 ADDitude webinar that available data and observational evidence suggest that many women could benefit from estrogen supplementation and increased stimulant dosages during this phase.

“Perimenopause is when you need the most help, the most support,” Quinn said in her webinar, “Perimenopause in Women with ADHD.” “Estrogen… makes your stimulant more effective.”.

One roadblock to care is the fact that many healthcare professionals are ill-equipped to provide responsive, coordinated, evidence-based treatment and support to help women navigate menopause, according to a study published in The Lancet Obstetrics, Gynecology, & Women’s Health. The 2026 study cited inadequate medical training, paltry research, and persistent gaps in knowledge as reasons for the “fragmented care.”5.

Combined, these preliminary findings could open the door to a nonhormone therapeutic option for addressing perimenopausal executive dysfunction, according to researcher Tat.

“Our review found early signs that ADHD medications may help women feel more on top of their daily lives,” Tat said. “While it’s too early to recommend these medications, a quick screen for ADHD symptoms is a simple step that could help women get the right care.”.
