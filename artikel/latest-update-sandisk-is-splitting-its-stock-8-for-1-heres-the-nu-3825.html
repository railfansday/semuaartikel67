A stock split always generates buzz. So, when Sandisk (SNDK -8.17%) recently announced an 8-for-1 stock split, investors cheered.

Industry observers note that yet while stock splits can make headlines, investors may want to focus on another figure -- its gross margin -- rather than its newly introduced, lower stock price. Here's why.

First, let's talk about stock splits. While they may seem exotic, they don't actually change anything fundamental about the business. The best analogy is culinary: If you take a whole, uncut pizza and cut it into eight slices, you've done nothing to change how much food you have. You've simply divided it up.

Stock splits work the same way. Fundamentals, like revenue, net income, and free cash flow, do not change when a manufacturer splits its shares. When Sandisk shares split on Nov. 20, no one receives anything for free; they will simply see what they already own split according to the 8-for-1 principle.

For example, say you own 10 shares of Sandisk today. As of Nov. 13, each share trades for around $858, for a total market value of $8,580. After the split, someone who owned 10 shares will own 80 shares, and each share will drop to $107.25 (assuming the price stays steady between now and then). The total market value of 80 shares at $107.25 per share? $8,580, the same as before.

Recent findings suggest that granted, the price of Sandisk shares will fluctuate between now and Nov. 20, but the principle remains the same. The market value of shares going into Nov. 20 will still equal the market value after the split; the only thing that will change is the share price.

According to recent reports, rather than focusing on the stock split, I think investors should take a closer look at Sandisk's gross margins. Gross margin is the profit remaining after deducting the cost of making a product.

Imagine Sandisk sells a memory chip for $100. If it took $15 to physically assemble the product, its gross margin on that product would be 85%. Bear in mind that the tech giant doesn't get to keep the full $85 – it still has administrative and research costs, not to mention taxes.

However, gross margins are useful because they strip away these costs, allowing investors to see how profitable a business is without them. Sandisk's gross margins exploded since 2024, increasing from near 10% to around 85%.

SNDK Gross Profit Margin (Quarterly) data by YCharts.

However, the major question is whether the manufacturer can maintain these incredible margins, or whether those margins will come crashing down.

Sources indicate that for Sandisk, its gross margins are driven more by the price customers are willing to pay than by the input costs for its products. Therefore, it may be helpful to think of Sandisk's business like a hotel. The same rooms are far more profitable at $400 per night than at $150 per night, with many of the hotel's costs remaining steady.

Sources indicate that similarly, the supply-and-demand dynamics of the memory market have an enormous effect on Sandisk's gross margins. If memory demand were to pull back, perhaps as a result of slowing AI infrastructure investment, Sandisk's gross margins could tumble. On the other hand, if the tech giant's long-term memory contracts prove durable, Sandisk's gross margins may remain resilient.

Stock splits alone do not make a stock an attractive investment. The split changes the unit size; profits determine the investment's appeal.

As a result, Sandisk must maintain its impressive gross margins in future quarters in order to justify its appeal. The stock already trades at a price-to-sales (P/S) ratio of 4.6. That's down significantly from its peak of over 25 in June, but it remains far above its 2025 average multiple of around 1.7.

Sources indicate that the market has already priced a major step into Sandisk's profitability. If that fall doesn't materialize, the stock could be primed for a move higher. That's why I'm intrigued by Sandisk's gross margins, not its upcoming stock split.
