Playing on school computers has been a rite of passage for many young PC gamers, whether through dedicated edutainment titles or rogue installations by chancing pupils. But fantasy novelist Scott Lynch went one step further: he took the school computers home.

It has been revealed that "My mother worked for our school district, so I'd occasionally have the chance to sneak into the computer lab, or we would borrow and Apple II over the summer," Lynch says. "When we did that, I borrowed Infocom games … text-only adventure games from the '80s like Zork, Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy, Planetfall, A Mind Forever voyaging. I was really into those when I was about 12.".

Industry observers note that text-based adventures would eventually become a defining feature of Lynch's life, albeit in a different format. His debut novel, The Lies of Locke Lamora, was published in 2006, charting the schemes and adventures of a group of master con artists called The Gentleman Bastards. Two further novels in The Gentleman Bastard Sequence have since followed: 2007's Red Seas Under Red Skies, and 2013's The Republic of Thieves.

In a recent development, lynch presently working on a novella in the same world called More Than Fools Fill Graves, as well as his highly anticipated fourth novel, The Thorn of Emberlain. "It is something that I am absolutely working on and trying to be as open and honest about as possible," he indicates. But he took a quick break from writing elaborate fantasy heists to show me around the treasure-filled vault of his Steam library.

I have reopened Morrowind for the first time in a number of years. Right around the time I was working on my first novel and had not finished it yet, I was playing it on the original Xbox. Other than the fact I was like 23, 24, I can't fathom how I did that.

I've been meaning to go back to Morrowind for ages, but I was put off by the jankiness of the UI, the quality-of-life experience, or the thought that there were just enough rough edges of this game that's now nearly a quarter of a century old, that I wouldn't enjoy it.

Industry observers note that I was talking to another author who maybe has a bad computer game habit, and he turned me on to OpenMW. He posted some screenshots on Bluesky of some beautiful-looking Morrowind vistas and some nice skies and vegetation. And I thought "A-ha! Here's a man that uses mods." So even though I own a copy of Morrowind, like the fourth copy I've bought in my life, I'm now using OpenMW, and I'm loving it.

The thing that I enjoy about Morrowind is the incredible granularity of its various subsystems and the game-breaking things that you can do with processes like alchemy and enchantment. And the fact you can enchant layers of clothing and be wearing enchanted underwear underneath your enchanted armour. [But] I set a bunch of rules for my character, because usually I play something along the lines of a wizard who can go anywhere, do anything, build high-powered potions to fly all over the landscape at high speed.

I decided that I would play something along the template of a Paladin, and I gave myself a very limited powerset that I could create utilising the custom spell-builders … so I have a very limited toolset, and I opted to spend more time actually wandering around the world doing quests, and less time sitting in one place making 10,000 potions.

In a recent development, I played Skyrim to death [with] many character concepts, and one of my favourites was a little ruleset that I called the Conan playthrough, where you play as melee-type character who cannot use magic at all … you place all these rules and limitations upon yourself. What I found is that narrowing the focus of the game experience can be as liberating as having a completely open world before you, and I've rediscovered that with this particular Morrowind playthrough.

In a recent development, it's a game from the late '90s called Star Wars: Rebellion, which over in the UK would have been known as Star Wars: Supremacy. It's elderly, it's janky, its UI and its quality of life are from a previous era of human development. It's absolutely full of problems. But it's also got kernels of brilliance, and there's never been anything like it ever since. As a Star Wars fan, I find myself returning to it over and over, as a dog to his vomit.

According to recent reports, I've still got Master of Orion 2, and it's still playable, which is from '90s. I love MOO2. I've played it to death in every possible permutation. I last played Master of Orion about five years ago. That would have also been in the pandemic.

You can see how nearly every game in the 4X space has been trying for the last three decades to, in some fashion, be that game and recreate its experience, and that's a very difficult thing to do … MOO2 just has a certain smoothness to it.

I've tried to get into other 4X games … but I just don't think that any 4X space game since MOO2 has successfully emulated that smoothness of pickup and that open sense of play, as in "Where's my civilization going to go?".

According to recent reports, to a certain extent when you play a game like MOO2, outside the bounds of creating a custom species and a custom culture, you're playing with somebody else's IP, and when I play a game like that, I look for a certain degree of possessiveness, a certain degree of personalisation. I want to play my species' story and I don't necessarily want to play with somebody else's narrative IP.

Sources indicate that all right, let's look at the embarrassing total. All I'm going to say about this is that if J.R.R Tolkien had had a Steam account, his numbers would be pretty embarrassing too.

In a recent development, I've got a couple of suspects. Star Wars: Rebellion, keeping in mind, this is since 2012, because that's when I noticed that it was on Steam and I went 'Oh, cool.' At 1,493 hours. That'll be hard to top. Fallout: Latest Vegas, only 630 hours, but that's also a bit of a lie because I had that game on two different Xbox 360s.

It has been revealed that but the winner is definitely BattleTech. I've got a truly embarrassing 1,564 hours. I spent a lot of time commanding a BattleTech company during the pandemic.

I have played this so much. I have experienced every bark, every random event, every edge and corner, I have played it to a point beyond which it was designed to be played … After you've played a game for 1,500 hours, you see problems that would not be visible to a normal human being.

It is janky. It is imperfect. It is a notorious memory leak rat bastard that can hang and slow down forever. But it is a marvellous, diverting game that does exactly what it promised, designed by people who really love the setting and really love the playstyle, with just marvellous production design. It really returns that feel of being 12 or 13, poring over old BattleTech manuals and wishing I had somebody to play the game with who was as obsessive as me.

Industry observers note that the whole '80s, early '90s mecha genre has always been a huge fascination for me. When I was 12 or 13, I saw [BattleTech]. I'm in a gaming store and I see this game that has the Robotech Mecha … I could not resist it. I've just retained a love of that. It's not a deep philosophical thing. One just loves to see giant robots slugging the hell out of each other with lasers and so forth.

The fun thing about BattleTech as a setting, and I'll put my writer hat on for a moment and attempt to recover some of my street cred—"What are your influences? Oh, Dostoevsky, Atwood, BattleTech." BattleTech's secret weapon is that it is a game universe that is full of holes and mysteries. It's a period of ongoing civil war after the collapse of the Star League, the great hegemony across the known galaxy, and so that means there are vanished cultures, vanished planets, vanished fleets, just entire holes in the column of historical knowledge where all sorts of things can be hiding.

In a recent development, so some of the fun of the BattleTech lore is that it's not for perfecting. It's not for memorising. It is inherently a major dark mystery that fills the entire galaxy.

Oh God, I am going to play Star Wars: Rebellion until the heat death of the universe. I've just accepted that now. I'm never going to uninstall Fallout: Newly introduced Vegas for as long as it's allowed.

Recent findings suggest that there was something else …. OK, Knights of the Old Republic. I'm not sure I will ever play it again, but it's a game I am deeply attached to because it is superbly written, beautiful production values, loads of fun, and a very good adaptation of the then-current Star Wars tabletop system.

It has been revealed that it came along just at the right time, because this was right after Attack of the Clones. I am a lifelong Star Wars fan. The original three movies might as well be tattooed on the back of my eyelids. But there was a time around 2001, 2002 when that love really needed some buttressing.

Recent findings suggest that all of a sudden, here comes Knights of the Old Republic, which is the full package. Sweeping emotion, great story, wonderful characters, full of details, beautiful soundtrack. It just restored that sense of 'OK, I know why I'm a Star Wars fan'.

If forced to remove that from my library, I would do it with trembling hands.
