Tech Report: Judge orders Kalshi to geofence four Upcoming Mexico tribes, clashing with CFTC access rule

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A federal judge in New Mexico has blocked Kalshi's sports-event contracts on the lands of four tribes and ruled that the Commodity Exchange Act does not override tribal gaming law. The order requires geographic restrictions just as the CFTC is telling exchanges to drop them.

Kalshi has lost a key round against four New Mexico tribes. On November 23, U.S. District Judge Margaret Strickland granted the Mescalero Apache Tribe and the Pueblos of Isleta, Pojoaque and Sandia a preliminary injunction that gives Kalshi 14 days to stop offering sports-event contracts on their lands. Strickland also denied its motion to dismiss, so the lawsuit now goes forward.

In a recent development, the tribes filed suit in May. They argue that Kalshi’s sports contracts violate the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act (IGRA) and the tribes’ gaming compacts with New Mexico. In their view, the contracts allow unauthorized wagering on tribal lands. Under IGRA, only tribes can authorize gaming on Indian lands.

According to recent reports, kalshi argued that the Commodity Exchange Act overrides both IGRA and the tribal-state compacts. Strickland disagreed. In her reading, that statute leaves the federal regulator without sole authority over the contracts, Tribal Business News reported. She also rejected Kalshi’s claim that IGRA’s injunction provision only allows suits against parties to a tribal-state compact.

In a recent development, location was central to the ruling. To Strickland, a Kalshi user taking a sports position from a reservation is like a casino patron placing a bet. In her reasoning, the wager takes place wherever the bettor is physically located. The order cites a declaration from a Mescalero Apache Tribal Gaming Commission agent. She opened Kalshi’s app at several places on the reservation and found sports contracts on offer.

Kalshi cited the CFTC’s impartial-access requirements in opposing the injunction. Strickland ruled that the harm to tribal sovereignty outweighed the compliance burden. She also noted that Kalshi already uses geofencing elsewhere.

According to recent reports, that reasoning runs straight into the CFTC’s latest directive on Regulation 38.151(b). The interpretation was published in the Federal Register on November 10, 2026, as document 2026-22863. The exchange therefore faces a court order to geofence alongside a regulatory push to remove such limits.

The legal theory also matters beyond Newly introduced Mexico. The tribes rely on IGRA and their compacts, not state gambling law. As a result, CFTC preemption arguments may not protect Kalshi on tribal lands. If other tribes copy the approach, prediction-market operators could face geofencing obligations on reservations nationwide.

Recent findings suggest that tribal results have been mixed so far. Earlier in 2026, a federal judge in Wisconsin refused a similar injunction sought by the Ho-Chunk Nation. He did so even though he found the tribe likely to succeed on the merits. In California, District Judge Jacqueline Scott Corley granted two tribes a preliminary injunction after a Ninth Circuit ruling in September. It was the first injunction any tribe had won against Kalshi.

The timing leaves little room. The CFTC’s deadline for exchanges to drop domestic geographic restrictions lands only days before Kalshi must comply in Latest Mexico, SBC Americas reported.
