Dig deep enough, and the temperature of the ground remains steady year round, a fun trivia fact that has the potential to improve efficiency and slash costs for heating and cooling across the country.

Geothermal heat pumps, which use the ground as a heat source in winter and heat sink in summer, have been around for decades, but they’re underutilized. Just a fraction of all buildings in the U.S. use them in part because it’s expensive to drill into the earth. But one startup, Bedrock Energy, claims its AI-directed rigs can slash drilling times by up to 80%, significantly reducing costs.

In a recent development, that time savings recently helped Bedrock raise a latest $24 million funding round, the startup told TechCrunch. Previously, it raised a $12 million Series A in 2025 and an $8.5 million seed round in 2023.

Bedrock has focused mostly on commercial properties to date, since they benefit from deep, accurate boreholes which maximize the use of their small footprints. But the startup has also been talking with data center developers, Joselyn Lai, co-founder and CEO of Bedrock Energy, told TechCrunch. Some might use it for cooling server racks, but others are interested in the technology’s flat load profile — deploying it on neighboring properties can open up significant capacity on the grid.

“We had someone in Michigan say, if we could free up a few megawatts, I think we might be able to operate that many months or even a year earlier,” Lai said. “A year of revenue for a data center is very meaningful.”.

Sources indicate that bedrock says its modernized approach to shallow geothermal drilling — up to 1,000 feet or so — sets it apart. Most geothermal heat pump drillers use rigs that are similar to those used for water wells. They work fine, but they’re crude instruments.

According to recent reports, instead, Bedrock uses directional drilling techniques borrowed from the oil and gas industry, guiding them using its own models of the subsurface. That combination allows them to optimize drilling and placement, the organization states, helping to ensure performance and longevity. Sensors in the boreholes help to refine those models.

The startup at the moment operates in Utah, Colorado, Upcoming York, Alberta, and its home state of Texas, and the new round will help the tech giant expand into New England, New Jersey, the upper Midwest, and additional states in the West, Lai remarked.

Sources indicate that to power that expansion, Bedrock will add more rigs to its fleet to serve customers in those regions. Presently, it has drilled boreholes in nine sites, and it has secured payments from new customers in anticipation of drilling in the next year or two, she added.

According to recent reports, once up and running, a geothermal heat pump requires far less maintenance than traditional HVAC systems. “You only have to install it once and then it works for a very long time, which is very good for infrastructure from a financial perspective,” Lai stated.

The fresh round was led by Clean Energy Ventures and Clearvision Ventures, with participation from GVP Climate, Susquehanna Sustainable Investments, Catalytic Capital for Climate and Health by Temasek Trust, MUUS, Great Wave Ventures, Autodesk Foundation, Titanium Ventures, Energy Impact Partners, Wireframe Ventures, Elemental Impact, and SFV Capital.
