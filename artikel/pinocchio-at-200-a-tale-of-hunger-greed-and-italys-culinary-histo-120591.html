Pinocchio at 200: a tale of hunger, greed and Italy's culinary history

Thirty-five red mullet with tomato sauce, four portions of tripe Parmesan-style and, because the seasoning was not quite satisfactory, three further requests for butter and cheese: this is the Cat's rather frugal dinner at the Red Lobster Inn, justified by a serious stomach complaint.

The Fox, evidently in better health, orders a "very light side dish of fattened pullets and young cockerels", followed by a cibreino made with partridges, grey partridges, rabbits and frogs.

Recent findings suggest that pinocchio, who is supposed to be the guest of honour, makes do with "a quarter of a walnut and a crust of bread". Naturally, he will be the one left to pay the bill.

It would be hard to find a more revealing menu with which to describe 19th-century Italy — and perhaps not just that period.

In the journey of the puppet who longs to become a real boy, growing up, taking responsibility and learning the difficult art of freedom are all shaped by encounters such as these. Learning how the world works also means discovering that, while everyone gets hungry, not everyone has the same chance to eat their fill.

Meanwhile, the ingredients, dishes and regional references scattered throughout The Adventures of Pinocchio make it one of the most unexpected culinary catalogues in Italian literature.

The cibreino, for a start, deserves a closer look. A traditional Tuscan dish, it is a rich preparation of chicken giblets, combs, livers and other offal, bound together with egg yolk and lemon.

It belongs to a culinary tradition capable of transforming the less prized parts of an animal into a refined dish. The Fox orders a decidedly fanciful version, packed with game and small animals, almost as if to demonstrate that even gluttony requires a certain gastronomic imagination.

The Cat's red mullet, served with tomato sauce, takes us instead towards the Tyrrhenian coast and the cooking of Livorno.

Sources indicate that fish appears again later, when the Green Fisherman prepares to fry Pinocchio alongside hake, grey mullet, sole and anchovies. First he coats them in flour, then drops them into boiling oil: an impeccable procedure, at least from a culinary point of view.

He even offers the puppet a choice: "Would you like to be fried in a pan, or would you prefer to be cooked in a saucepan with tomato sauce?".

Carlo Collodi, born Carlo Lorenzini in Florence on 24 November 1826, must have known a thing or two about food.

His father, Domenico, worked as a cook for the Ginori marquises, giving the future writer an insight into the world of kitchens and pantries that separated aristocratic dining rooms from the tables of ordinary people.

Recent findings suggest that A patriot, journalist and satirical writer, Collodi also possessed a particular talent for observing human weaknesses. After all, people's characters reveal themselves with remarkable honesty around the dinner table.

In 1883, when The Adventures of Pinocchio was published as a book, Pellegrino Artusi had yet to publish Science in the Kitchen and the Art of Eating Well, which would appear in 1891.

The two books emerged just a few years apart, in the same city, Florence, and seem to tell, each in its own way, the story of the difficult construction of a national identity.

Artusi brought together regional recipes and traditions in a shared language; Collodi sent a puppet travelling through an Italy still deeply divided by economic circumstances and everyday habits.

The novel features tortellini from Bologna, Milanese risotto, Neapolitan macaroni, roast pigeons, chicken breasts and even capon galantine.

The last of these, in Artusi's cookbook, is a small feat of culinary engineering: the bird is deboned, stuffed, cooked in broth and served cold, often accompanied by aspic.

Recent findings suggest that it is a dish requiring time, money and familiarity with the kitchens of grand houses. Nothing could be further removed from Geppetto's humble home.

There, the menu changes radically.

Sources indicate that breakfast consists of three pears, which the carpenter has saved for himself but gives to his hungry son. Pinocchio insists that they be peeled, only to devour the skins and cores as well.

It is one of the book's most delicious scenes: what the puppet initially rejects out of childish whim suddenly becomes desirable when hunger takes hold.

And hunger accompanies Pinocchio from his earliest adventures.

When he finds an egg, he imagines all the ways he might prepare it: as an omelette, cooked on a plate or in a little frying pan, sunny-side up or even swallowed raw. In just a few moments, Collodi offers a miniature treatise on cooking eggs.

But as soon as Pinocchio breaks the shell, a chick emerges, politely says goodbye and walks away.

Food multiplies in his imagination, promises happiness and then disappears.

Consider the wonders prepared by the Blue Fairy.

To celebrate the puppet's newly introduced diligence, 200 cups of milky coffee and 400 bread rolls buttered inside and out are laid out.

Elsewhere, there are candied fruits, cakes, panettone, almond sweets, large wafers with cream, sweet liqueurs and alchermes, the traditional Italian red liqueur.

A child's imagination transforms the world into an enormous confectioner's shop.

But the Fairy also understands the value of a simple vegetable. In the Land of the Busy Bees, where everyone works, she offers Pinocchio a plain plate of cauliflower dressed with oil and vinegar.

The novel constantly reverses the relationship between those who eat and those who are eaten.

The enormous whale swallows Geppetto and his son, but inadvertently provides the carpenter with a stock of provisions that allows him to survive.

Tinned meat, ship's biscuits, wine, raisins, cheese, coffee and sugar all come from a vessel swallowed by the monster.

Inside the creature's belly survives an entire collection of 19th-century food-preservation techniques, while the modern world enters the fairy tale through the supplies of a ship.

On the bicentenary of Collodi's birth, rereading Pinocchio through food means rediscovering an Italy — indeed, a world — in which capon galantine and a pot of beans belonged to vastly different lives, and a child might know the names of countless dishes without ever having tasted a single one.

At the Red Lobster Inn, after all, the Cat and the Fox have already grasped a rule that, unfortunately, still works remarkably well: sometimes, all you need to eat your fill is to find someone willing to pick up the bill.
