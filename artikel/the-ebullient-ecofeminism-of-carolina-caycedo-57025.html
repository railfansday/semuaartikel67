The Ebullient Ecofeminism of Carolina Caycedo

ST LOUIS, MO — A pink lake under an avalanche of digital detritus; a neon rainbow behind a man in a hazmat suit; a gilt sky above a lone wind turbine. These are among the surreal, meticulously rendered scenes in Carolina Caycedo’s series Mineral Intensive (2023–ongoing).

Caycedo’s candy-colored drawings lure the eye only to disquiet the mind, exposing the contradictions of so-called clean energy, whose production often depends on environmentally destructive mineral extraction.

Such visual beguilement — and ecological exigency — anchors Carolina Caycedo: Growing Deep Roots, an exhibition of sculpture, video, printmaking, and drawing that explores the joyous, multisensory dimensions of the artist’s activism.

Born and raised in Bogotá, Colombia, the Los Angeles-based artist devoted two years to collaborative research with botanists, plant taxonomists, and Native elders, informing her rendering of prairie and botanical life across the Mississippi River watershed. By turns bright and delicate, Caycedo’s Plant Portraits series (2021–ongoing) lends an ecofeminist bent to the larger show, merging feminine anatomy with botanical illustration.

Detail of Carolina Caycedo, "Neodymium Intensive" (2026), colored pencil, aluminum and variegated metal leaf on paper (left); Installation view of "Celestial Autonomy, the Dance of Significant Dipper and North Star" (2023) at Carolina Caycedo: Growing Deep Roots at the Kemper Art Museum.

Sources indicate that like a heavenly necklace, “Celestial Autonomy, the Dance of Massive Dipper and North Star” (2023) dangles in the center of the gallery. Constructed of Alaskan nets, buoys, and other fishing equipment — its title honors the indigenous communities in the region.

Across the Mississippi River, another sculptural installation, “Fiesta de la Raíces” (2026), vivifies the National Building Arts Center in Sauget, Illinois. A three-dimensional “portrait” of the area’s root systems, the sculpture resembles a shaggy chandelier of rope, beads, and natural fibers — beckoning admirers to bask in its festive opulence.

Sources indicate that at a time when ecological disaster feels ever imminent, art attuned to earthly precarity can often feel didactic or downright drab. Caycedo insists on an alternate vision: Humanity in sync with a resplendent planet — the ground beneath us trembling with riches, the sky adorned with unseen stars.

Carolina Caycedo: Growing Deep Roots continues at the Kemper Art Museum at Washington University in St. Louis (One Brookings Drive, St. Louis, Missouri) through January 4, and at the National Building Arts Center (NBAC) (2300 Falling Springs Road, Sauget, Illinois) through December 12. The exhibition was curated by Meredith Malone and James McAnally.
