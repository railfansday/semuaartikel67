5 proposed changes would stretch the asylum work permit wait to 545 days

🌐 Source: Qz • 📅 25 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 7 min read (1315 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways
  • A DHS proposal would add a 365-day filing wait and a 180-day review.
  • For an asylum seeker in the U.S., the wait for a decision on a claim can be long, and a work permit decides whether that wait comes with a paycheck.
  • Industry observers note that holding on to a permit has also become harder.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A DHS proposal would add a 365-day filing wait and a 180-day review. Here are 5 changes that could delay asylum seekers' work permits.

For an asylum seeker in the U.S., the wait for a decision on a claim can be long, and a work permit decides whether that wait comes with a paycheck. The permit lets applicants earn a living lawfully while their claims are pending, and the government was falling behind on those permits well before anyone proposed latest rules. The stack of undecided asylum work-permit requests grew fast in a single year, reaching 136,932 cases in September 2025 against 75,982 the September before, according to TRAC. Any new deadline has to be read against that pile of unfinished cases. A rule can promise a schedule, but the real schedule depends on how promptly the government works through its backlog.

Industry observers note that holding on to a permit has also become harder. The Chicago Sun-Times reports that many people renewing a permit lost an notable protection in October 2025, when a latest rule did away with automatic extensions that had allowed them to keep working for as long as 540 days during a review. As many as 100,000 Illinois residents could lose work because of that rule, the office of Illinois Attorney General Kwame Raoul estimates. "Immigrant workers with legal immigration status are falling out of the authorized workforce," Conchita Cruz, co-executive director of the Asylum Seeker Advocacy Project, told the Chicago Sun-Times. The new proposal for asylum work permits would tighten a system that is already losing authorized workers.

El Cronista laid out how a Department of Homeland Security proposal would lengthen asylum seekers' wait for a work permit, through changes that range from case-by-case denials to tougher biometrics rules and a possible freeze on upcoming applications. Some would write today's delays into the rules, while others would add burdens that no backlog explains.

According to recent reports, the first hurdle an asylum seeker would meet under the proposal is a date on the calendar, the day they are allowed to ask for a work permit at all. Applicants can now file after 150 days, and under the proposal they would have to wait a full year, according to El Cronista. The review, the decision and every piece of paperwork can begin only after an application is filed, so moving that date back pushes the whole process far later than it runs today.

The proposed rule itself sets this first stretch of the wait, and the speed of the agency's work doesn't change it. Clearing every pending case would shorten the review that comes later, but an asylum seeker would still have to sit out the full 365 days before filing. The change applies specifically to the asylum-based work permit, filed on Form I-765 under category (c)(8). A longer wait to file means a longer stretch with no legal paycheck, written into the rules before any office handles the case.

For an asylum seeker, the year-long wait to file would be the first and fixed part of every delay that follows.

Industry observers note that under the proposal, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services would have up to 180 days to decide an asylum seeker's work-permit application, up from the 30 days the current rule allows, according to El Cronista. Added to the year an applicant would have to wait before filing, the longer review brings the total to 545 days, about 18 months, before a permit can arrive. The 30-day deadline it would replace has applied to asylum work permits since December 1994, according to TRAC.

For many applicants, the 30-day rule had already stopped describing how long a decision takes. TRAC reports that under half of asylum work permits were completed within 60 days at the end of fiscal 2025. That is the strongest argument for the change, because a deadline closer to the agency's real pace gives applicants a more accurate idea of when to expect an answer. The proposed rule gives no reason for choosing 180 days over any other period, though, and additional vetting could add time to the 545-day total, which makes that total a minimum for many applicants rather than a promise.

Every change an asylum seeker has faced up to this point concerns time, and this one concerns the outcome. An applicant who sits out the full wait would no longer receive a permit by default, because approval would become a case-by-case discretionary decision with new grounds for refusal such as criminal history, late filing or unlawful entry, according to El Cronista. The agency would control the verdict as well as the calendar. For an asylum seeker, the risk is no longer a late paycheck but no paycheck from legal work at all after more than a year of waiting.

Recent findings suggest that applicants also couldn't measure their own risk against a fixed set of rules. Criminal history, late filing and unlawful entry are examples of the new bars rather than a complete list, and a person who clears all three still couldn't assume they qualify. The unlawful-entry bar depends on how a person arrived in the U.S., and no later filing can change that. An applicant who entered that way would bring a ground for refusal into the officer's review, however carefully they prepare the paperwork. This burden doesn't come from a slow office or a growing backlog. It is a new point of decision that the rules themselves would create.

It has been revealed that under this change, the government could stop accepting new asylum-based work-permit applications altogether, and the long delays that could set off that pause already exist. El Cronista reports that new applications could be halted once asylum processing averages more than 180 days for 90 consecutive days, and that current backlogs already run well past 180 days. The slower the government handles asylum cases, the less chance a newcomer would have to ask to work legally at all. The measure is still a proposal, and no pause is in force today.

Sources indicate that the pause would fall on people who are seeking an asylum-based work permit for the first time. It concerns initial applications, and the proposal as described doesn't say whether renewals would also be halted. People who already hold a permit therefore can't assume they are outside its reach, and that matters to workers whose jobs depend on a permit that will eventually need renewing. For a first-time applicant, the stakes are more basic. The 545-day count begins only once a person is allowed to file, and any time spent waiting out a pause would come on top of that total.

For some applicants, the longest wait could come before the count even begins.

Under this change, an asylum seeker who waits out every delay would still face an officer's judgment at the end.

The proposal would also set stricter rules for how asylum seekers file their work-permit applications and for the biometrics they provide, according to El Cronista. Most of the other changes concern how long an applicant waits, and this one concerns the application itself. Nobody filing today faces these rules, because the measure is only a proposal, and until the rule is final, no one can say exactly how strict the requirements will be.

It has been revealed that delays at the agency play no part in this change. Hiring more officers or clearing old cases would not make the filing and biometrics requirements any lighter, because they concern what an applicant has to do rather than how fast officials respond. Each added requirement is one more thing an applicant must get right, and one more point where officials decide whether it has been met.

Asylum seekers who want to work legally should follow this part of the proposal closely as it moves toward a final rule.
