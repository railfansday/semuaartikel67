How to Defuse Looming Threats to U.S. National Security.

Recent findings suggest that ivo H. Daalder and James M. Lindsay.

Industry observers note that democrats won a historic victory in the U.S. midterm elections by running on a key voter concern: affordability. They promised to lower gas and diesel prices, curb electricity and water bills, and cut borrowing costs. Now voters expect results.

Making progress will not be easy, given that the affordability crisis is inextricably linked to a set of thorny policy issues. Soaring gas and diesel prices stem from wars in Europe and the Middle East that have overextended the United States and provided China, Iran, North Korea, and Russia openings to exploit. Rising electricity and water bills are driven by the growing number of data centers powering an artificial intelligence revolution that is nearing a point of no return, when machines improve faster than humans can understand or control. And higher prices and borrowing costs are the result of an unsustainable U.S. national debt, now above $40 trillion, that requires exorbitant interest payments and has foreign governments questioning the safety of the dollar and U.S. Treasury bonds.

These problems not only strain Americans’ pocketbooks but also threaten the security and prosperity of the United States—and the world. If Beijing and Moscow try to take advantage of a weakened United States by escalating simultaneously in Taiwan and eastern Europe, those moves could be the first steps toward World War III. If AI escapes the bounds of human control, no one will be safe. And if the national debt continues to soar, Washington could trigger a fiscal crisis that devastates the American economy and leaves the United States far less able to meet the challenges it faces overseas.

Action to address the United States’ problems therefore cannot wait. But politics may stand in the way. The White House is more likely to obstruct than to cooperate with congressional Democrats over the next two years, as President Donald Trump’s appointment of the pugilistic Stephen Miller as the latest White House chief of staff underscores. Moreover, one electoral victory did not erase the country’s deep partisan and policy divisions. Going into the 2028 presidential election, Republicans know that a successful Democratic Congress would endanger their chances of keeping the White House. Trump and congressional Republicans, therefore, have considerable motivation to derail Democratic intends.

It has been revealed that democrats are not without options, however. They can pursue legislation, seeking cross-party collaboration when possible and pushing ahead even in the face of presidential vetoes. Sometimes it is the president, not Congress, who blinks. When he doesn’t, voters will know who stood in the way. Democrats can also use public hearings and the investigative powers of Congress to shed light on the threats facing the country and to build a case for change—much as the Fulbright hearings did during the Vietnam War and the Church Committee did to highlight Cold War–era intelligence abuses. They can put the defense secretary and military commanders, AI executives, and Treasury and budget officials under oath to account for how they prosecuted the Iran war, raced ahead on AI without adequate safeguards, and exploded the deficit. They can also call on economists, scientists, and strategists to explain how to steer the country away from peril.

According to recent reports, congressional efforts may not be enough to meaningfully change Trump’s policies. But the risks of major war, unconstrained AI, and eroding economic competitiveness are too great to wait two more years to address. These problems, and the affordability crisis they have generated, will only get worse. If Democrats in Congress do not start advancing a serious agenda to address them now, these dangers may become impossible to contain.

The first order of the new Congress’s business should be to end the Iran war. Operation Epic Fury has sent energy prices soaring and put freedom of navigation at risk. It has also highlighted the United States’ vulnerabilities in ways that could prove dangerous—and costly—in the years ahead. Iranian missiles and drones wreaked havoc on American military bases in the Persian Gulf. U.S. efforts to defeat these attacks depleted supplies of critical munitions, showing that the U.S. military has become overly reliant on very expensive, hard-to-produce high-tech weapons. The economic, diplomatic, military, and intelligence support that China and Russia have provided Iran revealed the extent to which autocracies are cooperating to stretch U.S. capabilities and undermine American power. The U.S. Navy is effectively bottled up in the Arabian Sea, limiting its presence in the Atlantic and Pacific. In theory, U.S. allies could help fill the gaps, but Trump’s decision to launch the war without consulting them and his adversarial attitude toward them have strained those relationships, weakening deterrence of a Russia and China bent on securing strategic advantage.

Industry observers note that perceptions of weakness, even when they are wrong, are often why wars begin. Moscow and Beijing might see the next two years as a window of opportunity. Europe and Japan are rearming, but that buildup will take years. The American military is stretched thin, and political divisions make decisive action difficult. Together this could be an invitation for U.S. adversaries to engage in aggression in Europe and Asia. Russia is already pursuing a shadow war against NATO and could test the alliance’s unity further by probing the Baltic states. China could impose a blockade on Taiwan. It is not difficult to see how, if Moscow and Beijing take these steps in concert, the resulting conflicts could escalate into a global war.

The fresh Congress will have limited ability to forestall these events. The U.S. Constitution puts much of diplomacy beyond the reach of the legislature; only the president decides when to negotiate, what to discuss, and with whom. But Congress does have tools to wield. If the Trump administration remains in a standoff with the Iranian regime when Democrats take control in January, Congress should immediately invoke the War Powers Resolution to pass binding legislation demanding that Trump either secure congressional authorization for the war or end it. Congress can also use its power of the purse to condition or halt further spending on the war, preferably as part of must-pass legislation, such as a defense appropriations bill, to avoid a potential Senate filibuster. Trump would likely veto any such legislation, and Congress has a poor record on overriding presidential vetoes. But challenging a war that Americans clearly oppose would fulfill the promise Democrats made on the campaign trail, reduce the likelihood that Trump would escalate in dangerous ways, and mark a step forward in Congress’s reclamation of its war powers.

In a recent development, there is more the Democratic Congress can do to lower the risks of global conflict, too. It can start by taking a stronger line on Russia. Moscow has repaid Trump’s conciliatory policies—including his waiving of sanctions Congress authorized earlier this year with the bipartisan Lindsay O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act—with aid to Tehran and an intensified war on Ukraine. Only greater pressure can force the Kremlin to stand down. Congress should amend the Graham Act to give itself the right to review and overturn any presidential waivers of sanctions mandated under the law. It should also reaffirm the current ban on energy purchases from Russia and prohibit the administration from using government funds to follow through on its proposal to restart Russian gas and oil exports to Europe through a U.S. intermediary. Congress should resume military aid to Ukraine, ensure the availability of Starshield communications to Ukrainian forces, and refuse to fund any reduction in the permanent U.S. military presence in Europe or Asia that it has not approved itself.

It has been revealed that finally, to help dissuade China from threatening Taiwan, Congress should use defense spending bills to speed up the sale of arms to Taiwan. And Congress should legally codify the 1982 Six Assurances, which include a promise by Washington not to consult Beijing on arms sales to Taiwan. Many of these measures could garner sufficient Republican support to pass with veto-proof majorities, forcing Trump’s hand.

Additionally, Congress should use its oversight powers to assess the vulnerabilities that the Iran war has revealed. It should evaluate whether the U.S. intelligence agencies and the Pentagon adequately anticipated Iran’s response to U.S. attacks, the scale and causes of the shortages of high-tech weapons, and the risks that AI-aided drone warfare poses to U.S. overseas bases. Congress can also review the extent of Chinese and Russian support for Iran; assess NATO’s defenses against Russia, including its response to the escalating shadow war; determine U.S. readiness for Chinese efforts to coerce Taiwan; and evaluate proposals to make Pentagon acquisition more flexible and less expensive. An essential step toward enhancing U.S. deterrence is to identify what is broken in the U.S. defense posture and what needs to be fixed.

The latest Congress will also need to rein in the AI industry. Opposition to AI data centers and other infrastructure galvanized voters in the midterms. Across the country, 46 of 47 ballot measures passed, placing new restrictions on new data centers, which have brought rising power and water bills and explosive land costs. Many Americans fear what AI will do to jobs today and for the next generation. They distrust the tech executives who control it and question who will truly benefit from its development.

Those concerns are justified. AI is advancing with increasing speed and introducing unknown risks. Five months ago, AI generated one percent of Anthropic’s research and development; today, it accounts for more than a quarter. Lab leaders predict that AI will become self-generating within a year. That is a scary prospect, not only for displaced computer scientists but also for society at large. AI could pose an existential threat to global computer networks; rogue AI agents have already colluded to evade human control. Leading firms have warned of even greater dangers. Alphabet noted recently that its products could lead to “severe or catastrophic security and societal incidents,” and Anthropic has cited “catastrophic or existential risks to humanity.”.

AI also poses profound national security challenges for the United States. The Trump administration and the tech industry have invoked an innovation race with China as a reason not to regulate the technology. That race is real, but many of the most notable stages are not the ones Washington is fixating on. They are also ones Beijing is winning. Perhaps U.S. firms will be the first to develop artificial general intelligence. But in the meantime, the rest of the world and many U.S. companies are adopting Chinese open-weight AI models. Alibaba’s Qwen model now accounts for more than half of all open-source model downloads worldwide. As cheap, easy-to-adapt Chinese models become the global default, Beijing gains leverage over the digital infrastructure on which other countries’ economies and militaries will run. The companies that build these models are legally bound to cooperate with Chinese intelligence services. And because these models spread with few safeguards, they are easier to turn to malicious use.

Meanwhile, AI is transforming war. Autonomous weapons are essential to Ukraine’s defense against a superior military, shooting down long-range drones and targeting Russian forces, supply lines, and strategic industries. This year, the Pentagon quietly revised its doctrine to let AI select and initiate strikes while humans merely monitor, which risks errors and escalation at a speed no human can catch. And as AI lowers the barriers to entry for launching cyberattacks and developing newly introduced biological weapons, every country is in danger.

Sources indicate that the Trump administration is clearly reluctant to address any of these threats. It is beholden to figures such as Jensen Huang, Elon Musk, and Mark Zuckerburg who have wanted to keep the AI industry unregulated and unconstrained. Huang and Nvidia, the manufacturer he led, had long pushed the administration to relax U.S. export controls that restricted the sale of AI chips to China and other countries; the firm’s current scandal has revealed just how loose those restrictions had become. Now, the administration wants China to report serious AI-related incidents through a newly introduced bilateral channel yet opposes requiring American companies to do the same. Given how rapidly AI is advancing, Congress cannot afford to stand idly by. There is a slew of legislative proposals already circulating, many of them with bipartisan support, that would require incident reporting, auditors in AI labs, and mandatory safety testing before deployment. Other AI industry leaders, such as Anthropic’s Dario Amodei and Alphabet’s Demis Hassabis, have supported these ideas, but the Trump administration has ignored them, asking instead for the industry to police itself.

Finding the right regulations and safety mechanisms, and then codifying them in legislation, will take time. In the meantime, Congress should use its convening power to seek input from industry leaders, experts, and the public about how this technology can be controlled. The leaders of frontier AI labs should be asked, under oath, to explain not just the upside of AI but also its risks. Elon Musk, for example, told The Economist this summer that within ten years, work will be “optional” because of AI—but he has also said there is a ten to 20 percent chance that AI will lead to the extinction of humanity. He and others who share this belief should be forced to justify why this risk is acceptable and why tech leaders are the ones to decide on behalf of humanity. They should also explain what it would take to reduce the ten to 20 percent chance to zero.

Public hearings that expose both the dangers and the opportunities of AI will make it easier to galvanize action to harness the technology’s promise safely while preventing the worst possible outcomes. And Congress has some levers it can pull now to address threats to national security. It can use the defense authorization bill to require that humans retain meaningful control over lethal force and to demand an accounting of how AI is reshaping military doctrine. It can also fund efforts to make American models, rather than Chinese ones, the default choice for countries around the world. If legislation takes too long to pass, hearings can at least lay the groundwork for a future administration to act.

Finally, Congress must address threats to the United States’ economic power. With $40 trillion in public debt, an annual budget deficit of $2 trillion, and annual interest payments topping more than $1 trillion, the U.S. economy is living on borrowed time. China and Russia are working to “de-dollarize” their economies by encouraging the use of cryptocurrency and other means of settling international transactions and by creating alternatives to the U.S.-dominated global banking system.

Even U.S. allies are reducing their reliance on that system: Norway’s sovereign wealth fund has cut its holding of U.S. dollars, the Dutch and French central banks have pulled their gold holdings from New York, and European Central Bank President Christine Lagarde has called for a “global euro” that would serve as an alternative to the dollar. If the dollar were to lose its status as the world’s premier reserve currency, Washington would be forced to spend even more money servicing the debt than it does now, intensifying the country’s fiscal problems, slowing economic growth while driving up inflation, and diminishing its ability to use economic and financial sanctions to pressure adversaries.

Industry observers note that an immediate objective for the Democratic Congress will be to rein in Trump’s tariffs, because they raise prices, unsettle the markets that finance U.S. debt, and make U.S. producers less competitive globally without making a meaningful dent in the deficit. Congress will be operating from a disadvantage. Over the past century, it has delegated to the White House considerable tariff-setting authority, which Trump has eagerly exercised. Clawing back that power will be difficult. Significant disagreement exists among Democrats and between Democrats and Republicans about how to rewrite tariff law, and Trump will likely fight to defend his favorite foreign policy tool. Yet one area where concerted congressional action might be possible is in walking back Trump’s unilateral tariffs on Canada, which not only run counter to the U.S.-Mexico-Canada trade agreement that Trump negotiated and Congress approved in his first term but also hurt Republican voters in Maine and across the Midwest. Congress can aim to repeal Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930, which allows the president to impose tariffs of up to 50 percent on countries that treat U.S. goods differently from those from other countries. The provision was a forgotten relic until Trump invoked it in August to impose a fresh round of tariffs on Canada. And legislators should narrow the reach of other presidential tariff authorities by mandating expedited congressional review, instituting joint resolutions of disapproval that cannot be filibustered, and adding sunset provisions to emergency tariff authorities so that the president needs congressional approval to keep them in place.

Tariffs, however, are only part of the problem. A comprehensive solution to the country’s fiscal troubles would require raising taxes or cutting entitlements, which neither party has been willing to do. But Democrats have other cards they can play to start undoing the damage of the past two years. Trump’s 2025 One Substantial Beautiful Bill Act will cost the Treasury some $4.5 trillion over the next decade; Congress should repeal the law’s tax breaks for corporations and the wealthiest Americans and restore the Internal Revenue Service’s ability to collect taxes that are already owed. It should also write the Federal Reserve’s independence into law, so that no president can force interest rates down to make government borrowing cheaper. And Congress should establish a bipartisan commission modeled on the 1981 Greenspan Commission, which resolved a Social Security funding crisis, to recommend steps the United States must take to put its finances on a sustainable path and to ensure its global economic competitiveness.

In his 1948 campaign, Harry Truman salvaged his presidency by running against what he called the “do-nothing” Congress. That history should be a warning to Democrats. Voters handed them a congressional majority to bring down costs, and they must start right away to address the forces driving those costs—wars stretching American power to the breaking point, an AI revolution racing beyond human control, and a fiscal hole eroding the foundations of U.S. economic power. If the Democratic majority spends the next two years relitigating the administration’s misdeeds but failing to make progress on the concern voters care most about, Trump and the next Republican presidential candidate will be happy to brand it the do-nothing Congress of 2028.

Recent findings suggest that passing bills that can become law, forcing the president to veto those that cannot, and using hearings to show Americans what is at stake may still fall short. Ending and averting war, regulating a powerful tech industry, and protecting the United States’ economic competitiveness are difficult for Congress to do without presidential cooperation. But two years without any progress would only leave the affordability problem in place and the United States in a more dangerous global position than it is in now. A Congress that confronts these problems directly can at least make headway. And if it does its job right, it will hand the next president an agenda equal to the moment.
