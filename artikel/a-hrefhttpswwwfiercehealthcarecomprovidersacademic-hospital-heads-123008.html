Dozens of academic health system and teaching hospital chief executives are turning to Congress in a bid to blunt a controversial subsidy cut coming to Medicare Part B in the fresh year.

Earlier this month, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services locked in looks to reduce reimbursements for outpatient drugs dispensed by participants in the 340B Drug Pricing Program during the 2027 calendar year.

Specifically, the agency said it will move from the current rate of average sales price (ASP) plus 6% down to ASP minus 28%, with a corresponding 7.5% increase in non-drug outpatient payments to maintain budget neutrality. CMS said the change will reduce Original Medicare drug payments by an estimated $4.35 billion and drug payments from beneficiaries by $1.1 billion during the policy’s first year.

Hospital groups—particularly those that chiefly represent nonprofit or safety-net hospitals—have denounced the plan’s disproportionate effect on hospitals participating in the decades-old subsidy program, as well as a gift to for-profit hospitals that do not participate but will benefit from the budget-neutral offset.

With their formal opposition to the policy’s proposal set aside by CMS, 95 executives whose organizations are members of the Association of American Medical Colleges have now penned a letter to leaders of the House and the Senate pleading for a two-year delay to the rate cut’s implementation.

“This policy will exacerbate already negative Medicare margins at a time when teaching hospitals are already grappling with rising financial challenges resulting from inflation, workforce shortages, manufacturer-driven restrictions on the 340B program and an expected rise in uncompensated care costs,” they wrote.

The executives acknowledged that the final policy’s rate of ASP minus 28% is “slightly smaller” than CMS’ initial proposal of ASP minus 33.4%, but mentioned the change was still a threat to the country’s healthcare safety net. They noted that the cuts represent a nearly $5.5 billion reduction that would be disproportionately shouldered by AAMC-member teaching hospitals, who comprise 17% of all impacted hospitals but will see almost $3 billion of that total.

Recent findings suggest that those funds are necessary to finance critical care services at academic hospitals including trauma care, inpatient behavioral care, transplant surgery and labor and delivery, the signatories warned lawmakers. They also noted that their institutions are major referral hubs that accept “the most complex transfer cases from lower acuity settings, such as rural and community hospitals,” in a way that for-profit hospitals and others who will benefit from the final rule do not.

In a recent development, “While we understand that Congress has an interest in improving the 340B program, and are broadly supportive of meaningful transparency and accountability for covered entities, we also strongly believe that cutting Medicare payment to 340B hospitals is not the appropriate policy mechanism to achieve these goals,” they wrote. “Therefore, we respectfully request that you support a two-year delay in the [Outpatient Prospective Payment System] rule’s payment cuts for 340B-acquired drugs.”.

Among the letter’s 95 signatories were the heads of Cedars-Sinai, Stanford Health Care, Yale New Haven Health, Tampa General Hospital, Rush University System for Health, Mass General Brigham, Nebraska Medicine, RWJBarnabas Health, NewYork-Presbyterian, UNC Health and School of Medicine, OHSU, Jefferson Health, Penn Medicine, Vanderbilt Health and Froedtert ThedaCare.

It has been revealed that the academic health systems and hospitals’ turn to lawmakers comes after their rebukes of CMS’ 340B policy have fallen on deaf ears. The agency and broader Trump administration have often sided with critics of the subsidy program on related initiatives like a pilot rebate program, citing 340B’s rapid growth and contribution to increased healthcare spending. A Health and Human Services Office of the Assistant Secretary for Planning and Evaluation policy brief defending the rate cut made those views crystal clear with claims that the program has incentivized provider consolidation while doing little to serve patients’ needs.

Meanwhile, Congress, spurred by public pressure to reduce healthcare costs, has proposed its own legislative fixes to 340B. Though each bill aims to preserve the program and subsidize safety-net care, some are more friendly to the hospital industry than others.
