4 places where cutting AI costs is a mistake

🌐 Source: Fastcompany • 📅 19 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 8 min read (1557 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways
  • It has been revealed that every leader claims the same thing when they roll out a newly introduced AI tool.
  • According to recent reports, every leader claims the same thing when they roll out a new AI tool.
  • A scarcity mindset took hold, driven by the assumption that controlling costs means restricting employees’ access to tools.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that every leader claims the same thing when they roll out a newly introduced AI tool. “Give it to our employees, let them run!” What actually happens three months later is that access gets capped, usage gets metered, and exploration gets restricted to the people with the most seniority. What happened? A scarcity mindset took hold, driven by the assumption that controlling costs means restricting employees’ access to tools. Here are the four places a scarcity mindset shows up most frequently, and what you can do about it. 1. Metering access like a utility bill Companies like Tesla, Uber, and Meta have started to cap spending. Tesla, for example, now limits employees to $200 per week. On paper, that sounds reasonable. But for developers who have built these tools into their daily work, hitting the limit doesn’t magically make their deadlines or responsibilities disappear. Instead, developers are forced to make a decision. Do they use the assistance now to improve their work, or save it for a harder assignment later? This kind of metering is the equivalent of telling employees that their laptop batteries last just one hour and can be charged only once a day. The far better way to control costs is to match the tool to the task. While it’s easy to reach for the most powerful model every single time, think of it like renting a car for your next trip to the grocery store. A Ferrari costs far more than a Toyota, and doesn’t make any material difference in terms of getting your groceries home. Likewise, a routine coding task doesn’t need the most expensive artificial intelligence model to produce an excellent result. There are now tools that automatically route coding tasks to models capable of doing them well at a lower cost. Employees should be able to focus on the work without having to choose a model or calculate the cost of every request. 2. Keeping up with the Joneses Companies often default to using whatever tool the rest of the market has already crowned the winner, without evaluating whether it actually fits the job. Using a popular model feels like the safe, defensible choice, and if everyone else is using it (the thinking goes), then you can’t get blamed for choosing it, too. Safe, maybe, but not smart. A better option is to focus on finding the tool that will get you the outcome you want as efficiently as possible. One note: Data matters here too. Before anyone pastes firm information into a tool, leaders should know how the provider stores it, whether it can be used for training a model, and how the contract actually protects your information. 3. Sticking with what worked 10 years ago A tech leader I spoke with recently argued that some tasks are better handled by conventional software than by AI, especially when they follow fixed rules and must produce the same result every single time given the same inputs. Fair enough. You don’t need a language model to calculate 1+1=2. But consider this. An investor recently gave me blunter advice. You either swim against the current and probably will not make it, or you move faster by swimming with the current. Opting out of AI is not an option, because competitors who adopt it will outpace those who don’t. Rationing the chance to try something new is how you end up behind. So give developers room to test a better way, then judge whether the results justify changing the process. 4. Limiting exploration to senior staff only This pitfall is extremely common and leaders should avoid it by all means. Junior team members often have the freshest instincts for a new tool, and they’re the ones kept from experimenting, on the assumption that only senior staff can be trusted to test things out. Give every developer room to keep trying, testing and learning, regardless of seniority. Leaders can make the freedom to experiment feel safer by setting clear boundaries around which data, systems, and websites each tool can access. The costume of prudence All four of these behaviors come from a scarcity mindset wearing the costume of prudence. For leaders who recognize their own organization in any of this, the place to start is simple. Think about your own laptop, and whether you would accept being told you could only use it one hour a day. That is what metering access feels like to the people doing the work, whether they are developers or not. Teams already know the difference between a leader who trusts them with power and one who doles it out. Which one are you?
