Latest Update: US extends chip sale restrictions to more Asian countries to reduce risk of ‘diversion’ to China

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Commerce dept runing criminal investigation into Nvidia-linked Megaspeed.

The US Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) has decided to ban remote access to certain GPUs and servers running in datacenters located in Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Brazil.

The agency has also introduced a requirement to secure a license before selling what it describes as “advanced super intelligence (SI) chips and servers” that are sufficiently powerful to “be used for training or running of advanced SI models” into the five nations.

The interim final rule [PDF] containing the new regulations says they’re needed “to prevent certain foreign actors from leveraging advanced computing power based on US technology to threaten US national security and foreign policy interests.”.

Similar language can be found in previous US bans on sales of GPUs and other advanced tech to China, which Washington fears will use advanced tech to improve its military capabilities.

Sources indicate that US authorities understood that export bans wouldn’t prevent Chinese entities from renting GPUs housed elsewhere. So from 2021 to 2024, Washington introduced a series of regulations that required US-based clouds to make sure they didn’t serve Chinese customers.

In a recent development, no such obligation existed for cloud operators elsewhere, which created a market for resources that Chinese customers could access from afar. It is widely felt that datacenter building booms in Malaysia and Thailand, both of which are generally on good terms with China, aimed to cater to Middle Kingdom customers who wanted to rent GPUs they could not import.

Megapseed – whose former employee Alice Huang had an intimate relationship with former Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang and may have been a Chinese intelligence asset – built datacenters in Malaysia and was suspected of catering largely to offshore customers.

Neil Shah, vice president of research at analyst firm Counterpoint, mentioned the upcoming rules shift the regulatory burden of complying with export controls from hardware manufacturers to cloud operators.

“Data center operators in hubs such as Singapore, Malaysia and Indonesia will now face massive ‘Know Your Customer's Workload’ requirements,” he told The Register by email, likening it to the KYC restrictions imposed on financial services firms. “They must prove to US regulators that their compute is not being funneled back to embargoed nations, or risk losing their own access to US silicon.”.

He feels the fresh rules represent “a severe short-term compute bottleneck for Chinese tech giants” and “a massive long-term catalyst for decoupling.”.

“Washington is forcing domestic capital and engineering talent to double down on full-stack sovereign alternatives, accelerating ecosystems like Huawei's Ascend.”.

Shah’s observation came days after Alibaba decided to spin out its T-Head chip design unit, which it described as “a leading provider of full-stack data center chips which enable system-level compute solutions in China.” That description suggests Alibaba doesn’t think T-Head has strong prospects of selling its wares outside China.

In a recent development, it remains to be seen if the new BIS rules will slow datacenter development across Asia and Brazil. All five nations the US has regulated are all enjoying the economic impact of the AI boom. They’re also trying to manage its impact. Singapore limits datacenter construction, as befits a tiny and resource-constrained island nation. Malaysia is very much open for business but has introduced increasingly strict building codes and requirements to minimize energy and water consumption. Thailand recently introduced new regulations that new builds of large datacenters must take place in designated industrial zones. Indonesia also has strong regulations but is not currently a major datacenter hub.

Brazil requires datacenter operators to reserve at least 10 percent of capacity for local users, suggesting some operators are more interested in offshore customers.

Interestingly, the upcoming BIS rules don’t impact infrastructure used for “commercial recommender systems, such as those used by online retailers or social media companies, as these functions do not pose the same national security threat.”.

It has been revealed that chinese e-tailers operate at vast scale and have already attracted regulatory attention in Europe and the USA. Asian and Brazilian datacenter operators therefore probably do not see the likes of Temu and Alibaba as a lifeline, but might see a role as regional hosts for outfits like Amazon or Meta. ®.

UDPATED AT 05:45 UTC, November 17 to add news that late on Monday the US Departments of Commerce and Justice confirmed an ongoing criminal investigation into Megaspeed.

In the announcement, the two departments detail investigative activities they conducted across 2024 and 2025, including a June meeting with Alice Huang in Singapore, and state those details "address inaccurate information circulating in public reporting.".

Singaporean authorities have also mentioned that meeting took place, but say they asked asked US counterparts for help with their own investigations and received frustratingly little support.

Singapore also investigated Supermicro's possible role at Megapseed, and complained that the server-maker didn't co-operate either.

Supermicro today told The Register it "does not conduct business with these entities" and operates a "robust compliance program.".

Industry observers note that "We are committed to complying with all applicable U.S. export and re-export control laws and regulations, and we require our distributors and resellers to do the same," according to a Supermicro statement sent to The Register.
