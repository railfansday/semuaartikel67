Forget the Film Stars—2026 Belonged to the Best-Dressed Directors

According to recent reports, while scrolling through my Instagram feed last week, I came across a pair of T-shirts made by Sofia Coppola for Mack Books. To celebrate the release of her mother Eleanor’s documentary, Making Marie Antoinette—a portrait compiled from archival footage shot during the making of Coppola’s 2006 cult classic—as well as the original film’s 20th anniversary, Coppola has released a limited-edition capsule collection printed with on-set photography by Andrew Durham. Naturally, I had a T-shirt in my basket faster than you could say “Let them eat cake.” (In case you were wondering, I went for the one with Kirsten Dunst looking wistfully out from a rococo horse-drawn carriage, a camera rig looming in the foreground.).

But it also got me thinking, not for the first time, about film directors and fashion. There’s a reason why the behind-the-scenes photos from that movie—Dunst and Jason Schwartzman in powdered wigs and full Versailles regalia listening to their iPods, Coppola crouched behind a monitor in a button-down and jeans; you know the ones—have remained such an enduring touchstone within the online cultural consciousness, from Tumblr to TikTok. There’s something both fascinating and lightly absurd about the friction between the practical, gorpy clothing worn by most on a film set and the breathtaking opulence of the period costuming. And in Making Marie Antoinette, Coppola’s casual but stylish on-set wardrobe of well-cut mid-aughts basics is, counterintuitively, almost as captivating as Milena Canonero’s glorious 18th-century costume confections. Which I guess is partly why Coppola made those T-shirts.

It has been revealed that but it’s also partly why the best directors—those who spend their lives building worlds and poring over the details of what their character might say, do, and, most importantly for our purposes, wear—have become as compelling to watch on the red carpet as the contract-bound movie stars they direct. (It’s little wonder that some of history’s greatest filmmakers were also some of history’s most stylish, possessing an innate understanding of the storytelling power of clothes: Agnès Varda, David Lynch, Wong Kar-wai, the list goes on.).

Which brings me to the 2026 awards trail—and the 2027 awards trail, which is now kicking into gear—where the sartorial approaches have been refreshingly eclectic. While promoting Sinners, Ryan Coogler’s killer red carpet looks included a three-piece Thom Browne suit with a diamond brooch at the Golden Globes, a slouchy Fear of God suit at the SAG Awards, and a sharp Louis Vuitton tux at the Oscars.

Ryan Coogler (with his wife, Zinzi) in Thom Browne at the Golden Globes.

Coogler in Louis Vuitton at the Oscars.

For Chloé Zhao—who, not coincidentally, earned a spot on this year’s Vogue best dressed list—campaigning for Hamnet appeared to involve taking cues from the world of her film. (Turns out directors can do method dressing too.) Working with Gabriela Hearst, she wore a gold gown with a Tudor-inflected Basque waist at the BAFTAs, while at the Academy Awards, she wore a headline-grabbing gown inspired by Victorian mourning dress—complete with draped bell sleeves, Bulgari diamonds, and a sheer black veil—that she described as “the alchemical image of the crow.” Try getting that level of commitment from a leading man in a black tux and a brand-deal watch.

Chloé Zhao in Gabriela Hearst at the BAFTAs.

Zhao in Gabriela Hearst at the Oscars.

Recent findings suggest that thankfully, this year, there’s a new crop of fashion-fluent directors ready to take up the mantle. Just take Javier Calvo and Javier Ambrossi, known collectively as Los Javis, who have happily matched the maximalist, operatic sweep of their Lorca-inspired epic La Bola Negra with their steady stream of playful tailoring—coordinating Loewe suits at the Biarritz Film Festival, say, or oversized shirting and denim to the Chanel show over the summer—proving their flair for the visual extends well beyond the silver screen.

Los Javis—or Javi Ambrossi and Javi Calvo—at the GQ Men of the Year Awards in Madrid.

Ambrossi and Calvo in Chanel at the brand’s haute couture fall 2026 show.

Sources indicate that meanwhile, Olivia Wilde has been delivering some of the year’s sharpest high-low looks while promoting The Invite—everything from a custom Saint Laurent dress with an ab-baring cutout at the Los Angeles premiere, to a boxy black vintage tee and double-pleated pants from The Row for a SAG-AFTRA event. “I really love not having to dress up in the same way as I have as an actress,” she told Chloe Malle on the Vogue podcast earlier this year. “I do feel like after decades of tarting myself up for press tours, I am so relieved to be like, ‘You know what? They wear T-shirts. I’m gonna wear a fucking T-shirt.’” (Granted, wearing a T-shirt hits a little differently when it’s tucked into pants that cost roughly the same as a secondhand Honda Civic, but the point stands.).

Olivia Wilde in Saint Laurent at The Invite’s Los Angeles premiere.

Wilde in a vintage x Karla tee and The Row pants at a SAG-AFTRA Foundation event.

Then, there’s Greta Gerwig, who’s gearing up for the February release of her rock-opera-inspired Narnia: The Magician’s Nephew, whose looks this year—Bottega Veneta, Stella McCartney, Diotima by Rachel Scott—hint at a bold (and largely female-designed) press tour wardrobe to come.

Greta Gerwig in Bottega Veneta at the PGA Awards.

Gerwig in Ashlyn at the opening of the Lucas Museum of Narrative Art.

According to recent reports, and finally, for a new vanguard of filmmakers, the press tour has become a place to subvert the traditional codes of men’s tailoring. On that front, there’s Club Kid’s Jordan Firstman and the Bottega Veneta suit he wore for the Los Angeles premiere, with its oversized powder-pink collar peeking over the top of the lapels, or the Tom Ford by Haider Ackermann jacket for the New York premiere that featured a silk floral boutonnière with a flowing ribbon. Or Jane Schoenbrun, who debuted their queer slasher, Teenage Sex and Death at Camp Miasma, in Cannes with a series of offbeat looks that included a particularly delightful puffball skirt suit with powder-blue tights and Mary Janes.

Jordan Firstman in Tom Ford by Haider Ackermann at the Fresh York premiere of Club Kid.

Jane Schoenbrun at the Cannes premiere of Teenage Sex and Death at Camp Miasma.

Recent findings suggest that it helps, of course, that in 2026, the lines between luxury fashion houses and cinema have all but dissolved. Fashion is no longer just dressing actors and directors for the premiere, or awards show; it’s actively financing, producing, and inserting itself into their work. In September, Dior revealed a two-year creative partnership with A24 before doubling down just this week with a upcoming Sundance Institute fellowship supporting women filmmakers. The Anthony Vaccarello–founded Saint Laurent Productions has cemented itself as a Cannes and awards-season powerhouse, backing auteurs such as Jim Jarmusch and Claire Denis. And some designers are stepping into the film set trenches, too—most notably Jonathan Anderson, who followed up his work on Challengers and Queer by designing the costumes for Guadagnino’s hotly anticipated Silicon Valley satire Artificial.

Clothes maketh the man. All of these directors know that clothes maketh the character. So let’s hope that, this awards season, clothes continue to maketh the filmmaker, too—and if they ever run out of ideas, I’ve got a Marie Antoinette T-shirt they’re welcome to borrow.
