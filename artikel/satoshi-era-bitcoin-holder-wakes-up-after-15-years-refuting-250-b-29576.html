A Bitcoin address that had not moved coins since February 2011 transferred 75 BTC on Nov. 17, according to Galaxy Research. At the current price of $64,935 per BTC, the coins are worth around $4.87 million.

The address is listed as a defendant in a $250 billion lawsuit in Latest York Supreme Court, in which the plaintiffs are trying to have early Bitcoin wallets declared "lost." Galaxy Research mentioned that several other addresses from the same list have also moved coins in 2026.

According to recent reports, according to Galaxy Research analysts, early on Nov. 17, in block 976,209, the address 1ALpEWtXApR19hLXvcG8NfkPyGsUMhFPMS sent coins for the first time. It was first funded on Feb. 8, 2011. At that time, Bitcoin was trading at about $0.90. After 15.8 years, the coins went to an unknown address with a 399-satoshi fee, or about 26 cents, for a paper gain of more than 7,000,000%.

According to recent reports, onchain databases list the wallet as Noah Doe #3409 (Salomon Client Dusted). That label is how it ended up among the defendants in the high-profile case ABC Company, XYZ Company, and Noah Doe v. John Does 1–39,069.

Industry observers note that according to the lawsuit, an anonymous plaintiff using the pseudonym Noah Doe and two companies want the rights to 39,069 Bitcoin wallets that have not moved coins for more than five years. The plaintiffs said that these wallets hold about 3.8 million BTC, and at the current price, the coins are worth around $250 billion.

The plaintiffs filed the lawsuit under Upcoming York's lost-and-found law, Article 7-B of the Personal Property Law. Under the law, a person who finds lost property has to give it to the police, and the property goes to the finder if the owner does not claim it in time. In the lawsuit, the plaintiffs valued each address at less than $10, and they argued that the waiting period for such items was one year.

They "found" dormant addresses with an algorithm, saved them onto USB flash drives and took them to the Upcoming York Police Department (NYPD). Then they served the owners on the blockchain, through microtransactions with embedded messages (OP_RETURN). The practice is known as "dusting," and the campaign is tied to Salomon Brothers Strategic Advisors. Once the waiting period ran out, the plaintiffs asked the court to recognize the coins as theirs.

The case has been put on hold several times over motions to dismiss and amicus curiae briefs from outside experts. At a hearing on Sept. 8, the Bitcoin Policy Institute asked to intervene as a defendant, and The Digital Chamber asked to join the case as amicus curiae. Galaxy Research described it as a case "in which anonymous plaintiffs are seeking to claim legal title over Satoshi's coins.".

Galaxy Research's Will Owens, who attended the hearing, said that The Digital Chamber's arguments were "well pleaded" and that "the judge sounded sympathetic to them.".

Sources indicate that critics of the lawsuit argue that Article 7-B was written for things you can pick up and hand over for safekeeping. It wasn't written for blockchain records when the "finder" doesn't hold the private keys.

In September, Galaxy head of research Alex Thorn also noted that the Lost and Found Property Law includes "protection from 'Noah Doe-style' dormancy attacks on legal ownership.".
