How to Explain Adoption to a Child

November is National Adoption Month, a time when we celebrate families formed through adoption and acknowledge there are children who still need safe, loving, permanent homes. Yet adoption is more than the creation of a latest family. It also involves separation, loss, unanswered questions, and the lifelong search for self.

It has been revealed that every adoption carries a responsibility to help children understand their story, not simply how they joined their adoptive family, but what happened before. Children do not enter adoption with a blank slate. A child's need to talk about adoption helps them make meaning of their experiences, build self-esteem, develop trust, and understand their identity.

When children are not given explanations, they create their own. I know this personally. I entered foster care at 15 months old and was adopted at seven and a half. I will never forget my adoption day. My foster-adoptive family drove me to the courthouse, and I refused to get out of the car. I wrapped my arms around the seat and held on for dear life, terrified that I was being given away again because no one had explained what was happening to me.

To the adults, adoption was a legal process for permanency. To me, it felt like another separation, another terrifying moment when adults were making decisions about my life without telling me why. This is why explanations matter.

Children need language to make meaning. Just because a child is not asking questions does not mean they are not thinking about them. Children will wonder: Why was I adopted? Why couldn't my birth parents keep me? What were they like? Did they love me? Are you going to give me away, too?

When these questions remain unanswered, children internalize the experience as rejection. They may conclude, I must have been a bad baby, or There must be something wrong with me. Young children are egocentric. They believe they are responsible for events happening around them. And they will equate that separation from their birth family means they were unwanted or unlovable. So, they need to hear repeatedly that the separation was never their fault. They did not cause it, could not have prevented it, and were never responsible for the adults' circumstances. As I often remind children, babies are simply not that powerful.

As early as possible. As soon as parents are educated, emotionally prepared, and have gained as much knowledge about their child’s history.

Adoption should never be presented as one dramatic disclosure. It is an ongoing conversation that must be revisited throughout childhood, a term called “recycling information.” At every newly introduced developmental stage, children will revisit facts with greater cognitive, emotional, and psychological understanding. Adoption is understood differently at age 4, 8, 12, and 16. New questions emerge as awareness develops, and information that seemed factual suddenly carries deeper emotions.

Parents should gather photographs, letters, birth information, and gifts the birth parent may have shared. Make copies of precious photographs so children can safely hold, explore, and revisit them.

Parents should become comfortable initiating conversations rather than waiting for their child to ask, gently following the child's lead, respecting their readiness, and answering only the questions they are asking, age-appropriately.

Ask permission before sharing sensitive details. "Are you ready to learn the answer to your question?" via The Question Box Intervention from my book The Traumatized & At-Risk Youth Toolbox. A child who receives difficult information may respond with anger, disbelief, sadness, or confusion. Try not to correct these reactions; acknowledge and validate how difficult this may be to hear. Remind them that you are available to listen and support them. Also, make sure they have downtime to process and integrate this newfound information.

According to recent reports, parents can become anxious or uncomfortable when their child expresses curiosity about their birth family. Children are highly sensitive to these emotional reactions and may stop asking questions to protect their parents from discomfort. Some children may even fear that expressing interest in their birth family could threaten their relationship with their adoptive parents or lead to another rejection.

In a recent development, if you notice yourself becoming defensive, anxious, or threatened by these conversations, I strongly encourage you to work with an adoption-competent therapist. Your child needs to know that their curiosity is not disloyalty and that their birth family can be discussed without threatening their place in your family.

So, during this month, I encourage parents to remember that explaining adoption is not about finding perfect words or eliminating painful feelings. It is about being present when children need answers, even when those answers are difficult, because we cannot change what happened to them, but we can change how they experience their past by being emotionally available in their healing.
