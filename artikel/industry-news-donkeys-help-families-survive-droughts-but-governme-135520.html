Industry News: Donkeys help families survive droughts, but governments often overlook them

Donkeys help East African families survive severe droughts, yet governments and aid organizations often overlook their essential needs.

Families in East Africa depend on donkeys for many everyday tasks. The animals carry water, feed for livestock, and household belongings. They have also been part of local culture for generations.

According to recent reports, in a drought their job gets even more important. Once the water close to home has dried up, donkeys trek long distances to bring it back.

Industry observers note that yet when drought relief arrives, donkeys are rarely part of it. Aid has tended to go to the food-producing animals, and donkey-owning households end up worse off for it.

A team from the University of Portsmouth, Maasai Mara University and Haramaya University looked at how far that neglect goes.

They went through 77 documents and talked with 41 people on the ground. Donkeys, it turned out, are mostly missing, from the policies right down to the vet schools.

Industry observers note that from 2020 to 2023 East Africa was hit by drought. Crops failed widely and some places lost up to 70% of livestock.

Around 26 million people were threatened with food shortages, and conflict over the dwindling resources went up.

Many of the worst affected families are pastoralists, meaning their living depends on herds that often move with them.

Donkeys have been part of that life since they were domesticated in Africa thousands of years ago.

Recent findings suggest that when nearby sources dry up, the water a donkey brings back keeps people, crops and herds going. Donkeys also get people to health facilities, aid distribution centers and markets.

Recent findings suggest that kenya and Ethiopia both rely heavily on them. Ethiopia has about 10.8 million donkeys, and Kenya around 1.2 million.

Professor Leanne Proops works on animal behavior and welfare at the University of Portsmouth and supervised the project.

“Working donkeys are a lifeline for many communities during drought, helping people access water, transport essential supplies and sustain their livelihoods,” said Proops.

“Yet their contribution is largely overlooked in the policies and support systems designed to help communities cope with these crises.”.

Of the 77 documents, 51 were Kenyan and 26 Ethiopian, issued anywhere from 1964 up to 2025.

It has been revealed that animal health, agriculture, climate change, disaster risk and water were the areas covered. Equids, that is donkeys, horses and mules, came up in just 17.

Quite often the donkey got one mention in a species list and was absent from the rest of the policy.

“Livestock” was a word used a lot, but most documents never remarked what species it took in. In reality it meant cattle, sheep and goats.

Donkeys, on the occasions they did appear, sat outside conventional livestock under labels such as “working animals” or “emerging livestock.”.

Not one of the documents made a link between donkeys and drought, even though camels were mentioned in that role.

According to recent reports, on welfare the picture is not much better. Kenya’s Animal Welfare and Protection Bill has been in deliberation since 2019 without being enacted.

Up until 2025, Ethiopia’s criminal code only made cruelty an offense when it happened where the public could see.

A 2025 proclamation now prohibits overloading or overworking any working animal, though no penalties are set out in that part.

Recent findings suggest that references to animals in the policies were heavily based on use, with meat, milk, hides and exports driving them. Kenya’s 2024 Livestock Bill does define donkeys as draught animals but welfare hardly features.

According to recent reports, the interviews pointed the same way. If a drought is forecast, preparedness plans kick in with worming and vaccination for the other livestock.

Recent findings suggest that “The donkeys were generally left out. Nobody was thinking about them,” one participant in Kenya told the researchers.

Later on, supplementary feed rations get handed out in both countries and again the donkeys are not counted.

A participant in Ethiopia noted that when fodder runs short, “the priority is not for donkeys, not for mules, just for animals with salvage value.”.

Industry observers note that in the only program one Kenyan participant had worked on with donkeys included, they got rabies vaccination and no food. The rest of the animals were counted for supplementary feeding.

Sources indicate that participants also noted that droughts are far more frequent than before. Money often comes towards the end, when much has died.

Pastoralists summed this up in their own words: “money comes with the vultures.”.

Dr. Emily Haddy, lead author of the paper, did the work at Portsmouth and is now at the University of Nottingham.

“Recognising donkeys in livestock and disaster planning is not just an animal welfare issue,” said Haddy.

“By including working donkeys in drought preparedness and emergency responses, governments and aid organisations can help protect animal welfare and strengthen community resilience.”.

Industry observers note that the veterinarians taking part spoke of getting little or no training on donkeys. One participant in Kenya stated they try to extrapolate from horses, which does not always hold.

“We try to extrapolate what we know from the horse to the donkey, which is not always true,” the participant mentioned.

Sources indicate that vets specializing in donkeys are few and tend to be in urban areas such as Nairobi. Pastoralist communities therefore often have no access, and lose animals that could have been treated.

Medications suitable for donkeys are also in short supply, particularly anesthesia and pain relief.

“I don’t even think there is any firm in Kenya which makes feeds for donkeys,” another participant noted.

Sources indicate that it should be noted the vets interviewed were older graduates. Donkey medicine in the Kenyan vet curriculum has recently improved, though its effect on the ground may take time.

According to participants, the priority for government is animals bringing in foreign currency directly, through sale of meat and hides.

Sources indicate that the services of a donkey are largely invisible. That makes them hard to quantify and keeps them out of national economic reports.

Industry observers note that one participant described a child-focused relief scheme for milk. Donkeys play a vital role in that milk chain, but they were not taken into account.

“They see the human being first, and they see the cattle, the producer of the milk, and the consumer of the milk, the in-between connection they don’t see,” the participant remarked.

Donkey deaths are also not systematically recorded, so they usually do not appear when authorities list the animals that died.

In a recent development, international guidelines do mention working equids. The Livestock Emergency Guidelines and Standards, aimed at livestock projects in humanitarian crises, include them as pack animals.

The World Organisation for Animal Health gives advice on managing equids during feed shortage too. These were poorly reflected in the national documents.

Professor Tesfaheywet Zeryehun of Haramaya University put the two kinds of welfare side by side.

“Our findings reinforce the fact that donkey welfare cannot be separated from human wellbeing,” stated Zeryehun.

“In drought-prone communities, protecting working donkeys is equally about protecting the livelihoods, resilience and dignity of the families who depend on them.”.

“Their contribution must therefore be recognized in policies and emergency preparedness programs.”.

Documents had to be online and in English to be included. That likely cut the Ethiopian count, as many are in Amharic.

Recent findings suggest that only one pastoralist representative from Ethiopia was interviewed, so those views are probably underrepresented. Fewer than 15% of participants were women.

People were found by snowballing through the authors’ own contacts, and that could bring some selection bias with it.

What’s missing above all is hard evidence on how much donkeys are worth economically. Several interviewees felt numbers on that are what would capture the minds of policy makers.

In a recent development, the authors recommend that equids be explicitly included in livestock definitions. Doing that would make donkeys eligible for the feed and veterinary support that other livestock can get.

They also suggest fodder reserves kept for donkeys, donkey modules in vet training and welfare laws that are actually enforced.

The impact of severe drought on donkey health is named as a research priority.

In a recent development, dr. David Obiero of Maasai Mara University said the gaps reach beyond policy itself.

“The lack of recognition of working donkeys extends beyond policy into veterinary training, access to healthcare and vaccination programmes,” remarked Obiero.

According to recent reports, “These animals are vital to community livelihoods, yet the systems designed to support livestock often overlook them. Addressing these gaps is essential to supporting pastoralist communities during time of drought and beyond.”.

With recurrent droughts expected to intensify, the animal fetching the water is still largely outside the aims to help.

The full study was published in the Journal of Rural Studies.
