Visa, Mastercard theft included info useful for phishing, but not draining bank accounts, say researchers

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

No financial details, but plenty of other clues.

Visa and Mastercard customer info leaked by the data thieves and extortionists who claim to have stolen it from the financial giants doesn’t include card numbers, passwords, or home addresses.

On Thursday, Visa, Mastercard, and JPMorgan Chase confirmed to The Register that they are investigating third-party breaches that may involve customer information.

Ransomware and extortion crew n0n claimed to be responsible for all three, and on Thursday published samples of the stolen files – data-theft groups typically leak some of the compromised data ahead of the official extortion deadline in an effort to put more pressure on victim companies to pay the ransom demand.

Researchers at SOCRadar, a threat-intelligence firm that has been tracking n0n since it emerged in September, have parsed all 14 files in the n0n’s published Visa and Mastercard archives, and told us that the files don’t contain any financial details belonging to banking customers.

Recent findings suggest that “We found no card numbers, expiry dates, CVVs, passwords, dates of birth, or home addresses in any file,” Ensar Seker, SOCRadar chief information security officer, told The Register. “Visa's statement that no card numbers were affected matches our scan.”.

Mastercard previously noted that stolen data included a “limited amount of email addresses – non-payments information” from a subset of cardholders in a benefits program.

Sources indicate that according to SOCRadar’s analysis, the leaked files do contain mobile numbers, Bank Identification Number (BIN) prefixes, and dated airline, hotel, and restaurant bookings. So while the data doesn’t include card or account numbers that crooks could use to drain customers' bank accounts or rack up massive credit card bills, “that detail is what a fraudster needs to pose as a concierge desk or a bank,” in an email or voice-phishing social engineering attack, Seker said.

It has been revealed that it’s also worth noting that the published files only represent about 0.4 percent of the data n0n claims to have hoovered up in the breach: about 4.3 GB and 1.9 million marketing events. “So a further release is possible,” according to Seker.

Industry observers note that plus, both datasets come from the same upstream provider, we’re told. They use the same 27-column booking layout, placeholder text,  and CRM ID format, Seker said, noting that the most recent record in each stops 69 seconds apart on Oct 26.

Industry observers note that “This looks like two card programs run on one provider's platform,” he remarked. “The pattern looks like a batch copy from a marketing and reporting store.”.

The security shop hasn’t analyzed the Chase customer data that n0n also claimed to have published.

All three financial firms attributed the compromises to a third-party provider, and while nothing in the stolen files name the vendor, the timing matches up with a Qubole supply-chain campaign disclosed earlier this month by Wiz, working with Google's Mandiant incident responders and AI data security tech giant Sentra.

In that attack, which the threat hunters said began on October 14, cybercrims used compromised identity and access management (IAM) credentials associated with Qubole's AWS account to access dozens of downstream-customers’ environments, stealing secrets and other sensitive data from S3 buckets.

Sources indicate that N0n also claimed to have carried out this attack. “The timing fits the Qubole supply-chain campaign,” Seker remarked, adding that this is his company’s assessment, not a confirmed finding. "If it's right, Qubole would be the access path rather than the holder of the data, and the files point to a concierge-services operator behind the brands.”.

Sources indicate that qubole’s parent company Idera did not respond to The Register’s inquiries. The financial institutions declined to answer our questions about which provider(s) was compromised in the breach, and whether Qubole was the access path by which their customers’ data was exposed.®.
