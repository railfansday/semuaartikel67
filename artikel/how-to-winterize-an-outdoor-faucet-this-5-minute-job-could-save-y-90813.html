Picture a hose still screwed onto the spigot in December. The water behind it has nowhere to go. Once nights stay below 32°F (0°C), it can freeze solid, and copper and brass don't have much give. Something splits. That repair bill usually runs well past what the faucet cost, so it pays to sort out how to winterize an outdoor faucet now.

Industry observers note that it's a fall chore, best done the same day you winterize sprinklers. The annoying part is where the damage hides. From the yard, the faucet can look fine while the pipe behind it has cracked a few inches inside the wall. Nobody sees that until the water's back on.

It has been revealed that outdoor faucet winterization is mostly about getting water out and keeping it from getting trapped again. Take off the hose, shut off the supply, drain what's left, and add a cover for backup. Try to finish alongside your other gardening winterizing tasks, and before the first hard freeze demonstrates up in the forecast.

If you only get to one step today, make it this one. Any hose left on the spigot holds water inside the faucet, and that goes for frost-free models too – the water has nowhere to drain while the hose is attached. Splitters, watering timers, and quick-connect fittings trap water the same way, so pull them all off.

It has been revealed that then empty the hose. Stretch it down a slope or drape loops over a fence rail so the water runs out both ends. Leftover water can freeze and split it, and a drained hose stored out of the weather generally holds up longer. In a recent development, start inside. Standard faucets usually have their own shutoff, somewhere close to where the pipe goes out through the wall, often in a basement, crawl space, or utility room. Look up between the ceiling joists, too. A round handle turns clockwise until it stops. If it's a lever, a quarter turn puts it crosswise to the pipe, and that's closed.

In a recent development, not every home has one, and older houses often don't, which makes it harder to winterize hose bib lines without shutting off water to the whole house. If you're not sure what an unlabeled valve is, leave it alone. Close the wrong one, and it's no fun to find out halfway through washing dishes. Recent findings suggest that for faucets without a dedicated indoor shutoff, a Freeze Miser outdoor faucet protector offers another option. It screws onto the spigot and automatically releases a small amount of water when the water temperature drops below 37°F (3°C), helping prevent freezing. Unlike conventional winterizing, this method requires leaving the water supply on and the outdoor faucet fully open.

Head back outside and open the faucet all the way. Some water should run out, slow to a trickle, and stop. Did it keep flowing? Then the indoor valve isn't closed all the way, or it's not the right valve at all.

Recent findings suggest that some shutoffs have a little cap on the side, called a bleeder. Leave the outdoor faucet open so air can get in, set a bucket underneath, and back the cap off slowly. Water will dribble out. When it quits, screw the cap back on. Many plumbers will leave the outdoor faucet open all winter. Some valve makers say to close it, though, so go by whatever came with yours.

Sources indicate that plenty of people hear frost-free and figure that means forget about it. That's about half right, maybe. From the yard, a frost-free outdoor faucet looks like any other spigot. Its handle turns a long stem, and the part that stops the water sits way back at the end of it, where it's warmer. Close it, and the water in that stem should run out the spout. That only works if the faucet slopes a little toward the outside.

Industry observers note that correctly installed ones usually don't need the supply shut off and drained, but the manufacturer's instructions get the final say. Homes without an indoor shutoff can have a plumber fit a replacement such as the SharkBite one mentioned above.

Sources indicate that if the faucet won't run or ice has formed around the spout, skip the torch, even if one happens to be sitting right there in the garage. No open flame, and nothing else that blasts heat at it, either. There's framing and insulation right behind that siding. Hair dryers are slower. Point it at the faucet and the wall around it and give it time. Keep the cord out of puddles.

Suspect a leak? Shut off the water to that line, or the main valve if there's no separate one, and call a plumber. Frozen pipe damage isn't always visible right away. A crack of ⅛ inch (3 mm) can let out up to 250 gallons (946L) a day, and it may not drip at all until the ice thaws or the water goes back on.

Most of what it takes to protect outdoor faucets from freezing – and to prevent frozen pipes behind them – is getting the water out and keeping it out. Get that done before the first hard frost, and the faucet should be ready come spring, with dry walls behind it.
