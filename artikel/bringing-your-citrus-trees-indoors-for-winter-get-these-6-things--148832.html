Bringing Your Citrus Trees Indoors for Winter? Get These 6 Things Right to Avoid Leaf Drop and Pests

Recent findings suggest that A potted lemon or lime spends all summer on the patio soaking up sun. Then the cold arrives. Citrus can't take a hard freeze, so in colder climates, potted trees have to come inside for winter.

Industry observers note that the move itself is a shock, though. Gardeners who try growing citrus indoors often find a scatter of leaves on the floor soon after. Indoor light is weaker, the air is drier, and pests that rode in on the branches can spread to nearby houseplants.

Recent findings suggest that that goes for a Meyer lemon, a key lime, an orange, or a little calamondin alike. Six checks before the tree comes through the door can head off much of that.

It has been revealed that most citrus can be damaged below 32°F (0°C), and a potted tree is more exposed than one in the ground. Lemons and limes are usually the most tender, while kumquats, satsumas, and calamondins can handle a little more chill. Aim to have the tree inside before nights start dipping into the 40s (4–9°C). Wait for the first freeze warning, and you'll be dragging a heavy pot from full sun straight indoors. Give it a week or two in a shady spot near the house first instead – it adjusts to lower light that way.

Flip the leaves over and look along the midvein. Brown soft scale (Coccus hesperidum) shows up there as small, flat, tan bumps. Two-spotted spider mites (Tetranychus urticae) leave pale speckles and, eventually, fine webbing where the leaf meets the stem, and white, cottony clumps in the leaf joints usually mean citrus mealybug (Planococcus citri).

If you find anything, spray the tree outdoors with an insecticidal soap or a horticultural oil labeled for citrus, such as Monterey horticultural oil concentrate. Initiative on more than one round. Once inside, keep it away from other houseplants for three or four weeks.

Sources indicate that bugs don't only ride in on the leaves. Tip the pot on its side, look in the drainage holes, and lift the saucer. Slugs, sowbugs, and earwigs all like that damp spot underneath, and ants sometimes leave small piles of potting mix they've hauled out. A slow soak with the hose drives many of them out.

Industry observers note that check any roots poking through the holes, too. Firm, light-colored roots are a good sign, but dark, mushy ones and a sour smell point to waterlogged soil. Unless the roots are in real trouble, wait to repot citrus trees until spring.

It has been revealed that citrus needs far more light than a pothos or snake plant. Indoors, aim for at least six hours of bright light a day. A south- or southwest-facing window is usually the best spot, with the tree close to the glass but not touching it. Up north, short winter days often fall short – that's when a grow light helps. Glowrium sells a dual-head LED grow light on Amazon that's just under 6 feet (1.8 m) tall, with heads that bend toward the canopy. Trees moved from full sun to a dim corner overnight often shed a good share of their leaves.

Hold a hand over a heating vent or radiator when the heat kicks on. That hot, dry air blows straight across the leaves of a nearby tree, and a cold, drafty window or door is no better. Sudden swings like those commonly trigger leaf drop. Somewhere around 65–75°F (18–24°C) by day and a little cooler at night, about 55–65°F (13–18°C), works well.

Humidity near 50% helps in a heated house. The Pure Enrichment MistAire humidifier from Walmart runs up to 25 hours on a tank, and the ThermoPro digital hygrometer can sit by the pot to track both.

Recent findings suggest that on a hot patio, potted citrus can need water every day or two. Indoors, with cooler rooms and less light, it uses much less, and the summer routine can drown the roots. Push a finger about 2 inches (5 cm) into the mix, and water only when it feels dry at that depth. Deep pots are harder to judge, which is where a moisture meter, like the Xlux Long Probe Plant Soil Moisture Meter, is invaluable.

Water until some drains out, then empty the saucer. Set the pot on a few terra-cotta Pot Toes to keep the drainage holes clear. Don't overcorrect, though – a tree left bone dry for long stretches drops flowers and can lose leaves.

Some leaf drop in the first few weeks is common. Less light, drier air, and new temperatures all hit at once, and a few leaves on the floor are just part of it, mostly.

Industry observers note that look closer if leaves keep falling after the first month or whole branches go bare. A wet, sour-smelling mix points to overwatering, while a mix that's dry all the way down means it went too long between drinks. Check how close the tree sits to a vent or cold door, and whether that window is as bright in December as it seemed in October. Sticky spots, webbing, or cottony clumps under the leaves mean pests got past the quarantine.

Industry observers note that get through those six checks, and your tree has an easier winter ahead. Come spring, once nights stay above 50°F (10°C), it can head back out to the patio a little at a time.
