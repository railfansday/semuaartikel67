Patrick: Upcoming article about surge protection by Chad McClung.

Grant referred to a house where he recently stayed as a “tiny house,” describing it as being about 16 ft. x 30 ft. I guess I have lived in a “tiny house” for 20 years! Ours is a Cape, built in 1953 by the people from whom we bought it. It has a 24-ft. x 26-ft. footprint and no dormer.

As discussed on the podcast, there are a lot of great things about a house this size. However, it is, in fact, too small. It is too small to comfortably fit furniture or closets or a family to gather to watch a movie. Our “primary bedroom” is 10 ft. x 11 ft. with a small closet out of that boundary.

Industry observers note that the full house could be arranged to be a very nice size for a couple, but it feels too small for four of us, especially with only one bathroom. I’m not sure how we’ve worked out the lack of closet space and single bathroom with three women plus me, but we have. When my daughters were in high school, I found it emotionally uncomfortable that most of their friends had bigger and nicer homes.

It has been revealed that putting my hurt ego aside, I feel they are lucky to be able to appreciate future living spaces that will likely be more luxurious than they are used to. My oldest daughter definitely had a better bedroom at college and easier access to a bathroom!

Discussion of Chris Briley’s recent low-voltage lighting article on Green Building Advisor.

This article, “Low Voltage Lighting,” is from Chris’ Pretty Good House blog. Chris suggests that given the low energy consumption of LED lighting, do we still need line voltage lighting circuits anymore? Randy, can you briefly explain Chris’s premise? What do you think as an electrician? Does it make sense to switch our wiring methods for modern lighting? Ian, what do you think about this?

When you look at a finished house, what are the small details that make you feel the builder really took the time to do things right?

I’m not talking about expensive finishes or high-end appliances. I’m thinking more about the little things that might not stand out in photos but make a difference once you actually live in the house—clean trim work, well-planned storage, good lighting, quiet doors, sensible outlets, properly finished transitions, etc.

According to recent reports, I’ve noticed that some of the most enjoyable spaces aren’t necessarily the most expensive ones. They just seem to have fewer little annoyances because someone thought through how the space would actually be used.

For those of you who have built or remodeled homes, what is one relatively small detail you’re especially glad you included? And is there anything you wish you’d thought about before construction was finished?

I’m curious whether the things people appreciate most are usually visible design choices or the less noticeable details behind the walls and throughout the house.

We talked about what gives you confidence in a builder, so now let’s talk about what you see most often on construction sites that is done poorly or incorrectly?
