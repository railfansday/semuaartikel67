Nvidia fallout: Commerce targets loophole for China's data center use

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The Commerce Department on Monday moved to close a loophole that has let Chinese companies lease Nvidia's advanced computing power from data centers in third countries, effectively circumventing controls on sensitive chip technology being exported to China.

The interim final rule from Commerce's Bureau of Industry and Security is the first U.S. policy response to the scandal that led Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang to resign last week after reports that he had an affair with an alleged Chinese spy who worked for Megaspeed, one of Nvidia's biggest Asian customers.

The rule was "written by and at the direction of" the Treasury Department and the White House National Security Council, BIS said in a press release.

The rule came after growing criticism of Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick for not doing enough to prevent possible diversion of Nvidia's most advanced chips to China in violation of American export controls.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, in an interview on Friday with CNBC's "Squawk Box," blamed the Commerce Department for not putting Singapore-based Megaspeed on its "entity list," which would restrict certain exports to it.

Share prices of Nvidia and other companies linked to the artificial intelligence trade fell Monday morning after the interim rule was confirmed, among them chipmakers Micron Technology, Intel and Advanced Micro Devices.

The rule is designed "to prevent certain foreign actors from leveraging advanced computing power based on U.S. technology to threaten U.S. national security and foreign policy interests," according to the text of the rule.

The new restrictions "will undermine the ability of companies headquartered in countries of concern from using" advanced artificial intelligence semiconductors produced by U.S. companies to train advanced AI models, the rule claims.

The rule also notes that a license will now "be required to export, reexport, or transfer (in-country) advanced semiconductors to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, or Brazil (and any additional country designated by the Secretary of Commerce, in consultation with the Secretary of State or the Assistant to the President for National Security Affairs) – countries which present a higher-than-normal risk of diversion.".

The New York Times reported on Nov. 9 that American authorities began investigating Singapore-based Megaspeed and one of its executives, Alice Huang, in 2024, "after the tech giant started importing billions of dollars' worth of advanced Nvidia A.I. equipment into Malaysia.".

In a recent development, "Megaspeed had been planning and constructing data centers around Southeast Asia to serve Chinese clients, including Alibaba, the Chinese tech giant," The Times reported that day. "While that behavior is legal, U.S. officials were concerned that some of the Nvidia chips the company had bought were illegally routed into China.".

According to recent reports, jensen Huang resigned as Nvidia CEO last week after reports that he had an affair with Alice Huang, and that she was a spy working for China. The two are not related. China's government has denied the claim that Alice Huang was a spy.

Secretary of State Marco Rubio, who, as national security advisor, leads the National Security Council, warned President Donald Trump last spring that Jensen Huang was in a close relationship with Alice Huang, The Times has reported.

Chris McGuire, a former deputy senior director for technology and national security at the National Security Council, in a post on X called the new rule "a major step forward to countering Chinese smuggling of and access to advanced AI chips.".

According to recent reports, "The work here is not done, as there are some critical loopholes that remain open and need to be closed in a follow-up rule (namely those related to activities inside the United States)," wrote McGuire, a senior fellow at the Council on Foreign Relations. "Commerce should move extremely rapidly to close these. But this is the first time in over 18 months that the administration is starting to take export controls seriously, and does represent a meaningful step forward.".

McGuire stated that the fact that the fresh rule was written by and at the direction of the Treasury Department and the NSC "is remarkable and unprecedented.".

"I have never seen a BIS rule that was not written by BIS," he wrote. "That implies that Commerce was dragged kicking and screaming over the finish line by the White House and Treasury, and instructed to publish this rule rather than the rumored weaker one that it preferred. Remarkable.".

Commerce's newly introduced rule was published a day after Sen. Maria Cantwell of Washington state, the ranking Democrat on the Committee on Commerce, Science and Transportation, in a letter to Lutnick said that Chinese companies such as Alibaba and ByteDance have "used overseas data centers equipped with advanced Nvidia chips to train advanced AI models.".

Industry observers note that "These companies are exploiting a loophole in U.S. export controls by using advanced American chips in foreign data centers even when those chips are restricted from the [People's Republic of China]," Cantwell wrote.

"Despite warnings from the Bureau of Industry and Security that certain arrangements may violate existing restrictions, the Department of Commerce failed to close this loophole, allowing PRC companies to advance their AI capabilities using sensitive American technology," she wrote.
