A re-evaluation of data from the Event Horizon Telescope (EHT) has corroborated the findings that yielded the first-ever photo of a black hole. After the blurry image was released in 2019, sceptics argued that the circular shape seen in the shot was an artefact of the team’s image-reconstruction techniques. Independent astrophysicist Samuel Lai and his team used different methods to reanalyse the original data and their results — including upcoming images of the black hole — largely match those of the EHT team.

The funding that voters in California have approved to earmark for immunology research — the largest ever infusion of cash for science authorized by a public vote in US history. (Nature | 5 min read).

In a recent development, researchers have catalogued almost 100 proteins that get a free pass through the blood–brain barrier to maintain the health of neurons. Most of these proteins were produced in distant organs, particularly the liver. The team found that the transport of some of these proteins is disrupted in mice with a brain disease that mimics early-stage Alzheimer’s disease. The work suggests that the brain actively imports the proteins it needs to stay healthy, and that the breakdown of that transport drives the dysfunction seen in neurodegenerative disease, states neuroscientist and study co-author Andrew Yang.

The launch of the Artemis II mission to orbit the Moon produced unprecedented large-scale atmospheric disturbances, say researchers. Data from GPS receivers found that the Space Launch System — the most powerful rocket that NASA has ever built — disrupted the atmospheric layer known as the ionosphere. The GPS disruption was potentially large enough to “have a strong impact not only on self-driving cars but also on drone positioning,” says space physicist and study co-author Charles Lin.

Reference: Geophysical Research Letters preprint (not peer reviewed).

Industry observers note that the scientific literature contains decades-old papers that detail fraudulent, unethical or merely incorrect results, but aren’t marked as such. Many of these papers are long neglected, but their digitization enables artificial-intelligence tools that summarize scientific literature on a topic to feed misinformation to a fresh generation. This leaves publishers to wrangle with the question of whether these historical papers should be retracted or flagged as suspect — a task that can be tricky if authors are dead, original records can’t be consulted or journals are no longer in print.

Recent findings suggest that “You need a container before you can get the chemistry” that enables cellular life, claims astrobiologist Bruce Damer. He’s among those researchers who favour a ‘lipid-first’ hypothesis for the origin of complex life: that the lipid membrane that corrals the cell is key to its success. Or even that bubbles of lipids, plus the chemistry they enclosed, could have propagated and evolved in a Darwinian-like way, before RNA (or DNA) arrived on the scene.

Social scientists should be involved in the process of finding suitable locations for new observatories, argues anthropologist Jaco de Swart, co-principal investigator of a proposed neutrino detector to be built in the Peruvian Andes. Many suitable locations for such projects are sensitive ecological environments with cultural significance for Indigenous communities. Social scientists can help to integrate environmental care into these projects and build relationships with local and Indigenous stakeholders. “To build scientific infrastructure that is responsible, just and resilient, humanities scholars must have leadership roles from the start,” de Swart writes.

Appearing in the Jurassic Park franchise is the strongest predictor of a dinosaur species’ popularity, according to latest research — an example of the ‘blockbuster effect’. The media should leverage this effect to put a spotlight on endangered species, indicates zoologist Pâmela Lima. (Science | 7 min read).
