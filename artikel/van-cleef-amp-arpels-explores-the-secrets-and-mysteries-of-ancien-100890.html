Van Cleef & Arpels Explores the Secrets and Mysteries of Ancient Egypt

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

After archaeologist Howard Carter and his team discovered King Tutankhamun’s tomb in 1922, regal diadems, golden collars, and bold cuffs brought a new wave of Egyptian Revival into the Art Deco movement. Van Cleef & Arpels took note and added Egyptian-inspired calligraphy, hieroglyphs, and semiprecious stones into its lexicon of deco bracelets, sautoirs, and brooches, bringing a historic feel to the frostbitten glamour that defined the roaring ’20s. Now, over a century later, the maison’s new Fascinating Egypt high jewelry collection explores more avenues of the region’s mystical splendor.

Sources indicate that from opulent collars to sculptural rings, each design harkens back to the arts, values, and beliefs that defined the Newly introduced Kingdom. But just as Egyptologists explored hidden messages beneath the grandeur of the 18th dynasty, Van Cleef conveys the mystique beneath its history. Shape-shifting necklaces function like secret passageways to new realms of expression, and Mysterioux brooches reincarnate sacred deities in gold and gemstones. Just take the Bennou, a terrestrial vessel for the sun god Ra, whose feathers of emeralds, marine-blue sapphires, lapis, cerulean slips of turquoise, mother of pearl, and drops of diamonds represent the colors of the earth. A blood-orange spessartite garnet with rays of diamonds hangs from a red spinel, like a drop of sunshine.

According to recent reports, “There are a lot of real animals that were fashioned into amulets and small statues, likely to imbue the owners with some of their traits, but also to revere the creature and the benefits it provided,” archaeologist Randall Hixenbaugh tells Bazaar. His namesake gallery in Manhattan’s Design District is home to artifacts sourced in accordance with the ethical standards set by the International Association of Dealers in Ancient Arts.

Talismans were worn like supplements to enhance the human experience—cats to spark joy, mongooses to offer protection from snakes, and frogs for fertility. Stones of certain colors were valued for their connotations with abundance and immortality. “The Egyptians prized bright blue and green materials, which were associated with the Nile, the sky, and life itself. Lapis lazuli was rare and valuable and therefore associated with the pharaohs. Its extensive use on King Tut’s gold mask highlights how highly regarded it was,” Hixenbaugh explains.

The cobalt stone finds its way into all kinds of Van Cleef’s latest creations. It’s used generously in the “Ornement de Saphir” necklace, whose central totem resembles an evil eye, with an oval-cut sapphire pupil plopped cleverly in an iris of pavé diamonds. Suspended from a panel of lapis lazuli interspersed with slivers of gold and baguette-cut rubies, the pendant is detachable and can be worn like a brooch, while its Corde chain incorporates clasps concealed beneath colorful rondelles that allow it to transform into a pair of bracelets. A concept conceived in the 1940s, the versatile signature speaks to the ongoing conversation between the house’s heritage and Egypt’s mystique.

Sources indicate that some of Van Cleef’s most striking interpretations of the ancient kingdom come through in Art Deco bracelets. The articulated cuff style was a staple of the 1920s, with ample real estate for planes of illustrious motifs to flourish. Instead of a painter’s palette, Van Cleef brought diamonds, emeralds, and rubies together like dots on a pointillist painting through the “mystery set” technique (a manner of setting gemstones sans prongs).

According to recent reports, the house patented the method in the 1930s and has continued to enlist it to craft seamless gemscapes, extending into a series of “Paysage” bracelets. Clad in fields of gemstones, the deco-style pieces bring Egyptian royals, celestial skies, and monuments to life. Openwork, a technique that minimizes the use of solid metals, enables light to pass through each bejeweled mural, amplifying the high shine of each cuff. Other inconspicuous elements, like golden hieroglyphic nuggets, rest beneath articulated columns, like artifacts awaiting discovery.

Sources indicate that the house’s bold fonts of design can be equally understood through the juxtaposition of lolly-like cabochons and sugar-white diamonds. Amid the sea of platinum and flashy Asscher cuts that defined Gatsby-era decadence, Van Cleef & Arpels brought teardrop emeralds into the mix. Suspended from pillars of diamonds set in platinum, the plump stones hung like enticing lozenges throughout a collaret necklace, circa 1929. The spellbinding masterpiece drew the eye of Egyptian royalty, Her Royal Highness Princess Faiza, who purchased the necklace in 1937. It was a convergence of brilliant facets and vivid, dome-shaped gems that bridged the gradual shift into midcentury modern shapes and matte stones. The provenance of the design influenced the maison’s updated rendition of the crown jewel, featuring luscious emeralds and slender pearls suspended from elongated pendants of white gold and diamonds.

In a recent development, by the 1950s, Van Cleef’s interpretations of Egyptomania embraced organic forms, space-age ellipses, and plots of semiprecious stones. Smooth, matte surfaces of turquoise, malachite, and coral enhanced the realism of biomorphic designs, like a scarab clip with wings of turquoise, circa 1957. (The house would lend the brooch to the Petit Palais for the debut of “Tutankhamun and His Time” nearly a decade later.) Such vibrant stones extended into contemporary aesthetics of the 1980s, such as a collar necklace slathered in slices of jade, stripes of onyx, and birds carved out of coral.

This collection revisited the era through the language of Ettore Sottsass and the Memphis Group, a 1980s decorative arts firm, for spherical shapes and elements of cubism to take flight. Some of Sottsass’s most recognizable pieces include a sterling silver fruit stand with zigzag pillars and a colorblocked bookstand named “The Carlton.” Van Cleef translates this friction between elements smooth and sharp, glossy and matte into pieces like a saucer-shaped Mythe Celeste clip, coated in sheets of lapis and malachite with buttons of coral—elements beheld by the Egyptians as modalities of abundance and immortality. By contrast, a Frise Divine ring juxtaposes an oval-shaped Sri Lankan sapphire with a rigid tile of coral, and rows of diamonds set in zigzag streams of gold.

All creations in the Fascinating Egypt œuvre are united by tradition, a preservation of heritage echoed in the foundations of other art forms, like ballet. In fact, the house’s seamless dances of emeralds, rubies, and diamonds compelled George Balanchine to choreograph Jewels for the New York City Ballet in 1931—a moment that came full circle when the jewelers debuted a collection of ballerina clips and brooches a couple of years later. The nature of their anatomically sound arms, gilded in gold and ribbons of gemstones, extends into the collection’s Muse Éternelle brooches. Modeled after Cleopatra, the palm-sized figures are enrobed in a variety of dazzling threads of gold, strewn with diamonds, sapphires, rubies, and emeralds.
