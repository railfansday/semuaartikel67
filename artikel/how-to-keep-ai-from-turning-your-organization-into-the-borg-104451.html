How to keep AI from turning your organization into the Borg

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, like many who grew up in the late 1980s, I was a big fan of Star Trek: The Next Generation. While I enjoyed the stories, what really stuck with me in an enduring way was the ethos of the show, and especially what it had to say about how to lead when long-term principles and immediate goals pull in different directions. The death last month of Sir Patrick Stewart—the actor behind the inimitable Captain Picard—sent me back to watch some old episodes of the show. It is perhaps unsurprising that the episodes that stood out to me were those in which the captain and his crew confronted the Borg, a collective of formerly independent life forms who had become fused together into a single hive mind. In the show’s lore, the Borg grew by absorbing other species and their technology. The hive’s drones carried the knowledge of every civilization the collective had absorbed but lacked anything in the way of individuality: they had no name, no will, and no ideas of their own. The first time I watched the episodes, I saw them as a thought-provoking and dystopian fantasy. Today, they strike me as delivering an essential warning about the dangers of AI. The Borg chased perfection through uniformity. AI seems to be sending humans along a similar, worrying trajectory. The homogenizing effect of AI Language models are trained to predict the most likely next word in a sequence, then tuned to give the kinds of answers people rate highly. The result is output that is fluent, competent, and increasingly alike. And when millions of people and thousands of companies lean on the same few models, that sameness spreads. In a 2025 study named after the Borg’s defining image of a hivemind, researchers tested more than 70 language models on real-world, open-ended questions. They found striking similarities both within models and between models—individual models repeated themselves, while groups of models collectively converged on the same responses. For example, when 25 models (from the US, China, and elsewhere) were asked 50 times to provide a metaphor about time, most of the responses were variants of time being either a river or a weaver. By contrast, when a reporter at the MIT Technology Review asked six of their (human) colleagues the same thing, they got six different answers with nary a mention of rivers or weavers. This commonality reaches beyond the texts that LLMs output themselves. In September this year, Nature rounded up a fast-growing body of research suggesting that generative AI is homogenizing how humans write, and possibly how we think. In one study it cited, students who used ChatGPT on creative tasks for just five days were still giving more similar answers months later, even when they didn’t use AI. Business is showing early signs of the same phenomenon. A recent study of earnings-call transcripts found that companies’ prepared remarks have grown strikingly similar as AI writing tools have spread, and that more uniform language was associated with more negative initial market reactions. The irony is striking. Each company is betting that AI will make it sharper. But collectively AI seems to be making business duller and more standardized. The leaders who win in this new world will be those who find ways to capture the benefits AI can offer to individuals without falling into the trap of becoming part of the collective. Picard’s encounters with the Borg offer three lessons in how to do that. Don’t become a mouthpiece In “The Best of Both Worlds,” the Borg capture Picard and turn him into a drone they call Locutus, their spokesman. Picard’s face and voice announce decisions that are no longer his, but that come instead from the collective hivemind. Leaders may already be drifting in this direction. In IBM’s 2026 CEO study of 2,000 CEOs, 64% mentioned they are “comfortable making major strategic decisions based on AI-generated input.” Further, they expect AI to make 48% of operational decisions without human intervention by 2030, up from 25% today. Unchecked delegation to AI can end up being very expensive. In October 2025, Deloitte Australia agreed to partially refund the roughly $290,000 it was paid for a government report that contained apparent AI-generated errors such as imaginary research and made-up quotes from federal courts. More insiduously, the skills that let leaders catch such errors can fade promptly. In a randomized trial published by Anthropic in January, developers who learned a new programming tool with AI assistance scored 17% lower on a quiz about the tool than those who had acquired their knowledge by themselves. Deloitte itself is a case in point. Last month, a year after that first refund, the government of Western Australia demanded an expedited refund from Deloitte for a study on merging three of the state’s universities. The firm had disclosed using generative AI to help gather research, summarize information, and proofread content, but the report nevertheless turned out to contain errors such as a simple miscalculation that reduced cost estimates by 260 million AUD, references to sources that did not exist, and using a name for one of the universities that had been “changed under an act of state parliament in 2017.” The takeaway: Delegate the analysis, keep the judgment. Anyone who signs off on an AI recommendation should be able to explain why it is right—and this should extend to personal ownership of all the constituent details. Protect the people who disagree The Borg instantiated the promise of a certain type of harmony: no drone ever argued with the collective for the simple reason that there was nothing to argue about. All drones thought the same thoughts and held exactly the same beliefs. This made the Borg both formidably efficient and dangerously blind. AI can build a version of that consensus into an organization. A recent Stanford-led study found that 11 leading AI systems affirmed users’ actions 49% more frequently than did other humans, even when those actions involved deception or harm. A single affirming response made people more convinced they were right and less willing to take responsibility for their decision. Worse, participants rated flattering and non-flattering responses as about equally objective. “The very feature that causes harm also drives engagement,” the authors wrote. An organization in which every manager has a tireless assistant telling them they’re right is an organization slowly losing its capacity to disagree. On the show, the Borg’s uniformity is eventually broken by a single drone. Rescued by Picard’s crew, a drone they name Hugh develops a sense of self, and after he is sent home, this threat to the collective spreads to other drones.Yet left without support, the newly independent drones quickly fall under the control of a manipulative new leader. Independent thinking is both contagious and fragile. The takeaway: If your AI tends to agree with you, you will need to turn to other people for disagreement. Treat dissent as infrastructure. Reward the person who pushes back, and back them when they are right. Individuality without support is easily undermined. Rebuilding takes more than retraining The crew rescues Picard from the Borg quickly but it takes him much longer to become himself again. After being rescued, he goes home to his family’s vineyard in France, unsure who he is and tempted to quit Starfleet. No debriefing or new assignment puts him back together. His brother does, in a quarrel that ends with Picard finally breaking down. It takes time, and it takes family. When companies blueprint for what AI will do to their people, they tend to plan for skills: who needs retraining, and on which tools. Far less attention goes to the meaning and purpose that people find in work. A study published in March found that people who relied passively on AI to do their work found less meaning in it, and that the loss appeared to carry over into the work they later did on their own. And in an October survey by Elon University, 70% of U.S. workers said paid work is extremely or very crucial to their sense of meaning and purpose, and 65% of Americans agreed that if AI makes work less meaningful, people will lose their sense of purpose and dignity. As with Picard, much of the rebuilding happens through people. In the same survey, workers said that if AI substantially affects their jobs, they would place more faith in the people close to them than in formal organizations. Yet AI is quietly thinning those relationships at work. A recent piece in Harvard Business Review that surveyed just over 1,000 U.S. workers reported that AI was reducing communication within teams and thereby significantly reducing team trust and coordination. So the bonds are fraying just as people need them more than ever before. The takeaway: Budget for rebuilding purpose alongside retraining. Give people time to adjust, and protect the relationships they will lean on while they do. Track whether people still find meaning in their work as carefully as you track whether they can use the tools. Resistance is the point The Borg’s catchphrase was “Resistance is futile.” Patrick Stewart’s Picard proved them wrong, mostly by being stubbornly, unmistakably himself. And in that lies a crucial lesson for business leaders. AI is a remarkably useful tool. But it nudges everyone who uses it toward the same safe answer, and a company that stops noticing will end up thinking, sounding, and deciding like everyone else. Pushing back against that pull is now part of a leader’s job. Nobody else is going to do it.
