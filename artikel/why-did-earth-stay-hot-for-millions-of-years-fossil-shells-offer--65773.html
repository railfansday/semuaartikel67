Why did Earth stay hot for millions of years? Fossil shells offer a clue

A weaker carbon pump in the Southern Ocean may explain about half of the rise in atmospheric carbon dioxide during an ancient warm period.

The Southern Ocean helps move carbon from the atmosphere into the deep sea.

Around 50 million years ago, during one of Earth’s warmest periods in the past 66 million years, that process appears to have weakened.

Chemical traces in fossil shells suggest that microscopic plants at the ocean’s surface used a smaller share of the nutrients available to them than they did in cooler periods before and after.

That left more carbon dioxide in the atmosphere, according to the study’s model.

The model suggests that the weaker carbon pump and the feedbacks it amplified could explain about half of the rise in atmospheric carbon dioxide at the time.

Simone Moretti of the Max Planck Institute for Chemistry in Mainz, Germany, led the study. His co-authors came from the Université libre de Bruxelles (ULB), the University of California, Santa Cruz and other institutions.

“Our study reveals that the Southern Ocean played a major role in regulating atmospheric CO2 during the early Eocene, millions of years before the onset of the recent ice ages,” Moretti mentioned.

Recent findings suggest that the Early Eocene Climatic Optimum ran from about 53 to 49 million years ago. Global temperatures were more than 18°F (10°C) higher than now, and carbon dioxide reached around 1,600 parts per million.

The causes of those levels are still debated. Volcanoes and reduced burial of organic carbon have both been proposed, but the timing of the eruptions lines up poorly with the warming.

So the team looked at the ocean’s biological carbon pump. Microscopic plants near the surface take up carbon dioxide as they grow. When they die, some of that carbon sinks and stays in the deep ocean for hundreds to thousands of years.

In a recent development, today the pump runs below its full potential. Deep water rising around Antarctica brings up nutrients that the plants never fully use.

Plankton prefer the lighter form of nitrogen when they take up nitrate, their main nutrient. The more completely they use the supply, the heavier the leftover nitrogen becomes.

It has been revealed that the nitrogen signature ends up in the shells of foraminifera, single-celled organisms that eat the plankton. The team measured the nitrogen trapped in fossil foraminifera from four sediment cores drilled by the Ocean Drilling Program.

Two cores came from the Southern Ocean. Two came from the mid-latitude Atlantic and Indian oceans, where nutrients are used up almost completely.

The gap between the two pairs of sites demonstrates how much nutrient the Southern Ocean plants left behind. It was widest exactly when temperatures and carbon dioxide peaked, and it narrowed or vanished before and after.

The team then ran a simple model of the ocean and atmosphere using the estimated 40% drop in the share of available nutrients that plankton used.

On its own, the drop raised carbon dioxide from 700 to about 900 parts per million.

When the researchers included the effects of warming and changes in seawater chemistry, atmospheric carbon dioxide reached about 1,100 parts per million.

That increase accounts for about half of the roughly 800 parts per million rise reconstructed for the period.

The leak hit harder than it would today. Eocene seawater was more acidic and richer in calcium, so it had less capacity to absorb a change in carbon dioxide.

“Because Eocene seawater had a much lower capacity to buffer changes in CO2 than today’s ocean, a weakening of the biological carbon pump could have had a far greater impact on atmospheric CO2 than under present-day conditions,” said François Fripiat, a co-author at ULB.

Over millions of years, rain and rock weathering normally pull carbon dioxide back out of the air and act as a thermostat.

The early Eocene had no Alps or Himalayas yet, and its flat, deeply weathered continents offered little fresh rock.

“With Earth’s natural thermostat operating less efficiently, the additional CO2 released from the ocean could remain in the atmosphere for much longer,” stated Mathis Hain, a co-author at the University of California, Santa Cruz.

“This would allow the Southern Ocean to influence climate not just over thousands of years, as during the ice ages, but over millions of years,” he remarked.

The Southern Ocean also released carbon dioxide during warm spells between recent ice ages.

The authors interpret the match across very different timescales as a sign of a dependable feedback.

The shells record that the plants left nutrients unused, but not why. Stronger mixing around Antarctica could have brought up more deep water, or the plants could have grown less.

Industry observers note that the authors argued that iron supply, which limits plant growth there today, likely changed little in a warmer world. That leaves less room for the growth explanation.

The estimate of the nutrient drop is deliberately conservative, because one of the mid-latitude sites falls between the others in its values.

And the carbon model is a simple one, built to show the size of the effect rather than reproduce the Eocene ocean.

The open question is whether the modern Southern Ocean will respond the same way.

Simulations run for centuries to thousands of years suggest deep-water formation near Antarctica strengthens once the deep ocean catches up with surface warming, which would match the Eocene pattern.

The team is now exploring the same feedback in other periods of sustained warming. The paper names the Middle Eocene, the Middle Miocene and the Pliocene as candidates.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Geoscience.
