Industry observers note that two thousand years ago, the Tamil poet Avvaiyar wrote a line that every child in Tamil Nadu still learns: Arithu, arithu, maanidaraai pirathal arithu. Rare, rare it is to be born human. And then she went further. Rarer still, she said, to be born whole, without the body failing you from the start.

I think about that verse every November, and this year more than most.

Industry observers note that in the past month, our team published a randomized pilot trial of yogic breathing and meditation for adults with long COVID, and a study in people living with Parkinson's disease. When you sit with these patients, the suffering is not abstract. A woman in the long COVID trial told our interviewers she had developed “a mental block about breathing because of all the breathing problems.”.

Think about what it means to be afraid of your own breath.

What we offered them was modest: a few minutes of breathing practice and a guided meditation, taught online, done at home. We did not cure anyone. The data showed that breathlessness eased by a clinically meaningful margin, fatigue and mood improved, and nobody was harmed. Temporary relief. Less anxiety. A way to live more comfortably with a condition that isn't going away.

In a recent development, and when you see them at one level, you feel something else, which is harder to say out loud. You feel fortunate. You were born, for now at least, without that burden. You have been handed the capacity to experience this life in its full depth, and most days, you forget to use it.

I am a physician scientist, so let me be precise about what we know. Across the published studies from our center, the measures move in one direction. Miracle of Mind, a single seven-minute guided meditation, shifted self-rated affect in 1,325 people, with the largest shifts in those who started lowest. High-density EEG in 103 people showed brain rhythms begin to reorganize within two to three minutes of the same practice and peak around seven. Long-term practitioners carry brains that read about six years younger than their chronological age on sleep EEG. And in the long COVID trial, three weeks of practice moved breathlessness, fatigue, and mood against a waitlist control.

None of this is mystical. It is what happens to attention, emotion, and physiology when a person turns inward in a structured way. Meditation has a distinct origin and an enormous body of technical work from India, and it belongs to everyone, East or West. At its core, it raises awareness. And raising awareness is the most reliable tool I know for the stress and anxiety that come with simply being alive right now.

Which brings me to the machines.

Sources indicate that we are living through the construction of a trillion-dollar artificial intelligence industry. These systems solve mathematical problems that defeat most of us, and they connect islands of knowledge in ways no single human mind can. They matter. They will make medicine safer, work easier, and life more comfortable, and I use them every day.

In a recent development, what this industry has really done is put very strong technology into the hands of the common person. A capability that belonged to institutions a few years ago now sits in a teenager's pocket. Some of us may even be over-equipped, holding more power than our habits are prepared for. It is a knife with two edges. At one end, it accelerates learning in ways that would have seemed miraculous to my teachers. At the other, it can be misused, carelessly or deliberately, at a scale we have not seen before.

Recent findings suggest that that makes this the moment to hold the instrument with precise awareness. No regulator, and no model, can do that for the person holding it. It is left to us. And that is why raising individual awareness and individual consciousness is not a spiritual luxury alongside the technology. It is the one investment that keeps pace with it.

Industry observers note that but the machines are not going to give us the human experience. The profoundness we are seeking does not arrive through an interface. It has to come from within. There is no evidence that these systems have such experiences themselves, and we do not know how we would tell if they did. What they can give is comfort. What they cannot give is the goosebumps.

Sources indicate that consider basketball. Analytics tell every team to shoot the three, and on average, the teams that listen win more regular season games. Then the playoffs arrive, and something else takes over. Composure. Fear. The ability to stay present when the arithmetic claims you should already have lost. Teams built on the average falter there, because the average was never the game. The same is true of art, of music, of the moment a surgeon's hands know something before the mind does. The human experience is where greatness lives, and it is not a calculation.

The yogic tradition described the mind with a precision we are only now catching up to. It named 16 parts in four categories: buddhi, the intellect that dissects and decides; ahankara, the sense of identity that tells the intellect whose side it is on; manas, the vast silo of memory, running back through evolution; and chitta, pure intelligence, unsullied by memory, the dimension that is simply aware.

Industry observers note that A machine can be built to imitate the first three. It can reason, be given a persona, and hold more memory than any of us. Chitta is a different matter. That is the intelligence no one can touch, and it is the ground on which every meditation practice stands.

The same tradition that mapped the mind also told us how to live in it, and the instructions are more practical than people expect.

Sadhguru, my spiritual master, claims: Be a mother to the whole world. That is not sentiment. It is a standard. Absolute inclusion, absolute compassion, extended without first checking whether the other person deserves it. No algorithm optimizes for that, because it is not an outcome. It is a way of being.

Then there is acceptance, which is widely misread as passivity. Consider your work. You may or may not get the promotion you seek or the position you have earned. You have only two choices. Stay, and keep raising your capability until the question answers itself. Or step back, look at the whole picture, and if the picture states so, leave. Both are acceptance. What acceptance rules out is the third option most of us choose: staying and resenting it.

In a recent development, and there is agency. Sadhguru notes, every action you take is yours. Every reaction, too. The moment you accept that, you stop waiting for the world to arrange itself before you can be well, and you become, in the most literal sense, the author of your own experience.

It has been revealed that inclusion, acceptance, agency. These are enormous pieces of intelligence, worked out over millennia by people who had no instruments beyond their own attention. The machines can remind us of them. They cannot practice them for us. That part, like the goosebumps, is ours.

In a recent development, so, this Thanksgiving, I am grateful for the things my instruments can measure, and more grateful for the things they cannot. I am grateful to have been born human, and whole enough to sit on a cushion and close my eyes. I am grateful for the patients who do the practice anyway, breath by careful breath. And I am grateful for a tradition that asks me to be a mother to the whole world and gives me the tools to try. Above all, I am grateful for the goosebumps that arrive, unbidden, in nearly every meditation, the surest evidence I have that something here no industry will replicate.

If you have seven minutes this week, spend them with your eyes closed doing the Miracle of Mind. The machines will still be there when you open them. So, I hope you will.
