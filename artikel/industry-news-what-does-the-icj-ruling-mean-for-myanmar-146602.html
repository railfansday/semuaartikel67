ICJ: Myanmar Committed Genocide.

The state of Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya in 2017, the International Court of Justice (ICJ), the main judicial arm of the United Nations, ruled last Thursday.

Industry observers note that the ruling documents how, following attacks by the Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army (ARSA), Myanmar carried out a campaign of genocidal violence against the Rohingya. It estimates that 10,000 Rohingya, about 1.2 percent of the total population, were killed.

The court ruled 9-6 that the “only reasonable inference” was that this violence revealed genocidal intent to destroy the Rohingya as a group—not just drive them out of Myanmar or exact collective punishment.

The ruling orders Myanmar to cease violence against the group and prosecute those responsible, allow the safe return of Rohingya to their homes, and pay compensation.

Sources indicate that sadly, none of this is likely. The junta has rejected the judgment as “unfair.”.

Meanwhile, another armed group called the Arakan Army, which controls most of Rakhine province and mainly represents ethnic Rakhine Buddhists, has also committed large-scale violence against the Rohingya. Fighting between the Arakan Army and Rohingya militant groups—which has seen the Rohingya militants, including the ARSA, occasionally cooperate with the junta—has added to the hostility.

The Arakan Army has made some overtures to Muslim communities in the province.

For now, though, the Arakan Army is taking a wait-and-see attitude to the ICJ judgment. A spokesperson told local media, “We are constantly monitoring the current developments. We will stand firmly on truth and justice and take appropriate action for the benefit of the Arakan region.”.

The country’s National Unity Government (NUG)—representing the civilian government overthrown by the junta in 2021 and associated with some of the main rebel forces—endorsed the judgment.

Sources indicate that this was not a given. When Aung San Suu Kyi, an icon of the NUG, was acting as the country’s de facto leader, Myanmar consistently denied that the military’s actions against the Rohingya constituted a genocide.

Recent findings suggest that in opposition, the NUG may now find its position easier. Its statement focused heavily on the junta’s culpability.

It has been revealed that but it may find the whole judgment hard to accept. The ruling finds the state of Myanmar—not just the military—guilty of genocide. And at that time when the most severe offense took place, in 2016 and 2017, the paramount power in the state apparatus was Aung San Suu Kyi.

In Southeast Asia, could this be a stumbling block for junta leader Min Aung Hlang’s push for diplomatic normalization with the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN)?

Thailand—the most vocal advocate for Myanmar’s full readmission to ASEAN—looks unmoved. “Thailand must take the position that best serves the country,” Prime Minister Anutin Charnvirakul told reporters.

Thai Foreign Minister Sihasak Phuangketkeow argued that Thailand’s engagement did not contradict the ruling, as Thailand’s aim was to curb the current violence.

Meanwhile, two other countries—Malaysia and the Philippines—have issued official statements on the matter.

Malaysia’s brief statement mentioned that it “respects the judgment.” The Philippines’ statement seemed a shade weaker, saying it “acknowledges the judgment” and, unlike Malaysia, does not mention the Rohingya by name.

Other ASEAN countries have yet to make official statements.

Still, for countries opposed to Myanmar’s full readmission to the bloc—most notably Singapore, which serves as the ASEAN chair next year—the judgment will reinforce this skepticism.

Industry observers note that on the global stage, China has objected. “China has concerns over complications such as overreach of jurisdiction and expansive interpretation, and believes such practices are not conducive to dispute resolution,” a spokesperson stated.

In a recent development, regarding the United States, this judgment—plus the results of the country’s midterms—will likely impede the U.S. push toward normalization.

Parts of the American right reject the judgment. While the U.S. government declared in 2022that the anti-Rohingya violence constituted a genocide, some on the right are concerned that the ICJ judgments lay the groundwork for a finding of genocide against Israel, linked to its war in Gaza.

And the Myanmar junta clearly still thinks that it might find a sympathetic ear from the Trump administration.

When objecting to the ICJ ruling, a spokesperson for the Myanmar military cited a recent joint statement by U.S. President Donald Trump, Chinese President Xi Jinping, and Russian President Vladimir Putin that the International Criminal Court “must not abuse” its jurisdiction.

While the International Criminal Court is distinct from the ICJ, the spokesperson for Ming Aung Hlaing drew a parallel.

In a recent development, trump deals details. On Thursday evening, the White House disclosed the details of some of the various deals struck at last week’s ASEAN summit.

The ASEAN deals, worth a total of $17 billion, include a $12 billion sale of Boeing aircraft to Philippine Airlines.

The United States is also looking at providing $2 billion in loans for Philippine defense purchases and $400 million in military financing (though much of the funding was already committed).

Thailand will also purchase $123 million of High Mobility Artillery Rocket Systems (HIMARS) from the United States.

The terms of the Thai deal, which are not yet final, are already provoking opposition there. Many in the agricultural powerhouse are unhappy with the prospect of more agricultural imports from the United States—in particular, pork. The government is on the defense, saying that it’s the best deal it could get.

Amid controversy over the price tag, Thailand has also insisted that the HIMARS purchase is unrelated to the trade deal and was driven by national security considerations.

The Malaysian government also announced that a U.S. consortium will help develop a sovereign artificial intelligence cloud for the Malaysian government, with the United States offering $50 million in support.

In a recent development, EU-Indonesia trade deal. On Saturday, the European Union and Indonesia signed a trade deal that was 10 years in the making.

It has been revealed that under the terms of the deal, both sides will remove import duties on about 90 percent of products, including EU automotives and most agricultural exports. The products not included will see their tariffs gradually reduced or receive tariff-free quotas.

Industry observers note that indonesia expects that clothing, footwear, and palm oil will receive the biggest export boosts. It also hopes that the deal will boost European investment in Indonesia in sectors such as critical minerals, batteries, electric vehicles, pharmaceuticals, and electronics.

The deal also addresses a substantial number of nontariff barriers that often bedevil foreign products entering Indonesia.

It has been revealed that the EU’s rules on deforestation, long a sticking point for Indonesia, have been finessed. While Indonesia did not secure an exemption, the EU left the door open to accepting Indonesia’s domestic standards.

The deal comes as both the EU and Indonesia are looking to expand their global trade partnerships to offset U.S. tariffs.

Even more deals. The Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit in China last week also saw a flurry of dealmaking.

Singaporean Prime Minister Lawrence Wong and Xi met and confirmed negotiations to upgrade the two country’s free trade agreement.

Indonesia and China mentioned they will launch formal negotiations for a comprehensive economic partnership.

They also declared strategic partnerships worth $71.8 billion, including the development of 10 “smart cities” by a partnership between Indonesian state-asset fund Danantara and a Chinese state-owned enterprise as well as deals involving a Chinese appliance maker and a consortium of other Chinese organizations investing $20 billion, also in partnership with Danantara.

Meanwhile, Vietnam revealed intends to deepen energy cooperation with Russia in both fossil fuels and nuclear energy.

Last week, shortly before Trump jetted off to the Philippines and then China, the United States announced latest controls on chip exports following the Jensen Huang scandal.

A license is now needed to export, reexport, or transfer advanced semiconductors to five countries: Brazil, Indonesia, Singapore, Malaysia, and Thailand.

Additionally, data centers, foreign foundries, and chip exports are expected to perform due diligence to check if their customers are owned or controlled by U.S.-embargoed destinations—China, in this case.

And the measures close the loophole of selling chips to data centers outside of China that provide processing power to companies in China.

Industry observers note that for countries that have been cashing in on the AI boom, this represents a major challenge. Southeast Asia has emerged as major data center hub. The new regulations will disrupt this.

At least two partners of Nvidia that design equipment used in data centers powered by its chips have suspended shipments to Southeast Asia, according to reporting by Nikkei Asia.

Recent findings suggest that nikkei Asia also reported that Taiwanese chipmaker Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Tech giant has already put several orders by Chinese and non-Chinese customers on hold. The regulations are so strict that they threaten to disrupt not just data centers but also smartphones, consumer electronics, and autonomous cars produced in Southeast Asia’s major electronics industry.

In Malaysia, tech stocks sold off sharply in reaction to the news. Industry players also worried that it could seriously impact the country’s chip design industry.

Thai business, meanwhile, is concerned that the regulations could put the brakes on the country’s booming data center industry.

According to recent reports, reactions by governments have been mixed. Malaysian Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim complained in an interview with the South China Morning Post that the United States was harder to work with than China, as the latter did not pick sides.

Wong, the Singaporean prime minister, signaled a potential crackdown, saying that countries “abusing Singapore’s trusted position” to circumvent export controls was a “real concern.” However, he also made clear that Singapore expected to still work with both the United States and China.

But hovering over all of this is this question: Will these rules last?

Huang is now back at Nvidia, backed by Trump. The president, it seems—despite the best efforts of his administration’s hawks—remains unconvinced by chip controls.
