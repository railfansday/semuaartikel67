📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Few medical providers are adequately trained to treat menopause, a latest study finds, and even fewer understand how hormonal changes aggravate ADHD symptoms.

In a recent development, suffering through menopausal hot flashes, night sweats, sleep disturbances, mood shifts, and memory problems? Wondering why your ADHD medication has suddenly become less effective? Considering a visit with your doctor to better understand and treat these deeply disruptive symptoms?

Sadly, your healthcare provider may be less informed than you might hope.

A disturbing but unsurprising analysis, published recently in The Lancet Obstetrics, Gynecology, & Women’s Health, found that many healthcare professionals are ill-equipped to provide responsive, coordinated, evidence-based treatment and support to help women navigate this often-challenging stage of life. It cited inadequate medical training, paltry research, and persistent gaps in knowledge as reasons for the “fragmented care.”1.

The analysis of 89 studies on health system responsiveness across the U.S. and several other countries also found that women in marginalized groups disproportionately experienced barriers to care and received limited information about available treatment options by providers.

A woman who has gone 12 months without a menstrual period is in menopause. The median age for menopause is 51, though many women with ADHD are known to experience the onset earlier.

Declining estrogen levels during perimenopause and menopause are associated with reduced dopamine, leading to worsening ADHD symptoms and emotional dysregulation in many women – even those who had previously managed their ADHD symptoms well with medication, according to a separate study published in the Journal of Attention Disorders.

Recent findings suggest that in surveys conducted over recent years, thousands of women have disclosed to ADDitude how midlife hormonal changes exacerbated their ADHD symptoms and adversely affected every area of their lives, from work performance and sleep to relationships with family and friends. Many said they sought help from providers who had limited knowledge and few clinical studies to consult.

“Perimenopause and menopause play a significant role in my inattentive ADHD, and I wish more was known by those who diagnose and treat us,” said Cindy, a ADDitude reader from Santa Clara, California.

“The fact that females are massively underdiagnosed and understudies is horrible but not surprising,” said another ADDitude reader who was diagnosed with ADHD at age 38. “How symptoms may change during perimenopause and menopause, how this affects your home life as a wife and mother, is not talked about enough.”.

“It’s insane,” noted another ADDitude reader, “and nobody is helping us to treat the super storm of symptoms, including brain fog, exhaustion, headaches, and so on.”.

Menopausal hormone therapy is considered the most effective treatment for alleviating common symptoms and is often recommended for women experiencing adverse effects of perimenopause. Non-hormonal medications and complementary approaches, including cognitive behavioral therapy, have also shown benefits in symptom management, the Lancet analysis noted.
