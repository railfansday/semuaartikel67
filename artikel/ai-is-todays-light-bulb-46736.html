📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, in 2004, I set out to do something many in the energy business thought was crazy: digitize the last mile of the electric grid—the wires and meters between the substation and the homes and businesses it serves. My bet was that internet protocol, the common language of the world’s computers, would eventually connect the grid too. Once it did, every meter and sensor could talk to the software running it. Plenty of smart people told me it would never happen. Utilities, like pipeline, rail, and water operators, initiative in decades; they cannot afford to get reliability wrong. So the real work was finding the handful of utility leaders willing to go first. Two decades later, more than three out of four electric meters in the United States are smart meters, over 128 million of them, according to the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission. I bring this up because I believe we are at the start of something much bigger now, that reaches beyond the grid to every system the economy runs on. AI is today’s light bulb: It’s what everyone can see. But the revolution is the network underneath it, and most leaders are looking at the wrong part. EDISON’S REAL INVENTION WAS THE NETWORK When Thomas Edison switched on his Pearl Street Station in lower Manhattan on September 4, 1882, he was selling light. Within a month, 59 customers were using it to light about 400 lamps. By 1884, it served more than 500 customers. In The Age of Edison, historian Ernest Freeberg indicates that what remade American cities wasn’t the light bulb itself but what it required. Every building needed a wire, and every wire needed a power supply behind it. Once that network was in place, it outgrew its first purpose almost immediately. Electric light, along with the electric elevator, allowed buildings to grow. Factories ran through the night. Cities stayed awake after dark. Then came the unexpected uses. The first household appliances had nowhere to plug in, so people screwed fans and irons into the sockets hanging from their ceilings. In 1904, Harvey Hubbell patented a separable plug that made the connection safer and simpler. Manufacturers kept selling incompatible designs for years before settling on the parallel-blade shape that Hubbell patented, still in your wall today. Safety rules came about the same way. According to Freeberg, insurance companies, tired of paying for electrical fires, pushed the electric companies into agreeing on the first common standards. Once the plug was standard, anyone could build something that used it. Refrigerators, radios, washing machines, and eventually computers all came through that same wire. The light bulb may have started it, but the network built the twentieth century. THE NETWORK IS THE PLATFORM For more than a century, the grid’s job was to move power, while pipelines, rail lines, ports, and water systems moved physical goods. Increasingly, every one of those systems also has to move data. Every device connected to critical infrastructure, from a car charger to a data center to a pipeline valve or a rail switch, is becoming a device that sends and receives information. AI deserves the excitement it’s getting today. But it only creates value when it can reach the equipment it’s meant to run. For utilities, pipelines, and water systems, that means the network is no longer back-office plumbing. It’s the platform every latest capability will run on. The network itself is changing. Satellites are beginning to connect directly to the devices ground networks serve, so a sensor on a pipeline far from any tower can be reached from above. The most valuable networks will work on the ground and in orbit as one, and the licensed airwaves connecting them will be among the economy’s scarcest resources. THIS IS BIGGER THAN THE INDUSTRIAL REVOLUTION I realize that being bigger than the Industrial Revolution is a bold claim. The Industrial Revolution gave machines the power to do physical work. What is happening now gives machines the ability to decide and act on work as it happens, like software that spots a fault on a power line and reroutes electricity around it before customers notice. The same shift is coming to pipelines, rail lines, ports, and water systems. It is also moving much faster. Electricity took half a century to reach most American homes. The smart meter transition took about two decades. This one will be measured in years. The network decisions infrastructure companies make now will decide which of them can take part. WHAT THE WINNERS HAVE IN COMMON Robert Metcalfe, who co-invented ethernet, observed that a network’s value grows with the square of the number of devices on it. Think of the fax machine: One is worthless, but each machine added makes the others more valuable. The same is true of the networks running critical infrastructure. The devices on them wear out and get replaced. A well-built network keeps carrying whatever comes next, for decades. Nobody in 2004 was talking about AI. The utilities that built those smart grid networks are among the best positioned to use it now. Pipeline, rail, port, and water operators now face the same choice. The operators that wait will have to retrofit—tearing open the walls to run wire that should have been there from the start, and paying more for it. And the winners plan for a network without edges, one that reaches a city substation and a remote pipeline, so they never have to bolt it on later. HERE WE GO AGAIN I was called crazy in 2004 for saying the grid would run on a network. I expect some people will say the same thing now when I say networks will run all the infrastructure the economy depends on. Edison thought he was selling light. He was founding a century of infrastructure and innovation. Whoever builds the networks now, on the ground and in orbit, is founding the next. Scott Langis president and CEO of Anterix.
