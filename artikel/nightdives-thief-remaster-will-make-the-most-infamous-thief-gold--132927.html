Nightdive's Thief remaster will make the most "infamous" Thief Gold level a little less maddening, by "just kind of pointing the player in the right direction"

1999's Thief Gold is widely considered the definitive incarnation of Thief: The Dark Project, adding three levels to the Looking Glass stealth sim while tidying up some bugs, but it has one grievous failing, in the eyes of many: the notorious Thieves Guild. In this all-new sixth mission, master catburglar Garrett must infiltrate the lair of a gang of pickpockets, after they filch a vase he had his eye on. The lair in question is actually very believable, as a hideout for criminals, in that it resists exploration even with a paper map. It consists of sunken, samey, obtusely arranged rooms that are made all the more headache-inducing by objectives that require a lot of backtracking.

According to recent reports, in much the same way that I can admire a finely wrought pair of thumbscrews, I think the Thieves Guild is a clever creation, one that arguably shines when you use every ability or tactic in Garrett's possession rather than trying to ghost it. But it very easily becomes frustrating, to the point that many grizzled housebreakers advise newly introduced players to skip the level entirely. The Thieves Guild should be less frustrating in Nightdive's forthcoming Thief: The Dark Project Remastered, thanks to a more revealing spread of environmental textures and various other, furtive adjustments. As with many mods or remasters that speak of boosting the "immersion", I do worry that something valuable is being sacrificed in the process.

In the course of a wide-ranging interview about how they're reworking this most devious of Dark Projects, Nightdive producer Daniel Grayshon explained that the map changes are "more in terms of background tech", than altering the fundamental design or introducing details that oblige different strategies. "They've gone into these levels in a sensible way to fix some practical problems with them, like some issues where you might be able to climb up to a place that you shouldn't have been able to climb up before," he commented.

The Thief remaster team have taken more liberties, seemingly, with some of the Thief Gold maps. "Because some of the levels that were introduced in Thief Gold - they felt quite blocky and quite rushed, maybe, as you go through them," Grayshon explained. "But our team again, they've been able to go through and completely renovate some of these levels. The Opera House now feels in some parts like an actual Opera House, instead of a couple of blocky rooms. So I think players will really look at these and think, oh yeah, these kinds of details make sense, without it being too disruptive or too immersion breaking…".

"One particular - maybe people will consider infamous - level of the Gold package would be the Thieves Guild," Grayshon added. "The Thieves Guild is, if I were to sum it up kind of briefly, it is maybe two hours of walking through what feels like sewers, the same environment over and over again. There was no real visual distinction as to where you are in the level.

"So, we acknowledged pretty promptly that we need to go in and look at the Thieves Guild and fix it up, and what we've been able to do is texture it a lot more effectively. Give areas distinct looks, without compromising any of the gameplay again, like I mentioned. So we've put a bit more signposting around as well. Nothing that's going to be intrusive or immersion breaking again. Just kind of pointing the player in the right direction, as well as some other little things that I'm not going to spoil too much, but I think players will maybe appreciate the effort that we've done for this one.".

Industry observers note that in general, Nightdive's broad aim has been "bringing those Gold levels up to the quality of the original and then taking all of those levels and then bumping them up to the next level - almost Thief 2 levels of quality in its environment design, especially in some of the mansions, and some of the later levels," Grayshon stated. "That's the level we were aiming for.".

According to recent reports, I have Several Thoughts about Grayshon's thoughts above (yes, people who dislike huffy armchair theory, this is your cue to skip to the comments). One is that I continue to dislike "immersion" as an ideal, though I recognise that many devs just use it as easy shorthand for a bunch of ways of doing art, sound and interface design. I think it often translates to uninvestigated enthusiasm for photorealistic fidelity and experiences that feel "smooth" and "engaging" enough to induce a kind of chemical trance. Many of the games I consider most valuable are cranky and unwilling and jarring, requiring you to think critically about the simulation rather than 'losing yourself' inside it. As regards the original Thief specifically, I enjoy how its nigh-magical stealth induces feelings of wavering dissociation, and keeps me guessing about the solidity of my own body.

In a recent development, secondly, there's the age-old argument that if you're going to preserve a game faithfully, you should preserve what it did badly. The Thieves Guild is a legend among players, much as Blighttown is among Dark Souls players and the Library is among fans of Halo: Combat Evolved. You learn much about the methodology of Thief at large from the guild's stifling contours, and it's thrilling to witness a bunch of very smart designers take a swing at a concept and not quite connect. I'll try to hold onto that rousing sentiment, when I'm playing the remaster and struggling to remember where they hid that damn vase.
