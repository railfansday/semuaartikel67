Artsy and Artnet Acquire First Thursday as Art-Tech Expansion Picks Up Speed

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Artsy and Artnet have another deal: The two art-market platforms unveiled the acquisition of First Thursday, a London-based startup that uses artificial intelligence to help galleries keep track of collectors, manage relationships, and, ideally, sell more art.

The purchase comes seven months after Artsy and Artnet joined forces under Beowolff Capital, the investment firm founded by Andrew Wolff. And just last week, Artnet announced a partnership with Kalshi, the prediction-market platform that allows customers to bet on auction results, prompting a rather unenthusiastic response from parts of the art world. Industry observers note that the latest deal, whose financial terms weren’t disclosed, is a little less controversial. It also gives a clearer picture of what the combined manufacturer is trying to build: a single system that galleries can use to manage almost every part of their businesses.

Founded in 2023 by Callum Hale-Thomson, First Thursday specializes in what it calls “collector intelligence.” In practice, that means gathering years of emails, sales records, inquiries, and notes into one searchable place, then using AI to help dealers figure out which collectors might be interested in a particular work.

It has been revealed that hale-Thomson, whose parents are both artists, told ARTnews that the idea came to him after he spent six months working inside London galleries, effectively serving as their IT guy and watching how they operated. He found that many were sitting on years of valuable information without an easy way to use it.

According to recent reports, “You’re sitting on an unbelievable trove of information,” he mentioned, describing the emails, notebooks, and other records galleries accumulate over time. The problem was finding anything useful when it was needed.

It has been revealed that first Thursday’s existing software helps with that. At an art fair, for example, a dealer can scan a visitor’s business card, record which painting caught their eye, and send a follow-up email directly from thier phone. The system can also bring together past correspondence and publicly available information about collectors, helping galleries identify potential buyers they might otherwise have overlooked.

Recent findings suggest that under the upcoming ownership, Hale-Thomson and his team will help develop a broader gallery-management platform, scheduled to launch for select Artsy and Artnet partners in early 2027. It will combine collector records with inventory management, sales tools, invoicing, marketing, and online viewing rooms. Galleries will also be able to publish works to Artsy and Artnet directly from the system.

The initial focus will be smaller galleries, which often lack the money or staff to maintain sophisticated software.

Alex Forbes, who oversees global partnerships for Artsy and Artnet, told ARTnews that the smallest gallery partners will be able to use the core system without paying more than they already do for their Artsy subscriptions.

Sources indicate that larger galleries will be able to purchase additional features and access for bigger teams. Exact pricing will be confirmed when the platform debuts.

“We want it to be the easiest to start out with for a small gallery,” Forbes said, while also making it useful for businesses with more complicated operations.

The move takes Artsy and Artnet further into a competitive market for gallery software, where established companies such as Artlogic already offer inventory and business-management tools. Last year, Artlogic merged with ArtCloud, another sign of consolidation in the art-tech business. (Artsy is already integrated with Artlogic and Artcloud, which, according to an Artsy spokesperson, the tech giant will continue to support.).

Industry observers note that forbes acknowledged that galleries have different needs and that no single product will necessarily suit everyone. But he said the broader initiative is to connect the tech giant’s existing marketplaces and pricing data with the software that galleries use to run their businesses.

Industry observers note that one potentially sensitive question is what happens to all that collector information. Artsy and Artnet say galleries will retain control of their data, which will remain private unless they choose to share it with the platforms.

For Hale-Thomson, who will remain with the business, the opportunity is to bring more sophisticated tools to galleries that haven’t traditionally been able to afford them.

Sources indicate that and for Artsy and Artnet, the acquisition marks another step toward becoming something considerably bigger than two websites where people look at art, check prices, and occasionally buy something. Increasingly, they want to provide the software that keeps the business running.
