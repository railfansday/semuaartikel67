On November 5, 2026, the United Nations Human Settlements Programme (UN-Habitat) and the Climate Prediction and Applications Centre (ICPAC) of the Intergovernmental Authority on Development (IGAD) rolled out a short practical guide for city governments to help them prepare for the upcoming season. On November 5, 2026, the United Nations Human Settlements Programme (UN-Habitat) and the Climate Prediction and Applications Centre (ICPAC) of the Intergovernmental Authority on Development (IGAD) launched a short practical guide for city governments to help them prepare for the upcoming season. The guide offers considerations applicable to different climate regions, but it is framed by IGAD in the context of preparedness, anticipatory action and resilience in the Horn of Africa (Djibouti, Eritrea, Ethiopia, Kenya, Somalia, South Sudan, Sudan and Uganda). Its aim is to offer actionable guidance to improve urban resilience, protect vulnerable communities and reduce the impacts of climate-related hazards.

The guide, "El Niño Preparedness: An Anticipatory Action Framework for City Leaders," is grounded in two premises: that urban preparedness is a governance function, and that climate forecasts can be translated into local action at the scale of cities and municipal governments. From ICPAC's perspective, the value of a climate forecast lies in the action it enables by triggering solid protocols ahead of El Niño-driven rains. The guide encourages cities to use the forecast lead time to make decisions on the ground and turn early warnings into early action. The document proposes a protocol structure and checklists to translate climate forecasts into risk information based on pre-agreed thresholds. Triggered preventive actions include administrative measures, such as releasing funding and warning households, and practical measures, such as clearing drains and pre-positioning emergency supplies.

Industry observers note that climate-related humanitarian crises are not caused by natural events themselves, but by their consequences in informal settlements, clogged infrastructure, and fragmented institutions. In the methodology presented, a seasonal forecast that crosses a pre-agreed threshold automatically triggers practical action, from releasing funds to clearing drains. To organize and prioritize that action, the guide defines three scenarios (moderate, severe and worst case), based on UN-Habitat's experience in urban emergencies. Each scenario is defined by a quantified trigger, a set of anticipatory actions and a projected urban impact. The quantification depends on prior institutional agreements, based on an action checklist that city leaders agree on in advance.

In a recent development, the methodology is of general interest but was developed around local concerns in the Horn of Africa. ICPAC, the institution collaborating with UN-Habitat, is a specialized branch of IGAD and a World Meteorological Organization (WMO) Regional Climate Centre for Eastern Africa, providing climate services, seasonal forecasts and early warning information across the Greater Horn of Africa. According to ICPAC, communities in the area that face a rapid shift from dry conditions to heavy rains are at heightened risk of flash and riverine floods, landslides, displacement, outbreaks of human and transboundary animal diseases, concentration of livestock around water and grazing resources, rangeland degradation and disruption of established pastoral mobility routes.

The guide offers direction on six elements: early warning and forecast uptake through formal data sharing; impact-based scenarios that blueprint against consequences; pre-agreed triggers and anticipatory financing; contingency planning with clear institutional roles; sector- and infrastructure-specific preparedness; and safe havens and community mobilization.
