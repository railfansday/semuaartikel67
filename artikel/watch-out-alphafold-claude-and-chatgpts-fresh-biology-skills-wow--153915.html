Watch out, AlphaFold: Claude and ChatGPT’s fresh biology skills wow scientists

In a recent development, accurately predicting protein structures has been the preserve of specialist artificial-intelligence tools such as AlphaFold — but now general-purpose models are catching up. Credit: EMBL-EBI (CC0).

Sources indicate that earlier this month, Xinming Tu set out to test what artificial-intelligence models know about proteins. Tu, a researcher at an AI biology manufacturer called Phylo in South San Francisco, California, asked large language models (LLMs) from OpenAI, Anthropic and Google to predict the sequences of obscure bacterial proteins.

Industry observers note that he left the analysis to run overnight and woke up shocked at the results. The latest versions of Anthropic’s Claude models had outperformed other LLMs — including specialized protein-language models, which are trained on vast troves of sequence data and designed for such tasks.

Scientists are using AI to dream up revolutionary new proteins.

Scientists are using AI to dream up revolutionary upcoming proteins.

According to recent reports, when Tu and Leo Chen, a computational biologist at Harvard Medical School in Boston, Massachusetts, shared the results in a 16 November blog post, it set off a frenzy among computational biologists to see what other protein biology LLMs such as Claude could do. For instance, Tu, Chen and others have found that the tool could perform a key step in protein-structure prediction.

It has been revealed that and on 24 November, OpenAI announced that its latest language models had successfully designed new proteins, some of which behaved as expected when they were made and tested in the laboratory. The results hint that the latest LLMs might be gaining a sophisticated understanding of protein biology.

The research has kicked off a debate over the future of biological AI. Some scientists predict that ‘general purpose’ models such as Claude and ChatGPT will eventually outperform specialist tools such as AlphaFold at tasks including protein-structure prediction and design.

“We want to see a world where a scientist can just describe in natural language what they want a ChatGPT model to do, and the model can just be infinitely flexible and design exactly what the person wants,” notes Joy Jiao, who leads life-sciences research at OpenAI in San Francisco, California.

Sources indicate that but some researchers question the extent to which LLMs are learning underlying lessons in protein biology. They suggest that some of the models’ abilities could be the result of regurgitating training data.

The unveiling of AlphaFold2, Google DeepMind’s blockbuster AI tool for protein-structure prediction, in 2020, launched a revolution in biological AI. There is now a vast ecosystem of software — much of it open source — for modelling different properties of proteins, such as how they interact with potential drugs, and for designing new proteins.

According to recent reports, researchers have begun using LLMs to orchestrate these specialist models, but the LLMs have been used mostly as a time-saving measure, says Martin Pačesa, a structural biologist at the University of Zurich in Switzerland. “In most cases, a specialist will always perform better than a generalist for a given task,” he says.

Five protein-design questions that still challenge AI.

Five protein-design questions that still challenge AI.

Sources indicate that to see if an LLM could stand in for specialist protein-design tools, Jiao and her colleagues asked an OpenAI model called GPT-6.2 to design 3D protein structures capable of binding to five biological targets, including proteins implicated in cancer and immune conditions. After the LLMs generated the designs, the researchers used a specialist AI tool to determine a corresponding genetic sequence. Lab experiments confirmed that around 11% of designs were able to bind to their target.

In other experiments, the OpenAI team showed that its LLMs could design protein sequences, redesign existing antibodies, and do other tasks typically performed by specialist tools. In some cases, the LLM performed as well as some specialist tools, and in others, such as predicting the 3D structure of proteins, it was far off. OpenAI researchers describe the work in a blog post and technical paper.

Jiao emphasizes that the work is a proof of principle, and that specialized AI tools were still needed for key steps. But Masatoshi Uehara, an OpenAI researcher who was part of the effort, thinks that LLMs will only get better at protein biology. “I believe the large language model can technically beat every specialized model in each capability,” he claims.
