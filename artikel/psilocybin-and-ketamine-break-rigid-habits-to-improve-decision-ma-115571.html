Psilocybin and Ketamine Break Rigid Habits to Improve Decision-Making

It has been revealed that latest neuroscience research reveals that both psilocybin and ketamine improve decision accuracy by helping the brain rely less on rigid, repetitive habits and more on real-time sensory cues. The study indicates that rather than scrambling neural signaling, these compounds gently sculpt ongoing brain activity, trading a slight reduction in reaction speed for sharper, more flexible choices.

For the roughly one in five Americans living with a mental health condition, frontline psychiatric medications have changed remarkably little since the 1950s. Conventional antidepressants such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) often require weeks to take effect, demand chronic daily use, and fail to provide adequate relief for roughly 30% of patients with treatment-resistant depression.

Against this backdrop, psychedelic compounds, including classic serotonergic psychedelics like psilocybin and dissociative agents like ketamine, have sparked intense clinical excitement for their ability to produce rapid, durable improvements in mood, addiction, and post-traumatic stress disorder. Yet precisely how these drugs alter neural circuits to break entrenched patterns of negative thinking has remained poorly understood.

Sources indicate that now, in a study published in Science Advances, neuroscientists at Boston University led by assistant professor Dr. Benjamin Scott and associate professor Dr. Steve Ramirez have uncovered a shared pro-cognitive mechanism linking psilocybin and ketamine: both drugs weaken the brain’s reliance on automatic habits and heighten sensitivity to immediate sensory cues.

To test how psychedelics influence moment-to-moment decision-making, Scott, Ramirez, and colleagues trained mice on a perceptual task requiring the animals to use brief pulses of light to identify which of two ports contained a reward.

Industry observers note that untreated mice performed the task moderately well, but frequently fell into habitual traps—repeatedly returning to a previously rewarded location even when the light cues clearly signaled a change. Following administration of either psilocybin or ketamine, however, mice made significantly fewer errors. Rather than falling back on repetitive choice biases, treated animals anchored their decisions much more closely to real-time sensory information.

“These findings reveal how psychedelics reduce the reliance on habits and repetitive choices, which may explain part of their therapeutic effect,” explained Scott, a faculty member in Boston University’s Department of Psychological and Brain Sciences.

Although psilocybin acts primarily on serotonin receptors (such as 5-HT2A and 5-HT1A) while ketamine blocks glutamate NMDA receptors, their behavioral effects converged on sharper, less biased decision-making.

To observe what changed inside the brain, the researchers combined brain-wide genetic activity mapping with targeted neural recordings in the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), a prefrontal region essential for evaluating rewards, making decisions, and updating beliefs when rules change.

Sources indicate that remarkably, psilocybin did not erase or scramble existing task representations. Instead, core OFC activity patterns tracking sensory cues and choices remained largely intact, accompanied by widespread yet subtle adjustments across interconnected brain regions. According to the authors, this gentle sculpting of ongoing neural activity aligns closely with clinical observations that a person’s mindset and environment (“set and setting”) strongly shape the psychedelic experience.

Recent findings suggest that the team also identified an crucial behavioral nuance: while mice made more accurate choices after receiving psilocybin or ketamine, they took longer to deliberate, revealing a classic speed-accuracy tradeoff. While slower deliberation can benefit reflective cognitive therapy, it underscores why activities demanding split-second reaction times, such as driving, are unsafe under the influence of these compounds.

Looking ahead, Scott and Ramirez initiative to pinpoint the exact neural pathways responsible for breaking rigid habits, with the ultimate goal of developing targeted, next-generation therapies that unlock cognitive flexibility without hallucinogenic side effects.

How do psilocybin and ketamine improve decision-making in this study?

Boston University researchers found that both drugs help the brain pay closer attention to immediate sensory cues (like flashes of light) while reducing the tendency to fall back on repetitive habits, leading to significantly more accurate choices.

Do psychedelics scramble normal brain activity during a task?

In a recent development, no. Recordings in the orbitofrontal cortex highlighted that the brain’s core patterns for tracking cues and making decisions remained largely preserved. Instead of causing chaos, psilocybin induced widespread yet subtle shifts that gently sculpted how the brain updated its beliefs.

Were there any downsides to the improved decision accuracy?

Sources indicate that yes. Researchers observed a speed-accuracy tradeoff: while treated animals made fewer mistakes, they took longer to make their decisions, suggesting these compounds slow down reaction times even as they promote more deliberative thinking.

Psychedelics Shift Decision Strategies and Reconfigure Orbitofrontal Dynamics.

Psychedelics such as psilocybin and ketamine are attracting renewed attention as rapid-acting therapeutics, yet how they reshape cognitive computations remains poorly understood.

It has been revealed that to address this, we trained mice on a free-response perceptual decision-making task that disentangles bias-driven from sensory-guided strategies. Acute administration of psilocybin or ketamine slowed decision times while improving accuracy. Behavioral modeling combining drift diffusion and GLM-HMM approaches revealed that these effects were driven by increased decision thresholds and a shift toward sensory-engaged states.

Sources indicate that whole-brain c-Fos mapping identified modulation of distributed task-related networks, including the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC). Calcium imaging highlighted that OFC population dynamics were reorganized under psychedelic conditions, with preserved task selectivity but altered temporal structure and shifted population correlations.

Together, these findings indicate that psychedelics shift decision-making toward greater reliance on sensory evidence while reshaping cortical population dynamics, providing a system-level account of their effects on perceptual decisions.
