Details for Durable Decks

He focuses on building secure footings, ensuring proper ledger fastening, avoiding flashing mistakes, using correct connectors, and strengthening the framing.

I’ve built a lot of decks, some still standing after 30-plus years. I’ve also worked with several deck-building rock stars to create articles on the subject. I’ve even helped companies like Trex create a bunch of how-to content. In all my years of research and hands-on experience, I’ve seen plenty of examples of decks built to last.

On the flip side, I’ve come across structures that range from somewhat wobbly to absolutely unsafe due to feeble footings, poorly installed or absent ledger flashing, undersized framing, and missing or misused hardware and fasteners.

Building to code and following the requirements spelled out by your local building department will get you most of the way to a safe and durable deck, but even a code-compliant deck can be improved with some due diligence and attention to detail.

In this article, I’ve focused on some vulnerabilities that may not be addressed by the building code or caught during a deck permit strategy review or site inspection. Think of this as an above-and-beyond checklist of details worth considering on your next deck build..

According to recent reports, for years I worked for a production builder. They used to insulate a home’s rim-joist cavity with batt insulation and no vapor retarder, which was code-approved at the time. This caused all sorts of moisture damage from water vapor that was driven through the cavity.

Industry observers note that instead of switching to rigid or spray foam, their first fix was to install SIP rim joists, the most common of which have a core of EPS insulation sandwiched between OSB sheathing. This solution lasted less than a year because uninformed deck builders and homeowners assumed they could use standard lag screws to then attach deck ledgers to the rim joist.

The result was that most of those fasteners were secured to only the two layers of plywood that made up the SIP, rather than solid-sawn lumber—a serious problem when the connection is responsible for supporting the deck.

In a recent development, so, don’t assume there’s a conventional solid-sawn rim joist behind the sheathing. The floor system might include an SIP rim joist or floor trusses with just a ribbon board instead of a solid ledger. When building a new deck, a permit plan review or site inspection will hopefully catch this, but if you are resurfacing an existing deck, now is the time to confirm a solid ledger connection.

It has been revealed that if you discover an SIP rim joist or a floor system that can’t support a conventional ledger connection, try to track down the manufacturer of the components in question for an approved attachment solution. If that’s not an option, you could consult an engineer or add a beam and posts near the house and build the deck as a freestanding structure.

Don’t attach a deck ledger to the rim joist of a cantilevered bump-out or bay window, or directly to a stud wall.

Recent findings suggest that neither provides the load path assumed by conventional prescriptive ledger details. Nevertheless, this was common practice back in the day.

In fact, the ledger on my own son’s house is attached to a bay. A site inspection will likely catch this code violation, but if you are just making cosmetic improvements that don’t require a permit, do your due diligence.

In some cases, these connections can be made to work, but they typically require an engineered design and structural modifications, which might involve tearing into a finished ceiling to add blocking or other framing reinforcement. As with any questionable ledger connection, the simpler solution is to treat the deck as freestanding.

In a recent development, add a beam and posts close to the house so the deck loads are carried directly to footings rather than relying on a questionable ledger connection. That’s the fix I proposal to make on my son’s house. In the meantime, I warned him against throwing any parties on the deck.

In my work as a contractor, I replaced several patio doors after either the door sill or ledger flashing failed, allowing water to rot the subfloor and some of the framing below it. I came across this problem primarily when deck boards were installed right up under the door sill, where splashing rain and melting Minnesota snow combined to wreak havoc over the years. To help prevent this, whenever possible I would install the deck a few inches below the door sill. I also instructed my customers to keep snow from piling up against the door.

The main hurdle to installing a deck below the door sill is maintaining proper ledger-fastener placement. On a large deck, the ledger may be as deep as the house rim joist and dropping it a few inches can make it impossible to meet the required fastener edge distances in both the ledger and the rim joist. One solution is to use smaller deck joists and reduce their span by adding another beam. If this doesn’t work, you may need to consult an engineer for an alternative connection detail.

Of course, lowering the deck surface below the door sill isn’t as much of a concern in warm, dry regions—and it isn’t always an option when someone in the house has mobility challenges.

In a recent development, the only deck callback I ever received happened after the first time I worked with composite fascia boards. I didn’t use enough fasteners. I thought the manufacturer’s recommended screw spacing was preposterous overkill, but I was wrong. The fascia buckled, and I returned to the scene, tail between my legs, to add more screws.

In addition to protecting your reputation, following manufacturer recommendations can also preserve warranties. If you use unapproved fasteners or hidden clips on composite decking and there’s a factory defect, some companies might not stand behind their product. Most factory warranties stipulate using only approved installation methods and materials.

Double-check that the structural hardware you grab is rated for exterior use with the treated lumber you’re using. I nearly walked out of a home center with interior-grade joist hangers that were in the wrong bin. They looked almost identical to the exterior versions. Also, don’t mix stainless-steel and galvanized connectors and fasteners; dissimilar metals can accelerate corrosion.

Sources indicate that just as important, use the fasteners specified by the connector manufacturer. Short hanger nails, roofing nails, deck screws, and whatever happens to be in your tool pouch aren’t necessarily acceptable substitutes. Pay particular attention to double-shear hangers: The angled fasteners often need to be longer than the face nails so they penetrate through the joist and well into the supporting member. And fill the required holes—structural hardware only provides its rated capacity when it’s installed the way it was tested.
