Harvard’s tiny UV light chip could help shrink quantum computers

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Ultraviolet (UV) light is already essential to a wide range of modern technologies. Beyond its natural presence in sunlight, it is used to disinfect surfaces, make biological materials fluoresce, and manufacture computer chips through a process called photolithography. In the future, microscopic sources of UV light built directly into photonic chips could enable smaller quantum computers, ultra-precise atomic clocks, and other advanced technologies.

However, shrinking UV light sources to fit on a chip has presented a significant engineering challenge. Ultraviolet light rapidly loses power as it travels through optical waveguides, the tiny channels that carry light across a chip. This limitation has made it extremely difficult to develop compact UV sources with enough power for practical applications.

In a recent development, now, a Harvard-led research team has demonstrated a microscopic photonic device that produces approximately 120 times more UV power than previous comparable devices. The researchers, working in the laboratory of Marko Lončar, the Tiantsai Lin Professor of Electrical Engineering, achieved the breakthrough using a widely studied material called thin-film lithium niobate.

Published in Nature Communications, the findings establish lithium niobate as a promising platform for generating powerful ultraviolet light in compact devices while minimizing optical losses.

Turning Red Light Into Ultraviolet Light.

Rather than trying to deliver ultraviolet light directly through microscopic channels, the researchers designed a device that generates UV light from red light within the chip itself.

The approach relies on a process known as frequency upconversion. Inside the lithium niobate crystal, two photons of red light combine to produce a single ultraviolet photon with higher energy. Lithium niobate is particularly well suited to this process because of its exceptional ability to convert light from one frequency to another.

"Our group is perhaps best known for utilizing lithium niobate for photonic devices that operate at longer, infrared wavelengths -- closer to the realm of telecommunications signals," Lončar stated.

However, the team found that this transparent crystalline material, which has become increasingly key in integrated photonics, can also support light sources and guide light at much shorter wavelengths, including those in the ultraviolet range.

Sources indicate that "When people think about [thin-film lithium niobate], they don't think of it as a UV material, but we show that it is," stated co-first author Kees Franken, former research fellow in the Lončar lab. "We also show that there are some other nonlinear effects happening that we don't fully understand yet.".

A Fresh Technique Overcomes a Major Engineering Barrier.

The device uses optical waveguides, microscopic channels etched into the lithium niobate film that direct light through the chip in much the same way electrical wires carry current.

For red light to be efficiently converted into ultraviolet light, the orientation of regions within the crystal must be repeatedly reversed at precisely controlled intervals along the waveguide. This manufacturing process, called poling, is a specialized technique used in nonlinear optics.

One of the biggest challenges has been creating these patterns with accuracy at scales smaller than a micrometer across devices that extend for centimeters.

In a recent development, previous manufacturing methods required researchers to make an undesirable compromise. Poling the entire film before forming the waveguides made it difficult to correct manufacturing imperfections. Alternatively, creating the waveguides first reduced conversion efficiency because the electrodes were positioned too far away, allowing only part of the film to be successfully poled.

To eliminate this tradeoff, the Harvard team developed a technique they call "sidewall poling.".

Precision Engineering Dramatically Improves Light Conversion.

Instead of positioning the electrodes exclusively above the lithium niobate film, the researchers placed precisely patterned metal structures resembling tiny fingers directly alongside the etched waveguide. During fabrication, they applied a small voltage to permanently reverse the orientation of the crystal domains in a carefully controlled pattern.

It has been revealed that "The key idea was: could we just put the electrodes directly on the waveguide?" stated co-first author and former graduate student Soumya Ghosh. "It's a high-precision fabrication process to position the electrodes but requiring about 50-nanometer accuracy.".

This arrangement enabled the researchers to reverse the crystal domains throughout the entire cross section of the waveguide, allowing light traveling through the material to undergo much more efficient frequency conversion.

Industry observers note that the team could also fine-tune the spacing of the reversed crystal regions along the device. By incorporating existing "adapted poling" techniques previously developed in Lončar's laboratory and elsewhere, the researchers compensated for variations in the thickness of the lithium niobate film and the shape of the waveguide.

A 120-Fold Increase in Ultraviolet Power.

The resulting device generated 4.2 milliwatts of ultraviolet power directly on the chip at a wavelength of 390 nanometers. That represents approximately 120 times the output power achieved by the best previous thin-film lithium niobate devices operating in this wavelength range.

Earlier experiments using the material generated only tens of microwatts of UV power. Although those results demonstrated that the approach was scientifically feasible, the output was too low to support practical applications.

The substantial increase in power could be particularly valuable for trapped-ion quantum computers. These machines use electrically charged atoms to store and process quantum information. Many of the specific atomic energy transitions required to operate them involve light in the near-ultraviolet portion of the spectrum.

"If you want a scalable quantum computer that isn't the size of a truck, you need to scale everything down to the chip level, and this includes the light sources," Franken mentioned.

Sources indicate that the technology could also benefit ultra-precise atomic clocks and environmental monitoring systems. According to the researchers, the same range of ultraviolet wavelengths is well suited to compact sensors designed to closely monitor greenhouse gases and other atmospheric pollutants.

Franken and Ghosh attribute the breakthrough partly to the collaborative structure of Lončar's laboratory, where researchers carry out every stage of device development in one place, including theoretical research, engineering design, cleanroom fabrication, and optical testing.

"The hands-on intuition that we gained for how to make a device, while also keeping the zoomed-out view of what this device is for, and how we were going to characterize it -- that's a massive part of what enabled this project for us," Ghosh noted.

In a recent development, "Milliwatt-level UV generation using sidewall poled lithium niobate" was further co-authored by C.C. Rodrigues, J. Yang, C.J. Xin, S. Lu, D. Witt, G. Joe, G.S. Wiederhecker, and K.-J. Boller.
