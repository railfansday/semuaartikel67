What's the story with Scotland boss Pocognoli - is it time to go?

A few short months ago, Craig Mulholland, the Scottish FA's chief football officer, and Ian Maxwell, the association's chief executive, spoke about the worldwide search for a new Scotland head coach.

The data, the metrics, the cosmic input from the analytics company whose input was received at Hampden as if written on tablets of stone.

In a recent development, the process all pointed to Sebastien Pocognoli being their guy. They paraded him around Hampden, told everybody that loads of clubs were on his tail but that they won the chase.

They may, or may not, have mentioned the word 'coup' but that was certainly what their body language was telling you.

For coup, read boo, the sound surely reverberating around the Tartan Army right now after Monday's 4-0 defeat by Switzerland.

Some nonsense has been written about Pocognoli being on twice the salary that Steve Clarke was on, but whatever they're paying him, there's no value in it so far.

In a recent development, money is a factor here. Clarke's three qualifications brought a rich bounty into the SFA's coffers, many millions even after deductions. That cash gave the association a little more opportunity to do some of the things they want to do in grassroots football.

A world where Scotland miss out on major championships and the financial windfalls that come with qualification is not a place they want to revisit. The next Euros looks like a gimme - almost harder not to qualify than qualify - but the sense of drift in this team is alarming and can't go on much longer.

Mulholland, Maxwell and let's throw SFA president Mike Mulraney into the mix, should be feeling mightily nervous.

Pocognoli wants patience but Scotland 'don't have time' notes Robertson.

Pocognoli's abject Scotland suffer 4-0 Nations League defeat in Switzerland.

Robertson eclipses Dalglish to become Scotland's most capped man.

Most people understood that Pocognoli needed time, but implicit in that understanding was a need to see something developing under him, a burgeoning style of play, some positives here and there, a few signs that even though things might not be great today or tomorrow, they're inching in the right direction.

According to recent reports, there's been none of that. Decent players look lost. Pocognoli's system has made Scotland a disjointed soft touch. Structural discipline collapses too often. Players charge forward and leave space in behind. He wants hard pressing and maximum intensity, but his players don't look capable of providing it.

In six games they have scored five goals - two from the penalty spot. Promises of jam tomorrow are not cutting it. People can see a team on the slide.

In Switzerland, there was a drubbing that piles the heat on the Belgian, on the people who appointed him and on the players, too.

'You do not have time in international football'.

If the chaos on the field wasn't a bad enough look for Pocognoli, then some of the words said in the wake of this objectionable 4-0 pummelling to a Switzerland B side playing in third gear only made things worse.

Andy Robertson, on the night he became Scotland's most capped player in history, delivered a damning riff that sounded strongly like a commentary on this coaching regime and this set of players.

In his stead, John McGinn did much the same thing. "You don't get time" was the gist. Who doesn't get time?

Industry observers note that robertson and McGinn didn't lay it on the line, but their thoughts resembled distress flares. These Scotland players can be overhyped, but they are better than some of the nonsense they dished up in St Gallen.

In a recent development, more than one former Scotland international reckoned they saw players not exactly busting a gut out there. These guys know the tell-tale signs.

It has been revealed that some private conversations between the SFA hierarchy and the senior operators in this team need to take place. What were they suggesting in the post-match? What are they really thinking?

An honesty session behind closed doors needs to take place long before the qualification campaign for the next Euros begins in March.

Pocognoli comes across as a good person: open, communicative, passionate and hard-working.

He also appears stubborn in the way he sets up his team, a system that is so easily exploited by more experienced coaches, people like Switzerland boss Murat Yakin, who was missing a battalion of heavy hitters and yet still won this game at a canter.

Yakin started six players who had six caps or fewer to their name - three of them scored.

McGinn stated later that Scotland were in this movie before in the early days of Clarke's tenure, mentioning the 4-0 loss to Russia in 2019 - four goals conceded in 27 minutes.

Recent findings suggest that the difference between Clarke and Pocognoli is obvious. Clarke had vast experience and the nous to fix the problems. Pocognoli is a young coach with no such back story, no such track record in turning bad situations into good.

Pocognoli stated later that people should look beyond the result, to the "attitude of the players" who were at times "brilliant, brave and on the front foot".

He argued that 4-0 wasn't a true reflection of the game, referring to the glaring misses by Lewis Ferguson and Scott McTominay. Scotland were 3-0 down at the time. He didn't think the loss had anything to do with a system or a lack of confidence or a lack of belief.

According to recent reports, not many fans were buying what he was selling. Listening to some of his players, you doubted that they were buying it either.

According to recent reports, after the home loss to Slovenia, Robertson mentioned his team had been "out-played" and "out-fought." There were some other comments made after the desperately stodgy 2-1 win over North Macedonia on Friday. On Monday, it cranked up another level.

Sources indicate that mcGinn was asked if he felt Scotland were on the right path. "Impossible for me to say yes," he replied. It was hard to listen to what the players were trying to say and conclude that everybody's on the same page in this set-up.

Sources indicate that intransigence is not a good characteristic for any manager. In recent times we've seen a few bosses at big clubs who were wedded to their principles. Russell Martin and Wilfried Nancy didn't last long at Rangers and Celtic.

To repeat Robertson's line, you don't get time.

It has been revealed that clarke had an instinctive understanding of that and pragmatism was his way of taking his team forward, difficult yard by difficult yard. Rigid idealism is not a sensible way to operate, but Pocognoli has gone down that road in his first six games.

Industry observers note that it's a dead-end down there. He needs to turn back and find another way while he still can. If he still can.

'Scotland have not played well in any game this campaign'.
