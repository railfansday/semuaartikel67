Sources indicate that AI writing tools are now part of everyday work for students, marketers, developers and editors, and a parallel category of tools has appeared to answer one question: was this text written by a person or a model? Teachers, publishers, hiring teams and content managers use these detectors to screen submissions, but the technology is probabilistic, its accuracy claims vary, and the cost of a wrong call can be high.

This guide compares six AI detector tools, CudekAI, GPTZero, Copyleaks, Originality.ai, Turnitin and Sapling, on what their own pages state. It then explains how detectors work, summarizes the research on their limits, and ends with a short script you can use to test any detector on your own samples. The tools are listed in no ranking order. Feature details, limits and prices change often, so confirm current information on each vendor’s site before you commit.

• A detector gives a probability, not proof. Every vendor in this list warns that results can be wrong, and several advise against using a score alone.

In a recent development, • Limits differ widely. Character limits, supported languages, models detected and free allowances vary between tools and even between pages of the same tool.

Industry observers note that • Bias and short text are known weak spots. Research has found detectors misclassify some non-native English writing, and most vendors say accuracy improves with longer text.

Industry observers note that • Vendor accuracy claims are not comparable. Figures come from different datasets, thresholds and models, so test on your own material.

Sources indicate that • Use detection as one signal. Combine it with drafts, version history, interviews or other evidence before acting on a result.

Industry observers note that • False positives. Human writing can be flagged, especially when it is short, formulaic or highly predictable.

• Bias risk. A research paper titled GPT detectors are biased against non-native English writers found that the detectors it tested consistently misclassified non-native English writing as AI-generated, and warned against using such detectors in educational settings. Turnitin’s page reports the opposite for its own tool, which is a reason to test on your population.

It has been revealed that • Evasion. Paraphrasing or editing AI text can lower scores. The same research found that simple prompting strategies could both reduce bias and get text past detectors.

According to recent reports, • Mixed authorship. A document that is partly AI-assisted can get a low overall score while individual sentences score high, as Sapling’s page points out.

In a recent development, • Moving targets. Newly introduced models change the text distribution, so a tool trained on older models can lose accuracy.

Industry observers note that • Shifting benchmarks. Vendors report different datasets and thresholds, so numbers are not comparable.

OpenAI’s own experience is a useful reminder. Its AI text classifier correctly flagged 26 percent of AI-written text on a challenge set while mislabeling 9 percent of human text, and the page states that the classifier was withdrawn on July 20, 2023 due to its low rate of accuracy. Detection has improved since, but the underlying difficulty has not gone away.

Recent findings suggest that most detectors are classifiers trained on large collections of human-written and machine-generated text. They look for statistical patterns, such as how predictable the word choices are or how uniform the sentence structure is, and output a probability. Some systems add features such as sentence-level scoring, writing-process data, or checks for hidden provenance signals.

It has been revealed that • Statistical signals. Text that a language model finds highly predictable tends to look machine-like, which is why formulaic human writing can be misflagged.

It has been revealed that • Trained classifiers. Detectors learn from labeled examples and enhancement as upcoming models appear.

Recent findings suggest that • Writing-process evidence. Tools such as Writing Replay record how a document was typed or pasted, which is stronger evidence than a style score.

• Provenance. Standards such as C2PA Content Credentials aim to record the origin and edits of content when the creating tool supports them, which is different from guessing after the fact.
