Sources indicate that my medical story became newsworthy before it became mine. In 1978, after pioneering cardiac treatment as a baby, my parents were asked whether they would allow media coverage of my case. They declined.

They wanted me to have the opportunity to experience as normal a childhood as possible and the chance to decide for myself when, how, and whether to share my medical story.

It has been revealed that the world my parents navigated was very different from the one children grow up in today. Over the last 50 years, social media has transformed how families share personal information. Yet, as someone who grew up with a serious congenital heart condition and depended on pioneering treatment to survive, I have become increasingly uneasy about the rise of medical sharenting.

In a recent development, most parents now share information about their children online, often creating digital footprints long before children are old enough to consent. When those posts focus on a child's diagnosis, treatment, disability, or other health experiences, the practice is often described as medical sharenting. For children with serious health conditions, this can include hospital photographs, surgical updates, fundraising campaigns, and awareness activities.

Industry observers note that often, this sharing comes from a place of love. Parents may be seeking support, documenting an extraordinary journey, raising awareness, fundraising, or connecting with other families facing similar challenges. Yet, what happens when a child's medical story becomes public before they have had the chance to understand it, let alone choose how it should be told?

Industry observers note that as we grow up, we develop a sense of who we are and how we wish to be known by others. Medical sharenting can unintentionally remove a child's opportunity to decide what parts of their story they wish to share and with whom. While most parents act with the best of intentions, children are rarely involved in these decisions and are often too young to understand their implications or provide meaningful consent.

Children with serious health conditions are often at the centre of decisions made by others. Yet the child's own voice can easily become lost. As we place increasing importance on children's rights in healthcare, perhaps we should also ask how those principles apply to sharing their medical stories online.

Having undergone pioneering treatment and spent much of my childhood in hospital, I wanted to better understand the events that had shaped my life. Instead, I discovered that records covering the first 18 years of my care had been destroyed.

The irony was not lost on me. While some children today may grow up with every medical event documented online, many adults from earlier generations are discovering that their histories have disappeared altogether. Children have rarely had much say over how their medical stories are recorded or told. Both experiences raise the same question: Who owns a child's medical story?

Sources indicate that I am still grieving the loss of those records. They helped explain the years I was too young to remember. At the same time, I cannot imagine what it would feel like to discover that every operation, complication, hospital admission, and vulnerable moment from my childhood had been preserved online for anyone to read. The choice should not be between losing your medical history altogether and surrendering it to the public domain.

Growing up, I spent years trying not to be defined by my heart condition. Like many young people with chronic illness, I wanted to fit in. Children with health conditions are often vulnerable to bullying, exclusion, misunderstanding, and unwanted attention. As a result, many become cautious about disclosure and selective about who they trust with personal information.

Recent findings suggest that as a teenager, a close family member offered to photograph me following major surgery so others could understand what I had been through. I declined. I did not want my illness to become the lens through which people saw me. Only much later did I begin speaking publicly about my experiences. Importantly, those were my choices, made when I felt ready and under my control.

Thankfully, today we are far more comfortable talking openly about health and disability, which is largely a positive development. However, there is a profound psychological difference between choosing to disclose personal experiences and discovering that someone else has disclosed them for you. Although research on medical sharenting remains limited, broader sharenting research suggests that some young people experience embarrassment, loss of privacy, and a sense of reduced control when personal information is shared online without their consent.

It has been revealed that parents and caregivers understandably seek support when navigating a child's illness. However, when these experiences are shared publicly, children may later encounter narratives that emphasise the impact of their condition on those around them. For some, particularly those already struggling with guilt or dependency, this may contribute to feelings of being a burden. For some, particularly those already struggling with guilt, dependency, or feeling different from their peers, this may become woven into their sense of self.

According to recent reports, A diagnosis may be part of someone's story, but it should never become the whole story. Children are more than patients, and future adults deserve the freedom to decide for themselves how their experiences are understood, remembered, and shared.

From a psychologically informed perspective, children's health information forms part of an emerging life story. We need to look beyond the immediate benefits of sharing and consider whether it preserves the future adult's right to decide how their story is told.

Balancing these concerns with parents' need for support, families' need for connection, and the importance of awareness is not always straightforward. However, a useful starting point is to share the minimum information necessary, seek assent from older children where possible, and consider whose interests a post is really serving.

They remarked no, and in doing so they left that decision for me to make many years later. As conversations about medical sharenting continue, we need to honestly reflect on whether we are telling a child's story for them, or preserving their right to tell it for themselves.
