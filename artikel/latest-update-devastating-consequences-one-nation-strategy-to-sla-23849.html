One Nation has vowed to cut Australia’s refugee intake by 90% and scrap the permanent parent visa program under its latest hardline plan to slash migration.

In a recent development, pauline Hanson confirmed the policy on Wednesday, labelling it a “tough” approach to migration. It was quickly criticised by political opponents and advocacy groups who labelled the announcement a “predictable and cheap” attack on refugees.

The rightwing party said if it held power it would cut the refugee program from 20,000 places a year to 2,000 and claimed that Australia would still remain above the OECD average for places per capita.

“The cost [to] … the taxpayer is enormous,” Hanson told reporters. “The cost is, on average, about $500,000 a year in a lifetime of expenditure per person in the country.

Industry observers note that “We’ve got to take a tough stance on this. My concern is for these people out there … that are doing it tough. You don’t bring more people into the country if they cannot fund their own bed or hospital beds.”.

Other nations, including the US and Canada, have tightened their migration programs, with Canada reducing its parent visa program.

Hanson noted One Nation was not barring migrant parents from moving to Australia but they would have to be sponsored or they could visit their families on temporary visas.

“We’re not saying parents can’t come out … [but] they will actually be sponsored by the families, not the taxpayers.”.

Under One Nation’s proposal, thousands of parents and grandparents of migrants in Australia waiting for a permanent visa would be refunded their $50,000 fee and kicked out of the country.

“One Nation seems to be living in a fantasy world where basic human decency doesn’t exist, and neither does economic logic,” the Labor senator Tammy Tyrrell told parliament.

“Farms and businesses will be left without the workers they need, which means fewer jobs for Australians too.

Recent findings suggest that “Someone has to pay for that hole. It means higher taxes, more debt, and cuts to services. We need migration for our economic prosperity,” she remarked.

There are about 150,000 migrant parents in the queue for a visa, with some already on bridging visas.

The home affairs minister, Tony Burke, warned skilled workers would not fill critical jobs in hospitals and nursing homes if they could not bring their families with them.

“What One Nation is proposing is a complete attack on the services that Australians rely on,” Burke remarked.

Hanson claimed that her strategy would save the budget more than $140bn over 10 years by slashing the refugee intake and scrapping permanent parent visas.

Zaki Haidari, who arrived in Australia from Afghanistan as a refugee in 2013 and is now a citizen, remarked the discussion was retraumatising for former refugees like him.

Industry observers note that “Being a refugee is not a choice,” he said. “It’s between death and life.”.

Haidari, who now works as a campaigner for Amnesty International Australia, said policies like One Nation’s dashed the hopes of those waiting years in refugee camps for their turn at safety.

“They’re living with that hope that one day the lottery will be for them as well,” he mentioned.

Under One Nation’s policy, migrants from countries on the Albanese government’s “do not travel” list, including Afghanistan, Iran, Lebanon, Syria and Somalia, would still be banned from entering the country.

One Nation indicates analysis based on Treasury modelling, adjusted for inflation, found the lifetime cost of a humanitarian entrant was $523,000, while accepting the parents of visa holders was estimated to cost the budget $514,000 per person per year.

Treasury modelling done in December 2021 found the cost was $400,000, with 90% of it borne by the federal government.

Labor has committed to retaining the refugee intake at 20,000 a year while the Coalition has promised to halve it to 10,000.

The foreign affairs minister, Penny Wong, accused One Nation of trying to divide the country. She remarked Australia had the right balance and questioned Hanson’s claim that Australia would remain above the OECD average under the minor party’s blueprint.

The OECD reports that Canada and Australia have the most generous refugee settlement programs per capita, but it only uses data for refugees resettled through the UNHCR process.

The intakes of other OECD countries, including Germany and the US, are made up largely of asylum seekers who cross their borders and claim protection, meaning their intake levels appear much lower under the OECD figures Hanson has used.

The opposition leader, Angus Taylor, ruled out further lowering the Coalition’s policy of halving the refugee intake to 10,000, adding “some of the greatest Australians came here as refugees to this country”.

The Greens leader, David Shoebridge, accused One Nation and the two major parties of being “engaged in a race to the bottom to turn back refugees, lock them up in offshore detention, and blame them for the cost-of-living crisis”.

“Pauline Hanson’s attack on refugees is just the latest shameful chapter in a decades-long campaign against people seeking safe shelter,” Shoebridge said.

Meanwhile, the Asylum Seeker Resource Centre called it a “predictable and cheap attack” on refugees, and said Victorians would reject One Nation at the upcoming state election.

The ASRC’s deputy chief executive, Jana Favero, remarked the public wanted leaders to bring communities together, not divide them.

In a recent development, “People are sick of politicians finger-pointing and blaming our newest arrivals for problems,” Favero noted. “Reducing the humanitarian intake will not make Victorians richer or safer. People want solutions, not scapegoats.”.

The Refugee Council of Australia noted the humanitarian program only accounted for about 6.6% of net overseas migration.

“Cutting it would do little to affect migration levels but would have devastating consequences for people fleeing war, persecution and violence,” the council’s co-chief executive, Adama Kamara, said.

“Many refugees in our region already have few opportunities to find safety and face the constant risk of being returned to countries where their lives and freedom are at risk.”.
