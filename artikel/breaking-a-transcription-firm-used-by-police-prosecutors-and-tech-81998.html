Breaking: A transcription firm used by police, prosecutors, and tech giants exposed customer data online

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A transcription manufacturer that caters to law enforcement, government departments, and Silicon Valley tech giants has resolved a security lapse after leaving a large amount of internal data, including customer transcriptions and their source materials, exposed to the web.

The data security lapse at San Francisco-based Tigerfish exposed transcriptions of police interviews, body-cam footage, and customers’ raw audio and video footage. The exposed data also included invoices of the organization’s customers, which include police departments and prosecutors’ offices.

According to Anurag Sen, a good faith security researcher who found the data, the files were located on an Amazon-hosted (S3) cloud server that was openly listed and accessible to anyone via an easy-to-guess web address — no passwords needed.

The security lapse underscores how simple flaws or misconfigurations can result in sensitive information getting exposed online, especially as AI makes it easier to uncover weaknesses that can put companies and their data at risk.

According to recent reports, sen discovered the exposed data online in October and alerted the firm to the problem, but did not hear back. Sen later provided details to TechCrunch in an effort to flag the incident with the company for resolution.

Recent findings suggest that sen, 30, is no stranger to TechCrunch. For the past decade, he has searched for sensitive datasets exposed online with the goal of notifying the companies and organizations that their information is open to the internet. Like many security researchers, much of his efforts are driven by a duty to secure the internet, and he describes his work as an “unpaid air-traffic controller.”.

Given Sen’s propensity for finding often highly confidential information online, we verified the source of the leak and the data, and contacted Tigerfish.

Soon after making contact, Tigerfish secured its exposed S3 cloud bucket.

Industry observers note that “We’ve blocked all public access to the bucket you identified, including directory listings and individual files,” Tigerfish president, Adam Goldberg, stated in an email. “We’ve also verified that all of our other S3 buckets are blocked to public access.”.

“We’re continuing to review and strengthen our storage security more broadly,” added Goldberg.

The tech giant said that Sen’s email disclosing the data leak was caught in its spam filter.

Recent findings suggest that it’s unclear if the manufacturer has notified its customers. Tigerfish did not respond to a follow-up email.

Discoveries of exposed datasets have consumed a large part of Sen’s early cybersecurity career so far. He scours the web daily using automated scanners and specialist search engines to identify databases, cloud storage, or other sensitive information that likely shouldn’t be left unprotected online.

The modern web runs on absolute trust, he stated, and operates under the notion that the world’s data is kept secure. But he described how his findings reveal the reality of how “fragile” the internet is behind the scenes.

Despite the recent focus on how AI is accelerating people’s ability to discover vulnerabilities, Sen remarked that many of the data leaks and exposures that he finds come down to basic human errors.

“An engineer spinning up a test cloud database on a Friday, disabling authentication to test an API, and forgetting to shut the door before heading into the weekend,” was just one example, he recounted.

Sen’s findings over the years highlight the ongoing problems caused by such security mistakes, just as the new wave of vibe-coding and AI-generated development is creating new security problems due to insecure and unchecked code deployed to the web.

Similar to Amazon’s recent efforts to make it difficult to make S3 buckets public without going through clear multistep warnings, newer vibe-coding platforms like Lovable and Supabase have also made changes to their platforms to prevent user mistakes.

Recent findings suggest that “Everyone often fears shadowy hackers,” Sen mentioned. But he argues that the real crisis is leaving sensitive systems and databases “wide open to the public internet without a password.”.
