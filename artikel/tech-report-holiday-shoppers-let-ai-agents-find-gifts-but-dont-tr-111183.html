Tech Report: Holiday shoppers let AI agents find gifts — but don't trust them with their credit cards

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that amando Komoda runs a Minneapolis manufacturer that sells supplies for dry-aging meat, and he uses an AI agent called Instinct to set up alerts for hard-to-find parts, such as a particular Raspberry Pi chip, so he doesn't have to search for them himself. Often he still buys the part himself. When he does let Instinct pay, he approves the purchase first and gives the agent a credit card number that works only once, so it can't charge anything he hasn't agreed to. "I wouldn't give it access to my best credit card," he told Business Insider. "I've watched enough sci-fi movies that I can't trust it completely.".

According to recent reports, komoda's caution is the opposite of what Meta $META and OpenAI promised when they launched their agents this fall. Meta and OpenAI pitched agents that would shop day and night and find the cheapest option without their owners having to check. Millions of people signed up. Meta mentioned on Oct. 21 that more than 10 million people had set up Muse, the agent it launched in September.

According to recent reports, far fewer have let their agents pay. Just 12% of U.S. shoppers, about one in eight, say they've let their own AI agent buy something for them, according to a holiday survey that Salesforce $CRM, the business software organization, published Nov. 12. Shoppers don't trust AI agents to spend their money yet, and Meta can't earn much from Muse until people let it complete their purchases.

Loni Stark, Adobe $ADBE's vice president of strategy and product, has built two AI agents herself and expects tools like them to take over the part of shopping people dread. "We don't like the anxiety of wondering whether we got the best price or not," she told Business Insider. "We're going to offload that to agents." She still decides when her agents spend her money. Museli, the agent she built on Muse, handled an entire purchase of ballet shoes from Nordstrom $JWN's website, but it got her permission before it paid. Her other agent, Molty, researches products she's thinking about buying, and she has never connected it to a way to pay.

People can approve every payment themselves and still end up paying with the wrong card. Katie Notopoulos, a senior correspondent at Business Insider, asked Muse to order her a salad while she was reviewing three agents. Muse offered her a debit card or a Mastercard $MA from the accounts she had already linked. She picked the Mastercard because she didn't recognize its last four digits and assumed it was the new credit card that had just come in the mail. When she checked her wallet, she found she had chosen her health savings account card, which works only for medical expenses. She was so busy sorting out the mix-up that she never told Muse to place the order, and Muse never sent it. "This turned out to be a real fiasco," she wrote, "and I learned my lesson: it would have been infinitely easier for me to just do it myself.

Recent findings suggest that like Notopoulos, most U.S. holiday shoppers still want to handle their own payments, even when AI does the searching. About two in five U.S. shoppers now rely mainly on generative AI for at least one holiday task, the consulting firm Deloitte's annual holiday survey found, yet fewer than half of them would trust the tool to complete a purchase, even with their permission. Nearly twice as many people would trust an AI agent to pick gifts for friends and family as would let it pay with their card, even if they could check its work first, a September survey by the customer-service software tech giant Talkdesk found. Shoppers are ready to let an agent find the gift but not to let it spend their money.

Muse people do buy things. More than a quarter of Muse's weekly users shop with it, Meta told analysts on its Oct. 21 earnings call. In Muse's first week, people bought so much more than anyone expected that Meta had to work with the payments firm Stripe to raise credit limits.

According to recent reports, across the web, though, agents mostly browse. HUMAN Security, which sells software that spots and blocks bots, found that AI agent traffic on the sites it protects grew about eightfold from August to October, and most of it landed on shopping sites. Nearly all of the agent visits were for browsing. Most went to product and search pages, and fewer than one in 50 reached a checkout or payment page. HUMAN counts where a request lands, not whether anyone bought anything, so even a visit to a checkout page doesn't prove a sale.

Recent findings suggest that many of the sites HUMAN protects keep Muse out entirely. Blocked agent requests hit a record in September, and HUMAN said Muse was the main reason. The manufacturer's fraud software blocks Muse unless a site chooses to allow it, and because the agent is so fresh, many sites still haven't. Susan J. Li, a Meta executive, told analysts on the earnings call that some businesses are "understandably focused on protecting customer relationships." On sites that block Muse, the agent can't buy anything, and its owners have to shop for themselves.

Even where agents get in, the puzzles and texted login codes that stores use to confirm a person is buying can stop them before they pay. Komoda, the Minneapolis business owner, often finishes purchases himself because Instinct can't always solve the Captcha puzzles some checkouts require. When Business Insider reporter Notopoulos had OpenAI's agent, Dot, get her lunch, Dot kept getting stuck on the six-digit login code sent to her phone, and she had to request the code three times before the order went through.

Industry observers note that walmart $WMT and Shopify $SHOP have built checkouts for agents, and both keep a person in charge of every purchase. Walmart declared on Oct. 21 that each AI agent would get a profile of its own inside a customer's account, under a system the company calls Agent Profiles. By default, the agent has to ask for approval before it buys anything. People can instead let it buy on its own up to a limit they set, such as $50, and the agent never sees their card number.

It has been revealed that shopify asks people to approve every payment an agent makes at its merchants' stores. Shopify's checkout inside Meta's AI services, Muse among them, is switched on by default for eligible stores, and it covers buyers in 11 countries, including the U.S. In every purchase Shopify describes, people review the order and approve the payment before it goes through.

Meta's plan to make money from Muse depends on how often people approve a purchase. On the earnings call, CEO Mark Zuckerberg remarked Meta would start making money from Muse sooner than it usually does with a upcoming product, under a model in which it collects "a percent of the value that we are helping to create for people." But Meta collects its percentage only on the sales people let Muse finish. Li, the Meta executive, told analysts that the organization expects many Muse experiences to "remain AI assisted rather than fully autonomous" in the near term.

Once Meta collects a share of each sale, people have one more reason to check the price and the seller before they say yes. Shoppers already worry about AI pushing them toward the wrong purchase. Nearly three in four people in Talkdesk's survey want stronger rules on how retailers use AI, and 40% worry they could be misled into buying something that isn't right for them.

This Cyber Monday, many shoppers will send agents to find gifts but still read the price, check who's selling, and type in their own card numbers. The agents save them the search. Checking out is still a job people do themselves.
