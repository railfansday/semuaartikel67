12 Features of the Experience of Addiction

Do all addictions share one defining feature or characteristic? I tend to think not, and instead argue that addictions are better understood as having a family resemblance. Members of an extended family may not share one trait but rather a host of traits that overlap, crisscross, and connect the individuals. With addiction, there are patterns and weaves that connect the behaviors classified as addictions.

Here are 12 features or dimensions that I believe to be common characteristics of addiction.

It has been revealed that addiction is one way that a person can lose themselves. The loss can take multiple forms depending on the individual, the substance they use or behaviors in which they engage, and the contexts in which they live.

According to recent reports, addiction is a condition that imposes some limitations on people. Based on a wealth of data from my own experiences, I am certain that I cannot drink socially. I may be OK for a while, but I have good reason to believe that my drinking will pick up steam like a runaway locomotive. This certainty is bedrock for me and enables me to impose limitations on my own actions. I don’t go into bars even if you tell me that it’s a restaurant that serves drinks. I know a bar that sells food when I see it, and I am not going in it.

Sources indicate that as I have gotten older and spent more time living in recovery, I’ve come to see that there is freedom within limitations. Too often we assume that having limitations imposes constraints that make us less free. This is true to some degree, but it obscures an crucial dimension of freedom.

Many philosophers would say that absolute freedom of choice is utterly terrifying; we humans would overload on the possibility and choice. I get overwhelmed trying to select a particular Triscuit cracker I want now that there are about 20 versions of that classic cracker. Getting overwhelmed by choices often leads to bad decisions.

Recent findings suggest that A defining feature of addiction is that there are always negative consequences. This is a necessary condition. Addictions take time to develop. While early on, use may bring some benefit, at some point, the costs to a person become greater than the benefits.

In a recent development, addiction is a multilevel condition with personal, interpersonal, and broader social dimensions. On the personal level, it is the person—not their brain—that is addicted. Within a person, it is a biochemical condition where drugs and certain behaviors affect functions of the brain but also different organs and systems.

Addiction is a full-bodied condition. My hangovers lasted days, often causing terrible nausea, shakes, and blurred vision among a host of physical ailments. When not drinking straight out of a bottle but out of a glass, my right hand always held the glass and swirled the contents around. I still catch myself doing that when served seltzer in a short glass. It brings me right back. The smell of beer turns my head while the smell of red wine turns my stomach. Our bodies hold memories and meanings.

There are important psychological dimensions to addiction as well. How we feel about ourselves and the beliefs we hold affect our use, which in turn affects how we regard ourselves. Addiction shapes our sense of self and how we see ourselves in the world.

For some, addiction is a spiritual condition, where drugs and behaviors try to fill a void or create meaning that seems to be missing.

Addiction is also a moral condition in which certain moral goods such as dignity, self-respect, care for our own selves and others, friendship, and flourishing are harmed. To claim addiction is a moral condition is not to claim it is a moral failure.

Addiction is also an interpersonal phenomenon. We humans are social creatures who are always in multiple relationships with family, friends, colleagues, neighbors, and other community members. Some of these are intentionally and freely chosen; others are a matter of circumstance or chance.

Industry observers note that for many, the use of alcohol or other drugs made social interactions easier—at least at the beginning. Most of us learned how to use with others. We may have had drinking or using buddies, and many of our social interactions may have revolved around our use. When we progressed past a certain point, others may have cut us off, or we pruned people from our lives who questioned our use.

It has been revealed that addiction is a political phenomenon as well. By “political” I don’t mean electoral politics but the way that power flows through all the dimensions of our lives and communities. Power shapes the choices that some people face.

We are all free to quit paid employment, for example, but if we need to work to support ourselves or our families, or because we need insurance, we are free in theory rather than in practice. Also, we may feel compelled to adjust our careers or other significant life choices because of what others want for us or what society expects of us. Thus, we face all sorts of political or power pressures each day and try to make the best decisions possible.

Sometimes, we are caught between a rock and a hard place on the political level, and this may lead to unhealthy or bad decisions on both the personal and interpersonal levels.

Recent findings suggest that in one sense, addiction is an equal opportunity condition; no one is immune to it. No one can be “addiction-proofed.”.

In another sense, addiction is a very unequal opportunity condition. Some will have more social, economic, or educational protective layers that enable them to head off a problem before it gets really bad or to mitigate the bad consequences of their use. Others who lack these protective features and insulating layers may be subject to many more stressors and pay a much steeper price for their behaviors.

All of these dimensions—personal, interpersonal, and political—overlap and crisscross, creating a very dense fabric of tangled issues. This dense tangle can contribute to the development of an addiction.
