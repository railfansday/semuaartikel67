Daily smokers are growing more negative about vaping

🌐 Source: Earth • 📅 17 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 6 min read (1189 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Daily smokers report more negative views of vaping and less disapproval of smoking, a shift researchers say could complicate efforts to help them quit.

Smoking bans and health campaigns helped make smoking less socially acceptable. But adults who smoke daily now report less disapproval of the habit from people around them.

Daily smokers in four countries were surveyed between 2016 and 2022. Over those years, smokers reported less disapproval of smoking and more disapproval of vaping.

The researchers called the pattern concerning because smoking is the more harmful habit.

In a recent development, the analysis was led by Giang Vu at the University of Queensland. She is a researcher at its National Centre for Youth Substance Use Research.

Her focus was on social norms. These are a person’s sense of what the people around them do and approve of.

“Social norms are linked to whether people quit smoking or take up vaping, so these shifts matter for public health,” Vu remarked.

It has been revealed that only attitudes were examined in this analysis. Quitting and switching to vaping were not tracked.

The answers came from the International Tobacco Control Four Country Smoking and Vaping Survey.

Sources indicate that it was carried out in 2016, 2018, 2020, and 2022. Canada, the United States, England, and Australia took part.

Answers from 19,915 adults who smoked daily were analyzed by Vu’s team. Most of them took part in only one of the four survey rounds.

Participants were asked four questions about smoking and the same four about vaping. How many of their five closest friends smoke? What do the people important to them think about their smoking?

The other two questions covered the general public’s attitude and the smoker’s own overall opinion.

In 2016, 63.5% of daily smokers said the people vital to them disapproved of their smoking. That share had dropped to 54.3% by 2022.

Less disapproval was also sensed from the wider public. In 2016, 83.3% believed the public disapproved of smoking. Six years later, 72.5% did.

In a recent development, smokers’ own views shifted too. A negative opinion of smoking was held by 52.1% in 2016 and by 43% in 2022.

In a recent development, this decline did not start with the new surveys. An earlier analysis of the same survey program covered 2002 to 2015. It found that disapproval of smoking among daily smokers generally weakened in that period.

Taken together, the two studies point to a long-term shift, Vu’s team noted.

One measure indicated no statistically significant change.

In 2016, 19% of smokers said more than half of their five closest friends smoked. The share was 17.6% in 2022.

For vaping, the answers changed in the opposite direction.

It has been revealed that in 2016, 31.3% of daily smokers mentioned the people crucial to them disapproved of vaping. By 2022, the share had grown to 41.4%.

In a recent development, smokers also sensed growing disapproval of vaping among the wider public. The share who believed the public disapproved of vaping rose from 41.2% to 48.3%. Both shares were highest in 2020 and somewhat lower in 2022.

Smokers’ own opinions of vaping turned more negative as well. In 2016, 32.8% held a negative opinion of vaping. By 2022, 49.1% did, compared to 43% with a negative opinion of smoking.

According to recent reports, even so, vaping had not overtaken smoking on the other two questions. Smokers sensed more disapproval of smoking than of vaping from the public and from people important to them.

It has been revealed that vaping also became slightly more common among smokers’ friends. The share who said more than half of their five closest friends vape went from 1.6% to 2.8%.

Vaping is regulated very differently in the four countries. In England, nicotine vapes are sold in shops. They are also promoted through the national healthcare system as a way to stop smoking. Australia formalized a prescription-only system in October 2021.

Sources indicate that levels of disapproval differed by country. Smokers in England sensed the least disapproval of both products. Smokers in Canada sensed the most disapproval of smoking.

Industry observers note that but the direction of change was largely shared. On seven of the eight measures, no evidence was found that trends differed by country.

The exception was negative opinion of vaping, which increased least in England.

“These trends were broadly similar regardless of smoking or vaping policies,” Vu said.

Her team suggested that international media coverage and major events may shape attitudes alongside national policy. One such event was a 2019 outbreak of lung injuries among people in the United States who vaped.

It has been revealed that the outbreak was later linked mainly to an additive in illegal cannabis vapes. According to the team, it was wrongly blamed on nicotine vaping.

Concerns about lung health during the COVID-19 pandemic may have added to the disapproval, they wrote.

Vaping is not risk-free. But current evidence indicates that it poses substantially lower health risks than smoking, the team wrote. There is also evidence that vapes help people quit.

“A growing disapproval of vaping may discourage people who smoke from switching to a less harmful alternative,” Vu remarked.

Recent findings suggest that the team is also worried by weaker disapproval of smoking. People who smoke daily would benefit most from quitting. Earlier research has linked disapproval of smoking to quit attempts.

In a recent development, efforts to make smoking socially unacceptable may be losing their effect on this group, the team wrote. Approaches that build smokers’ confidence and address the barriers to quitting may be needed.

Vu called for public health messages that convey the risks of both products accurately.

“This would allow people who smoke to make informed choices, while continuing to protect young people who have never smoked,” she mentioned.

The study can’t show that these attitudes changed what smokers did.

Every participant smoked daily, so the results do not represent the general public. The team also cautioned against applying them to young people.

Attitudes toward smoking and vaping have followed different patterns in that group.

Industry observers note that the group of daily smokers may itself have changed over time. Smokers open to alternatives may have already switched to vaping or quit. That would leave a group with firmer opinions against vaping.

The vaping questions asked about approval and opinion. People were not asked how harmful they thought vaping was. Rising disapproval may therefore partly reflect its social image.

Industry observers note that public attention in those years centered on a rapid rise in youth vaping, the team noted. Vaping may have come to look less like a quitting aid for adults and more like a youth problem.

The answers were self-reported. The study also can’t show that any country’s policies shaped its smokers’ attitudes.

Future studies could follow smokers over time. They could test whether a change in attitudes predicts a quit attempt or a switch to vaping.

The full study was published in the journal Nicotine & Tobacco Research.
