How a Screen-Free Retreat in South Africa’s Cederberg Rewired My Nervous System

As the sun dropped behind craggy peaks, the heat-baked sandstone dissolved into a deep twilight, and the silence thickened until the valley became a dark amphitheater. I had come to Bliss & Stars, an off-grid retreat perched above the Doring River deep in South Africa's Cederberg, for Orion's Odyssey, a program timed to the new moon, when the sky is at its darkest. As we neared midnight, the Milky Way's galactic core rose above the ridge, so dense and richly textured, it looked like the luminous, flowing reflection of the river below.

Recent findings suggest that my phone had lost signal hours earlier, somewhere on the winding, vertiginous drive into this private reserve of over 3,300 acres. Initially, I had found that confronting, until I made peace with what I stood to gain. The eye needs around 20 minutes of true darkness to adjust, and a single glance at a bright screen largely undoes it. Without a signal, I had no reason to look down at it. It was just a camera.

Cradled by ancient rock, Weaver’s Nest is a villa for two above the Doring River.

That encompassing darkness is what brought Heine Rasmussen, an astronomy and nature guide and science communicator, and his wife Daria, a meditation teacher and somatic practitioner, here. In 2018, they left behind the security of corporate life in Copenhagen for a quest to find the least light-polluted skies. After searching around the world, they found them in the Cederberg, just over four hours north of Cape Town.

They acquired the virtually untouched land in 2019, and first had to construct a rudimentary access road before they could start building the lodge. Everything came in by pickup truck and trailer, and was carried by hand for the last stretch. The chalets were wedged between boulders or into the rock face above the river. More recently, the couple added a villa, bringing the total to six units that can accomodate no more than 10 guests.

Sources indicate that on these monthly, stargazing-centered retreats, the nights belong to Heine. After dinner each evening, we gathered on a circular, open-air viewing deck in front of the observatory, where mattresses were laid out so we could lie flat and look straight up. One night Heine led us on what he calls "a little mental space walk," a star meditation designed to unground rather than ground us, during which he recounted the history of the universe. With our eyes closed, we breathed the night in through the nose and out through the mouth while he invited the city's stress to leave with each exhalation, until gravity seemed to loosen its grip and our arms and legs felt as if they might float. Falling asleep, he assured us, was perfectly fine.

After a night of stargazing, morning breathwork and yoga with Daria help put life into perspective.

The Cederberg's sandstone boulders have been sculpted by wind and water over aeons.

Recent findings suggest that at the 20-inch telescope, one of the largest privately owned in South Africa, he taught us without jargon, finding a nebula and talking our small group through it while we took turns peering into the eyepiece. Guests here range from billionaires seeking quiet to people who come for free on the lodge's bursary program, and all of them arrive curious, looking for purpose, or simply to disconnect from the world they’ve left behind.

Heine's first subject: time. Moonlight, he told us, reaches us about a second after leaving the moon; sunlight about eight minutes after leaving the sun. The two pale patches in the southern sky, the Magellanic Clouds, are satellite galaxies of our own, and their light set out more than 160,000 years ago, when our species was young and still almost entirely in Africa. From there he moved to the iron in our blood and the calcium in our bones, forged in stars that exploded before our sun ever existed. I had heard the stardust line many times in other remote locales but never within the context of physics, explained step by step, or woven into a meditation under a sky like this one.

Daria took care of the mornings, which began with a thick, warm ceremonial cacao, readying the nervous system for what was to follow. I missed my regular wake-up coffee, the richness of the chocolatey cacao not compensating for the familiar kick of caffeine. From there, we headed to our yoga mats in an open-air studio where Daria led us in somatic breathwork, referencing the enormity of the night sky. Her view is that star-studded skies like those the Cederberg reliably delivers do not make daily concerns disappear; they merely change their size, put them into perspective. Much of what she imparts during the morning breathwork sessions is about making room to allow yourself to feel that. Daria works with the stress the body becomes so good at absorbing—shallow breaths and a clenched jaw, in my case. From hysterical laughter to sobs of grief, more surfaced for our small circle than any of us had bargained for.

The Cederberg has some of the darkest skies on earth.

After brunch, we hiked narrow paths where Cape mountain leopard tracks were pressed into the sand and raptors like Verreaux's eagles and rock kestrels caught thermals overhead. We scrambled over the orange sandstone rock, carved into fantastical shapes piled like giant, irregular building blocks, before washing off the dust and dry heat in the Doring River, the water surprisingly cool even though it was the end of summer. In the overhangs we marveled at San rock paintings between 2,000 and 5,000 years old, a collection of sites which Heine and Daria are still discovering on their land. There are more than 2,500 known rock art sites in the wider Cederberg, and whenever I see them I can't help thinking that the people who left their mark here once stood in this same silence, under much the same stars.

Every plant-based meal is consciously plated to nourish body, mind, and spirit.

Industry observers note that with so much of the day spent on the move, in silence or alone, meals at the long wooden table in the lodge's open-blueprint kitchen became the social high point. With caffeine, refined sugar, meat, and alcohol off the menu, dining still felt indulgent despite being based on vegetables, fruit, and whole grains. Daria has little patience for the guilt and pressure so often associated with clean-eating dogma; in the retreat's cookbook, Eat, Thrive, Repeat, she writes about reaching the point where "appetite becomes information rather than judgment." In practice, that meant buddha bowls, ancient grain salads, roasted vegetables, and sweet treats at brunch like beetroot flapjacks with a miso caramel of dates and coconut oil.

One night, after hiking into the sunset and a surprise dinner in a cave, Heine gave an informal introduction to one of his passions, astrophotography. I learned how to hold a shutter open long enough to gather light the eye cannot. I stood still in the pitch black with a torch under my chin and came away with a portrait of myself against thousands of stars, the camera collecting photons that had been traveling for centuries, some for millennia, before they reached its sensor.

As you fall asleep, the night sky is framed in floor-to-ceiling glass.

On the final night, the telescope and the table worked in tandem. Dinner with Stars ran to six courses, eaten between turns at the eyepiece, and, read in order, the menu told the life story of the universe much as Heine had been telling it to us since we arrived—from Massive Bang, a baked carrot dish, through to The White Dwarf, a wild strawberry dessert named for the small, dense ember a star like our sun leaves behind when it dies. The carbon on my fork was made inside stars that died before our sun existed, scattered into space, swept into the cloud of gas and dust that became our solar system, and pulled from the air, eventually, by the carrots on my plate.

Recent findings suggest that because it was summer and we had to wait for the sky to turn inky black, nights ended well past midnight. Back in my chalet, on a bed stuffed with horsehair, seaweed, and coconut fiber, I sipped from a flask filled with locally grown rooibos sweetened with wildflower honey before drifting off to sleep. The chalet's floor-to-ceiling windows face east over the river and are free of curtains to encourage waking with the morning light—a gentle but powerful way to reset one's circadian rhythm. When I opened my eyes in the small hours, the night sky filled the glass. I had never seen it framed like that.

Running year-round except July in South Africa's Cederberg, Bliss & Stars hosts intimate, all-inclusive retreats in six off-grid units sleeping a maximum of 10 guests.
