How job hunting became an expensive cash suck

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

There’s an expense that’s easy to overlook when talking about the cost of living: getting and keeping a job. We tend to think of work as the thing that pays the bills, but commuting and the other costs associated with it come with a price tag of their own. In a recent TikTok video posted in October, content creator Char shared that she had just had to quit her job because she could no longer afford the commute. Char, who lives in the UK, mentioned the amount of money she used to spend on a full tank of fuel now only filled her car halfway. And with fuel prices showing no sign of easing, she said, the commute was only going to get more expensive. “I can’t afford to keep driving to work,” Char said. “I can’t afford to live. Something has to give.”  And right now, she decided, that something was her job. She would have to search for a position closer to home. In the comments, people shared they had been in a similar position recently, also highlighting how everything else had hit them financially, such as grocery prices and rent. Commuting costs are also affecting people looking for work. Some job seekers have shared online how the expense of traveling to interviews is becoming difficult to manage. One creator said in a recent TikTok that she can no longer afford the job hunt. “Assuming you get past the 12,000 rounds of phone screens and Zooms, and you are invited to an on-site interview, what if you don’t have the money to commute?” she said. Compound that with the fact many are interviewing at several different companies at once, she said, and the costs quickly rack up. The costs aren’t necessarily limited to getting from A to B. Job seekers may also need to pay for childcare, buy appropriate clothes, or cover other expenses associated with making a good impression. For those who have been unemployed for some time, and have to interview in person several times, they can easily end up hundreds of dollars out of pocket. An Indeed/Harris Poll survey found that 84% of job seekers had spent money attending an in-person interview. Of those who had spent money, 30% said they spent at least $100 on their most recent interview. Meanwhile, a 2026 Monster survey of 1,005 employed US adults found that 49% of workers had turned down a job opportunity because of commuting costs or the length of the journey. More than three-quarters (76%) said current gas prices increased their financial stress, and 68% believed employers should help with commuting expenses when employees are required to work on-site. Commuting costs highlight a widening inequality between people who can afford to look for work and those who can’t. For example, job seekers with savings or financial support from their families may be better positioned to travel to interviews, pursue roles further afield, or absorb the costs of a lengthy search. For others, the price of getting to work limits the opportunities they can realistically pursue. Work is supposed to be the most obvious way of providing financial security, but that isn’t necessarily the case anymore. For some, even getting their foot in the door is becoming something they have to budget for.
