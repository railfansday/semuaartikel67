In brief: 5/5. Grand Theft Auto VI (reviewed on PS5, out 19 November) is the most convincing open world I’ve played.

It has been revealed that I laughed out loud multiple times within minutes of starting Grand Theft Auto VI. Not a chuckle or a smirk, but proper laughs, grinning ear to ear, as a particularly large resident of Leonida giggled, ran, stumbled, staggered and tripped over a railing into the river below. It was ridiculous and completely unexpected, and I loved it.

We've waited 13 years for GTA VI and from the opening credits, Rockstar has crafted a hyperreal but intensely personal world where every corner of Leonida and Vice City offers a surprise. I've been playing for three weeks, finished the story, seen some endings, and I'm still playing to find out what random secrets Leonida has left to share. Much of it has nothing to do with the fate of Lucia and Jason, GTA VI's modern-day Bonnie and Clyde. (Read our GTA VI coverage for more on the game.).

Industry observers note that driving around, you'll spot Just Stop Oil-style protests blocking traffic, teenagers dropping rocks onto motorway cars, or posters and flyers that lead to mysteries and new characters to call from your iFruit phone's contacts list. These aren't side quests in the traditional sense, with a marker on your map and a list to tick off; they're connections to random people, and the sense that this world exists beyond the screen.

Those teenagers dropping rocks? I detoured to teach them a lesson. Later, I stopped for someone posting for social media while balancing on a bridge, and failed to save him when he fell, and I actually felt bad. I pulled over for some teens daring each other to jump from a bridge into a river, decided to show them how it was done, and while I was swimming, they stole my car.

It's not risk and reward; it's just life in Leonida. It's incredibly fun.

In a recent development, the scale of GTA VI is incredible, but not in the usual sense of a vast map to cross. It's the depth and detail that gets me: the branding, signage, store shelves of snacks and comic books that can be read, snatches of conversations from passersby muttering about their lives or something they heard on the radio, the same thing I heard on the radio. Most buildings can be broken into and explored, and I've spent far too long sneaking into warehouses that have no obvious reason to exist beyond making the world feel authentic and the possibility that something of value could be snaffled away inside.

Shops, bars and clubs are lovingly detailed, and once inside you're free to toy with the world. Buy something, drink something for a stat bonus, steal something, change your clothes, or earwig and people watch. Sometimes you'll need to change outfits, because it throws off the cops chasing you; sometimes just because you feel you should wear beach clothes on the beach, because I'm completely hooked into this world.

The geography is considered too. Vice City is a maze of overpasses and winding spaghetti motorway junctions; the city itself is a muddle of back alleys, malls, and beachfront hotels, while the roads out in the country are simpler and more relaxed; the swamps need care to navigate; the sandy, wooded isles of the north are a stolen boat. Beaches, swamps, forests and distinct neighbourhoods all have their own character, and you feel it: stressed in Vice City, calm and playful up north, the mullet of map design, if you will. Even the residents and police behave differently depending on where you are.

Early on, Jason and Lucia visit a rich, enclaved community filled with jewellery shops, polished pavements and high-fashion stores practically demanding to be robbed. A policeman on a bicycle stops them and warns them that their kind isn't welcome. I pushed my luck, choosing confrontational dialogue options, refused to obey, and he called in backup from other pedal-bike cops who made it clear our kind wasn't welcome. I returned at night and stole everything, and it felt so good.

Giving you an emotional reason to do things is one of GTA VI's biggest successes. You start to understand the people who live here, the divisions between communities and the little injustices that make you want to cause trouble.

There's always something happening, too. An armoured car is robbed, so I follow it to the thieves' hideout and steal everything. A farm sign says 'pilot wanted', so I drop in and end up in a whole side story about meth-strawberries that eventually riffs on a Marvel character I never expected. Leonida's residents are 'Karens on crack', too. Scrape someone's car, and they'll chase you down; look at someone too long, and they'll demand a fight; drive down a motorway, and hyped up teen will lean out of their car to challenge you to a race. Once I planned a bank heist, only to find a random gang shooting the place up, and I got caught in the melee. It's layer upon layer of random, living events, and even on a mission, it's easy to get sidetracked as gunshots ring out.

According to recent reports, GTA VI rewards your downtime too; it wants you to simply enjoy the place and the moment because conversations eavesdropped on in a bar are hilarious but also tease secrets about the world, whether residents are complaining about the weather or arguing over culture-war nonsense raised by the State's shock jock, who is furious about that weekend's Manatees football result and the demise of 'mansculinity'. Some can be engaged with and will share random but useful news about Leonida.

It has been revealed that there's no Ubisoft-style checklist here. Stunts, hidden collectable cards (which you can sell in-game), fishing challenges and sporting events are there if you want them, but the world grows as you explore, meet people and build up your contacts list. You just exist in Leonida and enjoy exploring, competing and completing things.

The weather is a massive part of that experience, because it looks beautiful – golden morning sunlight cuts through thick mist over a wildflower meadow with realism, dramatic storm clouds and dense golden fog engulf a city skyline, obscuring buildings and bridges in an atmospheric haze.

But weather also affects how you engage with the world. Days feel long enough to play in, different things happen at different times, and the weather is dramatic and distinct. That hazy mist can obscure your visibility, while hard rain can limit distance and disrupt sound, something used to dramatic effect in a nighttime kidnapping rescue where large halogen lights pierced the rain and mist, creating drama but affecting line of sight in some spaces.

Sources indicate that likewise, a plane handles differently in wind, rain and cloud, as the controls judder and inputs feel less responsive. Flying through a storm, with lightning sparking and rolling across the horizon, gives you a real sense of place and moment but also puts you slightly on edge.

In a recent development, jason and Lucia's relationship really surprised me in GTA VI, and it's one area where Rockstar picks up where Red Dead Redemption 2 left off. Both are well-rounded protagonists with motivations and shared dreams that you help build. Lucia is alive and sprightly, Jason is dour and determined, and how their connection develops, or doesn't, becomes a game of its own.

In a recent development, A subtle romance and empathy system is at play. How much time the pair spend together, the conversations you choose, the dates, time at home (yes, you can make love in the shower, on the sofa, in the kitchen and, well, all sorts of places) and even how and when you proposal and commit crimes all feed into how their relationship develops, and it can affect how their story ends.

Industry observers note that what you do on a job matters too. Kill lots of people, and you get a different criminal rating than if you spare lives, and the duo seem to have a healthier relationship if you play to control a room with threats or shoot to injure rather than murder. I wanted a loving crime couple, so almost without meaning to, I started shooting people in the legs and arms to make them fall or drop their guns instead of headshotting everyone in a room. I wanted my Lucia to like my Jason.

Likewise, when Jason went undercover as 'the poolboy' for a saucy film shoot, I held back on how far I took things. Lucia was getting madder in my ear as Jason applied lotion to an actress, and when given the choice to go all in, as it were, I pulled out, as they say, as it were, because I wanted Lucia to still like Jason. I still don't know how far you can take that mission, but this is Rockstar, so I imagine it's all the way (and that's what she said).

Few games get that emotional connection right. It doesn't come only from good writing and characterisation, but from all the little conversations, actions, reactions and animated moments when they meet up or succeed. You'd have a heart of stone to go out of your way to ruin their romance, because you can. You monster.

It has been revealed that controlling when and how they meet up is part of GTA V's character-switching development. Moving between them is nearly instantaneous: the camera pulls out to a global view, zooms back down, and you can message the other to hook up or tell them to meet you somewhere. And you do get committed: when I occasionally Irish-goodbye'd Jason, I felt the need to call him and check up.

It has been revealed that the central narrative picks up what worked in Red Dead Redemption 2, splitting the story into chapters that each introduce a new gang or cast and a grand setup to proposal towards. The structure gives you plenty to play with, and the story spans more years as the couple bounces from backwater crimes to epic, militarised heists. These feed into cinematic tentpole moments that draw on everything from the later Fast and Furious movies to Assault on Precinct 13, with even a little nod to Die Hard as Lucia sparks a lighter in an air vent, laughs and paraphrases the classic line, "Come out to the coast, we'll get together, have a few laughs.".

It's also where Rockstar's love of a good heist movie really shines. Some of the bigger set pieces have the clockwork precision of an Edgar Wright film, with different characters, objectives and moving parts all working towards one carefully choreographed finale. The editing is part of the fun: the action cuts between Jason and Lucia as they tackle their separate jobs, each sequence dovetailing with the next as the plan unfolds, the clock ticks down, and the stakes rise.

The grand finales of each chapter are proper spectacles that never play out the same way and deliver 'cinema'. You might be flying gunships over a stormy ocean, or swapping between Jason and Lucia as they complete different parts of a larger initiative. Jason races to a rooftop to stop mobster reinforcements while Lucia hacks a vault, and you're jumping between the two as the action cuts back and forth, the tempo building with each switch. One moment you're holding off a gang; the next, you're working through the other side of the operation, and it all clicks together like clockwork. It's a wonderfully cinematic way to make character switching part of the action, not just a convenient way to get around the map.

Industry observers note that around these set pieces that take time to build are lower-key missions, some tailored to each character, and this is where Rockstar earns its rewards. The satire of modern America, social media, celebrity culture and consumerism that we love about GTA is here in abundance. A favourite is a storyline in which Lucia becomes embroiled in a bored-housewives vape cult that's like Goop meets Elizabeth Holmes.

Then there are the random encounters that make Leonida feel active, and never run how you think. An elderly woman screaming for help on a back road took me to her farmhouse, where I expected a robbery or a rescue. Instead, a client had 'died' in a bondage orgy gone wrong, and I needed to dispose of the body in a river. Even that didn't end as I expected.

Even 40-plus hours into the main story, Rockstar finds upcoming ways to surprise me. It introduces a vault-hacking mini-game that uses haptics and controller prompts for one bank job, then drops in a drug-dog sidekick for a single mission. Ideas are picked up, enjoyed and dropped before they go stale. GTA VI has so many that it never has to hang around with one for long.

In a recent development, shooting and weapon handling are superb, and each gun feels distinct. The cover system and enemy AI respond nicely even in busy rooms, and stealth is reliable and effective. While the substantial set-piece story missions can feel a little restrictive and scripted because they need to reach the cinematic heights Rockstar is aiming for, anything can happen outside them.

Sources indicate that A bank heist, for example, needs proper planning. Park a getaway car, choose the right vehicle, pick the rear door lock, hack the cameras, wear a disguise, and don't overstay your welcome. The world is so well designed and systematised that everything feels connected. Next time, go in through the front door, or steal a truck and pull an ATM from the wall. Heists in backwater towns are easier because there are fewer police around. And nothing plays out quite as you expect; every bank is unique, and after two seamless jobs I go for a third and it all goes wrong – an unexpected teller wanders into the office for a coffee, an alarm is tripped, and before I realise it, I'm shooting my way out, bags shouldered, and looking for a latest car to steal… the best-laid plans are shot.

The police react to all this more deeply than in previous GTAs. There's no longer just a star rating and a zone to escape, but how you escape matters. Swap cars and clothes, and they'll lose you, because they're looking for your description, not you, me, the player. All the old tricks are still here, from roadblocks and air support to increasingly aggressive police AI, but you have upcoming ways to shake them off beyond just driving fast.

It ties into car ownership too, another Red Dead Redemption 2 influence. You can't carry everything, so it's worth stowing a change of clothes and weapons in your car. Carrying a gun openly gets the police involved, but some Vice City passers-by will also decide to take you out as well.

Driving feels familiar from GTA V, but with enough nuance to feel like an evolution, as cars have different weights and handling, and each one reacts differently through the DualSense triggers: sports cars resist less than family saloons when you pull to accelerate. Boats, bikes, planes and helicopters all work as you'd expect, but even here there are subtle shifts in how responsive each can be – one helicopter I stole was uncontrollable and spun into the swamp.

Character models, facial animation, skin, hair, and clothing all deliver the hyperrealism I expected from a new GTA, though they're still larger-than-life caricatures built to feel relatable rather than photorealistic.

Sources indicate that animation, including the transitions between gameplay and cutscenes, is where GTA VI takes its biggest strides. Lucia and Jason are wonderfully handled, whether it's the little snuggles and embraces or the way they hold up a bank and play off one another, but the breadth of animation across GTA VI is what impresses. Every Leonida resident feels uniquely animated, and it's fun just to people-watch. Early on, a drunk girl follows Jason around a bar and is authentically annoying, and the nightlife feels realistically overwhelming. Bar bands are animated to sell their next song, and even the wildlife has moments: I watched a white-tailed doe and buck nuzzle each other lovingly and naturally through Jason's rifle scope. It was cute enough to make me take a breath, but Jason needs more money, so the shot rang out and the wildlife scattered.

According to recent reports, leonida has a recognisable visual identity, bold and vibrant, with every corner full of quirks, from the deep forests of the north to the out-of-town mall and rows of copy-and-paste suburban houses. Fictional adverts, billboards, shops, signage, and TV build the world, as if you see an ad for a beer or car, and you can buy it (or steal it). Beyond signage, graffiti walls, sculptures, exhibits, and more invite you to linger and just take in the world.

Sources indicate that it also feels like a realistic evolution of the 1980s Vice City I knew and loved, a city with a history inside a broader world, with references to Liberty City aplenty, from car number plates to Jason and Lucia's accents. It's somewhere to wallow, whether you're having a cocktail under palm trees swaying in a breeze or tearing up Vice City in a neon-lit supercar while crypto bros trade and wannabe rappers parade.

It's a wonder this all runs on a base PS5. I remember the mess of equally ambitious games like Cyberpunk 2077 at launch, but GTA VI is in a different league. The frame rate is locked at 30fps, and it's fine: stable, with little slowdown or stutter, and controls and handling feel spot on. There's occasional soft pop-in, but few visual compromises or trip-ups. You can feel the work that's gone into making everything run smoothly, and GTA 6 is polished to perfection.

Recent findings suggest that I'm really only scratching the surface. Familiar street races, bank heists, and chilled fishing and hunting trips are joined by crypto rug pulls, golf tournaments, and deep-sea treasure hunts. Or I just drive, hunt a swamp thing, stop to solve a mystery from a local ad, or relax with a game of pool and a Patriot beer. You can take on the State's sports and races, go sailing, or listen to 80s FM hits, the biggest dance tracks, or to the MAGA-inspired Richard Bastion Show – "more volume than logic" – who's mad about everything.

This game has video games inside it that feel more complete than some real games I've played, from the top-down sci-fi shooter Time Jackers: Rewind to a lightgun score-chasing bar game Bang For Your Buck. And in typical Rockstar fashion, they all have a satirical edge, like the indie auteur game Tenebrae Lux, which pokes fun at the po-faced walking sims that were all the rage: it's black-and-white, minimal, and filled with overwritten prose like a Taylor Swift song about metaphors.

According to recent reports, GTA VI is a world that exists beyond its main story, where everything feeds into and off each other. It's the best world-building I've experienced in a game in years, partly because its systems all work in a coherent, purposeful and layered way, but also because it's not afraid to be weird, whether that's discovering the dumpster lawyer, becoming a frog, slobbing on the sofa and watching an episode of Whose Dick's Whose, or finding an influencer in the boot of a stolen car who wants to be kidnapped for clout.

Industry observers note that there are minor restrictions, mainly when the big cinematic missions take over and funnel you down a more scripted path, but for good reason, and the 30fps cap won't suit everyone (yet it's really not an issue). But in a game that otherwise gives you this much freedom, they're the exception.

13 years? The wait has been worthwhile. I still set off with a blueprint, get distracted by something ridiculous at the side of the road, and lose another hour to a game I thought I'd finished. I laughed within minutes of starting, and I laugh every time I play. I've also been stressed, revolted, astonished, baffled, heartbroken and euphoric. I've run the full gamut of emotions, and I still do each time I return to Vice City.
