In a recent development, meta has justifiably attracted more scrutiny than ever this year, along with multibillion-dollar judgments and settlements, over how its business practices affect young social media people. The tech giant, however, is not retreating.

In fact, the tech giant has made a major push toward drawing teenagers to one of its flagship platforms, Instagram, by targeting them at their schools.

According to documents obtained and reported by NPR, Meta debuted an initiative called Project Gryffindor, named after a Hogwarts house in the Harry Potter series, to create school communities on Instagram in order to boost user counts and engagement on the platform.

In a recent development, meta’s goal of approaching young people where they spend the most time offline was not a secret. As recently as late September, the manufacturer said it was expanding its School Partnership Program, which includes 17,000 schools, with a stated goal to “help students and parents stay informed.”.

Industry observers note that the firm’s internal documents reveal some of the motivations behind Meta’s push for school adoption and Project Gryffindor’s proposed features, such as school banners on teen profiles and the ability to search school directories by class year. The details were acquired as part of discovery in a multistate lawsuit over whether social media platforms are designed to be addictive to users under 18. Meta is a defendant in the case, along with other companies, including TikTok, Snap and Google.

Industry observers note that in 2024, Instagram introduced Teen accounts and has since been trying to assure parents they’re safe while rolling out additional protections. Studies, however, have suggested that young teens regularly encounter unsafe content or deal with unwanted contact. Recruiting more owners at an early age without resolving these risks could make teens more vulnerable to mental health issues and increase the chances of being bullied or harassed.

A representative for Meta did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

It has been revealed that to anyone familiar with Meta’s long history of privacy oversteps on platforms like Facebook, the documents aren’t surprising. They reveal a organization primed to give school-age children upcoming ways to chat and connect with each other, and to encourage them to bring their friends along, expanding Instagram’s user base and engagement.

Industry observers note that like other social platforms, Instagram primarily makes its money from targeted advertising. User retention is a key part of Meta’s strategy.

In one of the document slides, titled “Why schools, why now?” which preceded Gryffindor’s launch, Meta noted, “Competitors are exploring school social use cases, but IG may be better positioned for school connections thanks to our differentiator of meeting new people and building relationships with acquaintances.” In other words, Instagram’s size and scale, with about 3 billion active owners globally, automatically make it a draw for students.

The NPR report comes within a week of a filing against Meta alleging that it’s withholding evidence in a major case involving social media’s effects on teenagers.

One of Meta’s marketing pitches for its School Partnership Program is that it’s trying to create online safe spaces for students, while giving parents and schools more oversight on what’s accessible on the platform. But experts warn that Meta’s history makes it hard to trust the organization with protecting kids from predators or scammers.

Katie Paul, director of the Center for Accountability’s Tech Transparency Project, which has been researching how Meta handles child-safety complications, noted that Instagram Teen accounts “are not safe as advertised” and that nothing is “legally requiring the company to make these safe.” Paul pointed out that Instagram introduced these accounts only when lawsuits over social media harm started rolling in.

“It’s all part of a broader marketing (effort) to claim that these are just safer for kids because they are Teen accounts,” Paul said. That marketing effort has included paid endorsements from therapists, psychologists and parent influencers to promote Teen accounts. The case for building up partnerships with schools is a way of “passing the buck on responsibility” to schools and parents, Paul said.

Parents, in particular, have less power in Instagram’s school groups: Unlike the school administrators tasked with managing school groups, parents have no powers to moderate or manage those groups; they can only view them. For Teen accounts in general, parents can’t view direct messages sent to or received by their children.

Meta’s business interests in its push for student engagement and school partnerships are easy to see through. Because kids are likely to be lifetime people on these platforms, getting them hooked early has long-term financial value.

“Ultimately, Meta is the one profiting off of the data collection of these kids while simultaneously failing to keep them safe,” Paul remarked.
