An Internal Locus Of Control Makes You A Happier, Wealthier Investor

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

An internal locus of control is the belief that you control your outcomes and can solve problems as they come up. An external locus of control is the belief that outside forces run your life. Luck. The economy. Your boss. Some CEO you’ve never met.

Peter Gray, a research psychologist at Boston College argues in his upcoming book, Restoring Childhood, that kids have become more anxious and depressed over the decades because adults took away their free play and independence. Children who never get to solve their own problems never build an internal locus of control.

And psychologists have long known that a weak internal locus of control is strongly linked to anxiety and depression.

Industry observers note that it makes sense. If you believe you have little control over what happens to you, the world feels cruel. At the extreme, you feel helpless. And when you feel helpless, darker thoughts start creeping in.

According to recent reports, as a father of young children, this hit home. As an investor, it hit my wallet. Recently, I got a $50,000 lesson in what it feels like to have zero control.

From an investor’s point of view, the active investor has an internal locus of control. He believes his research and decisions can beat the market.

According to recent reports, the passive investor has more of an external locus of control. He accepts that he can’t beat the market, so he buys the whole thing and lets it ride.

The irony is, despite the better feeling of being in control, most active investors underperform over time. I agree with that, which is why about 60% to 70% of my public investments are in the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq.

But I have two problems with fully going passive.

First, I don’t want to earn the same returns as everybody else. I want to outperform so my family’s buying power grows faster than the masses. If not, we'll just be running in place with every other passive index investor.

Recent findings suggest that second, accepting that I have no control makes me less satisfied. I want to believe my decisions matter. So I keep a chunk of my portfolio in individual stocks. One of them was Nvidia for the past several years.

My Nvidia position was worth about $150,000 in October 2026. It’s now worth about $100,000.

In a recent development, about $30,000 of the decline came from the broader AI selloff. Fine. That’s the market. I can live with that.

The other $20,000 came from Jensen.

Industry observers note that first he admitted to an affair with Alice Huang, an executive at Megaspeed, a major Nvidia customer. U.S. officials reportedly believe she was a Chinese intelligence asset.

Then the New York Times reported a second relationship, this time with an Nvidia executive who reported to him. Days later, Jensen resigned as the board moved to oust him. The stock dropped again.

Finding and keeping love truly is harder than getting rich.

In a recent development, losing $50,000 is about a year and a half of food for my family of four. It’s also about a year and a half of the unsubsidized health insurance premiums we pay.

As the household’s provider, I feel like a failure for blowing up so much money. Without a steady day job, our investment returns are what keep us going. Every loss hits harder.

But mostly, I’m annoyed with myself for putting too much capital in one organization.

It has been revealed that here’s the uncomfortable part. Blaming Jensen is the external locus of control response.

In a recent development, an external locus of control means believing outside forces decide your fate. When I say “Jensen cost me $20,000,” I’m pinning my loss on a guy I’ve never met and can’t control.

It may be true. But it leaves me helpless. I can’t stop the next CEO from falling for someone he shouldn’t. I can’t make a board build a succession plan. And I definitely can’t make billionaires stay faithful.

So the internal locus of control question is: what could I have controlled?

Plenty, it turns out. I chose the position size. I chose to put about 40% of my public portfolio in individual stocks, instead of a more conservative 20% – 30% as suggested in my article, the Recommended Split Between Passive And Active Investing. I broke my own rule.

In a recent development, jensen made the mess. I decided how much it could cost me.

It has been revealed that I also never priced in key man risk. Jensen ran Nvidia for 33 years and the firm had no successor ready. When one person is the company, his personal life is part of your investment. I need to bake that into my model.

Industry observers note that and here’s the kicker. Going passive wouldn’t have 100% saved me. Nvidia is one of the largest holdings in both the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq. My index funds got dinged too. Passive investing doesn’t remove the Jensen risk. It only dilutes it.

Recent findings suggest that since the Fed started aggressively hiking rates in 2022, real estate has gotten little love. Mortgage rates follow the 10-year Treasury yield, which shot up, and demand softened across the country. Meanwhile, the S&P 500, especially tech and AI stocks, grabbed the spotlight.

It has been revealed that this Nvidia debacle reminded me why I prefer owning real estate over stocks. Living in your primary residence and having it appreciate it the background is easy.

Sources indicate that as a landlord, I have a high internal locus of control. I choose the tenants. I decide when to fix the roof, remodel the kitchen or replace the carpet. I set rents within the law. If the property underperforms, it’s usually on me.

And because I can make it better, I feel more confident and happier owning it.

No CEO can tank the value of my house with a bad decision in his personal life.

Recent findings suggest that real estate still has risks. Insurance covers the physical ones like fire and water damage, and with separate policies, earthquakes and floods. But insurance won’t protect you from a local recession, a spike in mortgage rates or a shift in rent laws.

Your property can absolutely lose value. It just won’t drop 20% in a week because one person made a bad decision.

Sure, real estate may only appreciate 3% to 5% a year compared to roughly 10% for the S&P 500. But with 20% down, 4% appreciation is a 20% return on your equity before costs.

According to recent reports, plus you collect rent every month, and that rent doesn’t shrink the property’s value the way a dividend shrinks a company’s cash pile. In this regard, rental income is superior to dividend income.

According to recent reports, the older I get, the more I value steady over spectacular. I’d rather not watch $20,000 vanish in a few days.

Here’s the funny thing. Say it turned out I was the International Personal Finance Playboy of the World, with multiple girlfriends and a dozen kids like Elon Musk. As an Asian American, I'd be crucified and it would be a scandal in the personal finance community.

If Financial Samurai were a publicly traded penny stock, the shares might drop. Then again, bad PR is still PR. Some media outlets might cover it, traffic would surge and the stock would probably go up.

But my real estate wouldn’t care either way. However bad a husband or father I might be, my properties won’t lose value because of my behavior. As an an investor, that's a beautiful thing.

According to recent reports, the only ways my real estate goes down are if the local economy turns or I neglect the properties and stop keeping tenants happy and rents at market. Both are mostly in my control.

According to recent reports, so real estate remains my favorite asset class for building wealth. It provides necessary shelter and generates a lot more income than dividends to help you break free from work sooner.

For the playboys and playgirls out there, it might be even more valuable.

According to recent reports, now for the contradiction. The older and wealthier I get, the less I want to be the landlord dealing with leaky toilets and tenant turnovers. I’ve been slowly selling physical rentals and reinvesting in private real estate for more passive income.

Sources indicate that doesn’t that mean giving up control? Yes.

According to recent reports, but I’ve realized control was never the whole point. What I really want to avoid is single-person risk. A diversified pool of rental homes can't be taken down by a single person. One bad tenant or one bad house doesn’t sink it. You still have to trust the fund manager, so do your homework there too.

It has been revealed that the healthiest locus of control is neither fully internal nor fully external. Control what you can: your savings rate, your asset allocation, your position sizes and the properties you choose to own. Then accept what you can’t, without feeling helpless.

Peter Gray’s point is that kids build an internal locus of control by doing hard things on their own: playing, exploring, failing and figuring it out without an adult hovering.

According to recent reports, investing works the same way. You build confidence by making decisions, owning the results and adjusting. Losing $50,000 in Nvidia this rapidly stings. But if it forces me to size positions better, it’ll make me a better investor over time.

Industry observers note that kids learn the same lesson in a different way. That’s one reason I want mine helping with tenant turnovers and rental property maintenance. Patching a wall, helping me do landscaping projects, and seeing a rental get leased teaches them that their effort changes outcomes.

In a recent development, sometimes it’s nice to outsource your financial and parenting duties to competent people. Just make sure they’re actually competent.

The last thing you want is to pay someone for worse results than you could have gotten yourself.

Industry observers note that how much of your portfolio is in individual stocks, and have you ever lost substantial because of one executive’s behavior? Does owning real estate make you feel more in control, or more stressed? Do you factor key man risk into founder-led companies? And for the parents out there, are you giving your kids enough room to develop an internal locus of control?

AI may be the next general-purpose technology, but compute needs physical buildings and power. One way to get exposure without owning a single chip stock is through private real estate and venture.
