How Europe Comes Apart

MUJTABA RAHMAN is Managing Director for Europe at Eurasia Group.

The Four Elections That Could Seal the Continent’s Fate.

It has been revealed that the European Union is entering a period of political instability that could threaten its future—not through its formal dissolution, but through the gradual erosion of its capacity to act. In recent months, Europe has been engulfed in protests driven by a common crisis of affordability, deteriorating public services, and declining confidence in governments’ ability to improve living standards, especially for young people.

According to recent reports, the protests that have swept across France, Germany, Italy, and Spain are not a single, coordinated European movement, but they reflect underlying problems that are increasingly common to a growing number of EU countries: a mounting perception that governments are unable to deliver affordable housing, functioning public services, and economic security. This widening gap between the public’s expectations and the economic and fiscal reality confronting many EU countries is only likely to grow over time. It could prompt Spanish voters to unseat Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez this weekend and usher in a right-wing government that includes the far-right party Vox. And it sets up 2027 to be a strikingly consequential year, as nine elections across the EU could completely remake the continent’s complexion.

Four of those elections, in France, Germany, Italy, and Poland, will be particularly important. In each of them, far-right populist forces could rise to the fore and turn their countries against greater EU integration, support for Ukraine, and collective European security. But dangers remain even if the far right does not win outright at the polls. A plethora of electoral outcomes could still see latest governments pull away from Brussels and vitiate attempts to strengthen European autonomy and defense. Instead of a bang, the European project could die with a whimper.

It has been revealed that the most consequential race will be in France. A series of domestic and international developments, along with a volatile national mood, have combined to make Marine Le Pen the front-runner to win the April 2027 election and become France’s first far-right president since the dissolution of the Vichy regime in 1944.

In a recent development, in October, riots and looting marred a series of mostly peaceful protests against high prices and deteriorating schools in a score of French cities. Those images—along with the diffuse sense of a country in paralysis—have shredded whatever remained of the domestic credibility of President Emmanuel Macron and his minority centrist coalition. Édouard Philippe, the strongest centrist contender to succeed Macron, has been tarnished by association. The first poll since the riots, conducted by IFOP for Le Figaro, demonstrated Le Pen’s support among potential voters rising to 36 percent, her highest level to date, while support for Philippe fell three points from a month earlier to 15 percent.

Industry observers note that philippe and the other centrist challenger, Gabriel Attal, another Macron-era prime minister, were already losing support because of high energy prices caused by the wars in Iran and Ukraine. Both candidates remain stuck between seven percent and 15 percent in the polls, and their campaigns have generated little momentum or popular enthusiasm.

Meanwhile, an opportunistic coalition of far-left extremists and young looters from the poor multiracial suburbs of French cities who were responsible for much of the violence in October will hurt the far-left candidate Jean-Luc Mélenchon, who has been accused by the government of helping incite the unrest. He has certainly failed to condemn the disturbances. But the disorder has not dampened his allure, as support for his candidacy has risen to between 17 and 19 percent in polling, which makes him a credible contender to reach the second-round runoff against Le Pen. Unfortunately for Mélenchon, polls suggest that Le Pen would thump him in the second round, by at least 65 percent to 35 percent.

Recent findings suggest that the election seems very much Le Pen’s to lose. No presidential candidate since the Fifth Republic began in 1958 has held such a commanding first-round lead in opinion polls five months before election day and lost.

A Le Pen presidency would be nothing short of an earthquake for Europe. It would deliver a death blow to the EU’s ambition of greater “strategic autonomy”: Brussels’ goal of achieving greater political independence from the United States and China, primarily via more European integration. This was a vision espoused by Macron in a speech at the Sorbonne in September 2017, in which he called for a “sovereign, united, and democratic Europe.” Le Pen is committed to unilaterally (and illegally) withholding French contributions to the EU budget; to ending French and EU aid to Ukraine; to restraining all EU spending; to blocking the enlargement of the EU to neighboring countries; and changing the French constitution to enshrine “national” primacy in jobs and welfare, which would effectively destroy the European single market. She may be denied some of these ambitions, but a hostile Paris could very easily stymie any forward movement of the EU.

Recent findings suggest that centrist governments are on the defensive beyond France, as voters across the continent conclude that they do not have the solutions to address their problems. Germany is a case in point. A weak and unpopular chancellor, Friedrich Merz, is finding it difficult to hold off the rise of the far-right, neo-Nazi Alternative for Germany party, known as the AfD, which continues to make sweeping electoral gains across the country.

Merz has to steward an economy that is struggling after decades serving as Europe’s industrial core. Although it is true that Germany’s economic outlook in 2026 and 2027 has brightened after almost flat growth in 2025, its recovery is driven exclusively by the government’s debt-financed investment programs. Key industries, including those to do with automobiles, machinery, pharmaceuticals, and chemicals, are still suffering from high energy prices, crumbling infrastructure, and a shrinking workforce.

Sources indicate that china’s aggressive trade practices, deliberately aimed at undermining some of Germany’s core sectors, continue to put pressure on German companies. The unwinding of the international trade system also disproportionately hurts Germany’s export-driven economy. So does Europe’s fragmented and undercapitalized banking sector, which provides too little venture capital for German start-ups to scale up rapidly. For these reasons, Berlin will have a hard time limiting significant job losses in its industrial sectors in 2027.

The result will be unemployment and growing electoral disgruntlement that Merz’s government will be poorly placed to address. If anything, the divides between the chancellor’s conservative camp and his coalition partner, the center-left Social Democrats, will prevent the government from delivering decisive reforms related to pensions, taxes, health care, the labor market, energy, and climate.

Industry observers note that five state elections in 2027, two of which are in the key states of North Rhine–Westphalia and Lower Saxony, will exacerbate this divide. Merz’s Christian Democrats will be vying for seats with the Social Democrats, keeping the two sides in a permanent competition that could prevent the coalition from governing effectively at the federal level. And that competition will likely be in vain: state election results for both parties will almost certainly be disappointing, leading to growing restiveness and speculation about Merz’s future as chancellor.

According to recent reports, these centrifugal forces within the government will collide with the determination of both parties to stay in the coalition and avoid provoking a fresh election. This will result in a largely paralyzed government, discredited centrist politics, and a widening sense of malaise across an already unhappy and disaffected electorate.

In a recent development, as the center struggles, the AfD on the far right and Die Linke on the hard left will continue to gather national momentum. For the first time, the AfD will almost certainly penetrate deeply into the electorates of Germany’s western industrial strongholds in state elections next year, hollowing out the constituencies of both Christian Democrats and Social Democrats. With its sights on the 2029 federal elections, the AfD will increasingly look like a national contender rather than a protest movement rooted in eastern Germany. Die Linke, for its part, will continue to siphon off votes from left-leaning parties with its platform of economic grievance, disinterest in support for Ukraine, and the highly successful mobilization of young voters around support for Palestinians. The gains of both parties will further splinter state parliaments and make the process of government formation at that level increasingly cumbersome. The resulting state administrations will likely be unstable and doomed to performing poorly, deepening voter disillusionment with party politics and encouraging a greater willingness to experiment with radicalism.

These electoral pressures in Germany and in France make it likely that the relationship between Berlin and Paris will grow strained. A nationalist-populist president Le Pen would have little interest in sustaining the French-German “engine”—the traditional motor of European integration. Le Pen’s political priority of cutting French payments to the EU would rankle Germany, the largest contributor to the EU budget. And any government in Berlin would be unlikely to accommodate French positions, especially those of a disruptive Le Pen presidency, such as weakening the single market or abandoning Ukraine.

Without convergence between French and German policy, it will be harder for the EU’s other states to build consensus on the economic and security challenges that matter most. This prospect has encouraged European policymakers to look east, toward Poland, and ask what “new Europe” can do if “old Europe” ceases to drive the EU forward. Yet Warsaw faces its own political troubles, and it will struggle to galvanize the continent if France and Germany fall away.

Poland will stage consequential parliamentary elections next year. The vote could bring the overthrow of the current centrist coalition and the creation of a far-right coalition government involving the right-wing Law and Justice (PiS) party, the far-right Confederation Liberty and Independence, and the far-right Confederation of the Polish Crown (KKP).

A coalition formed by all three would mark a significant shift in Poland’s domestic and foreign policy. Such a government would adopt a more confrontational approach toward the EU, particularly on the problems of migration, climate policy, judicial independence, and the next seven-year EU budget. Although PiS remains broadly supportive of NATO and Ukraine, Confederation and especially the KKP have adopted a more critical stance toward Kyiv. Their participation in the government could therefore erode Poland’s support for Ukraine, including its military and financial assistance to its embattled neighbor, and lead to tougher policies regarding Ukrainian refugees, nearly one million of whom remain in Poland after fleeing Russia’s full-scale invasion in 2022, according to Eurostat.

Significant ideological differences among the three parties, particularly over economic policy, would probably make this far-right coalition unstable. PiS’s preference for social spending and state intervention sits uneasily alongside Confederation’s economically liberal agenda. Moreover, the leader of PiS, Jaroslaw Kaczynski, has repeatedly ruled out cooperation with KKP’s leader, Grzegorz Braun, describing him as “very dangerous” and his party as “completely unserious.” A far-right government could be quite short-lived, if it emerges at all.

An alternative but equally problematic scenario would involve a hung parliament in which neither Prime Minister Donald Tusk’s Civic Coalition (KO) nor PiS could assemble a governing majority, resulting in a weak minority government or even fresh elections. Fragmentation among smaller parties and disagreements over potential coalition partners could prolong negotiations, particularly if PiS refuses to cooperate with KKP while Tusk’s coalition lacks sufficient support from centrist and left-wing parties. Even if a minority government were formed, its dependence on opposition parties for legislative support could severely constrain policymaking, leaving Poland unable to address its mounting security challenges or play any leadership role in Brussels.

In a recent development, italy, meanwhile, presents a different risk. Unlike France, Germany, or Poland, it currently has a relatively stable government led by a prime minister, Giorgi Meloni, who has reconciled her far-right nationalist origins with a broadly pragmatic approach to European and foreign affairs. Indeed, Meloni has become something of a poster child of far-right moderation, held up as evidence that ultranationalists can become responsible European leaders. But that equilibrium could be upset by the emergence of a latest political force even further to Meloni’s right.

Meloni remains the favorite to win next year’s election. But she is threatened by Roberto Vannacci, a former paratrooper and veteran of the Iraq and Afghanistan wars who has introduced a newly introduced party, National Future (FN), that is peeling away right-wing voters from Meloni with a nationalist, populist agenda that is skeptical of NATO and support for Ukraine. His goal is to court Italians aggrieved by Meloni’s compromises with Brussels.

In a recent development, the worst-case scenario would be a right-wing majority in which FN holds the balance of power and chooses to use it. That outcome would require the party to outperform expectations, approaching or exceeding ten percent of the vote—FN is at the moment polling at 6.5 percent—while Meloni’s Brothers of Italy party slips to around 20 percent or below. The coalition would still govern, but its center of gravity would shift decisively to the right, and Meloni would no longer have a firm grip at the helm.

Vannacci would likely not moderate his positions once in government, as Meloni did. Instead, he would maximize his leverage, up to and including threatening to pull out of the coalition and trigger snap elections, to extract concessions regarding Italy’s relations to Europe. His strategy would be deliberately confrontational: squeezing Meloni between the demands of his right-wing base and the constraints imposed by the EU, NATO, and the bond markets. The aim would be less to shape policy than to build his own visibility and lure supporters away from Meloni’s Brothers of Italy. Faced with a direct challenge on her right flank, Meloni would be forced to prioritize her own political survival, pulling the government toward more unorthodox positions.

Sources indicate that the effects would be most acute in foreign policy, as Italy moved from the consensual position it has adopted on Ukraine to more active opposition, a major challenge at a time when EU decision-making on Ukraine, defense, and the EU budget depends heavily on unanimity among the EU’s largest members. Italy could transform from a pragmatic, generally constructive player in Brussels into a more disruptive force, blocking or threatening to veto decisions on bilateral and EU-wide support for Ukraine. Alongside a far-right Paris, Rome would also block Ukraine’s path to EU accession. On EU financial matters, Italy would straddle Germany and France, siding with Berlin in opposing any increase in the bloc’s next budget and with Paris in resisting cuts to agriculture and funding for the EU’s poorest regions, narrowing the space for a deal on the EU’s next seven-year budget and certainly curtailing its geopolitical ambitions. Under pressure from Vannacci, Meloni could also be forced to abandon NATO’s target for member states to spend five percent of GDP on their militaries and even forgo her planned incremental increases in military spending.

Recent findings suggest that the government would push hard for more flexibility on EU fiscal targets and, in a more extreme scenario, breach the rules outright, reviving bond market volatility. On migration, Italy could refuse to take back asylum seekers from other member states under the EU’s Dublin rules, undermining implementation of the bloc’s migration pact.

Recent findings suggest that in considering these elections together, it becomes clear that the danger facing the EU is not that the union will suddenly collapse but that the political consensus that underpins European integration could begin to unravel in its key member states. The risks are not all equal. A Le Pen presidency is the most likely and most consequential for the EU, while political paralysis in Germany also looks increasingly difficult to avoid. Although Poland’s elections could produce a moderate center-right government, a far-right government or a period of protracted instability now looks increasingly plausible. Italy’s trajectory is perhaps the least threatening, but a government dependent on Vannacci would create another impediment to broader European cooperation.

Nor do all four races need to deliver the worst-case scenarios for the consequences in the EU to be far-reaching. A Le Pen victory in France combined with paralysis in Germany would itself throw up major problems for the EU, including a possible bond market crisis in France that could infect the broader eurozone. If Poland and Italy also moved in that direction, the EU could find itself without a functioning coalition of major states willing and able to address the growing security and economic challenges the continent is facing.

The biggest victim of these looming changes is likely Ukraine and, by extension, Europe’s own security. The burden of supporting Ukraine financially would fall on a smaller group of member states in northwestern Europe, but they by themselves would not be able to fully compensate for the loss of support from the likes of France and Germany.

In a recent development, the EU has survived previous internal and external crises because its major powers ultimately judged that their interests were better served by collaboration and integration than by going their own way. The danger in 2027 is that this election supercycle will deliver outcomes that mean this assumption no longer holds. Even if the EU perseveres through the upheaval ahead, it could lose something much more profound: the underlying political consensus that has allowed the bloc to function since its creation, seven decades ago. That would not be the formal end of the European project. But it could mark the beginning of its irreversible decline.
