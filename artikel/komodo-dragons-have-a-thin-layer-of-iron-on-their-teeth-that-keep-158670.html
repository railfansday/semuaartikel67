Komodo dragons have a thin layer of iron on their teeth that keeps them razor-sharp for tearing apart prey

Researchers discovered that Komodo dragons coat the serrated edges of their teeth in iron, staining them orange and keeping them razor-sharp for ripping apart prey.

Recent findings suggest that look closely at a Komodo dragon’s teeth, and you will notice an orange tint along the cutting edges. It is not a stain from feeding. Even teeth that have not yet pushed through the gums are just as orange.

Recent findings suggest that the color actually comes from iron. In a 2024 study, researchers found a thin, iron-rich coating along the tips and serrated edges, concluding that it helps keep the teeth sharp.

Scientists had never seen anything like it in a meat-eating reptile before.

“Komodo dragons have curved, serrated teeth to rip and tear their prey just like those of meat-eating dinosaurs,” said Aaron LeBlanc of King’s College London, the study’s lead author.

In a recent development, leBlanc’s team looked for the same coating on fossil dinosaur teeth and saw no evidence of it. But fossilization may be the reason.

Industry observers note that komodo dragons are the largest predatory lizards alive, and scientists have studied their teeth before. One detail had still been overlooked.

LeBlanc’s team examined teeth from skeletons and from specimens preserved in fluid. Every unworn tooth tip and serration was orange.

Serrations are the small points along a cutting edge, like the ones on a steak knife.

In a recent development, the rest of each tooth looked white. The enamel there is transparent, so the white comes from the layer underneath, called dentin.

Recent findings suggest that the enamel on a Komodo dragon’s serrations and tooth tips is about 0.0008 inches (20 micrometers) thick. That’s roughly one-fifth the thickness of a sheet of printer paper.

Sources indicate that the team found iron in the outermost layer of that enamel with every method it used. At its thickest, the iron-rich layer makes up about a tenth of the enamel’s thickness.

Recent findings suggest that one of those methods was X-ray mapping at a type of particle accelerator called a synchrotron. Those maps showed the iron concentrated along the serrations.

Sources indicate that with another method, the team found more oxygen where the iron was concentrated. The authors took that as a sign the coating is an iron oxide. Rust is made of iron oxides too.

How hard is the coating? On a Komodo dragon tooth, LeBlanc’s team found that difficult to test reliably, because the coating is so thin.

So the team turned to a tooth shed by an American alligator at a British zoo. That tooth had iron-rich enamel thick enough to test.

It has been revealed that to test it, the team pressed a fine tip into the enamel of that one tooth 74 times. The iron-rich enamel was slightly harder than the enamel beneath it, by about 9%.

Like the team’s electron microscope and X-ray experiments, the hardness tests were each run once, on individual teeth.

Recent findings suggest that iron may make the outer enamel along a cutting edge more resistant to wear. For the Komodo dragon, that’s less certain. Its iron-rich layer is much thinner than the alligator’s.

Still, worn Komodo dragon teeth hint that the coating protects the serrations. Enamel can flake off most of a worn tooth and expose the dentin. On the serrations, the enamel appears to wear down gradually instead.

Komodo dragons replace their teeth swiftly. So the thin coating must be enough to keep each tooth sharp until a upcoming one takes its place, the authors wrote.

Meat-eating dinosaurs had serrated teeth with thin enamel, just as Komodo dragons do.

Recent findings suggest that the team also tested teeth shed by crocodiles and their relatives living in zoos. All four species, the American alligator among them, had an iron-rich outer layer of enamel.

So the team looked for iron in fossil teeth from tyrannosaurs and from the family that includes Velociraptor. Every technique gave the same result. The team saw no consistent sign of extra iron in the enamel on the serrations.

Sources indicate that leBlanc’s team gave two possible explanations. Either fossilization masks the iron, or these dinosaurs didn’t normally concentrate it along their serrations. For now, the fossils can’t show which explanation is right.

“With further analysis of the Komodo teeth we may be able to find other markers in the iron coating that aren’t changed during fossilization,” LeBlanc noted.

“With markers like that we would know with certainty whether dinosaurs also had iron-coated teeth and have a greater understanding of these ferocious predators.”.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Ecology & Evolution.
