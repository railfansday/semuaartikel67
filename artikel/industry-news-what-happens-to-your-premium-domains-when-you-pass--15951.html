​Some families inherit real estate, investment portfolios and businesses when a loved one passes away. Others inherit domain names potentially worth millions of dollars without knowing what they own, what those assets are worth or how to protect them.

It has been revealed that as a domain advisor and expert, I have spent the better part of the past decade working with owners of previous domains and various entities that understand how valuable digital real estate is today. One overlooked situation is what to do with these valuable assets after the owner passes away.

Industry observers note that for example, consider a successful businessperson who owns a substantially valued domain in the seven-figure realm, but the registration is tethered to their personal accountant. What’s more, all notices, like renewals, are routed to a private email address. In this situation, nobody really knows what to do should the owner suddenly pass away.

The problem isn’t necessarily inheritance; it’s the absence of a plan to preserve what was inherited.

Premium domains are more than website addresses. They hold value because they can represent brand recognition, customer trust, intellectual property-related value and significant commercial opportunities. In truth, protecting your domain is comparable to measures you might take to protect your own estate.

It has been revealed that even a domain that was acquired in the past for a nominal investment could vest and be worth a lot more in the present day. Short.com, for example, might garner interest from multiple parties, and established domains could be the centerpiece for the right company.

While some owners may carefully document their property, investments and bank accounts, they often leave their domain portfolios out of their estate planning entirely. An oversight like this can swiftly become expensive if you don’t plan properly in advance, while you are still alive.

Much like any tangible asset, domains shouldn’t be overlooked in succession planning. Preparing in advance means having the right documentation in place now. It means assessing inventory and defining domain value. It also means keeping careful tabs on access information, renewals, certifications and access.

After you have these credentials in order, your best plan of action is to work with an experienced estate attorney. They can use their expertise to assess your individual circumstances and create a strong plan that protects your assets into the future against the unknown.

Along the way, discuss key elements like access planning, credential management and who can access this information if the unthinkable occurs. This also means protecting these assets, securing important access information and ensuring ongoing obligations, such as fees and renewals, are handled accordingly.

In a recent development, domains have value and are digital real estate that can increase in value over time. Knowing this beforehand can help heirs capture the full value, even if they are unfamiliar with how domaining works. For example, a domain valuation may show $50,000, but the domain may be worth more because it offers strategic value to a growing brand. Ensure your assigned legal counsel and trustees understand the potential buyer market by properly documenting your domain now.

It has been revealed that not every inherited domain should be sold. Some may provide ongoing business value. Others may benefit from being held until market conditions improve. Certain portfolios may be better managed or sold through experienced professional representation.

The best advice I can offer is to blueprint for the asset, not necessarily its sale. This is because many domains can be held in a portfolio and continue to accrue in value over time. As market conditions improve, it’s beneficial to have experienced professionals manage the portfolio and know when to hold and when to sell.

Of course, the right decision depends on the asset, the beneficiaries’ objectives, the estate’s financial obligations and the commercial opportunities involved. Forward-thinking owners can make those decisions easier by documenting their intentions while they’re alive. Identify which domains support operating businesses, which are long-term investments, and which might reasonably be sold.

A valuable domain can outlive its founder. This is why it’s so crucial to protect its value in advance. Doing so, however, requires a plan made with qualified financial and legal experts that survives the person who built it.
