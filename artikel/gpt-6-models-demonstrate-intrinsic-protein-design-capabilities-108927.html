GPT-6 models demonstrate intrinsic protein-design capabilities

🌐 Source: Openai • 📅 24 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 21 min read (4214 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways
  • Our GPT‑6 family of models can design latest protein shapes and the sequence of their chemical building blocks using only reasoning and code, without calling specialized biology models to generate those designs.
  • An internal research model matched or outperformed specialized models on many protein design tasks.
  • In lab tests with Onava, proteins based on GPT‑6.2’s designs showed evidence of binding, or attachment, across five targets.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Our GPT‑6 family of models can design latest protein shapes and the sequence of their chemical building blocks using only reasoning and code, without calling specialized biology models to generate those designs.

An internal research model matched or outperformed specialized models on many protein design tasks. Researchers could also guide designs by prompting the model on where a protein should attach and what shape it should take.

In lab tests with Onava, proteins based on GPT‑6.2’s designs showed evidence of binding, or attachment, across five targets. Redesigned antibodies also retained binding. Specialized models helped supply sequences and select candidates for testing.

The models’ written reasoning and code suggest they draw on math, physics, chemistry, and biological knowledge to reason about protein shapes and interactions with other molecules.

In a preprint titled GPT‑6 as a Universal Foundation Model for Protein Biology: Beyond Specialized Models, we show that our GPT‑6 family of models has intrinsic capabilities for designing latest proteins: the models can design new protein structures and sequences without relying on specialized biology models, using only reasoning and general-purpose coding tools.

According to recent reports, proteins do much of the work in living cells. They drive the chemical reactions that sustain life, carry oxygen through the blood, and defend the body against infection. Incorrectly shaped proteins can also cause disease and dysfunction. Proteins are made from chains of chemical building blocks called amino acids. The order, or sequence, of these amino acids helps determine how a protein folds into a three-dimensional shape, which in turn influences what it does.

According to recent reports, scientists often start with a clear idea of what they want a protein to do. They might want it to block a signal that helps cancer cells evade the immune system or to help capture carbon dioxide from the air. The challenge is to work out what three-dimensional structure would enable the protein to do its job and which amino acid sequence would give it that structure. Specialized biology models capable of generating three-dimensional protein structures and sequences have made this work more practical.

Using and coordinating these specialized models requires substantial expertise. Each accepts particular kinds of inputs and constraints, and these models cannot easily accept newly introduced kinds of information without changes to their software or additional training. In contrast, a general-purpose model capable of performing the work of multiple specialized models can easily adapt to new instructions and requirements.

Our research illustrates the potential power of pairing natural language with intrinsic protein capabilities. We could guide our models at scales ranging from a single bond between two atoms to the overall shape of a protein. For example, we asked GPT‑6.2 to predict where and how a small molecule would bind to a protein. When we already knew which two atoms formed a bond, we could give the model that information and ask how the rest of the molecule would fit. We could switch between these tasks simply by changing the prompt, without retraining the model. We also asked our models to design small proteins called minibinders and marked where each should attach to its target, and further specified how many coils or strands each minibinder should contain. An internal model followed that instruction with near-perfect fidelity in its three-dimensional designs.

Points farther from the center indicate better performance. Newer generations of models improved on many of the 14 protein tasks tested.

In a recent development, we ran computational tests of whether the GPT‑6 models series could design small proteins to bind given targets, build proteins around specified structural features, predict protein structures and molecular interactions, and rank how sequence changes affect properties such as stability and binding. Our latest models performed comparably to or better than specialized models on most tasks, although predicting a complete protein structure remained a clear weakness.

The models’ written reasoning and code offer clues to how they approach these tasks. They draw on mathematics, physics, chemistry, and coding alongside knowledge of recurring patterns in protein sequences and structures. For example, when designing a small protein intended to bind to IL-7Rα, the model proposed how the two proteins might interact, then wrote and executed code to explore different protein shapes and amino acid choices that could support that interaction.

Industry observers note that the model’s written reasoning and code show it drawing on math, physics, chemistry and biology to design a protein intended to attach to IL-7Rα. It examines the target’s surface, arranges five coiled sections into a protein shape, and checks how the two proteins could fit together.

With our research partner, Onava⁠(opens in a newly introduced window), we also tested the models’ structure and sequence designs in the lab. First, Onava made small proteins based on three-dimensional structures designed by a pre-released version of GPT‑6.2. Specialized models supplied amino acid sequences and helped select candidates for testing. For each of the five targets we chose, at least one protein showed evidence of binding in the lab. In separate experiments, GPT‑6.2 designed new amino acid sequences for existing minibinders and antibodies. Following candidate selection with specialized models, many of the redesigned proteins also showed evidence of binding to the same targets as the originals.

Our first laboratory experiment tested whether GPT‑6.2 could design small proteins that would bind to their intended targets. We compared computational measurements of quality against RFdiffusion, a specialized model for protein design, and found that GPT‑6.2’s three-dimensional designs scored slightly lower while our latest internal models scored higher. For the lab experiment, we made proteins based on GPT‑6.2’s designs and measured how strongly they bound to their targets.

We gave GPT‑6.2 the three-dimensional structures of five target proteins and marked where we wanted a minibinder to attach. It then wrote code to create three-dimensional designs for potential minibinders, without using specialized protein AI models.

Industry observers note that to turn GPT‑6.2’s three-dimensional designs into proteins we could test in the lab, we needed amino acid sequences that would fold into the proposed structures. We used a specialized AI model called SolubleMPNN to suggest sequences for each design. Other specialized protein models helped us select candidates predicted to fold as designed and bind at the intended site on the target.

GPT‑6.2 designed shapes for small proteins intended to attach to chosen targets. Specialized models then proposed amino acid sequences—the order of the proteins’ chemical building blocks—and helped select designs for our research partner Onava to make and test in the lab.

We tested 322 minibinders against 5 targets in the lab. Of those, 35 (10.9%) tested positive for binding to their intended target. We found 5 hits for PD-L1 (a protein some cancers use to evade the immune system), 3 for IL-7Rα, 1 for TrkA, 8 for VEGF-A and 18 for IL-17A. The original RFdiffusion study⁠(opens in a fresh window) reported higher hit rates, but rates depend heavily on how strictly researchers filter designs before lab validation and their definition of binding. Nevertheless, our results provide early evidence that we can turn GPT‑6.2’s three-dimensional designs into proteins that bind to their intended targets in the lab.

An internal research model designed shapes for small proteins intended to attach to the target proteins shown in gray. The colored shapes compare each design with a computational prediction of how the protein would fold and fit against its target.

We tested whether GPT‑6.2 could design the sequences itself. We gave it three-dimensional models of how existing minibinders fit against their targets, withheld the original amino acid sequences, and asked it to propose new ones. Specialized models helped us choose which proteins to make and test in the lab.

It has been revealed that for three targets, we started with computationally predicted structures of confirmed minibinders whose sequences Onava had collected from earlier studies. In our initial lab screens, we saw binding in 75 of 88 designs for MDM2 (85%), 26 of 42 for VEGF-A (62%), and 8 of 59 for PD-1 (14%). The amino acid sequences proposed by GPT‑6.2 also often differed significantly from the original minibinder sequences, with a median of 50% for VEGF-A, 43% for MDM2, and 15% for PD-1.

It has been revealed that we also asked GPT‑6.2 to design upcoming sequences for three unpublished minibinder shapes it had generated earlier: two targeting PD-L1 and one targeting IL-7Rα. A specialized model, ProteinMPNN, had already supplied one sequence for each shape, and proteins made from those sequences had shown binding in the lab. Of the 107 proteins made from GPT‑6.2’s new sequences, 14 (13.1%) highlighted binding.

Recent findings suggest that given an unpublished structure, GPT‑6.2 had come up with two substantially different sequences that both showed signs of binding to the same target. For the same PD-L1 minibinder structure, GPT‑6.2 designed two sequences different from the original in 52 and 58 out of 90 total amino acids. The two also differed from each other at 44 positions, nearly half the sequence.

The unpublished structures and substantial differences in sequence give us strong evidence that GPT‑6.2’s capabilities extend beyond memorization. The results also suggest we could use GPT‑6.2 to intrinsically design both a minibinder’s structure and its sequence.

Researchers often redesign an antibody’s binding loops to help it grip its target more tightly, a process called affinity maturation. They may also want it to bind more selectively, so it’s less likely to attach to similar proteins. We tested a first step toward those goals by asking our models to design newly introduced loop sequences that preserved binding to the original target.

Industry observers note that we started with six small antibodies (VHHs) that bind to the same protein, IL-4Rα. For each antibody, we gave GPT‑6.2 its predicted three-dimensional structure and most of its amino acid sequence, leaving out the three loops that help the antibody grip its target. We asked the model to design new sequences for them. Of the 182 designs we tested, 128 (70%) demonstrated signs of binding to IL-4Rα.

Industry observers note that we then tested how precisely we could guide the model’s changes to existing therapeutic antibodies. This time, we gave the model the original sequences and asked it to change about half the amino acids in each of three binding loops while keeping the rest of the sequence intact. We tested the redesigned portions of the antibodies as fragments in the lab. Of 272 fragments based on five therapeutic antibodies, 179 (66%) showed binding to the original target, PD-1 or PD-L1.

A long-term goal of programmable structure-based design is to let researchers specify structural constraints in natural language. In our computational evaluations, our models consistently designed minibinders that contacted the required locations on the target. We could also specify the minibinder’s structural class (all helices, all strands, or a combination) and control the number of coils and strands with near-perfect fidelity. The same approach lets us express more detailed requirements for the binding interface, such as which target amino acids should be buried upon binding or whether the minibinder and target should interact through specific interactions such as “β-sheet–β-sheet”. As our model’s reasoning capabilities improve, we envision researchers guiding protein structure generation much as they guide image generation today, by describing the features they want.

Our model was asked to design small proteins with specified shapes: mainly coils (orange), mainly strands (blue), or a mix. Some instructions also specified how many coils or strands to include. The designs are intended to attach to PD-L1 or PD-1, shown in gray.

Most enzymes are proteins that speed up chemical reactions. When designing a latest enzyme, researchers may know how a few key amino acids need to be arranged at the site where the reaction happens. Those amino acids can lie far apart along the protein chain. The challenge is to design the rest of the protein so that, as the chain folds, it brings them together and holds them in place.

In a recent development, we tested our models on 30 challenges from MotifBench⁠(opens in a upcoming window), including examples drawn from enzymes and from sites where proteins bind to one another. We gave the models a specific three-dimensional arrangement of amino acids, called a motif, and asked them to use coding tools to design the rest of the protein around it. This task is called motif scaffolding.

According to recent reports, for each design, we used ProteinMPNN to propose eight possible amino acid sequences, and ESMFold⁠(opens in a new window) to predict how each would fold. We counted the design as a success if at least one predicted structure both preserved the motif and closely matched the overall structure the model had designed.

According to recent reports, in a preliminary analysis, 43.7% of the designs we evaluated from our latest internal model met both criteria, compared with 27.8% for RFdiffusion. While laboratory experiments are needed to find out whether these proteins could carry out the intended function, such as speeding up a particular reaction, this provides strong computational evidence of our models’ intrinsic protein capabilities.

An internal research model designed protein structures around a small region whose shape had to stay fixed, shown in gold. The colored shapes compare the model’s designs with computer predictions of how the proteins would fold. Panels showing only gold had no design that passed the computer checks.

In motif scaffolding, we specified how a few amino acids should sit relative to one another. We then determined whether we could also guide the design of an entire protein by specifying its overall fold. We gave our models a chain length and a code from CATH⁠(opens in a fresh window), a system scientists use to classify protein structures. The models then had to work out where each amino acid should go in three-dimensional space.

Industry observers note that many designs captured the broad layout we asked for. The harder part was getting the chain to follow the right path through it. Two proteins can have several coiled stretches packed together in a similar bundle, for example, yet differ in the order in which the chain connects them.

We counted a design as a success if at least one amino acid sequence was predicted to adopt the requested fold and closely match the model’s three-dimensional design. Across 200 challenges, 18.9% of the designs we evaluated from our latest internal model passed, compared with 17.1% for Proteina, a specialized protein design model.

According to recent reports, an internal research model was asked to design proteins with specified overall shapes, such as bundles of coils or layers of strands. The colored shapes compare selected designs with computer predictions of how they would fold, showing examples where the two closely match.

Many medicines, including aspirin, are small molecules, chemical compounds much smaller than the proteins they act on. Predicting where a small molecule binds to a protein and how it fits there is called docking. These predictions can help researchers decide how to modify a drug candidate so it binds more tightly to its target. To test our models, we started with experimentally determined structures of small molecules bound to proteins. We digitally removed each small molecule and gave the models the protein structure along with a text description of how the molecule’s atoms were connected (a sequence of characters called SMILES). We then asked the models to predict where the molecule had been bound and how it was oriented.

For our first docking test, we used the PoseBusters dataset⁠(opens in a newly introduced window), a collection of three-dimensional structures that show how small molecules bind to proteins. Researchers determined these structures experimentally, giving us a way to check the models’ predictions. Without being told where on the protein each molecule had bound, the models had to predict both where the molecule fit and which way it faced. GPT‑6.2 closely matched the molecule’s observed position and orientation in 93% of scored predictions, compared with 96% for our latest internal model and 50% for DiffDock-L, a specialized docking model.

In practice, researchers may already know something about how a molecule binds. One major advantage of a GPT model is that we can give additional information directly in the prompt. We tested this on 179 structures from the Scarpino dataset⁠(opens in a fresh window), where each protein structure forms a chemical bond with a small molecule, tethering the two together. We told our models which two atoms formed the bond and asked them to predict how the rest of the molecule fit against the protein. GPT‑6.2 closely matched the experimental structure in 92% of scored predictions⁠(opens in a new window), and our latest internal model did so in 97%, while DiffDock⁠(opens in a new window) reached 60%.

It has been revealed that an internal research model predicted where and how small molecules would fit against proteins, shown in gray. Purple shows the model’s predictions, and teal shows the molecules’ positions determined in earlier experiments. In these examples, the predictions closely match the experimental structures.

Industry observers note that where an antibody binds can help explain whether it blocks another protein from attaching to the same site. We tested how accurately successive generations of GPT could predict where an antibody binds and which way it faces. For hundreds of pairs of antibodies and targets, we demonstrated the models the two proteins separately and hid some of the atomic detail (all side chains) on their surfaces.

With the side-chain details hidden, the mean DockQ score (a commonly used metric in which higher values indicate a closer match to the experimentally observed arrangement) increased from approximately 0.05 for GPT‑5.6 to 0.28 for GPT‑6, 0.57 for GPT‑6.2, and 0.81 for our latest internal model, at each model’s highest evaluated compute setting.

In a recent development, because these structures were public, the models may have seen the same antibody-target pairs or similar ones during training. Even so, each generation of our models predicted more accurately how the two proteins fit together, even with all side-chain positions hidden. That progress gives us reason to be optimistic about harder docking problems.

In a recent development, each row compares four models’ predictions of how an antibody attaches to its target protein. Colored shapes show the model’s prediction; dark gray shows the position observed in experiments. Results improved with each successive generation of model.

It has been revealed that we can design far more proteins on a computer than we can make and test in the lab. Even after we settle on a promising structure, we may have many amino acid sequences to choose from. We tested whether our models could help researchers decide which versions to make first by predicting how changes to a protein’s sequence would affect its stability, activity, or ability to bind to a target.

In a recent development, we drew on four public datasets from experiments in which scientists had replaced one or more amino acids in a protein and measured the effects. For each test, we gave our models the sequence of the protein the scientists started with and the sequences of 50 modified versions. We told the models what the scientists had measured, withheld the results, and asked them to rank the 50 versions without using coding tools, by reasoning only.

It has been revealed that in one study of protein stability, we asked the models to rank 50 versions of a protein by how likely each was to be folded at room temperature. We compared the predicted order with the order observed in the lab using a measure called rank correlation. A value of 1 means the rankings match perfectly, 0 means there’s no consistent relationship, and −1 means the order is exactly reversed. Across 265 sets from the stability study, each model generation improved, from 0.41 for GPT‑5.6 to 0.52 for GPT‑6, 0.57 for GPT‑6.2, and 0.66 for our latest internal model.

In each of the four datasets, GPT‑6.2 and our latest internal model produced more accurate rankings on average than ESM2-650M, a specialized protein model.

In a recent development, earlier, we relied on specialized models to predict whether an amino acid sequence would fold into the structure our models had designed. We wanted to see how close our models are to making that prediction themselves. We gave our models the amino acid sequences of proteins with known three-dimensional structures. Using only basic coding tools, the models predicted how each chain would fold in three dimensions. We evaluated their predictions using a measure called TM-score. A score of 1 means a perfect match to the real structure, and scores above 0.5 generally indicate that the prediction captures the overall fold.

The biggest gains are visible in individual examples. For some proteins, our latest internal model closely reproduced folds that earlier models missed. Across the full test, the average score rose from 0.16 for GPT‑5.6 to 0.26 for GPT‑6.2 and 0.38 for our latest internal model. The specialized model ESMFold2 averaged 0.93, so there’s still a substantial gap in how reliably our models can predict a whole protein’s structure from its sequence alone.

What if researchers already know most of the structure and want to fill in a missing piece? We tested this on 100 antibody fragments. For each one, we gave our models the amino acid sequences and the surrounding three-dimension structure, hiding the six loops that help the antibody recognize its target. Our latest internal model often predicted both the shape and position of those loops quite accurately. ESMFold2 was still more accurate overall, even working from the sequences alone.

It has been revealed that it is important to note that our protein design campaign relied on specialized structure prediction models such as Boltz-2, OpenDDE, and ESMFold2 to filter candidates. Our results show that GPT models have intrinsic capabilities comparable to specialized models at two stages of the workflow: backbone generation, where researchers commonly use RFdiffusion; and sequence redesign, where they commonly use ProteinMPNN. Our results therefore do not yet establish that our models can generate de novo minibinders end-to-end in a zero-shot setting without help from specialized models.

According to recent reports, our work demonstrates yet again the jagged capability frontier. Predicting a complete protein structure from its amino acid sequence remains substantially harder for our models than several of the component tasks above. On that task, even our best internal models remained well behind specialized structure-prediction models.

Recent findings suggest that some benchmark results also require careful interpretation. In tests that asked models to infer amino acid sequences from protein structures, unusually high accuracy may partly reflect memorization or patterns left by the process used to generate those structures. Those results alone do not establish reliable performance on unfamiliar proteins.

The laboratory results provide a more direct test of whether proposed designs can work, but binding is only one step toward a useful research tool or medicine. Further work would need to establish properties such as specificity, stability, and biological activity, along with safety and efficacy for therapeutic applications.

This work is an early step toward programmable molecular design at OpenAI. Our goal is to let scientists describe what they want a molecule to do in plain language and use our models to help design candidates to test in the lab. To make that approach useful for developing medicines, we want to make progress in three areas.

Recent findings suggest that find minibinders for harder targets. We want to design molecules that bind to targets where our attempts so far have fallen short.

Recent findings suggest that design for what happens after binding. We want to test whether the molecules we design can produce a specific biological effect, such as killing a cancer cell. We also want to design molecules that bind selectively to their intended targets and are practical to manufacture.

It has been revealed that design a wider range of molecules. Our lab experiments focused on small protein minibinders and antibody fragments. We’d like to extend that work to molecules that bind to several targets at once, RNA, and small molecules, including a class called PROTACs that direct a cell to break down a particular protein.

It has been revealed that as we work to accelerate scientific progress safely and responsibly, we also seek to evaluate and reduce risks, particularly those related to biosecurity. The same capabilities that help scientists design proteins for new medicines could also be misused. Our Preparedness Framework⁠ guides how we assess these risks and test safeguards to reduce them.

While lab testing remains a critical part of protein design, our goal is to provide powerful models that can shorten each round of design by giving scientists more control and flexibility, and reduce the total number of design-test cycles needed to find a functional protein. Ultimately, we envision enabling scientists to design proteins and molecules simply by prompting our models in natural language, massively accelerating the development of latest biotechnologies and new medicines.
