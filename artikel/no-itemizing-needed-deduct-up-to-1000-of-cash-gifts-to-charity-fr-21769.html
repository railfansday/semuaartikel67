No Itemizing Needed – Deduct Up to $1,000 of Cash Gifts to Charity From 2026

Starting with your 2026 return, you can deduct up to $1,000 of cash gifts to eligible charities while taking the standard deduction, or $2,000 on a joint return. Give by check, card, or bank transfer straight to the charity, since donated goods, stock, and gifts to donor-advised funds don’t count. Keep a bank record or receipt for every gift.

The IRS’s Working Families Tax Cuts page says, “Beginning with tax year 2026, individuals who do not itemize may deduct up to $1,000 of cash contributions made to eligible charitable organizations ($2,000 for married taxpayers filing jointly).” It’s permanent, unlike the temporary version for 2020 and 2021.

Recent findings suggest that two words in that rule decide which gifts count. The first, cash, means money, given in bills or by check, card, bank transfer, or payroll deduction. Clothing, stock, and other property don’t count, and neither do deductions carried over from earlier years.

The second, eligible, rules out some gifts of money. Donations to a supporting organization, a charity set up to support other charities, don’t count, and neither do donations to a donor-advised fund, which only itemizers can deduct.

Industry observers note that look each charity up in the IRS’s Tax Exempt Organization Search. Its deductibility code reads PC for a public charity, which qualifies, and starts with SO for a supporting organization. A church can qualify without appearing there. For every gift, keep a bank record or receipt showing the charity’s name, the date, and the amount, plus the charity’s written acknowledgment for any single gift of $250 or more.

Say a married couple in the 12% bracket takes the standard deduction and gives $150 a month by card, split between their church and a food bank. That’s $1,800 for 2026, all deductible under the $2,000 limit on a joint return, so their tax drops by $216. Since no single gift reaches $250, their card statements are all the records they need.

From 2026, itemizers can deduct only the part of their gifts above 0.50% of adjusted gross income. If your itemized deductions come close to the standard deduction, count your gifts both ways before you take the standard deduction or itemize, and consider bunching two years of gifts into a year you itemize.

Recent findings suggest that make a gift by Dec. 31 for it to count on that year’s return. A mailed check counts the day you mail it, and a card gift in the year it’s charged, not the year you pay the bill.
