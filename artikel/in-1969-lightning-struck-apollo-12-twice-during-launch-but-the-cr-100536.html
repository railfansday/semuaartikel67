In 1969, lightning struck Apollo 12 twice during launch, but the crew reached the Moon and brought back a camera tied to a germ myth

  • The Apollo 12 moon mission lifted off at 11:22 a.m.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Lightning hit Apollo 12 twice in its first minute in 1969. The crew still landed next to Surveyor 3, whose camera later sparked controversy.

The Apollo 12 moon mission lifted off at 11:22 a.m. EST on November 14, 1969, into a dark, rainy sky over Florida. Thirty-six seconds later, the commander saw a bright flash, and many of the spacecraft’s electronics went offline.

Sources indicate that lightning had struck the rocket. Sixteen seconds after that, a second event knocked out the spacecraft’s guidance system.

The crew still landed on the Moon five days later, right next to a robotic probe that had been sitting there since 1967.

What they brought back from that probe started a story that has never quite gone away: that Earth germs had survived on the Moon.

The crew was commander Charles “Pete” Conrad, command module pilot Richard Gordon and lunar module pilot Alan Bean. Their mission was NASA’s second Moon landing, four months after Apollo 11.

Later crews carried living cargo on purpose, like the tree seeds an Apollo astronaut flew around the Moon in 1971.

A NASA photo reveals a bolt between the clouds and the ground 36.5 seconds after liftoff, when the vehicle was about 6,000 feet up. The first event took the spacecraft’s three power-generating fuel cells offline.

Industry observers note that the Saturn V rocket that was guiding the launch was not affected and kept flying normally. The spacecraft on top of it was another matter.

In a recent development, in Mission Control, a young engineer named John Aaron was watching the spacecraft’s systems. His data turned to garbage during both events.

It has been revealed that aaron had seen similar readings in a test a year earlier. He concluded that a unit called the Signal Conditioning Equipment had suffered some upset and gone offline, and that a seldom-used switch needed to be moved from Normal to Auxiliary.

According to recent reports, he told Flight Director Gerry Griffin, and the call went up to the crew through capcom Gerald Carr. Bean remembered that the switch was on his panel and flipped it. Several seconds later, Aaron had good data on his screen again.

Once Conrad understood what had happened, he radioed Houston: “I think we need to do a little more all-weather testing.”.

For about two and a half hours in Earth orbit, the astronauts and Mission Control checked that every system still worked. Then Carr told them they were go to leave for the Moon.

On November 19, Conrad and Bean landed the lunar module Intrepid in the Ocean of Storms, about 950 miles west of the Apollo 11 site.

They came down about 535 feet from Surveyor 3, an uncrewed NASA lander that had been on the Moon since April 1967. It is the only time Apollo astronauts landed near another spacecraft and touched it.

On their second moonwalk, on November 20, the two men walked into the 650-foot-wide Surveyor Crater. They cut off the probe’s 17-pound TV camera and also collected its motorized scoop, pieces of cable, aluminum tubing and glass.

Engineers wanted the camera to see how its gears, motors, optics, metals and lubricants had held up.

Scientists were most interested in the cable, because biological organisms had been trapped inside it, and they wanted to know if any had survived two and a half years on the Moon.

In a recent development, after splashdown, the camera went to NASA’s Lunar Receiving Laboratory in Houston. Sampling for microbes began on January 8, 1970.

Recent findings suggest that A team took 33 samples from 11 locations inside the camera body. One sample, a bit of polyurethane foam used as insulation between the aluminum plates of a circuit board, grew a colony of Streptococcus mitis, a bacterium that commonly lives in the human mouth.

The result was published in 1971. It gave rise to the idea that Earth bacteria had survived the vacuum and the extreme temperatures of the Moon, and that idea has circulated ever since.

The broader question is still studied today, as in research on whether Earth microbes can survive in hidden pockets on the Moon.

According to recent reports, decades later, John Rummel of East Carolina University, Judith Allton of NASA’s Johnson Space Center and Don Morrison reviewed the case. They tracked down the 16mm films of the sampling and watched them.

According to recent reports, their review, now hosted by NASA, lists several problems. No viable microbes were found in 10 of the 11 sampling locations inside the camera, or in 32 of the 33 samples.

None were found in the Surveyor cables or in any Apollo lunar samples, and a test camera that had stayed on Earth never yielded S. mitis.

S. mitis is not a spore former, and microbes like it are much more vulnerable to heat, even in a vacuum. NASA’s Manned Spacecraft Center, now Johnson Space Center, estimated that the camera reached a maximum temperature of about 158 degrees Fahrenheit (70 degrees Celsius).

It has been revealed that there was also no negative control to check whether the sampling itself added microbes. And S. mitis was one of the organisms found on the crew during routine microbial testing.

The films showed technicians in short-sleeved scrubs, with their arms exposed. Just before the foam was sampled, one worker leaned his upper body into the laminar-flow hood and examined the side of the camera where the foam was.

Morrison concluded that his breath could have contaminated the foam. Because the foam was the last sample taken, nobody could check whether nearby surfaces had been contaminated too.

“The general scene does not lend a lot of confidence in the proposition that contamination did not occur,” Morrison wrote.

The review does not prove where the bacterium came from. What it shows is that the claim of survival on the Moon rests on a single sample taken under weak controls.

The authors used the case as a warning for future missions that bring material back from Mars and other worlds.

It has been revealed that contamination should be avoided but anticipated, and a microbial inventory should be taken before launch. Other scientists have since proposed a Moon lab to screen samples for alien microbes.

It has been revealed that their summary also admitted something about the story’s staying power. Just because scientists do not believe it, they wrote, does not mean it is going to go away.

The information was obtained from NASA’s account of the Apollo 12 launch, its mission summary and the review of the Surveyor 3 microbe claim.
