Research Says Cold Muscles Produce Less Power. Here’s How to Protect Your Performance on Frigid Runs.

Industry observers note that when temperatures rise, running performance tends to drop. Both the research and anyone who’s put in miles on a hot summer afternoon can confidently tell you that much.

Sources indicate that but what about when the mercury moves in the opposite direction? Does colder weather in the winter—ranging from just a bit chilly to downright frigid—take a toll on your running too?

Sources indicate that A recent review published in Temperature suggests it does. According to the study’s authors, when our muscles get too cold, neuromuscular function becomes impaired. On the run, this can contribute to stiffness, less power output, and decreased endurance, all of which may cause your winter workouts to feel harder than usual.

In a recent development, the good news? You can still run strong when the temps drop—as long as you make a few key training adjustments before, during, and after you log your miles in the colder months of the year. Here’s how to do just that.

There’s no exact number on the thermometer at which runners can universally expect their power, speed, or endurance to decline, Stephen Cheung, PhD, the lead author of the aforementioned review, tells Runner’s World.

According to recent reports, temperature is just one of the many factors that contribute to how well we perform in the cold, Cheung explains. “It’s a function of the environment, as well as how much heat you’re generating, the speed that you’re moving at, and of course, the amount of clothing that you’re wearing,” he says.

Each of these factors varies significantly from runner to runner and affects how swiftly their bodies lose heat. You only need to consider those in your run group who run shirtless when it’s freezing versus those who bundle up when it’s 50 degrees Fahrenheit to understand that we all thermoregulate (maintain our internal body temperature) differently. And the equation only becomes more complex when you account for wind and moisture—both in the environment and what you create yourself from air resistance and sweat.

It has been revealed that heather Milton, CSCS, lead exercise physiologist at Primary MD and cofounder of Master My Run, agrees. “It would be great to be able to say, when the temperature outside reaches [a certain point], your pace slows down by this many seconds per mile—but that’s not how it works,” she notes.

Keeping this in mind, Milton indicates the cleanest reference point for runners to begin paying more attention to how they prepare for runs in the cold is when the temperature drops below freezing. Generally speaking, the colder it gets from there, the more noticeable the negative effects on performance will be, she explains.

In a recent development, cold weather leads to vasoconstriction, Milton explains, which means our blood vessels constrict more, so the muscles farther away from the core don’t receive as much blood flow. If your fingers or toes have ever gone numb in the cold, for example, you’ve experienced the joy of vasoconstriction. Our bodies prioritize heating our core over our extremities because it helps our vital organs maintain a consistent temperature, which is essential for survival in dangerous conditions.

This physiological response, however, doesn’t bode especially well for running performance.

“The cold slows nerve conduction so you have to work harder for the same power output,” Milton says. “And as temperatures drop further below freezing, that slower nerve conduction and the effects of vasoconstriction are going to be even more noticeable.” As a result, your overall exercise capacity decreases, meaning you may not be able to run for as long or at the same pace you normally would.

Recent findings suggest that what else might you notice when temperatures drop? “Physically, if your muscles and tendons are cold, they’re going to be tighter, less elastic, and you won’t be able to contract them as strongly,” states Cheung. “Stiff muscles can also put you at greater risk for tears or strain injuries.”.

Milton claims this analogy, used by an old coach of hers, provides an easy way to understand what’s happening: When your muscles and tendons get too cold, they’re like a cold rubber band that can snap when pulled.

If you’re a runner who dislikes the cold, you might wonder if the smarter choice is to just run on the treadmill this winter. Don’t worry: As long as you prepare appropriately and use caution, you can absolutely continue logging regular miles outdoors.

Industry observers note that running well outside in the cold starts with warming up indoors first, claims Milton. “An indoor warmup increases your internal metabolism and enhances blood flow, which helps prepare your muscles for contraction,” she explains. This also produces heat so your internal body temperature is more resilient to the cold outdoor environment.

Your winter warmup does not have to be any different or longer than your usual routine, Milton indicates. But you do want to make sure that you’re getting in a solid five minutes of prerun drills to adequately raise your body temperature and activate your muscles.

She recommends starting with slower, more controlled movements that help activate the core and the glutes, such as planks with leg raises, lunges with rotation, and hip CARs (controlled articular rotations). Finish with quicker drills that require your nerves to fire more rapidly, such as leg swings and butt kicks.

Equally vital to warming up is layering up—with an important caveat.

When dressing for winter runs, keep in mind how you personally respond to cold temperatures, states Cheung, because everyone has different tolerances and susceptibilities. “I get very cold in my hands, so I’ll wear really thick gloves, even when I may be wearing relatively little on my torso,” he adds. “Pay attention to what’s an problem for you and adapt your clothing to both the conditions and your own peculiarities.” Cheung emphasizes this because you don’t want to end up sweating too heavily, which can then leave you chilled and clammy.

Industry observers note that if you need a starting point for your winter running wardrobe, aim for two to three layers of breathable, moisture-wicking fabrics. If possible, choose a wind- and water-resistant shell for your outermost layer, suggests Milton. For your lower body, wear shorts over your tights to help keep your glutes warm. Finally, cover any exposed skin on your head, ears, and hands, and opt for a pair of wool socks—all of which can help ensure adequate blood distribution to the extremities, Milton adds.

When you head out for runs in the cold, slowly build up to your usual pace.

Sources indicate that gradually easing into your workout gives your muscles more time to adjust, and it can also help your airways get used to the colder temperatures, Milton notes. You don’t necessarily need to go slower for your entire run, but you should try to match your rate of perceived exertion (RPE) with your typical effort, rather than aiming for your usual pace or heart-rate zone.

“Think about it the same way as if you were running on an extremely hot day,” Milton says. “Let’s say your RPE for an easy run is about 3 out of 10. Keep that 3 out of 10, even if your pace is a little slower.”.

If you find that your run feels more difficult than usual, slow down if you need to, but don’t stop completely, Cheung advises. “Don’t take long breaks, because that’s when you aren’t generating as much heat. Just keep moving so you have that constant heat generation,” he says.

Industry observers note that if you start to get cold on a run, try speeding up a little bit to stoke that metabolic heat production, Cheung says. But make sure you do it safely: Don’t force the effort in icy conditions or when your muscles feel especially stiff. Scouting out run routes with more tree coverage to block wind on blustery days can also help you stay warmer, he adds.

As soon as you finish your workout, change out of your sweaty running gear and into warm, dry clothes, Milton claims.

Even if you’ve only sweated a small amount—for example, maybe you just have some dampness under your armpits—you still want to remove any wet fabrics against your skin. “Even that small amount of wetness can start to drop your core temperature,” Milton explains. If you wait until you’re near shivering, you’ll face an uphill battle to get yourself warm again.

You can also opt for a relaxing shower and hot chocolate—extra marshmallows.
