Industry News: Most of the world’s lost glacier ice comes from a tiny minority of glaciers

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A census of 216,502 glaciers finds the 3 percent that end in lakes, seas or ice shelves drive most glacier ice loss, and lakes speed it up most.

In a recent development, outside of Earth’s two massive ice sheets, there are roughly 216,500 glaciers, and 97 percent of them end on bare rock. Surprisingly, however, these land-based glaciers are not where most of the world’s melting ice is coming from.

Recent findings suggest that the other 3 percent end in a lake, the ocean, or a floating ice shelf. They are the ones shedding more than half of all the glacier ice lost between 2000 and 2019.

Industry observers note that those numbers come from a latest global census of where glaciers end. Glaciers that end in lakes stood out most. Relative to their size, they lost ice about 57 percent faster than glaciers ending on land. The researchers don’t yet know why.

Joe Mallalieu did the work at the University of Birmingham and is now at the University of Leeds, with Nicholas Barrand of Birmingham among the co-authors.

In an interview with Earth.com, Mallalieu said the lake result was a surprise.

“What surprised us most was how much faster glaciers ending in lakes lose ice than those ending in the ocean, relative to their size,” he said.

The team classified all 216,502 glaciers in a standard global inventory by what their fronts touch: land, a lake, the sea, or an ice shelf.

In a recent development, earlier inventories had flagged lake-ending glaciers in only three of the world’s 19 glacier regions. The team found the rest by matching glacier outlines against a satellite map of glacial lakes built from more than 250,000 Landsat scenes.

It has been revealed that then they combined that classification with existing global records of each glacier’s size, ice thickness, slope, flow speed and ice loss, plus the local climate. The full classification is public on Zenodo.

Lake-ending glaciers turned up in every one of the 19 regions. In Iceland, 65 percent of the glacier area drains to a lake, and in the southern Andes almost 40 percent does. Glaciers ending in the sea cluster in the Arctic and around Antarctica.

Glaciers that end in lakes or the sea tend to be far bigger than those ending on land. The typical lake-ending glacier covered about eight times the area of a typical land-ending one.

It was also roughly twice as thick, about 200 feet (61 meters) against 100 feet (31 meters).

Size alone explained a lot of the ice loss but not all of it. For its size, the typical lake-ending glacier lost ice equal to a layer of water about 1.6 feet (0.49 meters) deep each year.

That is roughly double the rate at land-ending and ocean-ending glaciers.

After the team accounted for climate, elevation, slope, thickness and other factors, the gap held. Lake-ending glaciers lost about 57 percent more ice for their size than land-ending ones, and they flowed about 10 percent faster.

Ocean-ending glaciers flowed 44 percent faster than land-ending ones, yet their ice loss for their size was no higher, the authors wrote.

Industry observers note that lake water can remove ice from a glacier’s front in several ways. It melts the ice below the waterline, and the undercut front breaks off as icebergs.

It can also lift the front until it floats and breaks, or seep beneath the ice and reduce the friction holding it back.

Any of those would remove ice faster than melting on bare rock does. But the lake-ending glaciers in the census were also larger and flatter at their lower ends than land-ending ones.

That shape alone can slow a glacier’s adjustment to warming and keep it losing ice for longer, the authors wrote.

Mallalieu told Earth.com that even the climate difference doesn’t account for it. “And it’s not just because they tend to be in warmer, wetter places: the difference holds even when we take climate into account,” he said.

Barrand stated the census only became possible recently. “The last decade has seen a huge increase in the global glacier and climate data available to researchers. Bringing these datasets together lets us compare how glaciers in different settings are behaving,” he said.

The categories themselves are moving. Glacial lakes grew by more than 50 percent in number and area between 1990 and 2018, according to a satellite survey in Nature Climate Change.

Sources indicate that many glaciers that once ended on land now end in water. Across the Arctic, meanwhile, glaciers that reached the sea are retreating onto land.

Asked by Earth.com what happens to those glaciers next, Mallalieu said the two switches should have opposite effects.

In a recent development, glaciers pulling back onto land are likely to slow down markedly. Glaciers newly ending in lakes are likely to speed up slightly and lose ice much faster for their size.

In the long run, he remarked, many lake-ending glaciers will retreat out of their lakes and end on land again. That matters for the models that project sea level rise and river flow.

According to recent reports, many of them don’t yet represent what happens where a glacier meets a lake, the authors wrote. Yet the glaciers that can respond this way hold more than half of the world’s glacier area.

The classification counts a glacier as lake-ending if any lake touches its front, however small the contact.

Glaciers with only a sliver of lake may behave more like land-ending ones, so the authors mentioned the real gap between the two groups is probably larger than 57 percent.

Flow speeds were available for 84 percent of glaciers and ice-loss rates for 94 percent. Furthermore, the lake map left out Svalbard and Antarctica.

Cause is also unproven: the study demonstrates lake-ending glaciers lose ice faster, not whether the lakes themselves are responsible.

For communities downstream, the open question is water. Mallalieu noted the census did not assess water supplies. But in the Himalaya, where large populations rely on meltwater, glacier lakes are expanding fast, he said.

Better models of these glaciers would give those communities more reliable forecasts of their future water.

Untangling the lakes from the glacier shapes is the next task. It means measuring individual lakes, their depth, temperature and how long they persist, glacier by glacier.

Until that is done, the 57 percent is a difference the world can see and not yet explain.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Communications.
