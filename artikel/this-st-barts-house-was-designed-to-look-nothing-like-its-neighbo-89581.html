This St. Bart’s House Was Designed to Look Nothing Like Its Neighbors

In a recent development, few clients ask their designer to make the bedrooms in their vacation home smaller and simpler, but that was just the edit the homeowners made to intends for their house in St. Bart’s, states designer David Ries. “They didn’t want these humongous bedrooms with huge floating beds and massive bathrooms.” Instead, the couple wanted to be able to host as many people as possible, a request that didn’t surprise Ries, who’d already worked with the family on homes in Manhattan, Long Island, and Telluride. “It’s what this client has always asked for. They can’t have enough bedrooms.”.

Situated on a steep hillside above one of St. Bart’s famously pristine white sand beaches, the house had initially been a boutique hotel. The plan was to turn it into a retreat for themselves and their adult children and grandchildren. The family are longtime visitors to the island—“the daughter told me it was her ‘happy place,’” claims Ries, lead designer of the Latest York-based interior design firm Ries Hayes—and knew exactly what they wanted. Not the white box look popular with luxury new builds on the island, but instead something reminiscent of the “old St. Bart’s,” says East Hampton-based landscape designer Edwina von Gal, who, like Ries, has collaborated with the clients on other projects.

Sources indicate that A Caribbean island is a challenging place to work under any circumstances. Everything has to be brought in from far away, including all the plants, which came by barge from Miami. St. Bart’s in particular requires anyone building to work with a local architect and engineer. In their initial proposal, this French team defaulted to that “slick and modernist” look, as von Gal describes it, with the oversized bedrooms and white color scheme that dominates much of the island’s luxury housing stock.

In a recent development, the design team pushed back—asking for a lot of bedrooms (eight) and generous living spaces. They also had strong opinions on the building materials. Given the island’s climate, the humid, salt-soaked air can be hard on everything. “If we did white paint, which is prominent throughout St. Bart's, it’s constant maintenance, constant painting,” says Ries. The design team insisted on cladding the buildings in Ipe, the durable tropical hardwood, which wears well. It mystified the local architects and set the complex apart from its neighbors. It also makes the buildings disappear into the greenery of the hillside.

According to recent reports, but the architects rapidly caught on to the new intends, and von Gal is eager to praise their technical accomplishments. Regulations require properties to conserve rainwater, so there are cisterns beneath the buildings, plus technology infrastructure and laundry facilities. “Working on the hill is so complex,” she claims. “It's such a tight little package of technology and function. What they did was amazing.”.

Sources indicate that the team decided to reconfigure the property as a series of pavilions that face the view of the ocean and surrounding hills. The main entry is at the top of the steep hill, along with parking. The next level down is for family bedrooms; below that is a primary suite and study; and finally the fourth level is the main living space, including a playroom. “We made sure this playroom was front and center,” says Ries. “My client loves her grandkids.”.

It has been revealed that the playroom and study have built-in daybeds that can be used as spare guest rooms in a pinch. But the clients did get their eight bedrooms. “We know how to make compact bedrooms and make them very efficient, make them very livable, which allows us to fit in a lot of space for a lot of guests, and then that puts a lot of emphasis on having main entertaining spaces that are big and gracious,” notes Ries. “The idea is that everybody hangs out together.”.

Everything is united by generous outdoor spaces, which wind between the bedrooms. “Every room has an amazing view,” indicates Ries. “It feels almost like your own little treehouse in a way.” The bottom living area opens out to a pool and an expansive deck where the family eats most of their meals.

It has been revealed that the house has an elevator, but stairs are the primary way the family moves between levels and finally down to the beach. “We hung on for a really great, gracious, fun set of stairs,” says von Gal. “They really introduce you to how amazing the different levels are and all the wonderful secret places.”.

According to recent reports, durable fabrics and materials were chosen throughout and Ries made sure everything could be easily moved around and reconfigured. Outside on the main deck, three long dining tables from Sutherland were outfitted with casters so they could be maneuvered into a single long table or several smaller ones; 26 lightweight dining chairs can be pulled from different parts of the deck. If the infamous “Christmas winds” bring a downpour, the tables can even be moved indoors so a dinner party can proceed in inclement weather. Outdoor seating can be arranged as a 15-foot sofa or several love seats.

“It's so flexible,” states Ries. “We know this from working with a client that their needs for entertaining change every day, or sometimes a couple times a day.”.

The kitchen is also efficient, designed to produce easy, clean meals. The family would rather spend time in the pool than over a stove. And no part of the living room is oriented around a TV. “They're readers,” notes Ries. “There’s always stacks of new books when you go to their homes. So there’s all these cool little spots you can tuck into.”.

Ultimately, the client and design team’s insistence on what they wanted paid off—but it wasn’t without a few moments of doubt. “When so many people are telling you ‘no, surely this is not what you want,’ it does start to erode your confidence a little,” states von Gal. “Then when it does come out well, there’s a real sense of triumph and satisfaction.”.
