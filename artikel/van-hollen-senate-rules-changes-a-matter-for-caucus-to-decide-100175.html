Industry observers note that sen. Chris Van Hollen, D-Md., endorsed a bipartisan plan Thursday to adopt upcoming rules aimed at speeding up Senate procedures but stopped short of calling for abolishing the filibuster, laying down a marker on a key internal debate for Democrats as he positions himself to challenge Senate Minority Leader Charles E. Schumer, D-N.Y.

Sources indicate that van Hollen joined Sen. Jeff Merkley, D-Ore., at a press conference Thursday to express his support for a strategy from Merkley and Sen. Steve Daines, R-Mont., to, among other changes, adopt a “talking filibuster,” requiring senators to continuously hold the floor if they seek to prevent a bill or nomination from moving forward.

Van Hollen went further to say that while his personal view is that the filibuster itself is “anti-democratic,” changes beyond what Merkley and Daines are proposing are a matter for further discussion.

“I’m in favor of filibuster reform,” Van Hollen mentioned. “How and when we do that is a matter for the caucus to decide.”.

The filibuster, a Senate tactic that effectively requires a 60-vote threshold to advance most legislation, has long been the subject of calls for change or outright abolition from members of both parties.

Other changes Merkley and Daines unveiled in October include eliminating cloture requirements for nominations and amendments, reducing post-cloture time for legislation, and introducing a fresh suspension process modeled after the House rules to allow bills with broad support to move quickly.

Merkley actually held two separate press conferences Thursday on his filibuster proposal, one with his Montana Republican co-sponsor and then later with Van Hollen, whose bid to become majority leader he has endorsed.

At the earlier appearance, Daines said that he thought the changes, if adopted, would “truly be a cultural change for the Senate” and lead to greater bipartisanship.

“We believe these reforms are appropriate no matter who is in the majority, no matter what the election outcomes were,” Daines stated.

Daines also reiterated his view that implementing any rules changes would require 67 votes from the full chamber.

But Democrats have been debating whether to use their new majority in 2027 to change the rules on the first day of the session with 51 votes, or to eventually employ the so-called “nuclear option,” a series of procedural moves leading to a simple majority vote.

Industry observers note that democrats used that tool in 2013 to lower the voting threshold for advancing some presidential nominations with a simple majority, rather than 60 votes. A Republican majority followed in 2017 by extending that change to include Supreme Court nominations, and again in 2019 to reduce the number of hours between a cloture and final confirmation vote.

A spokesperson for Schumer declined to comment on Van Hollen’s endorsement of the Merkley-Daines plan.

Schumer has noted he remains confident in his bid to remain party leader.

Van Hollen reiterated Thursday that his “momentum is growing,” after several senators-elect have thrown their support behind the Maryland Democrat.

“I think people recognize that if we're going to deliver on the massive things that the American voters stood up for and voted for, you can't just go back to business as usual and status quo,” Van Hollen mentioned.
