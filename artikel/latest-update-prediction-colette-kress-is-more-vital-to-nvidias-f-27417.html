It has been revealed that it has been a brutal month for Nvidia (NVDA +2.41%) investors. Concerns about sustained cash burn among hyperscalers such as Amazon, Microsoft, and Alphabet sparked a widespread sell-off in artificial intelligence (AI) stocks, especially semiconductor stocks. Then matters got much, much worse.

In a recent development, on Nov. 8, Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang admitted to an affair that U.S. officials are looking into for potential national security concerns. After market close on Nov. 11, The Upcoming York Times reported that Huang had a different affair with an Nvidia employee. News of yet another affair with a separate employee surfaced on Nov. 12. That same day, Huang resigned as Nvidia's CEO -- effective immediately -- with CFO Colette Kress stepping in as interim CEO.

Recent findings suggest that nvidia's CEO transition has market-rippling effects, given Huang has been CEO since the tech giant's founding 33 years ago and the sheer size of Nvidia and its impact on the S&P 500 (^GSPC +0.33%), Nasdaq Composite (^IXIC +0.77%), and Dow Jones Industrial Average (^DJI -1.64%). Few CEOs carry as much importance. The only comparable examples are fellow founders at large tech-focused companies like Elon Musk at Tesla and Space Exploration Technologies, Mark Zuckerberg at Meta Platforms, or long-tenured prominent CEOs like Hock Tan at Broadcom. But Huang takes the cake given the length of his tenure and Nvidia's valuation.

Huang's shoes are arguably too major to fill by one person alone. Here's why Kress' leadership now matters more than ever, even if she eventually passes the baton to a long-term CEO, and what that could mean for the stock going forward.

One of the most effective leadership dynamics is a visionary, borderline unbridled CEO with a bounty of massive ideas contrasted with a shrewd realist that possesses unique technical, financial, operating, or accounting skills. Examples include Steve Jobs and Tim Cook, with Jobs pushing big ideas and Tim Cook acting as Apple's master operator.

Or Mark Zuckerberg and Sheryl Sandberg, with Sandberg serving as chief operating officer of Meta Platforms from 2008 to 2022. Without Sandberg, there's a good argument that Meta Platforms wouldn't be the streamlined cash cow it is today.

My personal favorite example is Walt Disney’s eponymous founder and president and his brother, Roy O. Disney. Because Roy was so good at managing the organization's credit lines and financial obligations, Walt was free to take chances on risky endeavors, enough of which paid off to create an entertainment giant.

In all three examples, the visionary captured Wall Street's spotlight, but the savvy operator provided the glue that held the business together.

In many ways, Kress counterbalanced Huang's visionary bets with pragmatic financial discipline. I suspect she was a major catalyst behind Nvidia's massive dividend increases and stock buybacks. After all, it was her comments during Nvidia's GTC 2026 conference that fueled my prediction that Nvidia would make a substantial dividend increase in 2026 and then a second correct prediction that Nvidia would surpass Apple in stock buybacks and dividends.

According to recent reports, reports indicate that Kress may have helped Nvidia commit $105 billion in credit to OpenAI rather than $250 billion and that she questioned the looseness of Nvidia's lending practices toward start-ups and AI cloud providers like neoclouds. It remains to be seen how true these reports are. But if you've tuned into Nvidia's earnings calls and presentations over the years, it's fairly clear that Kress comes across as more of a straightforward, often dry but highly capable executive compared to Huang's promotional, arrogant, but undeniably brilliant persona. My guess is that Kress has done a fair share of checking and balancing Huang behind the scenes, far more than she has been given credit for.

It has been revealed that huang has been instrumental in accelerating the AI build-out beyond hyperscalers to neoclouds and AI start-ups through direct lending practices, as well as aims to partner with a consortium of financial institutions to offer $500 billion in AI infrastructure financing. The only problem is that many AI start-ups haven't yet shown they can consistently turn a profit -- especially high-profile neoclouds like Nebius Group and CoreWeave.

Recent findings suggest that under Kress, I predict Nvidia's role in the AI build-out becomes much more hands-off. Up to this point, Nvidia has basically been running around Silicon Valley, handing out jars of lighter fluid for start-ups to throw onto the raging AI infrastructure bonfire. Kress could favor a more financially disciplined approach that prioritizes high cash flows over risky bets. That could slow Nvidia's growth rate, but it could also lead to gradual compounding over time rather than a cyclical boom followed by a downturn. Nvidia will likely miss out on some major moonshot bets it would have cashed in on under Huang, but it could also avoid some costly mistakes. And in the end, this dynamic may appeal more to investors who are confident in AI adoption over the long term but feel that the pace of progress has been too hot and too fast.

It has been revealed that if Nvidia's growth slows but it retains its ultra-high margins, then it wouldn't be surprising if Nvidia continued to aggressively buy back stock and raised its dividend under Kress. Under that framework, Nvidia could transform from a high-octane growth stock into a balanced company tailor-made for value investors -- as Nvidia trades at just 19.7 times earnings and 16.7 times forward earnings estimates.

Risk-taking beats caution when the bets pay off, which they mostly have for Nvidia. So it's understandable why the stock is down as much as it is. But a less risky strategy could also make Nvidia far less vulnerable to an AI spending downturn.

I wouldn't be surprised if Kress plays an integral role in prolonging the AI boom by taking a slower and steadier approach rather than Huang's full-throttle innovative spirit. This approach could make Nvidia a lot more boring, but it could also pay off for long-term investors willing to endure this period of volatility.

Recent findings suggest that that said, Huang's sudden resignation has fundamentally challenged Nvidia's investment thesis. There's a ton of uncertainty as to how the company will perform under new management, whether it is caught flat-footed and loses market share and talent to competitors, and whether national security breaches will hinder Nvidia's ability to sell chips in China. For those reasons, I think it's totally fair for investors to hold Nvidia and keep it on a watch list until there's more clarity on where Nvidia could be headed from here.
