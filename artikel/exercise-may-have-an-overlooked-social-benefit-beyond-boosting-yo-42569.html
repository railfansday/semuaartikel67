Exercise may have an overlooked social benefit beyond boosting your mood

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Mice with running wheels fought less over food and warmth, suggesting that exercise can influence their social behavior.

Recent findings suggest that exercise is usually recommended for the heart, the lungs, and a better mood. In mice, a new study suggests that it may also alter how animals treat one another.

Less fighting over food and a warm resting place was observed in mouse pairs housed with a working running wheel.

Pairs whose wheel had been locked fought more often. Greater activity was also recorded in brain areas tied to social behavior among the mice that ran.

In a recent development, the work was led by Liz Kirby of Ohio State University. She is an associate professor of behavioral neuroscience in the Department of Psychology.

Recent findings suggest that the findings come from mice and were presented at a scientific meeting. They have not yet appeared in a journal.

According to recent reports, “Mice are known to be naturally social. They like hanging out with other mice, just like humans like hanging out with other humans,” Kirby said.

“They also exercise naturally. If you give them a running wheel, they run about six miles a night, voluntarily.”.

Mice were housed in pairs for six weeks by Kirby’s team. Each pair consisted of two males or two females.

It has been revealed that A running wheel was available to some pairs at any hour of the day or night. Other pairs were given a wheel that could not turn.

The same object was therefore present in every cage, yet only one group could run on it.

In a recent development, each pair was then given something to compete for. A tasty treat was used in one test. In another, the prize was a warm resting spot with room for only one mouse.

In a recent development, the researchers recorded behaviors linked to competitive fighting. These included attempts to steal the food or to push a cage mate off the warm spot.

According to Kirby, a food pellet was shared more readily by the mice with working wheels. Mice with locked wheels were not as good at sharing it.

According to recent reports, “They would rip it from each other’s paws and run away with it in a cage and chase each other down. Generally very rude behavior when it comes to mealtime,” Kirby mentioned.

A follow-up experiment limited some mice to scheduled wheel sessions. These sessions lasted six hours a day and were held five days a week. Kirby called them visiting the gym.

The same tests were carried out after six weeks on that schedule. Kirby said that living with a wheel and visiting the gym both seemed to reduce fighting over limited resources.

Constant access to a wheel therefore did not appear necessary for the mice to fight less.

Will Butke is a doctoral student in Kirby’s lab and the lead author of the study. The effects of running inside the brain were also examined by him and his colleagues.

A technique that marks recently active brain cells was used. Some regions linked to social behavior were more active in the mice that ran. Other regions remained unchanged.

“This points toward the idea that this isn’t just a brain-wide increase in activation, but instead that the activation is specifically in certain subregions of the brain,” Butke said.

According to recent reports, these measurements indicate which areas were more active in the running mice. They cannot show whether that activity explains the reduced fighting.

Clubs where people exercise together have grown quickly. The fitness app Strava reported that new clubs on its platform nearly quadrupled in 2025.

The total was put at 1 million clubs by Strava. Hiking clubs grew fastest and were followed by running clubs.

Meanwhile, loneliness has worried health officials.

Falling rates of social connection were described as “an urgent public health concern” in a 2023 advisory. It was issued by U.S. Surgeon General Vivek Murthy.

According to Butke, research on what happens in the brain when people exercise socially is limited.

“People like to run in groups or exercise together – not only because you get to have a person you can rely upon, but also it gets you into a social context where you can de-stress,” he said.

Sources indicate that mice and people are difficult to compare directly. Even so, Kirby said the work suggests that exercise has a direct social benefit, since competitive fighting was reduced.

“This adds to the list of potential benefits you could be getting by exercising,” Kirby noted.

“It’s not just going to make your heart healthier, and it’s not just going to reduce your risk for cancer, obesity and stroke, but it also might help strengthen your social ties.”.

According to recent reports, A poster is an early step, and a full paper has not yet been released. The number of mice tested and the brain regions that changed would be detailed in that paper.

It has been revealed that all of the mice lived in pairs, so the results describe exercise with a companion nearby. The behavior of a mouse that runs alone remains a separate question.

Future research could test whether exercise changes how people share and compete, as it did in these mice.

The work was presented on November 18 as a poster at Neuroscience 2026, the annual meeting of the Society for Neuroscience.
