“Of all the self-fulfilling prophecies in our culture the assumption that aging means decline and poor health is probably the deadliest.” --Marilyn Ferguson, author.

I was in an elevator with two preschool children in Halloween costumes, their parents, and an older couple.

The older man commented to the smiling children, “Oh, you are going to have fun on Halloween, I see.” The children and parents nodded at him in the affirmative.

Then he sighed and noted, “When you’re my age, you have a lot of aches and pains. Life is not as much fun when you get older.”.

I was taken aback. So many negative stereotypes about aging in two short sentences, including the classic myth that old age is an inevitable process of decline and deterioration. I responded, “I don’t agree. I’m 82, and I’m enjoying life more than ever.”.

Sources indicate that the man said, “Well, I’m 73. You look very young for your age.” Was he implying that I was the exception that proved the rule?

I’m not above fishing for compliments, but in this case, I was after something more notable—sending positive messages about aging to young children. I never realized I was going to need an “elevator message” to do it.

Recent findings suggest that usually, an “elevator message” refers to a business networking tool, consisting of one or two short sentences about who you are, what you do, and how your skills could benefit the listener, according to speaking coach Fred Miller. The idea of an elevator message is to convey the most key things about you during a short elevator ride. "Elevator messages" don't need to take place in actual elevators, of course. These messages can be employed at conferences, book signings, or even in the line at the coffee house. Writers, for example, can describe their background and latest book project; job-seekers can tout their most important accomplishments.

I had never previously considered that I might someday need an elevator message to combat stereotypes about aging. What I said was simply a gut reaction to the situation. And the reason I am still mulling over what I said, and what I could have said, is because there was a life-and-death situation at stake. Well, not exactly, but here’s the research.

In a recent development, psychology professor Becca Levy has researched the health and longevity effects of age beliefs. In one study, published in 2002, she and her colleagues followed 660 adults 50 and older and discovered that participants with a positive view of aging lived on average 7.5 years longer than participants with a negative view of aging.

It has been revealed that in her 2022 book, Breaking the Age Code, Levy cites other studies showing that people who held positive age beliefs scored 30% better on memory tests than those with negative age beliefs. She has also discovered that age stereotypes affect a surprising number of areas—for example, those with negative age beliefs had a slower gait, lower self-esteem, and exercised less. (For more details, check out this blog on how positive age beliefs can reduce dementia risk.).

According to recent reports, add to these results the amazing finding that most older people are happier people, as shown by numerous surveys. I was so shocked to learn the relationship between aging and happiness that I wrote a whole book (Silver Sparks) and numerous blogs about it. In addition, older people have good mental health, including a positive outlook on life. As researcher Laura Carstensen states: “People over the age of 65 have the most stable and optimistic outlook of all adults.”.

According to recent reports, of course, one negative comment in an elevator would be unlikely to mold the mentality of those young children. But I would suspect that a drumbeat of negative, ageist comments like the one I overheard could eventually have an effect on the mental outlook of those who hear them…which is everybody.

Sources indicate that young children and young adults are not the only ones who could benefit from an age-positive elevator message. A thoughtful message might also remind older people to challenge their own internalized ageism and recall the benefits of getting older.

Can we construct pithy elevator messages to replace the knee-jerk comments about ageism?
