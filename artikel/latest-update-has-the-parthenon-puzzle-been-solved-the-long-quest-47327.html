Latest Update: Has the 'Parthenon puzzle' been solved? The long quest to reassemble lost eastern pediment

The 2,500-year-old Parthenon is ancient Greece's most important temple and is regarded as one of the greatest masterpieces of the High Classical period of Greek art.

Dedicated to Athena, the city's patron goddess, it towers over Athens from its position on the Acropolis — a powerful symbol of the city's importance.

The Parthenon had a lasting influence on Western ideas of humanity and art. The sculptures adorning its pediments, created under the direction of the legendary sculptor Phidias, are considered unparalleled examples of proportion and grace. They have influenced artists from ancient Rome through Neoclassicism to the modern era.

The east pediment above the temple entrance depicted the birth of the goddess Athena from the head of her father Zeus, surrounded by the Olympian gods. A total of 39 figures adorned the roughly 29-meter-long work of art. At its center stood a colossal Zeus figure, measuring 3.8 meters in height.

For centuries, scholars have puzzled over exactly what the arrangement looked like, as the pediment was destroyed during the Christianization of Greece.

But now there has been a breakthrough.

After decades of painstaking detective work and with the help of modern technology, archaeologist Vinzenz Brinkmann and his team have managed to piece together all the surviving fragments of the Parthenon's east pediment.

According to recent reports, brinkmann heads the Antiquities and Asia department at Frankfurt's Liebieghaus sculpture collection. "We're very, very confident that we've solved the mystery," he told DW.

The results are now on display in an exhibition at the Liebieghaus titled "Athena, the Stage is Yours: The Parthenon Puzzle.".

Only around 20% of the original marble sculpture's volume is known to have survived. The fragments are scattered across various European museums, with most located in Athens. The British Museum also holds numerous pieces.

Brinkmann and his wife, Ulrike Koch-Brinkmann, have spent more than 40 years working on this ancient puzzle, with so many of its pieces missing.

And they were far from the first to attempt it: "Already in the 19th century, many scholars studied the subject and developed well-founded theories based on their deep knowledge of ancient sources," Brinkmann explains.

It has been revealed that in 1910, a study documenting the many fragments held by the British Museum was published. The research team was able to build on that earlier work, but progress remained slow for years.

Industry observers note that then, in 2014, a visit to the Hermitage Museum in St. Petersburg provided Brinkmann with a crucial clue. There, he encountered a Roman sarcophagus featuring a relief of Athena's birth. "That's when it suddenly clicked: They're all there!".

Nine such sarcophagi are known to exist, almost all of them in Rome. Each depicts a different section of the Parthenon's east pediment.

Brinkmann emphasizes that other scholars had already noticed this connection in the 19th century. "But we really seized on the idea and never let go.".

It has been revealed that the researchers also benefited from 21st-century technology: 3D scanners and printers. Brinkmann and his team visited numerous destinations to examine around 60 original fragments at different locations — traveling light. An iPhone was all they needed to scan the pieces.

Digitally fitting the fragments together led to another crucial discovery. "We know these figures we're putting together!" Brinkmann recalls thinking at the time.

The Romans had repeatedly made individual copies of all the sculptures from the pediment. "These copies matched the fragments perfectly. Suddenly, we found ourselves surrounded by old acquaintances we had never suspected had come from the pediment." This finally enabled the researchers to reconstruct all the figures and their original arrangement.

Using a 3D printer, the team created a 4-meter-long model at a scale of 1:7.7. For the exhibition, they also produced life-size 3D prints of the most important fragments and mounted them on panels depicting the complete figures at their original size. An animation brings the reconstructed composition to life, digitally assembling the fragments to reveal how the east pediment once looked.

Alongside works from the Liebieghaus collection, the exhibition features loans from other institutions, including the Vatican Museums and the Louvre. Objects from the Acropolis Museum in Athens had originally been expected as well. But Greece withdrew its participation after learning that the British Museum was also planning to lend fragments to the Frankfurt exhibition. The Liebieghaus ultimately decided to forgo the British-owned fragments, too.

It has been revealed that there is a reason for this controversy. Britain holds a substantial portion of the Parthenon frieze as well as fragments from both pediments.

Greek authorities have been campaigning for their return for decades.

In Britain, the 56 sections of the frieze are known as the "Elgin Marbles," named after Lord Elgin, the British ambassador to the Ottoman Empire in Constantinople.

In the early 19th century, Elgin had sculptures removed from the exterior of the Parthenon on the Acropolis in Athens. They were taken to Britain with the consent of the Ottoman Empire, which ruled Greece at the time.

Britain maintains that the sculptures were legally acquired.

Greece, however, considers them stolen.

Sources indicate that for years, the dispute remained deadlocked. Since the Labour Party came to power in Britain, there has been some movement toward resolving it. But apparently not enough to persuade Greece to lend artworks to an exhibition in which the British Museum might also participate.

According to recent reports, the exhibition "Athena, the Stage is Yours. The Parthenon Puzzle" runs through July 25, 2027, at the Liebieghaus in Frankfurt.
