The Carnivore Diet Is Peak Protein Extremism. How Healthy Is It Really?

  • It has been revealed that eating raw organs is a step too far, but maybe you’ve heard about the benefits of the carnivore diet and you’re curious.
  • Maybe plant-based eating leaves you gassy and unsatisfied.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

You know your obsession with protein has gone too far when you’re waiting in line at Erewhon for a raw meat smoothie. The speciality drink was created by Paul Saladino, who regularly preaches the benefits of following an animal-based diet. If you look inside the blender, you’ll find kefir, beef organs (like liver, heart, kidney, and spleen from Saldino’s Heart & Soil line), and “immunomilk,” which is freeze-dried cow’s colostrum. Yum. Saladino may be an outlier, but he certainly isn’t the only person pushing a meat-heavy diet. Brian Johnson, aka the “liver king,” follows an extreme ancestral carnivore diet, eating a pound of raw liver a day.

It has been revealed that eating raw organs is a step too far, but maybe you’ve heard about the benefits of the carnivore diet and you’re curious. According to a 2026 study published in Nutrients, the number of posts about the carnivore diet on Instagram is in the millions—and they didn’t even look at TikTok. Interest surrounding the carnivore diet has shown no signs of slowing down.

Maybe plant-based eating leaves you gassy and unsatisfied. You can’t help that you like wings. Before you take vegetables off the table completely, it’s important to know what the carnivore diet is and how it impacts health, both short-term and long-term. As experts explain here, it’s not all bad. But there are some important risks to be aware of.

The aforementioned Nutrients study on the carnivore diet explains that there isn’t one standard definition of what the carnivore diet actually is, but generally speaking, it’s a diet where you only eat minimally processed animal foods, including organ meats, fish, seafood, eggs, animal fats, and full-fat dairy. Plant-based foods are eliminated entirely.

According to recent reports, while eliminating foods like fruits, vegetables, and whole grains is extreme, Pouria Sefidmooye Azar, PhD, RD, a registered dietitian and assistant professor at the University of Central Arkansas, says the carnivore diet isn’t all bad. He says that short term, many people lose weight eating this way.

“Protein is more satiating than carbs and fats, so people following the carnivore diet often stay full for a longer amount of time, which means they reduce their caloric intake,” he notes. Logically, it makes sense. If you’re eating foods like beef, chicken, and salmon, you’ll be less likely to dig into the pantry for snacks than if you ate a bowl of noodles.

Recent findings suggest that dr. Azar indicates it’s crucial not to conflate weight loss with getting healthier. He says that by eliminating plants, you’re going to miss out on a lot of important nutrients the body needs. You can probably guess the big one. Yep, fiber. “"If you’re cutting out every plant food, you're not getting fiber or phytonutrients, which are protective compounds found only in plants,” says Chris Vogliano, PhD, RD, a registered dietitian and global food systems expert.

Industry observers note that while you might not suffer any consequences of this immediately, both dietitians say that continuing to eat this way can mess up your digestion. You need fiber for good poops, plain and simple. Dr. Azar says that we also need fiber to feed the healthy gut bacteria in the microbiome. This is notable for supporting mental health, immune health, and minimizing the risk of chronic diseases and cancers.

Dr. Azar specifically calls out the connection between a high-meat, low-fiber diet and the risk of colorectal cancer. For example, a 2015 Oncology Reviews study demonstrates a connection between red meat consumption and colorectal cancer risk, while a 2026 European Journal of Cancer study called low fiber consumption a major risk factor for colorectal cancer.

Industry observers note that considering that men are at increased risk for heart disease (the number one cause of death in the U.S.), it’s important to know how the carnivore diet can impact your cardiovascular health. Spoiler alert: It’s not good. “I would not recommend the carnivore diet as a healthy, long-term eating pattern,” indicates Sunny Intwala, MD, MBA, preventive cardiologist and the director of Prevention & Wellness at Northwell Health.

Dr. Intwala explains that this is because meat doesn’t just contain protein; it has a lot of saturated fat too. He claims that diets centering around red meat increase LDL cholesterol and blood pressure in a few short weeks. This, he says, can contribute to inflammation and plaque buildup that increases the risk of heart attack and stroke.

As someone who studies how the way we eat impacts the planet, Dr. Vogliano points out that the carnivore diet is pretty horrible for the environment. “From a food systems lens, a meat-only diet on a global or even national level would carry massive environmental costs from land, water, and greenhouse gas emissions. This is regardless of whether the meat is conventionally raised, organic, or even regenerative. There’s just not enough land to produce that much meat,” he says.

Okay, we need plants. But what about the Brian Johnsons and Paul Salidinos of the world preaching the benefits of eating raw meat? If you are going to eat meat, are there benefits to eating it raw?

According to recent reports, both dietitians say no, emphasizing that there is no scientific evidence showing that raw meat is more beneficial in any way than eating cooked meat. “Liver is a nutrient-rich food and a great way to get vitamin A, vitamin B12, folate, and iron. But you can get that from cooked liver; you don’t have to eat it raw,” Dr. Vogliano indicates. In fact, he emphasizes that eating any meat raw increases the risk of foodborne illnesses like Salmonella and E.coli.

Sources indicate that dr. Azar warns against pulling a Rocky and eating raw eggs either. “The protein in raw eggs is between 50 and 60% digestible. But the protein digestibility in cooked eggs is over 90%,” he says. A study from the ‘90s in The Journal of Nutrition backs this up.

Recent findings suggest that it’s worth noting that this isn’t the same for meat. A 2015 study in the same journal found that the cooking time for meat didn’t impact protein digestibility very much. So while you may get the same amount of digestible protein from raw meat as you would cooked meat, your risk of foodborne illness is greater.

Decades of scientific research make it clear that a plant-free diet is not a healthy diet. If you’re a meat lover, you might be wondering just how much meat you can get away with eating without compromising your health.

It has been revealed that dr. Azar’s advice is this: Meat can still be your primary protein source. Just eat some plants along with it. “If you love meat, you can still eat foods like beef and chicken, but you also need to eat fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and nuts and seeds because there are some nutrients you just can’t get from meat,” he states, adding that only eating meat will lead to nutrient deficiencies.

Recent findings suggest that dr. Vogliano delivers a dietary message you’ve likely heard many times before: The healthiest diet is a diverse one. “We know from scientific research that the more plant foods our diets consist of, the more insurance we have that we’re getting the nutrients the body needs,” he claims. Because of this, he advocates for a primarily plant-based diet and only eating meat occasionally.

Recent findings suggest that different governing bodies have slightly different recommendations when it comes to how much meat you can eat while still maintaining a healthy diet. The American Institute for Cancer Research states to keep red meat under 18 ounces a week, or roughly three portions a week. The American Heart Association doesn’t give a specific serving recommendation for meat, but says to mostly get protein from plants, fish, and seafood.

Industry observers note that dr. Azar emphasizes that there isn’t one universal way of eating that works for everyone. One diet may make one person feel energized and leave someone else feeling exhausted. What is key, he says, is making sure you’re getting all the nutrients your body needs. And what we know for sure is that you can’t do that by just eating meat.
