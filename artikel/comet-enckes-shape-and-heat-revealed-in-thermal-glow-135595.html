Comets are time capsules of the origins of the solar system, containing materials that have existed for billions of years. While comets primarily orbit in the farthest reaches of the solar system, specifically the Kuiper Belt or Oort Cloud, some make their way closer to the Sun. When this happens, the increased heat cause cometary icy material to evaporate, producing a spectacular tail while also revealing the rocky material underneath, known as a cometâs nucleus. But how well can astronomers use telescopic observations to study the composition and temperatures of a cometary nucleus?

Now, a team of researchers from Japan might be one step closer to scientists better understanding comets, specifically a cometâs nucleus. Through findings recently published in The Astronomical Journal, the researchers presented newly introduced analyses regarding the shape and temperature of a comateâs nucleus, providing researchers with key observations that could be used to understand both the formation and evolution of comets and the solar system.

According to recent reports, for the study, the researchers analyzed archival data on Comet 2P/Encke originally obtained from the Cooled Mid-Infrared Camera and Spectrometer (COMICS) on the Subaru Telescope in November 2003. During this time, Comet 2P/Encke traveled as close as 0.26 astronomical units (AU) from Earth, or about 37 million kilometers (26 million miles), enabling several ground-based telescopes, including the Subaru Telescope, to conduct mid-infrared observations of Comet 2P/Encke. This distance from Earth also put Comet 2P/Encke about 1.09 AU from the Sun, or about 163 million kilometers (101 million miles). Unfortunately, COMICS was retired from Subaru in 2020, leaving Subaru without a mid-infrared instrument, thus limiting its studies to archival data.

The primary goal of the study was to ascertain the composition of Comet 2P/Enckeâs nucleus while isolating its signal from the surrounding ice, gas, and dust that envelopes the comet and becomes larger and denser as the increased Sunâs heat evaporates it. In the end, the researchers found the nucleus of Comet 2P/Encke measured about 7 kilometers by 3 kilometers (4.3 miles by 1.9 miles) while its surface temperature is estimated to be about 328 Kelvin (55 degrees Celsius/131 degrees Fahrenheit).

"Our study of Comet Encke showed that mid-infrared observations can directly capture thermal emission from the nucleus itself," remarked Dr. Takafumi Ootsubo, who is assistant professor in the Department of Basic Sciences at the School of Medicine at the University of Occupational and Environmental Health in Japan, and lead author of the study. Despite COMICS retiring in 2020, Dr. Ootsubo is actively working to restart the program. "By restarting COMICS, we hope to study the shapes, temperatures, and surface materials of many more cometary nuclei in detail and get closer to revealing their true faces.".
