In a recent development, there's usually someone in every family, friendship group, or workplace whom people know they can count on. When problems in life arise, that person often gets called to support and fix the issue. Can you relate to being that person?

Sources indicate that you have probably heard the advice to take care of yourself before caring for others, or "put your own oxygen mask on first". For a lot of people, being the dependable one becomes central to their identity. But sometimes, being the person everyone counts on comes at the expense of taking care of yourself.

Reliability stops being a habit at some point and becomes who you are. You may start measuring your worth by how much you do for others and how little you need from them. Once that's set, asking for help may feel like breaking character.

That label, "strong one," can ultimately impact your self-image. It may make it more challenging to reach out when you need support. According to a 2025 review of caregiver experiences, participants reported that individuals, due to the pressure to stay strong and reliable, may feel that they are not living up to their role if they seek psychological help.

According to recent reports, you may be the person that others call when they need advice or that people turn to when life gets difficult. But when someone asks how you're doing, you change the subject or say “I'm good.”.

In a recent 2025 study, experts examined people's beliefs about seeking and receiving help. They found that some people believe accepting help makes them less themselves or threatens their independence. Those beliefs were connected to how people experienced receiving help and to their well-being. Accepting help can be hard, not because support isn't there, but because it can impact how people see themselves.

Industry observers note that you're probably used to running things and fixing problems on your own. Letting someone support you means admitting what's actually going on, and that gets uncomfortable when your whole identity is built on being the one who handles everything. To engage in vulnerability, try to ask yourself, "When did I last let myself be vulnerable?" Did it bring relief, or did it leave you reluctant to open up again? Letting someone help sometimes means letting go of control and showing them a side they don't usually see.

Industry observers note that it can be even harder when you feel pressure to appear "successful" and seem like you have everything under control. For instance, a recent study introduces another factor that can make asking for help difficult: competition. When employees viewed professional success as a contest, where one person's win meant someone else's loss, they were less likely to seek help from colleagues.
