A Latest Way to Bridge Divides During the Holidays

It has been revealed that when political differences emerge at family gatherings, we often judge relatives with opposing opinions as ignorant, stupid, crazy, or malicious. Sometimes, we see caricatures, not individuals with unique life experiences that inform their politics.

The usual advice for keeping the peace during the holidays is simple: Keep politics off the table. After all, nobody is likely to change their mind, so debating is useless—and can be unpleasant.

The assumption is that in the game of politics, you win if you persuade people to change their minds and lose if your opponent does. If you change your own mind, it’s game over.

Every so often, I write another post arguing that rather than trying to convince your relatives to change their minds, listening with genuine curiosity is the answer. Here's an additional key to bridging political divides.

Fresh Research on Respect and Connection.

International scholars of partisan polarization collaborated to test whether the perception of self-disclosure increased connection, respect, or willingness to interact with political opponents.

Earlier research found that participants across the political spectrum expected that grounding their arguments in facts would earn them greater respect from political opponents. In reality, however, political opponents had greater respect for those who grounded their positions in personal experiences rather than data. Researchers attributed this effect to personal experiences being less readily doubted than the veracity of facts supplied.

Across six studies, research published in 2025 found that participants reported feeling more connected to political opponents and expressed greater respect for them when those opponents’ statements seemed more self-disclosing. “When people self-disclose while sharing their political views,” the studies found, “it breeds connection and bridges divides—a promising strategy to reduce vitriol in an increasingly divided world.”.

Nicholas Epley, author of Mindwise: Why We Misunderstand What Others Think, Believe, Feel, and Want, argues that “introspection makes us feel like we know what’s going on in our own heads, even when we don’t.” A false sense of certainty means we’re also often wrong about what other people are thinking and why they come to their conclusions—especially when we think their ideas are misguided.

It has been revealed that even if we think our own political decisions are fact-based, what underlies our convictions—and the convictions of those with whom we disagree—may be profound existential priorities and universal human concerns. Existential psychiatrist Irvin Yalom calls these “ultimate concerns” such as death, isolation, freedom, and meaninglessness.

It has been revealed that in 2025, psychologist Todd Kashdan and his colleagues found that Americans underestimate how open-minded and intellectually humble their political in-group actually is. (Yes, their own group.) Regardless of political party, as politically closed-minded as partisans think the other side is, they also misunderstand their own group as being more narrow-minded than it is.

Recent findings suggest that if we believe our own group only sanctions a narrow set of views, it feels risky to be curious about views outside that range. Belonging is a fundamental human need, and ostracism is a profoundly painful human experience that activates some of the same neural systems implicated in physical pain. When a political party becomes an in-group from which we fear exclusion, self-censorship can become a coping strategy.

But we tend to share at least some values and political concerns with those we disagree with. We often prioritize them differently. For example, as I’ve written before: Someone passionate about gun rights may prioritize concerns about government intrusion into freedom and personal autonomy where the right to hunt or defend oneself is concerned, while someone passionate about gun control might be more focused on concerns about the harms caused by firearms to children, such as in school shootings.

Someone passionate about reproductive rights might prioritize concerns about government intrusion into freedom and bodily autonomy, while someone fervently pro-life might be more focused on concerns about harms caused by abortions to unborn babies.

Kashdan and his colleagues found that once people believe open-mindedness is acceptable to their in-group, they become more willing to be curious; based on that and self-disclosure research, we have fresh tools available.

When discussing your opinions, talk about your experiences rather than just data. What in your own life has led you to your conclusions? What experiences of harm—in your own life or in the lives of people you know—have shaped your policy preferences? Do your political preferences help you avoid similar harm?

Instead of debating, make curiosity and genuine listening your goal, even for ideas you find obnoxious. Listen for shared concerns, even if prioritized differently. And listen for what you can learn about how your ideological opponents see things.

Nobody’s mind needs changing—although it might happen. But if you learn something you didn't already know about someone at the holiday table, you’ve won the game.

"Listening is a rare happening among human beings,” theologian William Stringfellow in his 1967 book Count It All Joy. “You cannot listen to the word another is speaking if you are preoccupied with your appearance or impressing the other, or if you are trying to decide what you are going to say when the other stops talking, or if you are debating about whether the word being spoken is true or relevant or agreeable. Such matters may have their place, but only after listening to the word as the word is being uttered. Listening, in other words, is a primitive act of love.”.
