Emergency repairs at Etruscan tombs in Italy uncovered stone thrones and a jar that looters had missed

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Archaeologists in Cerveteri, Italy, uncovered Etruscan tombs with stone thrones, red-painted walls and a decorated jar overlooked by looters.

Emergency work at the Monte Abatone necropolis in Cerveteri, the Etruscan city of Caere, has turned up stone thrones, walls painted red, and a nearly intact decorated jar.

The work was carried out by Italy’s heritage authority for the province of Viterbo and southern Etruria, the Soprintendenza Archeologia, Belle Arti e Paesaggio. Some of the tombs had collapsed or become unstable.

According to recent reports, clandestine diggers had entered these tombs before the archaeologists arrived. They left the jar behind.

Industry observers note that the collapses and instability meant the tombs had to be investigated, documented and secured. The team cleared the entrances and, as a precaution, removed the fill that had built up inside.

The chambers were dug into tuff, a soft volcanic rock. Excavators have also found places on the hill where blocks of tuff were quarried in several stages.

During a 2019 excavation, a team including the University of Bonn documented a Monte Abatone tomb whose ceiling had partly fallen in along a crack.

The researchers linked such cracks to movements of the tuff layer over a less stable layer of clay. The Soprintendenza has not mentioned what caused the latest collapses, or when they occurred.

One of the chambers still keeps much of its original vaulted ceiling.

In another Etruscan chamber tomb, the funerary furniture was carved directly out of the tuff, including thrones.

A third chamber has extensive red painted decoration on its walls.

Sources indicate that similar furnishings are known from earlier work at Monte Abatone. In 2019, years before the emergency repairs, excavators recorded kline-type death beds, shaped like couches, and narrower benches lining the walls of another chamber.

Sources indicate that cerveteri’s larger Banditaccia necropolis and the Monterozzi necropolis at Tarquinia together form a UNESCO World Heritage property. At Banditaccia, thousands of tombs are laid out in a city-like blueprint, with streets and small squares.

The most complete find is an olla stamnoide, a jar shaped like a stamnos. It is almost intact. Its surface carries incised figures of animals, with some details picked out in red paint.

In a recent development, the jar will be cleaned and restored, then put on public display. No date or venue for the display has been announced.

The earlier clandestine digging compromised the original context of the burials. Some materials survived it and were recovered.

It has been revealed that looters have missed objects at Monte Abatone before. In the 2019 excavation, Tomb 642 showed signs of their digging, yet it still held a large pithos, a bucchero drinking set, Ionian bowls and Corinthian ointment jars.

Monte Abatone lies immediately south of the ancient city. It flourished in the seventh and sixth centuries BCE, according to a 2022 report on Monte Abatone.

Industry observers note that in the 1950s and 1960s, surveys and excavations explored 641 tombs there. That work was carried out on behalf of the heritage authority in response to heavy looting.

Burial types at the site include simple trench graves, chamber tombs and tombs under burial mounds, among them the Campana Tumulus.

That great mound dates to the middle of the Orientalizing period and had not been surveyed since the first half of the 19th century.

Older finds show that burials there continued from the late eighth century BCE into the Hellenistic period and the early Roman Empire.

In a recent development, researchers also examined the area west and north of the Campana Tumulus. It held tombs of the sixth to third centuries BCE, excavated in the past and partly backfilled, and funerary terraces facing the city.

It has been revealed that A research project on Monte Abatone began in the early 1990s. After a newly introduced project was set up in 2017, a group of Italian and German universities ran excavation campaigns at the site from 2018 to 2021.

Ground-penetrating radar was used across an area of about 7.4 acres (3 hectares) east of the Campana Tumulus. An earlier geophysical test had revealed a densely packed cluster of tombs.

A 2021 trial excavation opened about 13,500 square feet (1,250 square meters) south of the great tumulus. It exposed tombs with small mounds, up to about 36 feet (11 meters) across, set in two roughly parallel rows and all facing northwest.

Excavations on the south side also uncovered what the team describes as a “possible ‘family’ cluster of tombs,” used from the early Orientalizing period into the Archaic period.

“This suggests the existence of a planning by some form of ‘urban’ authority,” the authors of the 2022 report wrote about the rows of mounds.

In a recent development, laser scanners recorded the site in fine detail, producing a combined model of about 4.3 billion points, roughly 1.2 billion of them for the Campana Tumulus alone. A drone survey added a photogrammetric model of the plateau.

The information was obtained from the Soprintendenza for the province of Viterbo and southern Etruria.
