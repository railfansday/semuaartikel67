This is a summary of: Kuhlisch, C. et al. Synergy in viral–bacterial co-infection expedites algal bloom demise. Nature https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11243-7 (2026).

Recent findings suggest that marine algae are responsible for about half of the photosynthetic activity on Earth. A substantial part of that activity takes place in algal blooms that can cover thousands of square kilometres. Blooms of the coccolithophorid alga Gephyrocapsa huxleyi are often terminated by a giant virus called Emiliania huxleyi virus, with the entire population collapsing in a synchronized manner in days. Yet, our previous work has shown that less than 30% of the algal cells are infected at any time during bloom demise1. This indicates that other factors influence algal mortality, too. We suspected that bacteria were responsible because their abundance and community composition often change markedly as blooms collapse.

Recent findings suggest that to identify the microbial contributors to this unexplained algal mortality, we disentangled the main players of the bloom ecosystem: the alga, the virus and the bloom-associated bacteria. These three groups were isolated from a G. huxleyi bloom in Norway2. We reconstructed several synthetic tripartite communities (containing one species each of alga, bacterium and virus) in the laboratory using various combinations of the three and assessed their interactions using two metrics that we developed. The synergy index (SI) quantifies the extra algal mortality caused by bacteria during viral infection, relative to that caused by viral infection alone (Fig. 1a). The benefit index (BI) measures the growth advantage that bacteria gain from viral infection of the algae compared with their growth alongside uninfected algae. This approach revealed that virus-induced algal biomass loss increases by more than 20% when specific bacteria are present.

Sources indicate that the SI and BI values for bacterial species isolated from a bloom exposed four ecological strategies: opportunistic pathogens, which expedite algal death and benefit from it; killers, which expedite algal death without gaining from it; consumers, which benefit from algal demise without contributing to it; and agnostics, which neither expedite algal death nor benefit from it. Strains of Alteromonas bacteria were consistently opportunistic pathogens, with both high SI and high BI. Alteromonas were also prominent in bacteria that swim towards molecules derived from infected algae. We examined this in a bloom with an in situ chemotaxis assay3, using a microfluidic device with wells loaded with extracts from virus-infected or uninfected algal cells to investigate bacterial behaviour. When the device was placed into an induced algal bloom, Alteromonas were strongly attracted to the infected, but not the uninfected, extracts. Finally, we found that Alteromonas abundance increased during virus-induced bloom demise in the North Atlantic Ocean (Fig. 1b). Together, these findings point to viral–bacterial synergy as a previously unrecognized driver of algal mortality in oceanic blooms.

Industry observers note that algal-bloom demise provides an important source of carbon for marine microbial food webs. Virus-induced cell rupture can either fuel the microbial food web and recycle carbon in the ocean surface, or promote the aggregation and sinking of organic matter to the deep ocean4. Aggregation happens because the virus-infected G. huxleyi cells produce sticky polysaccharides. We propose that virus–bacteria synergy can tilt the balance between recycling of this carbon at the surface and export to the deep ocean, depending on the lifestyles of the bacteria present and the strength of the viral–bacterial interaction. Future studies could investigate the link between interactions at the microscale and global biogeochemical cycles.

We characterized bacteria in isolation and found that different species adopt distinct lifestyles during viral bloom demise. How these lifestyles operate in a complex microbial community in the natural environment and how virus–bacteria synergy controls the flow of carbon remain to be determined. Moreover, although synergy can be quantified in the lab, it is still challenging to measure it in nature because the molecular process that bacteria use to kill virus-infected algae remains unknown. We therefore cannot yet predict the magnitude of virus–bacteria synergy in the ocean.

We look forward to elucidating the genetic mechanisms and chemical signals5 that mediate virus–bacteria synergy, how bacteria benefit from this relationship and whether bacteria were honed during co-evolution with the algae. Deciphering these mechanisms would provide a deeper understanding of how these notable interactions will operate in diverse environmental conditions, as well as under climate change. — Assaf Vardi and David Zeevi are at the Weizmann Institute of Science, Rehovot, Israel.

This paper examines the impact of the tripartite interaction between algae, bacteria and viruses. The authors found that the addition of bacteria to alga–virus interactions often results in quicker mortality for the algae. Furthermore, they observe chemically directed movement towards infected cells and particles of the bacteria whose presence induced the quickest algal death. Looking at multiple, co-occurring domains is the next step towards understanding in situ dynamics.” — Elizabeth Harvey is at the University of Fresh Hampshire, Durham, New Hampshire, USA.

Industry observers note that the Vardi lab has been studying how giant viruses contribute to algal-bloom collapse for almost 20 years. Previous observations by the group found that viruses alone could not explain the coordinated death of algae during bloom collapse1, and that these viruses might cooperate with bacteria to accelerate death of algal cells and cause rapid bloom demise. The fruitful and rewarding collaboration between the Vardi and Zeevi labs started when Eliran Soffer, a PhD student in the Vardi lab studying alga–virus–bacterium interactions, approached D.Z. for computational advice. Together, we developed the SI and BI, which can reveal differences between bacterial strategies and enabled us to find the drivers of bloom demise. This study demonstrates how synergy between scientific disciplines — here, chemical ecology and quantitative biology — can expose synergy in nature. Like any good reaction, this research also required a catalyst, in this case Constanze Kuhlisch, a talented postdoctoral researcher in the Vardi lab who bridged the gap between lab work and marine studies. — A.V. and D.Z.

In a recent development, algal blooms have an important role in ocean carbon cycling. Although viruses trigger bloom collapse, fewer than one-third of algal cells are typically infected. Using synthetic communities built from bloom-derived algae, viruses and bacteria, this study shows that some bacteria cooperate with viruses to accelerate algal mortality by more than 20%. This viral–bacterial synergy helps to explain rapid bloom collapse and could influence whether carbon is recycled in surface waters or sinks to the deep ocean. — Editorial team, Nature.
