📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Anagrama builds the Cara de Vaca brand identity from Monterrey market signs, a custom typeface, and posters that shout in black and orange.

Cara de Vaca is a Monterrey restaurant built on fire cooking, natural wine, and classic drinks. Chef Chuy Villarreal cooks regional cuisine as an evolution of the Regiomontana barbecue, and Anagrama answers with a system borrowed from the hand-painted business signs of downtown Monterrey. Black fields carry the shouting, orange paper carries the warmth, and a smiling face and a small cow sit beside the wordmark like a stamp.

It has been revealed that the logo mixes fonts the way a shop owner mixes lettering on a hand-painted sign, and that looseness is the point. Anagrama drew a custom typeface, Lechon Sans, from the same source, built for social media lockups that spotlight a single word. The specimen shows the full set: capitals, numerals, punctuation, arrows, and small symbols that include the smiley face, the cow, and a pig.

Sources indicate that the lockups take their cues from Mercado Juarez, the downtown market where the studio photographed signs to dress the restaurant walls. Phrases stack in mixed sizes with quotation marks doing the work of a frame: BARBACOA, LECHON, CABRITO. VINOS NATURALES, BIEN FRIOS. GRAN DOMINGO. A word goes huge when it matters, small when it is a wink, and the quotes and rules hold the pieces together.

Sources indicate that the posters are physical objects first. One orange sheet curls at the corner against a black field, its type running diagonally across the page. A kraft sheet carries the same treatment, the paper grain showing through the ink. The black behind both does quiet work: it makes the orange and the kraft read as light in a dark room.

In a recent development, the system spreads across everything a guest can hold. Tote bags run in cream, green, and orange, each carrying a different phrase, and a yellow cattle ear tag hangs from the handles as the label. Business cards take the same marks in gold foil, the cow and the smiley pressed small into white stock.

Industry observers note that inside the restaurant the identity turns architectural. A neon CARA DE VACA sign glows red over the entrance at night: the logo lettering, now in light. Wood and metal frame the room, brand marks sit on walls and mirrors, and plants soften the space around the central bar, where natural wines and Mexican distillates pour.

The website carries the same voice, quieter: the personality, the menu photos, a reservation button, and the address. Social posts use the lockups the typeface was built for, phrases set large enough to read at a scroll. From a neon sign to a phone screen, the identity never changes its register.
