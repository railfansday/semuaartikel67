Eoin Keary Founder & CEO of Edgescan, an enterprise CTEM (Continuous Testing and Exposure Management platform) used by Fortune 500s.

Industry observers note that ​AI is fundamentally changing the velocity of the threat landscape, particularly the time between vulnerability discovery and weaponization. Vulnerabilities that historically took months, or even up to a year, to become active exploits can now move into the wild within days. AI also expands the attack surface itself as organizations deploy AI systems and agents across their environments, including systems that may not be fully visible to security teams.​.

Industry observers note that this creates a latest imperative: Security processes designed around periodic assessment and remediation must evolve to operate at the speed of continuous change. They should consider a different approach to security that employs AI to manage data volume while retaining a human in the loop for critical decision making. In this way, their organizations can rise with the tide of AI-driven threats by purifying their cybersecurity data to ensure it remains manageable and actionable.​.

The fundamental challenge in vulnerability management has never been finding vulnerabilities but determining which findings represent genuine, actionable risk. Organizations must distinguish between false positives, vulnerabilities that are real but not exploitable and vulnerabilities that are exploitable but mitigated by other controls.​.

Only after those layers of validation can security teams determine which remaining vulnerabilities represent the greatest business risk and should be addressed first. AI threatens to amplify the existing “vulnerability noise” problem by dramatically increasing the volume of findings entering the security team’s workflow. The answer can’t be asking already-constrained security and development teams to manually process an increasingly powerful “firehose” of alerts.​.

Slowing down detection isn’t the answer. A better answer is to add a validation layer between detection and action. The value of continuous assessment is its ability to provide a more current view of the environment. Without validation, however, it just produces more noise, faster. This is especially important as AI accelerates both vulnerability discovery and vulnerability exploitation.

According to recent reports, the temptation to use AI to automate remediation and allow systems to patch vulnerabilities without human intervention is obvious.​ Yet autonomous remediation can introduce significant operational risk when an AI system lacks the business context necessary to understand the consequences of its actions. AI should be used to make security professionals “more bionic,” processing information, accelerating analysis and supporting decisions, while humans retain responsibility for critical remediation decisions.​.

Recent findings suggest that as for technical vulnerability data, it alone is insufficient to determine what an organization should fix first. Effective prioritization requires context about the affected asset, its criticality, its role in the business, its data flows and the potential consequences of exploitation. AI can help organizations apply and process this context at scale, but only if the underlying security data is accurate, validated and meaningful.

Sources indicate that for teams already buried in findings, the promise of less noise is appealing. The questions start when we discuss what adopting it involves. How much of their time will we need? Will it work with their existing tools? Will the findings arriving on their desks actually be any better?

Industry observers note that in conversations with customers and prospective clients, these are always understandable concerns. They’ve heard plenty of promises about making security simpler. They want to know how much work it’ll take to get started and how much work it’ll save them afterwards. Sending over a mountain of alerts and wishing the team good luck is a fairly generous interpretation of helping.

It has been revealed that providers need to be clear about who does the work. If we offer validated findings, the validation is our responsibility. It’s important to establish which findings are real and investigate exploitability and assess relevant controls before passing the results to the customer.

According to recent reports, trust is another hurdle. Fewer findings can sound suspicious when the industry has spent years presenting bigger numbers as evidence of a better job. Customers need to see why something has been ruled out or deprioritized, what testing supports that decision and what remains uncertain.

According to recent reports, “AI said so” isn’t an explanation I’d fancy taking into a board meeting. Show the evidence and let people challenge the conclusion.​.

In a recent development, privacy and operational security deserve direct answers. What data will the provider access, and where will it be processed? What testing is permitted, and who approved it? Clear boundaries, documented data handling and named responsibilities help customers assess this service properly. They shouldn’t need a detective to work out what they’re signing up for.

Recent findings suggest that yes, cost always comes up, but the subscription is only part of the calculation. Investigating false positives, chasing findings that can’t be reproduced and passing inconclusive tickets between teams all costs money. Those hours don’t become free because they’re missing from the procurement spreadsheet. Providers must demonstrate the reduction in effort for the client and explain the price they charge.

According to recent reports, the practical route is to start with a defined scope and agree what success looks like with security development and the budget owner. Measure the time spent investigating findings, the quality of evidence reaching developers and how rapidly confirmed risks receive attention. Make implementation manageable, work with existing systems and show the results. That gives each team a reason to support adoption.​​.

Organizations need greater visibility into their attack surface and a continuous understanding of what they’re trying to secure. Continuous assessment and visibility will become increasingly notable as the pace of vulnerability discovery and exploitation accelerates. However, continuous assessment inevitably creates more data, making validation and suppression of irrelevant or low-value findings increasingly important.

In a recent development, organizations should prioritize security approaches that continuously assess the environment while filtering, validating and contextualizing findings before they reach security and development teams. The ultimate objective isn’t to automate every security decision but to ensure that even as the volume of cybersecurity data grows exponentially, organizations can reduce that information to a manageable number of accurate, contextual and actionable risks.​.

In an AI-accelerated threat landscape, the organizations best positioned to respond will be those that can rise with the tide by purifying their cybersecurity data rather than drowning in it.​​.
