Airplane Turbulence Is Extremely Hard to Predict, but Maybe Not for Long

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In the early 1500s, an Italian polymath named Leonardo da Vinci took a good crack at exactly the phenomenon that makes nervous fliers clutch their airplane seats in fear and flight attendants push their carts back to the galleys with haste: turbulence.

Recent findings suggest that da Vinci was fascinated by swirling, chaotic eddies of water, movements so complex that he made hundreds of drawings of his observations. Why, exactly, did water—any fluid, really, be it atmospheric air or blood in the heart—move in that way?

Now the world has ChatGPT and self-driving cars and solar farms, and it still doesn’t exactly know how turbulence works. “It’s the hardest remaining problem in classical physics, and it’s one we need to fly through,” claims Michael Murillo, a professor of theoretical and computational physics at Michigan State University.

Industry observers note that now a bumper crop of data-driven efforts to predict and avoid turbulence could get fliers closer to a smooth flight. Twenty terabytes of data are created by an airplane’s engine in one hour of commercial flying, according to the International Air Transport Association, with more broadcast by airplanes midflight: GPS position, altitude, speed. An “electronic flight bag,” basically a specialized iPad, is equipped with navigation charts and manuals and can transmit its own information, as long as the airplane’s internet connection cooperates. Satellites watch and collect it all.

Industry observers note that “Air traffic has gone up, and communication costs and the costs of putting computers on aircraft have come down,” states Larry Cornman, a physicist with the National Science Foundation’s National Center for Atmospheric Research who has been studying turbulence for decades. “The time is now to work on these things because we can do them.”.

This is good news for aviation safety. More than 200 people were seriously injured on US airplanes in turbulence-related incidents between 2009 and 2023, according to FAA data. Turbulence is also getting worse. Climate change alters the atmospheric conditions around the world’s jet streams, creating destabilizing wind shear. From the cockpit of an airplane, this translates into sudden, clear-air turbulence, difficult to see or predict. Historically, pilots’ only method of avoiding turbulence is heeding the warning of those who accidentally went through it before them, and hoping that nothing had changed.

According to recent reports, but just in the past two years, Cornman’s decades of work on turbulence has been put into the field in ways it couldn’t be before, part of several projects coordinated by the US federal government and the airline industry. One breakthrough came when Cornman and an FAA air traffic controller determined that aircrafts’ federally required broadcasts of their positions, altitude, and speed could be used to spot unexpected turbulence, without requiring latest hardware or software onboard. Researchers can use this crowdsourced, public data to make predictions about when and where turbulence will pop up.

In September, the National Science Foundation confirmed a $30 million award to nine universities to stand up a fresh center dedicated to predicting turbulence—in the air, but also in the charged particles inside a fusion reactor, and in the gas clouds that stretch across space.

It has been revealed that “Turbulence is the reason we don’t have fusion energy yet,” states Murillo, who heads up the new center, called Transformative Explorations in Multi-Physics and Engineering of Scientific Turbulence, or Tempest. The chaotic swirling of superhot plasma inside a reactor allows heat to leak out, preventing the material from reaching the temperatures needed to allow nuclei to fuse. The US federal government wants to get closer to figuring that out.

It has been revealed that “Closer,” because turbulence is impossible to perfectly predict, scientists say. For one thing, turbulence occurs at many different, small scales at the same time. Air swirling around an airplane breaks into eddies, then smaller eddies, and then even smaller eddies, with each interacting with each other—too many variables, happening too quickly, to mathematically account for. For another, tiny changes—wind speed, temperature—can eventually dramatically alter air movement, suddenly altering the turbulence once predicted. To make perfect forecasts, one would need a perfect picture of exactly what’s going on in the atmosphere at all times.

In a recent development, but increasing sophisticated applications of machine learning could allow scientists to get closer to knowing what’s going to happen when, notes Murillo. And while brute, computational force can’t solve the turbulence problem, AI agents might give the effort a boost. OpenAI’s blockbuster announcement this fall that it had resolved the Navier-Stokes problem—equations that are the beginning of all turbulence models—suggests LLMs could help.

“Turbulence has been a problem only human minds could work on; as of this year, that's no longer true,” claims Murillo.

Sources indicate that to make flying better, the aviation industry doesn’t necessarily need to solve turbulence. It needs to get better at knowing what sort of conditions create it and not send airplanes there.

According to recent reports, one manufacturer in Tel Aviv, SkyPath, uses those in-plane iPads and their accelerometers to collect and share crowdsourced data between different airplanes, even if they don’t fly for the same airline. (This sort of dating sharing can be a sensitive topic for competing companies; SkyPath claims it assuages these concerns by ensuring that data is anonymized before it’s shared.) More than 40,000 of these devices have SkyPath installed on them, says CEO Maya Shpak; the company says that several major airlines, including American, use the technology. SkyPath combines that collected data with additional information, including weather forecasts, and uses machine intelligence to make predictions about the future state of the air. The app helps pilots avoid turbulent air and warn passengers and crew members that choppiness is coming, 10 to 15 minutes before it happens.

Industry observers note that one pilot named Eric who uses SkyPath, who asked WIRED not to use his full name because he was not authorized to speak to the media, said the app is a helpful tool that allows him to warn passengers what to expect. His issue is the same as those studying turbulence: One can’t take measurements everywhere all at once.

Industry observers note that “It’s like any crowdsourced software,” says the pilot. “It only works if everyone uses it, and no one wants to use it until everyone else is.” Today, several large operators are using the app, which makes it especially useful in areas with lots of flights, he says.

According to recent reports, cornman, the physicist, wants to keep all these advances in perspective. “No matter what people say, we’re not getting close to 100 percent, 90 percent, 80 percent [predictive accuracy] anytime soon,” he claims. “It’s too complicated a problem.” Smoother skies, if not perfect ones, are ahead.
