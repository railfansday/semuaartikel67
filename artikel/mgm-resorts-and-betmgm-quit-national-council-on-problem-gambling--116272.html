MGM Resorts and BetMGM quit National Council on Problem Gambling over Kalshi membership

MGM Resorts and BetMGM have canceled their memberships in the National Council on Problem Gambling because the group accepted Kalshi. Their exit brings licensed operators into a split over prediction markets that had so far involved regulators and state problem-gambling councils.

The companies’ letter to the council is dated Nov. 18. Stephen Martino, MGM Resorts’ senior vice president and chief compliance officer, signed it alongside Rhea Loney, BetMGM’s chief compliance officer. It went to interim executive director Charles Jeffers and board president Derek Longmeier.

Industry observers note that the companies wrote that they no longer hold the principles the group once stood for. They argued that promoting gambling as a way out of financial trouble runs against responsible gaming principles. The letter also charged that Kalshi disregards state gambling laws when it sells sports event contracts in states whose regulators consider them unlicensed gambling.

Sources indicate that MGM and BetMGM are the first gambling operators to confirm they left specifically over the council’s work with Kalshi, SBC Americas reported. The Ohio Casino Control Commission had already ended its membership. Michigan had won a temporary restraining order against Kalshi on June 29, 2026. The Nevada Council on Problem Gambling and Washington’s Evergreen Council on Problem Gambling also left.

The council announced the deal at the center of the dispute on May 18, 2026. Besides its funding commitment, Kalshi received a seat in the NCPG’s Leadership Circle. That group is made up of top partners that help fund the organization.

Industry observers note that the Nov. 18 letter sets out the main grievances licensed operators hold against prediction markets. Kalshi lets 18-year-olds trade. Most licensed sportsbooks require bettors to be 21. Prediction markets pay little in state taxes, which leaves less money for problem-gambling programs. They also sit outside state self-exclusion programs.

Each of those points touches the core work of a harm-reduction body. The fight over whether prediction markets count as gambling has now reached the main US problem-gambling group.

Recent findings suggest that the NCPG has not backed down. The group describes its prediction-market work as a way to tackle new sources of gambling harm. It claims that work does not endorse particular companies or how they do business. In a Friday statement, the NCPG noted the risks of prediction markets will not go away when organizations refuse to engage. It added that leaving over this work may send a political message but does nothing to prevent gambling-related harm.
