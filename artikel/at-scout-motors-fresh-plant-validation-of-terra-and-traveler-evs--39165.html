At Scout Motors’ Fresh Plant, Validation of Terra and Traveler EVs Has Begun

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, at Scout Motors’ South Carolina Production Center, the automaker’s very first electrified trucks and SUVs are being welded, painted and assembled. The revived American automaker has begun the first validation builds of its upcoming Terra pickup truck and Traveler SUV at the Blythewood plant, but hold your electric horses — final production and customer deliveries are still a little over a year away.

Recent findings suggest that after a period of slow sales, American interest in electric vehicles and hybrids is on the rise, driven by high fuel costs and the steady growth of EV charging infrastructure in 2026. Scout, with its battery-electric and gasoline range-extended powertrains, could have both of those bases covered when it arrives to compete in America’s most popular vehicle classes (trucks and SUVs) in 2028. It’s worth keeping an eye on Scout as it inches toward production.

I donned my hardhat, steel-toe caps and high-vis vest to get an early look inside the plant.

The Volkswagen Group revived the Scout Motors brand (formerly International Scout) in 2022, and in 2024, the public got its first look at the Terra and Traveler concepts. The two were initially revealed as full-electric models with the option to add Scout’s Harvester onboard gasoline generator to boost the range.

However, demand for the extended-range EV configuration was overwhelming — roughly 87% of the 160,000-plus reservation holders selected the Harvester spec over the all-electric version — and Scout pivoted to delivering the EREV first, then following up with the pure battery-electric model later in production.

Both models are expected to start under $60,000, though Scout has not yet confirmed the exact price difference between the Harvester and pure BEV trims.

In early 2024, the automaker broke ground on the Blythewood Production Center, pledging to invest more than $2 billion in the plant and create more than 4,000 local jobs. A year later, Scout declared plans to add a $300 million supplier park to the complex, supporting around 1,000 on-site jobs at parts suppliers.

According to recent reports, today, construction is mostly complete. (Executives told me it’s about 80% to 95% done, depending on how you measure it.) The walls are up, much of the equipment is in place, and the first movement toward full production of the brand’s extended-range EV and, eventually, battery electric trucks and SUVs has begun.

Scout’s manufacturing and production teams have begun building the first validation builds — early-stage preproduction vehicles that allow factory planners to dial in vehicle quality, confirm the accuracy of the engineering and hone the efficiency of the manufacturing and assembly lines.

These aren’t complete vehicles by any stretch, being pulled from the line at various points to check and double-check that the new factory equipment functions properly and that the right parts arrive when and where they’re needed. Executives warned me that, at this point, there would still be inconsistencies as they dial in panel gaps, welds and the application of various coatings, but that Scout expects the process to be dialed in by the time full production starts in late 2027.

I was able to get up close to these validation vehicles, checking out a body-in-white bare chassis in the body shop, seeing various stages of coatings applied to bare metal, and watching workers complete calibration and alignment of lights and sensors in the final assembly area.

Sources indicate that along the way, Scout told me that more than 160 individual parts must come together to form the vehicle’s chassis, a process supported by more than 750 robots and 5,000 weld points. Once up to speed, the factory body shop is designed to produce around 40 vehicles per hour, with pickups and SUVs sharing the same line.

In a recent development, however, while I saw the robotic welding arms and paint sprayers performing their motions to apply color, no vehicles were actually moving through the assembly line. This early in the process, the automaker doesn’t need a continuous flow of validation vehicles, and many of the factory areas — such as the supplier park that will provide the batteries, electric motors and modified VW Group four-cylinder engines for Harvester models — are still under construction.

Additionally, the validation process gives workers hands-on experience, rather than having them learn from mistakes on cars that will reach customers or being confined to a classroom.

According to recent reports, as more parts of the factory complete construction and come online, the validation process will encompass more of the vehicle’s construction until the automaker is ready to start building full customer vehicles. Todd Johnson, Scout’s vice president of manufacturing, tells me that validation will continue into 2027 through multiple preproduction phases starting with the Traveler SUV.

The automaker expects the first customer deliveries of the Traveler Harvester to begin in early 2028, followed by the Terra Harvester and, eventually, fully electric versions of both.
