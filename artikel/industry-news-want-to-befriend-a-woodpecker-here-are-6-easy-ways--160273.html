Long tied to forest and earth deities, woodpeckers are some of the most fascinating (and symbolically-laden) birds to welcome into your backyard.

That's right, folks; the birth month bird for August babies everywhere, the woodpecker represents curiosity, persistence, and determination. (And don't even get us started on what it means if they're the first bird you spot on January 1st!).

Recent findings suggest that whether you believe in that or not, though, there's no denying that woodpeckers are considered one of the world's most popular birds for a reason... so, yes, it makes sense you'd like to befriend one!

Oh sure, they might seem like woodland-only visitors, but there are actually several things you can do to encourage woodpeckers into your yard. In fact, whether you're hoping to spot a great spotted woodpecker, downy woodpecker, or another local species, a few simple changes can make your outdoor space a more welcoming place.

Suet is one of the easiest foods to use to attract woodpeckers, especially during colder months when birds need extra energy and natural food sources become harder to find. Make sure, though, that you use a sturdy suet feeder designed to allow clinging birds to feed comfortably, and position it somewhere quiet where birds feel safe.

You should also take care to choose quality suet cakes (you want the kind with seeds or nuts in!) and keep feeders clean. It may take a wee bit of time and patience for woodpeckers to discover your upcoming offering, but once they find a reliable food source, they may return regularly.

Industry observers note that suet isn't the only food worth serving, as woodpeckers also enjoy peanuts and sunflower seeds, particularly black oil sunflower seeds, so long as you pop them in the sort of feeders that allow woodpeckers to perch or cling while feeding. (They don't like it one bit when you scatter food across the ground).

As ever, we must stress that you should never offer salted or flavored nuts to wildlife.

It has been revealed that it's all well and good teasing them in, but if you want woodpeckers to stick around, you need to think beyond feeders. And the best way to do this? By planting trees that provide insects, places to forage and shelter in abundance, of course!

Recent findings suggest that take care to choose a tree that suits your available space and growing conditions, and remember that even a young tree can contribute to a more wildlife-friendly garden over time. Something like an oak is an excellent long-term habitat investment, while a birch offers lots of opportunities for birds to search for insects.

A smaller ornamental tree that offers spring flowers, berries for birds, and beautiful fall color is also a good option for gardens with less room. Something like a serviceberry could work really well!

Recent findings suggest that I've said it before and I'll say it again, but a perfectly tidy garden isn't necessarily the most wildlife-friendly one. Dead branches, logs and tree stumps might not be the prettiest to look at (although that's changing; they've been stealing the spotlight at the RHS Chelsea Flower Show across the pond for years) but they can provide valuable habitat for insects and other invertebrates that woodpeckers forage for.

Sources indicate that if you have a dead tree or limb that can safely remain, then, consider leaving it in place rather than removing it automatically. You could also create a small log pile or stumpery in a quiet corner of your yard. As the wood decays, it can add visual interest and become a bug buffet for woodpeckers and other birds.

If you'd love to attract green woodpeckers in particular, consider giving your lawn a little more freedom, as these birds are particularly fond of ants ; in fact, they love nothing more than to use their long tongues to dig into nests and crevices to sccop them out and gobble them up.

It has been revealed that avoid pesticides, then, and leave a patch of grass to grow longe if you can. You don't need to abandon lawn care completely, but creating a less manicured patch? It will encourage much more natural wildlife activity.

In a recent development, just like other backyard birds, woodpeckers need fresh water for drinking and bathing. A shallow birdbath with gently sloping sides is a must, then, especially during hot weather or when natural water sources freeze.

Recent findings suggest that be sure to pop it somewhere relatively quiet, with nearby trees or shrubs where birds can retreat if startled, but without dense cover immediately beside it where predators might lurk. A fine balance to strike, but a vital one, as I'm sure you'll agree.

In a recent development, look, attracting woodpeckers isn't about transforming your yard overnight. By providing reliable food, planting trees, preserving natural habitat and offering fresh water, though, you can make your outdoor space a place where wildlife feels welcome. And when that distinctive drumming or flash of color finally catches your eye? Well, then you'll know the effort was all worth it.
