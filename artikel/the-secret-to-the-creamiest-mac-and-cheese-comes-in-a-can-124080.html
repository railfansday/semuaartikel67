In a recent development, making a truly excellent bowl of mac and cheese shouldn't take an entire afternoon. Here's the one ingredient I rely on to get the creamiest cheese sauce every time.

In a recent development, I’ve made enough macaroni and cheese over the years to have developed some fairly strong opinions about it. Having two kids has given me plenty of opportunities to experiment, from throwing together the familiar blue box to baking versions that require several kinds of cheese and a considerable amount of attention. I appreciate a proper cheese sauce as much as anyone, but there are evenings when dirtying another saucepan to make a roux and standing over the stove with a whisk feels like an unreasonable amount of ceremony for a bowl of macaroni.

Which is why I’m particularly fond of a method that requires just three ingredients: pasta, grated cheese and evaporated milk. It produces an exceptionally creamy mac and cheese without butter, flour or any of the usual fuss, and it’s so simple that even boxed mac begins to look complicated.

Sources indicate that evaporated milk is regular milk that’s been heated until about 60% of its water is gone. The result is thicker and more concentrated than ordinary milk, with a richness that makes it especially useful for cheese sauces.

More importantly, those concentrated milk proteins help the cheese melt into a smooth, cohesive sauce. Ordinarily, adding cheese directly to hot liquid can be a temperamental business. The fat may separate, the proteins can tighten up, and the sauce can become grainy instead of silky. Evaporated milk helps keep everything together, doing much of the work that a traditional flour-and-butter roux would otherwise accomplish.

Lindsay Parrill For Taste Of Home.

Sources indicate that one of the things I like most about this method is how little precision it demands. If you’re cooking a pound of macaroni, start with a 12-ounce can of evaporated milk and roughly 12 to 16 ounces of grated cheese. Sharp cheddar provides plenty of flavor, while Monterey Jack or fontina will give you a smooth melt. I generally prefer a combination, but there’s no reason to make a special trip to the store if you already have a perfectly good block of cheese in the refrigerator.

Industry observers note that I do recommend grating the cheese yourself, since the anti-caking ingredients in bagged shredded cheese can keep it from melting smoothly. Beyond that, there’s plenty of room for flexibility. There’s no need to measure everything down to the last ounce, which is part of what makes this method so wonderful.

Lindsay Parrill For Taste Of Home.

Sources indicate that boil the pasta in generously salted water as you normally would, then strain it and return it to the pot, reserving about a cup of pasta water. Add the cheese and evaporated milk, warming them gently over low heat. Keep stirring until everything melts into a smooth sauce, adding splashes of pasta water as you go until it reaches your desired consistency.

The same trick works beautifully in more elaborate recipes, whether you’re making a baked southern mac and cheese, a major pan of potluck mac and cheese or a comforting crockpot mac and cheese. All have their merits, but for an ordinary weeknight, I rather like knowing that one pot, three ingredients, and about as much time as it takes to boil pasta are all I need.
