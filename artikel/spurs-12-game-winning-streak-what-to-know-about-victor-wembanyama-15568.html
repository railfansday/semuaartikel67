Spurs' 12-game winning streak: What to know about Victor Wembanyama and the NBA’s hottest team

The NBA's hottest team resides in South Texas. After knocking off the Utah Jazz in Salt Lake City on Sunday night, the San Antonio Spurs enter a upcoming week riding a 12-game winning streak — the franchise's longest in more than a decade. Last season featured only two streaks longer than the Spurs' dominant dozen: a 16-game early-season jaunt by the Oklahoma City Thunder (which, as you might remember, these Spurs snapped in the NBA Cup semifinals) and a 13-game run by the Detroit Pistons … who, as luck would have it, will welcome the Spurs to the Motor City on Tuesday night.

Industry observers note that when a team goes on a winning streak this long, there's plenty of credit to go around. Let's highlight 12 things worth knowing about the Spurs' stampede to the top of the Western Conference — one for each win in the streak! — starting with … I mean, come on, where else can you start?

After the Latest York Knicks ended his phenomenal third season just short of a championship, Wembanyama entered the summer with a bitter taste in his mouth … and he seems to have entered the new campaign intent on washing it out by devouring everything in his path.

Wembanyama enters Tuesday's action third in the NBA in scoring (a career-high 28.6 points per game) and fifth in rebounding (11.6 caroms a night) while, for the fourth straight season, leading the league in blocked shots (4.3 swats per contest). He hasn't forsaken the 3-point arc, but he's taking a higher share of his shots than ever directly at the rim, and he's converting them at a predictably elite rate, shooting 79.5% in the restricted area — tops among players to take at least 50 point-blank shots.

Recent findings suggest that (Third on that list, by the way? Friggin' Dylan Harper. Don't worry: We'll get back to him.).

Big Vic is averaging — averaging! — 4.64 dunks per game. That's nearly two more flushes per game than last season (2.78), which was already a career high. As tactics go, "Let's get the biggest guy as close to the basket as possible and let him just put the ball into it" seems like a pretty smart strategy, and the Spurs have been reaping the benefits of it, scoring a scorching 126.6 points per 100 non-garbage-time possessions in Wemby's minutes, according to Cleaning the Glass — a mark that would rank head-and-shoulders above the Los Angeles Lakers' league-leading rate of offensive efficiency thus far.

In a recent development, when it comes to "season-opening heaters," most of the attention has gone to the man most responsible for that No. 1-ranked Lakers offense, Luka Dončić — and understandably so. It's worth noting, though, that the big fella is right now on pace to shatter the all-time record for player efficiency rating while leading the NBA in win shares, value over replacement player and total plus-minus; the Spurs have outscored opponents by 257 points in Wemby's 417 minutes, equivalent to a staggering 29.5 points-per-100.

Watch Wembanyama on a nightly basis — not just the "did he just finger-roll from past the free-throw line?!?" highlights, but the possession after possession where he completely warps the opposition's defense and defangs its offense just by standing there — and you come away feeling like the 22-year-old's control of the game is, or at least can be, total.

It has been revealed that "Opponents can't really put me in a situation where I can't answer," Wembanyama recently told reporters. "So now all I have to do is not beat myself.".

For more than three weeks, he hasn't — and, in a related story, nobody's beaten the Spurs, either.

All right, take it easy there, Thanos. (Not sure if you've heard, but it's all about Dr. Doom right now.).

Industry observers note that generally speaking, if you're in the top 10 on both ends of the floor, you can consider yourself a contender; if you're in the top five on both ends, you're in the inner circle. San Antonio enters Tuesday's action ranked second in the NBA in points scored and allowed per possession, according to Cleaning the Glass.

According to recent reports, where'd the Spurs finish last year, you might ask? Oh, I'm so glad you did. They were … third in points scored per possession, and third in points allowed per possession. If that was good enough to get them to the Finals, maybe bumping up to second both ways will be enough to get them to the top of the mountain this time?

San Antonio has a point differential of +180 for the season — the 21st-highest total ever through the first 14 games of a campaign, according to Stathead. Of the 20 teams above these Spurs, 17 made at least the conference finals; eight of them won it all.

Recent findings suggest that here's another: The Spurs are blitzing their opponents by 15.2 points per 100 non-garbage-time possessions thus far — the best net rating in the sport. Since the 2003-04 season, only four teams have finished a season outscoring the opposition by more than 12 points-per-100. All four of them — the 2007-08 Boston Celtics, 2014-15 and 2016-17 Golden State Warriors, and the 2024-25 Thunder — went on to win the NBA championship.

Sources indicate that according to Jared Dubin's adjusted efficiency metrics at Last Night in Basketball, which measure how much better or worse a team's offense and defense were than the league's average unit in that season, the 2026-27 Spurs presently have a net rating 11.4 points-per-100 better than league average. That ranks third in Dubin's database, which goes all the way back to the ABA-NBA merger in 1976 — behind only Michael Jordan's 1995-96 Chicago Bulls (who won the title) … and this year's Boston Celtics, who (Monday's loss to the Orlando Magic notwithstanding) have been pretty awesome this year.

According to recent reports, san Antonio's average margin of victory thus far is 12.86 points per game. Only one team has ever finished a full season higher: the '24-25 Thunder (+12.87) … who, again, won the title.

According to Basketball-Reference's Simple Rating System — a metric "that takes into account average point differential and strength of schedule" — this year's Spurs are 12.75 points better than league average. That's on pace to be the highest SRS rating in Basketball-Reference's database, ahead of teams like the '24-25 Thunder, 1970-71 Milwaukee Bucks, '95-96 Bulls and 1971-72 Los Angeles Lakers — all of whom (say it with me) won the title.

It's early, obviously, and 14 games is too small a sample to draw any definitive conclusions. This much, though, seems clear. If folks start digging into the historical record in search of points of comparison to what you're doing, and they keep coming up with teams that won the NBA championship? You're probably doing something right.

The backcourt logjam that some pundits anticipated last season never really materialized in San Antonio. De'Aaron Fox and Stephon Castle started; rookie Harper mostly came off the bench; the trio shared the floor for just 25 minutes during the regular season and 44 minutes in the playoffs, and the Spurs made it all the way to the NBA Finals. Not actually a problem, right?

It has been revealed that well, that seemed less likely to be the case this season, after Harper authored a breakout performance in his first postseason — and followed it up by making it pretty clear that he wasn't particularly interested in continuing to come off the bench. That led Spurs head coach Mitch Johnson to decide to open this season with all three of his high-octane guards in the starting five, backstopped by Wembanyama with Devin Vassell operating as an ostensible small-ball power forward. The early returns have been explosive:.

In a recent development, the Spurs have hammered opponents by 110 points in 141 minutes with Fox, Castle and Harper sharing the floor this season, according to PBP Stats. They're scoring and defending at league-best rates in those minutes … and yes, a lot of that stems from the fact that Wembanyama has been out there for every single one of them. It's also working, though, because the guards are each finding ways to get in where they fit in.

Sources indicate that they're flying up the court, with more than 23% of San Antonio's offensive possessions coming in transition in the three-guard lineup, and those units scoring at near-league-best levels on the break. Castle and Harper are almost exclusively taking shots at the rim or from beyond the arc in those minutes, getting to either catch-and-shoot or attack downhill after an initial action bends the defense.

Sources indicate that fox and Castle, both questionable 3-point shooters, have both been knocking down well over 40% of their triples in these alignments. When the pick-your-poison wheel spins to a center guarding Castle, the Spurs have shown their work in developing counters to that scheme.

According to recent reports, and while conventional wisdom would warn you against the defensive drawbacks of playing small, with no player listed taller than 6-foot-6 at the 1 through 4 spots, those lineups have rebounded fine, haven't given up a ton of opportunities at the rim, and have yet to be significantly exploited for their lack of size on the perimeter. (It seems that having that French dude back there goes a long way toward bucking conventional wisdom.).

"I understand the narrative around it. I always land on the same reasoning at the very end of it," Johnson told reporters about starting the three guards during the preseason. "They're three of our better players. [...] I think they can dribble, pass, shoot, defend, make winning plays.".

According to recent reports, whether they'll continue to do so in this alignment remains to be seen; when offseason signing/veteran power forward Tobias Harris heals up from the calf injury that has cost him the opening month of the season, Johnson will likely have a difficult decision to make. Then again, if the not-so-small-ball Spurs keep blowing everybody's doors off in this look … maybe he won't.

Sources indicate that "The biggest thing is, I've got handlers all around me — guys that we want to put the ball in their hands and pick-and-roll around me," Wembanyama said. "That makes things easier.".

Why mess with easier, you know?

Since opening the season with losses to the Thunder and Rockets, the Spurs have gotten the opportunity to get right with a pretty friendly stretch of road.

According to recent reports, they've had three games against the Jazz, two games against the Kings, and matchups with the Suns and Bulls. They've also caught the Timberwolves without Anthony Edwards or Rudy Gobert, the Mavericks without Cooper Flagg or Kyrie Irving, the Trail Blazers without Ja Morant, and the Warriors right after Stephen Curry went down.

Industry observers note that add it all up, and the Spurs' season-opening slate ranks among the softest in the NBA, according to multiple strength-of-schedule metrics. San Antonio doesn't have to apologize for that, of course. You play the teams that are in front of you, with whoever's in uniform for them when you show up. We've seen plenty of teams fail to capitalize on the opportunity to take down a team at less than full strength. (Looking at you, Mavs.).

Besides, when presented with matchups against shorthanded or sorry opposition, the Spurs have done what good teams are supposed to do: namely, win all of those games, with most of those victories coming by double digits. It's just something to keep an eye on, particularly with a tough opponent like Detroit up next … before three more against the Clippers, Pelicans and Kings. (The games to really keep an eye on? November 27 against Luka and the Lakers, and November 29 against that damn good Celtics team.).

According to recent reports, fox got raked over the coals this summer for his underperformance in the NBA Finals. (Which, it's worth remembering, came with him nursing a high ankle sprain that likely would've sidelined him for weeks had it been the regular season.) His struggles under the brightest lights, combined with Harper's stellar showing in his maiden postseason voyage, primed the pump for the shift to the three-guard lineup, and led to no shortage of calls for the veteran triggerman to cede the stage — and his spot in the Spurs' pecking order — to the rising sophomore.

Well, Fox is still here … and he's kind of threading the needle.

Fox has taken a clear step back in the Spurs' offensive hierarchy, averaging fewer minutes and points per game than he has since his rookie season, and posting far and away the lowest usage rate, time of possession and average number of touches of his 10-year career. He's taking seven fewer field goal attempts per 100 possessions than he did last year.

When Fox shares the floor with Harper and Castle, he's finishing just 15.7% of San Antonio's offensive possessions with a shot attempt, foul drawn or turnover — the kind of usage rate more commonly associated with a complementary player like Isaiah Hartenstein, Toumani Camara or Christian Braun than with a two-time All-Star who has averaged 21 points per game in his career. He's performing well in that reduced role, though.

Fox more frequently takes high-percentage shots — 32% of his attempts are coming at the rim, his highest rate in six years, while corner 3s account for 12% of his shots, a career high — and has almost totally excised long 2-pointers from his repertoire. That, in turn, has led to more efficient scoring. He enters Tuesday's action toting a .551 effective field goal percentage (which accounts for 3-pointers being worth more than 2-pointers) and a .621 true shooting percentage (which factors in 2-point, 3-point and free-throw accuracy) — both of which are by far the highest marks of his career.

He has paired that leaner shot diet with a voracious appetite for freebies, too drawing shooting fouls on more than 19% of his field-goal attempts — again, a career high. In fact, among 153 players who have logged at least 300 minutes this season, Fox's .496 free-throw attempt rate is the 11th-highest in the NBA, just ahead of free-throw-magnet massive men Joel Embiid and Giannis Antetokounmpo.

Sources indicate that pair that just-the-facts-ma'am scoring game with his ongoing facilitation — a team-high 5.8 assists per game, including 33 to Wemby, making them the third-most-frequent assist combo in the NBA thus far — and Fox has managed to continue providing value while still making room for the evolution of the young talent around him. Not bad.

Industry observers note that for fans of a certain age, thinking of the Spurs might evoke images of patient, methodical offensive possessions — all entry passes to Tim Duncan on the low block or swing-swing symphonies of "good-to-great" ball movement, where the pace of their play is secondary to the precision of their movements. These aren't your daddy's Spurs, though; these dudes have a predilection toward moving fast and breaking things. Like, for example, you.

It has been revealed that in terms of overall pace of play, the Spurs rank just 24th in the NBA, with their average game featuring about 98.8 possessions, according to NBA Advanced Stats. That's due in large part to their defense, though; San Antonio has the longest average defensive possession in the NBA this season, according to PBP Stats. That stands to reason: When you've got to try to figure out how to crowbar open a crevice to attack with Wembanyama menacing along the back line while the likes of Castle and Harper pressure the ball up top, it might take you a little while.

Once the Spurs get the ball, though, they hit the gas. San Antonio has the fifth-fastest average offensive possession this season, and is tied for the fifth-quickest average time to shot, according to Inpredictable.

As noted by the great John Schuhmann, the Spurs lead the NBA in both transition points scored and allowed per game, outscoring opponents by 13 points a night on the break — a massive gap. With their ability to force turnovers, finish possessions with rebounds and advance the ball to all those jet-fueled guards, more than 19% of their offensive possessions come in transition, according to Cleaning the Glass; that's the most in the league. But thanks to their ability to protect the defensive glass, take care of the ball and sprint back, fewer than 12% of their defensive possessions come in transition … which is also No. 1 in the league.

Since at least the start of the 2003-04 season, no team has ever finished the year No. 1 in the league at both generating and preventing transition opportunities. Just another way these Spurs are on pace (ha!) to dispense with conventional wisdom.

Winning the possession battle isn't exactly a revolutionary concept; coaches have been preaching since time immemorial the importance of taking care of the ball on offense and finishing possessions on defense. It's become an increasingly large point of emphasis in recent years, though, as front offices and coaching staffs engaged in the perpetual pursuit of optimization, hunting for every advantage on both ends of the floor. As it turns out, "making sure we get more chances to shoot and score than the other guys" is a pretty solid way to approach things!

The Spurs were pretty good at that last season. When you factor in how many offensive rebounds they pulled in against how many they gave up, how many free-throw attempts they racked up versus how many they conceded, and how many times they turned the ball over compared with how many times they forced opponents to cough it up, San Antonio generated 2.2 more offensive possessions than its opponents per game during the regular season — a good number, but not quite in the upper echelons of the league, like the team that knocked them off in the Finals.

This season, though, the Spurs have tightened the screws virtually across the board. They're grabbing an even higher share of their own missed shots, turning the ball over at the NBA's lowest rate and getting to the free-throw line more frequently, while also forcing more turnovers and curbing their foul rate. Add it up, and through 14 games, San Antonio is generating 7.2 more possessions per game than its opponents — a massive leap over last season, and the No. 1 mark in the league thus far.

Sources indicate that make sure you take more shots on goal than the other guys and you've got a great shot at winning a lot more often than you lose — especially when you put the ball in the hands of dudes who can make those shots. Dudes like ….

… the 20-year-old sophomore, whose combination of throwback cool and downhill explosiveness just keeps leaping off the screen pretty much every time you see him.

Harper has responded to his elevation into the starting lineup by continuing to ball out. His usage, free-throw attempt rate and scoring efficiency are all up; his turnover rate is down. Some of that, obviously, comes from more frequently sharing the floor with multiple other high-level ball-handlers, but still: pretty nice to have the guy who's second on your team in touches only coughing it up 8% of the time, one of the lowest rates of any guard in the league.

According to recent reports, coming off that sensational rookie season, Harper is pretty firmly establishing himself as one of the best players in the league when it comes to getting into the paint and finishing on the interior, shooting a frankly bananas 78.6% in the restricted area — better than high-efficiency bigs like Domantas Sabonis, Jarrett Allen, Walker Kessler and Giannis Antetokounmpo. Decent company for a 6-foot-5 sophomore.

According to recent reports, through 14 games, Harper is averaging 18.3 points, 5.5 rebounds, 4.7 assists and 1.0 steals in 31.5 minutes per game, shooting 55% inside the arc, 34% from long distance and 87% from the charity stripe. The list of players to average 18-4-4 for a full season by their age-20 season is just six names long: Magic Johnson, LeBron James, Tyreke Evans, Dončić, LaMelo Ball and Cooper Flagg. Of that group, only Magic shot at least 50% from the field, as Harper is right now.

There's a 7-foot-4 caveat to that, of course. With the exception of rookie Magic playing alongside MVP Kareem Abdul-Jabbar, those other preternatural playmakers didn't have a gravitational force like Wemby pulling attention away from them and opening up opportunities to slash, score and create. But opportunities only matter insofar as you can capitalize on them, and Harper continues to make the most out of his privileged position, perpetually pressuring the defense and looking for all the world like an All-Star in the making — sooner rather than later.

It's not all beer and Skittles in San Antonio, though. As lights-out as the newly introduced-look starting lineup has been, moving Harper into the first five has led to some predictable ripple effects in the second unit. The Spurs' reserve corps has a negative net rating for the season, and has — as you might expect — been significantly worse in the minutes when Luke Kornet checks in for Wembanyama, struggling to score even with at least one of Fox, Castle or Harper on the floor.

Industry observers note that some of those struggles might be mitigated by the introduction of Harris, whom the Spurs signed to a two-year, $31 million contract in free agency, and who's yet to play after suffering a left calf strain during training camp. But Johnson will have to shuffle his rotation to make space for the veteran forward. What impact might that have on the minutes and productivity of the likes of Julian Champagnie, whose quick-trigger 3-point shooting has proven so vital for this iteration of the Spurs? Or Keldon Johnson, the reigning Sixth Man of the Year? Or Harrison Barnes, one of just two Spurs (along with Champagnie) knocking down at least 40% of his triples? Or second-year power forward Carter Bryant, whose ongoing development is a clear priority for San Antonio's future aims?

It has been revealed that if Harris isn't able to hit the ground running — specifically by spacing the floor at a high level, as he did for Cade Cunningham in Detroit last season — it could throw some sand into the gears of a Spurs offense that has been humming despite not generating a ton from beyond the arc. San Antonio ranks just 26th in the league in the share of its shots that come from beyond the arc, and right around league-average in 3-point shooting accuracy.

Industry observers note that that lagging 3-point attempt rate clearly hasn't hampered the offense's effectiveness thus far; some of it stems from the fact that the Spurs hammer the rim as frequently and effectively as nearly any team in the league. After finishing above league-average in 3-point attempt rate the last three seasons, though, you'd imagine Johnson would prefer to find ways to get a few more up — if for no other reason than to be prepared to have multiple styles to play when the postseason comes around and defensive game looks prioritize clogging the paint to take away Wembanyama's rolls to the rim and the three-headed guard monster's driving lanes to the basket.

If opposing defenses are able to congest the interior and force the Spurs to sing for their supper from outside, it'll bring to the forefront perhaps the most significant matter carrying over from last season: whether they have enough bankable 3-point shooting.

In a recent development, as it stands, the entire starting five — Wembanyama, Fox, Castle, Harper and Vassell — currently sits below 35% from deep for the season. Reigning Sixth Man of the Year Johnson is at 22.7%; erstwhile unicorn-turned-indoor cat Kornet hasn't attempted a long ball since April of 2025. The Spurs can make a lot of magic happen in tight confines, thanks to the size and brilliance of Wembanyama, and the speed, skill and force of their guards. If hardly anybody in the rotation can make a jumper when they need to, though, problems will arise.

It has been revealed that those issues — the bench minutes, the shooting, etc. — are not exactly surprises to the Spurs. They're well aware that they've got room to improve.

"If we felt like we're the best version of ourselves at this point in the season, we'll be in trouble," Keldon Johnson recently told reporters.

It has been revealed that as excellent as their offense has been, the Spurs have actually underperformed their expected shot quality by the league's fifth-largest amount, according to Synergy Sports Technology's tracking — and all four teams above them in that ranking (Charlotte, Milwaukee, Utah, Chicago) have been terrible, without the talent to meaningfully bridge the gap between expectation and reality. That's certainly not the case in San Antonio.

If Wembanyama (35% from deep last season, 32% this season), Vassell (38% last season, 31% this season) and Johnson (36% last season, 23% this season) tick up toward their previous marks, and any of Fox, Harper or Castle nudge up toward league-average on their looks, this Spurs offense might have another level to reach. Honestly, it might even if only one of those guys keeps the arrow pointed north. You can probably guess which one.

It has been revealed that "I can still do more," Wembanyama told reporters after his 52-point masterpiece against the Mavericks. "I mean, my shots weren't falling tonight and that forces me into a style of play that is exhausting. I can do it. I mean, the proof is here. I can do it, but I can be much better if my shots fall. I know it's just a matter of time.".

If that happens — if the shots start to fall, the offense soars and the defense remains elite — then it might just be a matter of time before the Spurs are hoisting the Larry O'Brien Championship Trophy, too.
