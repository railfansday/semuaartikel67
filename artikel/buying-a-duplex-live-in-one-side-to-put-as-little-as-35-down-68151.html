Buying a Duplex? Live in One Side to Put as Little as 3.5% Down

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that lenders treat a duplex you live in as your home, so you can finance it with as little as 3.5% down on a government-insured FHA loan or 5% on a conventional loan. Buy one only if the other side’s rent, minus a quarter for repairs and empty months, leaves you paying less than a similar apartment rents for.

It has been revealed that buy the same duplex as a rental you won’t live in, and an FHA loan, which the Federal Housing Administration insures, is off the table. A conventional loan, one without government insurance, then takes 25% down.

It has been revealed that FHA’s 3.5% minimum needs a credit score of 580 or higher, and scores from 500 to 579 need 10% down. Freddie Mac, which buys many conventional loans from lenders, allows 5% down when its automated system approves the loan and the loan stays within a standard size limit, and 15% otherwise. Both loans require you to move in within 60 days and live there at least a year, after which you can move out and rent both sides.

Either way, the lender counts 75% of the rent the other side is expected to bring when it decides whether you can afford the payment. Fannie Mae, the other significant buyer, counts it only if you already pay rent or own a home.

Say you put $12,600, or 3.5%, down on a $360,000 duplex, and your full payment, with taxes and insurance that covers the rented side, is $3,000 a month. The other side rents for $1,900, and a similar apartment would rent for $2,000. The tenant’s rent leaves you $1,100 to pay. Set aside a quarter of that rent, $475, for repairs and months the unit sits empty, and your side costs $1,575, or $425 less than the $2,000 apartment. A gap like that makes the duplex worth buying if you’re ready to live next to your tenant.

Recent findings suggest that you’ll likely share a yard with your tenant, screen applicants, and take repair calls yourself. A duplex also draws fewer buyers than a single-family home when you sell. If those trade-offs outweigh the savings, there are other ways to cut your housing cost by renting out part of your home, from a spare room to a basement unit.
