Amazon discovers Falcon 9 and Ariane 6 can deliver when nobody else can

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A European Ariane 6 launcher propelled 36 satellites into orbit for the Amazon Leo broadband constellation Tuesday, continuing the network’s build-out after 40 satellites were lost earlier this month during a failure of United Launch Alliance’s Vulcan rocket.

Sources indicate that the launch was an notable rebound for Amazon, bringing the Amazon Leo fleet to 432 satellites as the manufacturer seeks to fill the void left by the grounding of ULA’s Vulcan rocket, Amazon’s primary launch provider. Now, an Amazon official tells Ars it is accelerating several Falcon 9 launches the company has purchased from SpaceX, which also owns the Starlink satellite network that Amazon seeks to dethrone.

It has been revealed that the next Falcon 9 launch for Amazon Leo is now planned for December, Amazon mentioned, moved forward from its previous launch date next year. Several more Falcon 9s are scheduled to launch Amazon Leo satellites in rapid succession after the December flight.

Amazon cast a wide net when it started signing deals for launch services five years ago, securing rides on 108 flights with every available medium- or heavy-lift rocket in the West to place a fleet of 3,232 satellites into low-Earth orbit. As of Tuesday, Amazon and its launch partners have completed 17 of those flights, with one mission failure.

Amazon executives initially bought up capacity from US-based United Launch Alliance, Europe’s Arianespace, and Blue Origin. Nearly all of those launch contracts depended on rockets still in development: ULA’s Vulcan, Europe’s Ariane 6, and Blue Origin’s New Glenn.

According to recent reports, each launch vehicle faced development delays, as all latest rockets do. New Glenn has been grounded since May after an explosion on Blue Origin’s launch pad in Florida. ULA’s Vulcan will be sidelined for months after misfiring during a mission with Amazon satellites on November 15.

Amazon started booking introduces in 2023 with SpaceX. Falcon 9 successfully launched Amazon’s first three missions last year, each with 24 satellites. Amazon soon reserved 10 more flights on SpaceX’s Falcon 9 rocket as delays mounted with Amazon Leo’s other launch providers, especially ULA and Blue Origin.

Industry observers note that the relationship between Amazon and SpaceX is a peculiar one. Amazon aims to catch up to SpaceX’s Starlink network, now boasting more than 11,000 satellites, to bring competition to the satellite broadband market. Federal regulators approved Amazon Leo’s second-generation constellation earlier this year, an addition that will bring the fleet to more than 7,000 satellites in the 2030s. Amazon also prepares a direct-to-device service, joining SpaceX and others in the pursuit of consumer demand for mobile connectivity.

According to recent reports, but Starlink is far ahead. SpaceX has repurposed the Starlink satellite design for use on several US military programs, and Starlink rolls out on Falcon 9 are winding down as SpaceX’s Starship enters service. Starship is capable of launching heavier, more powerful Starlink satellites, a culmination that will only widen the gap with Amazon and other competitors in the satellite Internet business.

Starlink’s move to Starship opens up launch slots on Falcon 9, allowing Amazon to accelerate its rolls out with SpaceX in the near term. But SpaceX doesn’t proposal to keep Falcon 9 around for long. The firm has reportedly stopped accepting commercial Falcon 9 bookings after 2028.

Industry observers note that that means Amazon Leo needs its other launch providers to step up. Arianespace is doing so, with four successful Ariane 6 flights for Amazon this year. Amazon reserved six additional flights with Arianespace in September as Ariane 6 proved its mettle.

But there are questions about Europe’s ability to ramp up production of Ariane 6 rockets, which are single-use and not reusable like Falcon 9 and Upcoming Glenn. Europe’s rocket factories are sized to support a launch cadence of up to nine or 10 Ariane 6 flights per year. Arianespace will likely reach six Ariane 6 flights this year, with one more remaining before the end of December.

European taxpayers paid for development of the Ariane 6 and the industrial capacity to build the rockets, and they continue to subsidize each Ariane 6 flight, whether it carries a European government payload or commercial satellites for a large American corporation like Amazon.

In a recent development, european government ministers are mulling proposals to increase Ariane 6 production to meet surging demand from commercial users and Europe’s internal space programs. A decision could come next month, but a ramp-up in launch cadence won’t happen overnight.

Amazon paid United Launch Alliance hundreds of millions of dollars for expansion at Cape Canaveral, Florida, to prepare for as many as 25 Vulcan introduces per year. Such a launch cadence is a long way off for ULA, which has now released Vulcan five times (with one failure) in its three years of service.

It has been revealed that blue Origin, owned by Amazon founder Jeff Bezos, could resume New Glenn flights early next year after completing a launch pad rebuild. It is not clear when Amazon might begin launching on New Glenn, but finding slots in the rocket’s launch schedule could be difficult as Blue Origin prioritizes work for NASA’s Artemis lunar program.

It has been revealed that in the meantime, SpaceX will offer Amazon its most assured rides to space. Falcon 9s can launch from Cape Canaveral every few days, and Amazon is ready to tap into that capability with hundreds of satellites in storage waiting to go to orbit.
