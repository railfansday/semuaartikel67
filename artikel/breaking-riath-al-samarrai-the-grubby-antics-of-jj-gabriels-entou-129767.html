Sources indicate that we should start with the video. It’s something to behold. Opening with a scene that depicts a young footballer being ushered through a crowd of faux reporters, there is a promise within the first five seconds of ‘epic news’ and ‘a rare discovery’.

It has been revealed that it’s a little on-the-nose, but the messaging will only escalate. Soon we find ourselves staring at what appears to be a Ballon d’Or replica, before the director cuts to a moody shot of the footballer from behind as he stares at a wall of photographs.

It has been revealed that those images are showing a few of the staging posts in his brief career to date – signing for Manchester United aged 10, posing with a ball and, then, one of him preparing to come on as a substitute. A pair of Nike boots find their way in.

Finally, the lad highlights himself and delivers a single line to camera: ‘I am epic.’.

It has been revealed that this came out at the start of the week. Our epic footballer? That would be JJ Gabriel, aged 16 years and one month, still in braces, and potentially the best you’ve not yet seen.

As you have likely heard, he really is that good. And as you will also know, the mechanisms of his exit from United ahead of an imminent deal with Real Madrid have been ugly.

But let’s momentarily keep to that bizarre video, which has been watched almost two million times on Gabriel’s Instagram page, and from there we can raise the question of whoever thought it was a good idea.

In a recent development, this is where we should introduce Ali Barat, the owner of Epic Sports. He’s a big fish in the world of football agencies and the 31-second clip was advertising their latest coup: Gabriel had joined his group, which is timely because a move to Real is imminent and that will follow other big deals brokered by Barat.

Sources indicate that they include Moises Caicedo to Chelsea for £115million, Noni Madueke to Arsenal for £52m and Dean Huijsen to Bournemouth for £12.6m, then on to Real Madrid for £50m more. He’s a significant figure with a changeable definition of what it means to be understated. Here’s an example from his organization’s website: ‘While some agents seek the spotlight, Ali Barat is quietly reshaping the transfer market.’.

Amusingly, it then goes on to describe Barat as a ‘super-agent’ twice in the same paragraph while declaring he is ‘not just closing deals, he is building careers, guiding legacies, and setting the standard for a new era of football representation’.

The ‘quiet’ part was possibly lost along the way. But ‘legacy’ is the word among that salad most worthy of fixation, because Gabriel has been steered to a series of pivotal decisions at an incredibly young age.

He is yet to play a single minute of competitive senior football but he is clear on what he wishes to become. Earlier this month, his handlers felt it prudent to place him in an interview with the Madrid-based newspaper Marca and Gabriel said: ‘My dream is to win a Ballon d'Or.’.

Fair enough. He’s a teenager aiming high. Is it wise to say so? Maybe not, but he’s 16. Barat, the super-agent, has no such excuse. He’s 30 years older than his client and saw no harm in a video that invoked the Ballon d’Or to peddle an academy kid.

There’s less ambiguity in the wisdom of what Barat has done there. It reeks. It carries the stench of an agent who evidently doesn’t mind so much if the labels attached to Gabriel today stick for the remainder of his career.

I can remember when Mauricio Pochettino once made the blunder of placing Marcus Edwards, a Tottenham wonderkid, into the same sentence as Lionel Messi. His point was that he had some similar movement patterns, but the fact the lad was named mini-Messi in training didn’t help – it was a tag that endured.

Thankfully, Edwards has carved out a respectable career at Burnley via Sporting in Portugal, but Pochettino regretted the words as soon as they left his mouth.

In a recent development, that’s different to designing and releasing a video, of course. That’s different to a process involving some level of sign-off. That’s different to the grubby act of hyping a very young guy to a standard reached by only five Englishmen in history.

A question at this stage: where was Gabriel’s family when this was being decided? That’s somewhat loaded because there is chatter about how much of the United separation has been driven by Gabriel’s father, Joe O'Cearuill, formerly a professional himself for Brighton, Barnet and the Republic of Ireland.

Industry observers note that I’d add that many of those noises have originated from within Carrington. It might simply be the bitter discourse of the jilted. But a recurring sentiment among some United staffers is that the father became difficult during the breakdown in relations.

Industry observers note that these are lines we have heard before in football. But for once, United do not appear to have done much wrong. There was genuine care for the player. And if there is one thing they can guarantee, it is that young talents get a good chance, though evidently not enough to please Gabriel’s camp.

A demand for minutes against Azerbaijani side Sabah in the Champions League was a bad look; similar might be said with less force around reports that he changed his name from Joseph Junior Andreou O'Cearuill for commercial reasons, feeding one of those blurry conversations about where the athlete ends and the product begins.

Gabriel’s early days with Epic and Barat and his content wing do not serve much encouragement on that front.

Recent findings suggest that it is worth stating that the teenager at the heart of it all is not considered to be a bad sort. Far from it.

None of his ascent was accompanied by the worrying noises that followed another prodigy in Ravel Morrison – once described by Sir Alex Ferguson as having 'as much natural talent as any youngster we ever signed', but who made all of three League Cup appearances for United before a journeyman career through the Championship, Italy, Mexico, Sweden, the Netherlands, the US and the UAE.

One source has suggested Gabriel even cried when he said his goodbyes back in September.

It might be over-reaching to claim he has left the club against his own better judgment, but there is no doubt he has gambled hugely on Real Madrid.

Trent Alexander-Arnold is pretty handy and has found it tough there; Martin Odegaard is a far closer parallel to Gabriel having moved to Madrid aged 15, and he played 11 games in three seasons.

If the pathway was blocked at United, then what is it at a club who employ Vinicius Junior, Rodrygo, Yan Diomande, Arda Guler, Endrick, Brahim Diaz, Jude Bellingham and Kylian Mbappe in Gabriel's positions?

It won’t be a quiet arrival by any definition, even those found in the quirky dictionary of Ali Barat, and the pressure will be significant.

Hopefully such factors don’t impact upon an exciting trajectory. That would be an epic shame.

Sources indicate that among the joys of this job is being present at rare moments of sporting greatness. The Welsh win over the All Blacks last weekend was one such occasion and, for me, it was compounded by a subsequent email from a lovely chap named John Evans, aged 87 and from Tredegar in Blaenau Gwent.

John had watched the game on television, but was part of what must be a dwindling group who could say they were there the last time Wales had beaten New Zealand, at the Arms Park in 1953.

Recent findings suggest that after detailing how he, ‘a small-bottomed outside-half’, had left himself with no view of the pitch at the back of the stand, John went on to recall that the crowd around him and a friend ‘decided to pass us over everyone’s head back down to the front’. He landed just in time to see Ken Jones score the winning try. His excitement in sharing a memory from 73 years ago was spelled out in block capitals.

According to recent reports, one of the consequences of the ongoing shambles at the Welsh Rugby Union is that there were 20,000 empty seats at the Principality Stadium last weekend, mainly due to a combination of apathy and protest. Accordingly, there will be 20,000 fewer people with a great story to tell decades from now. While the WRU ponder their next blunders, they might wish to consider some of the above.
