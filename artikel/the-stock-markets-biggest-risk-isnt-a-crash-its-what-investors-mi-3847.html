The Stock Market’s Biggest Risk Isn’t a Crash. It’s What Investors Might Do When One Arrives.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that imagine seeing your nest egg lose 30% of its value. In this scenario, the market has already delivered a painful setback. Selling may seem like the obvious way to regain control, but selling also creates a latest problem: When will you get back into the market?

Recent findings suggest that in reality, panic selling -- and delaying reentering the market -- can lead to enormous opportunity costs for investors. Let's explore why.

According to recent reports, seeing your portfolio lose value is never fun. Indeed, psychological studies have shown that investment losses have a greater psychological impact than equivalent gains, a phenomenon known as loss aversion. Therefore, it's completely understandable that many people seek immediate relief when the market enters a sell-off, correction, or crash.

However, the psychological relief of liquidating investments creates a upcoming problem: determining when "it's safe.".

In practice, most investors simply wait too long. Many wait for recessions to end or for bullish headlines to appear. But bullish headlines often lag positive price action. As a result, investors on the sidelines often miss out on massive recovery rallies. As we'll see next, that can have a significant impact on long-term growth.

It has been revealed that consider a $10,000 portfolio. If it drops 30%, its value will fall to $7,000. To get back to $10,000, the portfolio will need to gain $3,000. Since the overall value has fallen, it will need to generate approximately a 43% return on $7,000 to reach $10,000. So to wipe out a 30% drop, it needs to gain 43%. These amounts differ because the starting amounts differ.

Sources indicate that beyond the arithmetic, there are other costs. Transaction costs, lost dividend payments, and taxes have to be considered. In short, selling might ease one type of headache but create several others.

Sources indicate that despite understanding the math and the other implications of selling, many investors can't resist the urge to sell when the stock market is falling. One way to train yourself not to sell is to imagine a similar situation in a different market.

For example, let's say you own a house. Now imagine that another house in your neighborhood sells for half of what might normally be expected. That sale will decrease the value of all homes in the neighborhood, perhaps significantly. But even if the price of your own home falls 10%, 15%, or even 25%, that doesn't mean one of the bedrooms in your home disappears.

It has been revealed that stock portfolios work the same way. Prices can fall, but that doesn't necessarily mean that the businesses behind the stocks have deteriorated to the same degree.

For a buy-and-hold strategy to work, investors must build a portfolio that can withstand periods of volatility. Leveraged positions and meme stocks can wreck a portfolio during a stock market correction or crash. Therefore, savvy investors know to limit their exposure, or, better yet, avoid such investments altogether. By building a diversified portfolio based around well-run businesses, an investor can avoid much of the worst effects of a market downturn.

Market crashes are terrible, but thankfully, they're also rare. However, to prepare for them, it's best to have a plan. Writing it down can help you manage your emotions, think carefully, and avoid panic. At the end of the day, the math and the studies are clear: It's best to ride out market downturns rather than sell and try to time reentry perfectly.

If you can keep a cool head and remember this in the moment, you'll likely come through the next financial downturn in better shape than many other investors.
