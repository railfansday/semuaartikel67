📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Music Victoria has released an election campaign calling on the next state government to legislate a 1% levy on ticket sales at stadiums, arenas and government-owned venues, warning that dedicated funding for Victorian artists and music creation drops to zero in 2027.

Recent findings suggest that the Vote Victorian Music campaign, developed with creative agency Bolster, rolled out Tuesday (Nov. 17) across street posters, music venues and digital channels, using a profanity-laden slogan urging voters not to let politicians ruin “the music state.” Alongside it, the peak body published a scorecard grading each party’s commitments to the sector. In a recent development, the levy sits within a broader policy ask of roughly $114 million over four years. Proceeds would fund a permanent, independently governed Victorian Music Trust, which Music Victoria estimates would return about $4.4 million annually to the sector on top of government investment, insulating it from budget and election cycles. The organization claims no party has committed dedicated funding beyond 2026.

“All sides of Victorian politics continue to underestimate music as a proven catalyst for wellbeing, community and social cohesion, and a multi-billion dollar driver of jobs, revenue and export,” chief executive Fiona Duncan said in a statement. “Our message is direct because the stakes are high.”.

According to recent reports, victoria generates about a third of Australia’s music industry revenue — $3.55 billion annually and $1.28 billion in direct economic contribution, according to Creative Australia’s The Bass Line — and is home to the country’s largest concentration of independent record labels, 66% of its vinyl pressing capacity, half its independent record stores, 40% of its music managers and 31% of APRA AMCOS songwriters and publishers. Music Victoria says Melbourne alone has more record stores per capita than anywhere in the world.

According to recent reports, at its pre-pandemic peak, according to the body’s Live Music Census, Melbourne had more live music venues per capita than London or New York, and Melburnians attended more gigs than AFL matches. A more recent audit of Music Victoria data by Live Music Locator found the state is now losing a live music venue every 18 days.

In a recent development, public investment has not kept pace. Per-capita spending on music in Victoria has fallen to 63 cents, against $3.10 in Upcoming South Wales and $1.46 in South Australia, after more than a decade of flat baseline funding. Music Victoria claims the impact extends beyond Melbourne, with regional venues, festivals and music businesses supporting local jobs in hospitality, tourism, events and production.

A statutory levy would put Victoria ahead of comparable markets. In the U.K., the government has backed a voluntary £1 contribution on tickets to reveals above 5,000 capacity, but uptake has stalled at around 30% of 2026 tickets after a 50% target was missed, and ministers have warned they will legislate if the industry does not deliver. In Australia, the Australian Music Venue Foundation debuted in March collecting A$1.10 per ticket on participating tours.

Duncan noted some parties have promised red-tape reforms, which the body welcomes, “but the government cannot keep trading on Victoria’s music reputation without investing in its future.” The scorecard assesses parties across four pillars — invest, support, grow and protect — drawing on party responses and public commitments, and follows consultation with more than 250 people across the state’s music community.
