Netflix’s ‘The Body’ Is The Most Fun Teen Drama We’ve Had In Years

The following article contains minor spoilers for The Body.

Recent findings suggest that it’s been a significant few years for the teen drama, from Off Campus and Sterling Point to The Summer I Turned Pretty and Heartstopper. Nothing ever truly bad happens, but emotionally there’s always something at stake.

Quinn Shephard’s The Body, which came out on Netflix this month, is also a teen drama—but don’t confuse the latter with other iterations of the genre. The Body is winking, sapphic, and totally deranged. It has more in common with Mean Girls and Cruel Intentions, by way of Carrie and The Crucible, than the gentle high school stories of late. (Already there’s a Christian petition to cancel it that has gathered 90,000 signatures, so you know it’s good.).

According to recent reports, our story begins on Halloween in 2004, at a Catholic high school in a small town (we’re not told where, though filming took place around Ontario, Canada). Everyone’s in Juicy Couture, lips glossed to the nines, Chloé Paddingtons and Balenciaga Le City bags slung over their shoulders (never mind that the former was released in 2005; you’ll discover that The Body plays fast and loose with historical accuracy). In the opening episode, the school’s dance troupe, the Lions, throws a substantial party at an abandoned convent and everyone’s invited: even the weird outcast, Lisbeth (Geena Meszaros). But when they all take ecstasy and engage in a Satanic initiation ritual—leaving one of the dancers, Grace (Sofia Wylie) unconscious—the balance of the group is thrown into disarray.

What happens next is part mass psychogenic hysteria (when some of the girls’ hands start shaking, followed by seizures and visions, it brings to mind the 2011 story out of LeRoy, Upcoming York, in which 20 girls at a high school developed “conversion disorder”), part possible supernatural occurrence; we’re left hanging in the balance. What’s more interesting, though, is the idea of repression and religious guilt, compounded by the attitudes of the era (everything lame is described as “gay,” so extra points for accuracy there). Sapphic thoughts and feelings bubble beneath the surface, threatening to spill over. Burgeoning sexuality fizzes throughout these high schoolers’ lives. The series is an excellent portrait of girlhood; more times than once I was transported back to being 15 or 16 and acting like a girl possessed.

Yet while plenty of the themes are gritty (religious trauma, crystal meth addiction, internalized homophobia), this isn’t a serious show. The humor is dark and a little twisted (at one point, the girls are all screaming their confessions, which range from “I’m not a natural blond” to “my brother killed seven people in Afghanistan”). The fashion is a sort of Gen Z-ified version of millennial style (all J’adore Dior tank tops and bejewelled XXL shades, rather than, like, greasy side-bangs and knit hats with flames on them). The latter is fun rather than annoying or erroneous; much like in Mean Girls and Cruel Intentions, we’re given a slick and sexy version of high school. In many ways, that’s the most 2004 thing of all.

Recent findings suggest that of course, the eight-episode series is not completely flawless. There are moments when the pace sags a little, and a few plot points arise that are either hard to make sense of, or that disappear into the ether. Even so, in this era of mild-mannered and overly earnest YA indicates, The Body stands out as deliciously knowing and mischievous—even a little off-color. (In the opening minutes, as the teen girls stretch each other in the grass, the brilliantly wry Elise, played by Sara Boustany, comments, “Holy fuck, you guys look like a bunch of dykes.” Again, historically accurate.) In fact, I haven’t had this much fun watching a teen drama since the first season of Yellowjackets, or early episodes of Riverdale.

Recent findings suggest that as is often the case with Netflix, the ending leaves room for a second season, without there being any indication as of yet that there will be one. Reddit threads are already alight with fans stressing out over whether they’ll axe the show (my instinct is that a second season is a no-brainer, though that doesn’t really mean anything). Regardless, it’s been a truly addictive first outing. And the streamer would be possessed—nay, demonic—not to green-light another.
