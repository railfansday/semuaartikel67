The S&P 500 is in a correction. Bear markets and recessions don't always follow

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that the S&P 500 fell into a correction on Nov. 20 for the first time since April 2025 as stocks pulled back sharply from October's record highs.

The market correction, defined as a 10% decline from a recent high, came as investor concern mounted over a potential slowdown in AI spending, mega-cap tech valuations and the path of Federal Reserve interest rate policy. The S&P 500 fell 3.7% on Nov. 20 alone, closing at 6,910.39, its seventh consecutive daily decline. That left the index 11.6% below the Oct. 6 closing high of 7,818.93.

In a recent development, A correction can deepen into a bear market, defined as a drop of 20% or more from the most recent top. But history shows that even a bear market, let alone a correction, doesn't automatically mean the economy is destined for a recession.

It has been revealed that in a CNBC analysis, eight of the 13 S&P 500 bear markets overlapped with recessions, as defined by the National Bureau of Economic Research, and five did not. The NBER states a recession is "a significant decline in economic activity that is spread across the economy and lasts more than a few months.".

The 1973-74 oil shock, the dot-com bust in 2000, the 2007-09 financial crisis and the 2020 pandemic downturn all overlapped with recessions. But some major bear markets also occurred without one, including in 1987 and 2022.

In a recent development, deutsche Bank macro strategist Henry Allen remarked that every S&P 500 bear market since the 1962 "Kennedy Slide" has coincided with at least one of three factors: a recession, worsening economic data raising recession fears, or a cycle of Federal Reserve rate hikes. None of those are present today, at least so far.

"Much as last week's selloff was severe, it's worth remembering that it has several differences with many of the other substantial risk-off moves in recent times," the London-based strategist wrote on Tuesday.

In a recent development, allen noted there hasn't been any sudden reassessment of the growth outlook recently, which happened during the Covid-19 shock, when the pandemic pushed the global economy into recession and the S&P 500 plunged 33.9% in just 33 days. Nor is the current situation similar to the 2025 "Liberation Day" tariff period either, which also prompted a reassessment of the growth outlook.

Recent findings suggest that economic numbers aren't deteriorating either, with the November U.S. flash composite Purchasing Managers' Index, or PMI, at 54.6, above 50 and indicating that business activity continued to expand. Another factor that often coincides with a recession, Allen said, was a big hawkish shift from the central banks, seen in the 1966 and 2022 bear markets.

"Today however, the equity declines have not coincided with a hawkish repricing," despite widespread expectations that the central bank is set to raise rates again at its final meeting of the year, on Dec. 9, Allen said.

Today, markets are pricing fewer Fed rate hikes through September 2027 than they were on Oct. 6, when the S&P 500 set its record, Allen mentioned. "If anything, pricing has shifted slightly dovishly with the equity declines.".

Over more than six decades since 1962, bear markets have always coincided with at least one of those three circumstances, according to Allen.

"So it would be unusual to see one today without those" happening too, he said.
