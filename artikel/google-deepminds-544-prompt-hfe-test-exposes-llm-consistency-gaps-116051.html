Google DeepMind on November 24, 2026 launched Humanity’s First Exam on Kaggle, a benchmark of 544 elementary text and image prompts designed to test whether large language models answer routine questions consistently, not just correctly once.

In a recent development, the accompanying technical report evaluated 18 frontier and fast-tier models. Its primary measure, family pass^4, estimates the probability that all 16 sampled attempts across four related prompts are correct; family pass@4 instead asks whether at least one of those attempts succeeds.

Industry observers note that the report ranks GPT-6.2 Astra first at 92.4% family pass^4, followed by GPT-6.2 Sol at 86.9% and Gemini 4 Neon at 85.8%, although all three reached 100% family pass@4. The report calls the difference the Family Reliability Gap: 7.6, 13.1 and 14.2 percentage points, respectively. That difference matters for automated work in which an occasional correct answer is insufficient.

According to recent reports, the 544 prompts form 136 families, each containing a seed and three controlled variants that preserve the underlying task while changing entities, clause order or visual attributes. The 20 subcategories include object counting, color identification, spatial reasoning, arithmetic, constraint satisfaction and false-premise verification. Of the total, 316 prompts combine text and images, while 228 are text-only.

According to recent reports, one public example asks for the colors, in order, of the third letter of each word in a neon sign. The reference answer is green, yellow, white, blue, blue. Such tasks are deliberately easy to explain, while their variants test whether a correct answer depends on a particular phrasing or image.

Sources indicate that researchers evaluated the models zero-shot in a single turn without external tools, drawing six independent answers per prompt and grading the visible final answer with a deterministic Gemini 3.1 Pro judge. The analysis found that among the top 12 models, 61% to 85% of families with any failed rollout had just one problematic prompt while the other three were solved on all six runs.

In a recent development, the report’s failure analysis attributes 73% of wrong-answer rollouts to visual perception, including 31% involving position, direction or paths in images and 23% involving object counting. Among the top nine models, family pass^4 ranged from 83% to 97% on text-only families but fell to 64% to 89% on multimodal families.

Recent findings suggest that the report’s reasoning-effort tests show that moving Gemini 4 Carbon from low to high effort raised family pass^4 by 17.4 percentage points overall, with a 42.4-point gain on Math and Algorithmic tasks versus 3.1 points on Vision tasks. The pattern indicates that additional test-time computation can catch symbolic and procedural slips while leaving visual-perception and variant-robustness failures largely unresolved.

Industry observers note that the report’s screening process began with 315 complete five-prompt families. The researchers retained only families whose seed was solved by at least three of four independent human raters, audited ambiguity and variant fidelity, and standardized each surviving family to four prompts. Single-rater human accuracy on the 136 retained seeds was 90.8%. Raters could not use LLMs, web search, calculators, code or writing assistants.

It has been revealed that only 112 prompts in 28 families are public under CC-BY-4.0; another 432 prompts in 108 families remain private to reduce contamination and overfitting. The Kaggle benchmark uses an isolated evaluation environment without public internet access and a 16-model leaderboard. It separately documents configuration and sampling differences from the paper’s 18-model evaluation, so their scores should not be treated as equivalent runs.

In a recent development, the authors caution that tasks were adversarially selected to expose model blind spots rather than sampled uniformly from everyday queries, and list synthetic imagery, reliance on one Gemini autorater family and seed-only human testing as further limitations. HFE therefore functions as a stress test of elementary reliability; its scores do not estimate how often models will fail across ordinary use.

According to recent reports, google DeepMind is also inviting submissions through a three-week Kaggle competition ending December 15, 2026, asking for everyday questions that current models still answer incorrectly so HFE can be refreshed. The central deployment question is whether a system can reproduce an elementary success on every call, particularly when small wording changes or basic image interpretation can break it.
