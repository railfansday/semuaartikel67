7 Popular Garden Plants That Need Extra TLC Before Winter – Here's How to Protect Them From the Cold

Recent findings suggest that with winter just around the corner, your garden is busy adjusting to the changes in light and temperature and, while some plants won’t fail to return next year, others are more vulnerable and need your help to survive. Many of our favorite flowering plants are the most susceptible to harm from changing temperatures, deep freezes and drying winter winds, so if you want lots of blooms again next year, give them the winter protection they need.

It has been revealed that some of these garden stalwarts are tender perennials that simply won't survive outdoors in winter, while others are technically hardy but still a little vulnerable, so overwintering these plants well is vital. From protecting shrubs growing in the ground to digging bulbs up to take indoors, here's how to protect plants through winter so they wake up healthy in spring, ready for another bumper season of blooms.

Dahlias are some of the most varied and prized flowers of a late summer and fall garden, but they’re really only hardy in zones 8 through 11. Their tuberous roots are susceptible to getting mushy in wet conditions and frozen in cold winter ground so leaving them in the ground is risky, especially in zones 6 and colder.

The best way to protect these plants for flowers next year is to lift dahlia tubers out of the ground after the first deep frost blackens the foliage. Cut the stems back to a couple inches, then carefully dig around and lift out the crown and tubers. Let these dry for a few days to cure and then store dahlia tubers in a box of wood shavings – a compressed bale of natural pet bedding such as this from Amazon is a cheap and convenient way to buy. Keep the box in a cool but frost-free, dry storage area such as an unheated garage, and you'll overwinter dahlias to replant next spring.

Another plant that benefits from lifting is canna, which is only generally hardy in zones 7 through 10 because it grows from rhizomes that are vulnerable in freezing winter ground. Exact hardiness depends on the variety, so know your type and growing zone.

Sources indicate that once the foliage has blackened after a frost, cut the stems back to four to six inches (10 to 15 cm) and then carefully dig up canna rhizomes to store for the winter. As with dahlias, it’s best to let these dry for a few days before storing them in a material like wood shavings. Overwinter canna rhizomes in a dark, cool and dry storage area until spring weather has warmed up enough to replant them outdoors.

One of the most popular hydrangeas for its bright blue or pink flowers, bigleaf hydrangeas, also known as mophead hydrangeas, are typically hardy in zones 5 through 9. However, while the shrubs themselves cope with cold weather just fine, the issue with overwintering hydrangea shrubs that flower on old wood is the potential damage to next year’s flower buds that have developed before winter arrives. A cold snap can damage these buds, reducing the number of flowers you get next summer.

Sources indicate that to protect hydrangea buds, you’ll need to do more than simply covering the roots zone with mulch. Place four stakes in a square around the shrub and wrap burlap like this from Walmart for protection. In a very cold climate, increase the insulation by filling the space between the burlap and the plant with dry leaves.

Industry observers note that hybrid tea roses can be hardy from zones 4 through 9, but there is some variation by cultivar, and they're still a little vulnerable to cold weather compared to other types of roses. So, to overwinter roses in a region with a harsh climate, give them some winter protection to ensure they make it through to spring in a healthy state, ready for strong growth.

Your roses are vulnerable to damage caused by freeze-thaw cycles in the soil and winter damage to canes. First, clean up any plant debris on the ground to reduce the risk of disease, then loosely tie the canes together for extra support in damaging winter winds. Mound soil or compost around the base of the plant and for a final layer of insulation, top with a natural mulch such as Miracle-Gro All Natural Mulch, available from The Home Depot.

Sources indicate that rosemary is a Mediterranean herb that's generally hardy in zones 8 through 10. The best way to overwinter rosemary in colder climates is to bring it inside: trying to protect this plant outside with mulch is risky as it's very susceptible to wet, cold soil and drying winter winds. However, using a mulch is helpful in zones 7 or 8 to give the roots a little extra insulation.

According to recent reports, in colder areas, dig up your rosemary plant and move it into a pot that you can bring it indoors. Even better, grow rosemary in a pot year-round as it won’t appreciate being dug out of the ground once established. This Mediterranean herb is one of those plants that grow better in terracotta pots because of the well-drained conditions the porous clay walls create. And because the pot won't be left outside in freezing temperatures, a cheap terracotta planter like this from Lowe's is all you need.

It has been revealed that rosemary doesn't tolerate soggy roots so to improve drainage, mix perlite like Back to the Roots Organic Perlite, available from Walmart, into any all-purpose potting mix at a ratio of 1:3. Indoors, give your potted plant a cool, bright spot and avoid overwatering.

Species and varieties of Colocasia and Alocasia offer large, striking, tropical leaves in summer but are only hardy in zones 8 through 11. Elephant ears grow from corms (large underground structures similar to tubers) which should be dug up for winter in colder growing zones.

Industry observers note that to store Elephant ear corms over winter, let the foliage die back after a frost, then cut back the stems and leaves. Dig around the corm and lift it from the ground, allowing it to dry for up to two weeks before storing in wood shavings in a cool, dry spot. If your Elephant ear is growing in a pot, and small enough, the other option is bring it indoors for winter. While some types can be kept actively growing to enjoy as a houseplant, it's more usual to trim and overwinter tropical plants indoors in a semi-dormant state in a bright but cool spot.

Many salvias are winter hardy up to zone 3, but in regions with cold winters, more tender varieties are usually grown as annuals. Alternatively, you can try to protect these tender salvia varieties like Brazilian sage or gentian sage from winter weather, and this strategy works best in borderline zones like 6 or 7.

To overwinter tender salvias outside, use a thick layer of an airy natural mulch such as long-needle pine straw mulch like this from Amazon around the root zone and crown. In a more exposed garden, bark chips like these Pine Mulch Nuggets, also from Amazon, are heavier but create the same airy conditions. Leave the stems in place as they're hollow and trap air that can help insulate the crown.

If you're growing tender salvias in containers, simply bring them inside for the winter, placing them in a spot with bright light and reduce watering as growth will slow.

Timing for winter protection depends on the plant and your local climate and weather in your USDA zone. Tender plants should be brought inside before the first frost, but for plants that grow from tubers, rhizomes or corms that need lifting from the ground, wait until after the first deep frost.

For mostly hardy perennials that simply need a little protection to remain healthy outside, you need to strike a balance between acting before the ground freezes and waiting until the plant goes dormant. Insulating these plants from the cold by mulching or covering too early can delay dormancy and encourage growth that's quickly damaged once temperatures drop.

According to recent reports, protecting your precious plants before winter arrives isn't complicated or difficult. You simply need to know what you're growing and how the plants tolerate your local winters, then protect them accordingly. A little effort now will reward you next year with strong new growth as soon as temperatures rise in spring, and an abundant display of flowers.
