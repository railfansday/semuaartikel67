The S&P 500 Heads Into Thanksgiving Below Its Peak. Here's How the Holiday Week Has Usually Gone Since 1950.

Industry observers note that the S&P 500 (^GSPC -3.70%) finished on Nov. 16 at 7,416.59, around 5% under the all-time closing high of 7,818.93 it set on Oct. 6. The drop sped up in late October, and the index fell over 4% on Nov. 5 alone as tech stocks sold off.

Chipmakers were among the worst hit that day, amid worries that the midterm election results might slow the artificial intelligence (AI) build-out.

The market is closed on Thursday, Nov. 26, for Thanksgiving, and it closes early (at 1 p.m. ET) the next day.

It has been revealed that historically, this has been a better-than-average week to hold stocks. Since 1950, the index has climbed during Thanksgiving week in 53 of 76 years, or about 70% of the time, and the week's average gain has been roughly 0.7%. By comparison, normal weeks across the same stretch rose 56% of the time and gained roughly 0.2% on average.

But the record looks weaker in years like this one. In the 13 years the index entered the week 3% to 10% under a recent record close, it rose in just seven.

Sources indicate that I measured each year from the close on the Friday before Thanksgiving to the close on the Friday after, with the index's daily closes from Yahoo Finance (which count price moves only, not dividends). That normally includes four trading sessions around the holiday.

Notably, the gains haven't been spread evenly among them. Monday and Tuesday combined averaged a gain of only about 0.1% and finished up in 47 of the 76 years. The Wednesday before Thanksgiving, though, climbed in 58 years and averaged a gain of around 0.3%. The Friday after climbed in 54, with an average gain close to that.

So, the two sessions on either side of the holiday make up almost all the week's typical gain.

According to recent reports, the range has been wide, too. The best Thanksgiving week came in 2008, in the middle of the financial crisis, when the index jumped about 12% from around half its record. The worst was 2011, with a loss of nearly 5%.

Recent findings suggest that to match this year's setup, I looked for weeks that began with the index at least 3% but no more than 10% below its record close, with that record set within around four months. Thirteen years qualified, from 1951 to 2025.

Industry observers note that the week rose in seven of them, and the average gain was around 0.4%. That's still a little better than a regular week. But it's around half the average for all 76 Thanksgiving weeks, and the hit rate drops from about 70% to about 54%.

The two latest cases went in opposite directions. In 2018, the index began the week around 7% below its September record and dropped nearly 4% more -- and by Dec. 31, it was about 8% below where it started the week.

In a recent development, last year, it came in about 4% under its Oct. 28 record and climbed nearly 4%.

Also, the rest of the year was better for the group. In 10 of these 13 years, the index closed the year above its level on the Friday before Thanksgiving. Out of all 76 years, the same was true for 54.

For anyone owning an S&P 500 index fund like the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO -3.42%), the next 12 months may matter more than the next four sessions. Twelve of the 13 cases have a full year of data after them, and a year after the Friday before Thanksgiving, the index was up in six and down in six.

These years ranged from a rise of around 24% after 1963 to a drop of about 40% after 2007.

In 2007, the holiday week cost the index only about 1%. But the October record turned out to be the peak before a bear market that cut the index by over half. And 2018's bad week came before a rise of 14% over the next year.

Sure, 13 years is a small sample, and the fall from a 70% hit rate to 54% could easily be chance.

The Vanguard fund finished Nov. 16 around $680, about 5% under the $718.47 peak it hit within the last year.

I think the holiday week's history is a slight edge in investors' favor, and one that has mostly faded in years when the index started below a record. For a long-term owner of the fund, that isn't much to act on either way.
