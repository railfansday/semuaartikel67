It has been revealed that some Thanksgiving food combinations are obvious. Others might earn you some questioning looks at the dinner table.

It has been revealed that for years, my kids refused to let any two foods touch on their plates. I found this particularly maddening at Thanksgiving. There’s an entire table of food to fit onto a single plate and only so much room to negotiate separate territories for the mashed potatoes and green bean casserole! Thankfully, they’re a little more accommodating these days and, like me, have come to appreciate the occasional overlap between dishes.

In a recent development, of course, plenty of adults also have strong opinions about which Thanksgiving foods should mingle. There are those who insist on keeping every dish separate, those who permit a little gravy to stray onto the stuffing but draw the line at cranberry sauce, and those who deliberately assemble the perfect combination on every forkful. Then there’s always the unhinged relative who stirs the entire contents of their plate into a single, gravy-saturated mass and eats it with the serene confidence of someone who has never once questioned their choices.

In a recent development, personally, I’m firmly in favor of combining foods, although I do believe a little discretion is in order. Thanksgiving dinner offers a wonderful assortment of rich, savory and tart flavors, and knowing which ones to bring together is part of what makes the meal so satisfying. There’s a culinary reason that cranberry sauce belongs alongside turkey, and that stuffing and mashed potatoes are particularly good together. And once you understand why those combinations work, you can start building some excellent bites of your own.

It has been revealed that absolutely. In fact, some of the most familiar food pairings are built around the idea that one ingredient can supply something another lacks. Think of a rich cut of meat served with a sharp, acidic sauce, or a creamy dish finished with something crisp. Neither component is necessarily lacking on its own, but the contrast makes the combination much more interesting.

The same principle applies to Thanksgiving foods. Acidity helps balance richness, sweetness can temper salty or intensely savory flavors, and a change in texture can make a forkful more interesting. It’s not particularly complicated, but there’s a difference between understanding why two foods complement each other and simply hoping for the best.

Some combinations are simply better than others, though saying so at Thanksgiving dinner may invite more opinions than you bargained for.

It has been revealed that turkey and cranberry sauce: There’s a reason this pairing has endured for generations. Turkey, particularly the breast meat, is relatively mild, and the tartness of cranberry sauce gives it a welcome lift. It’s also an excellent remedy for turkey that’s spent a little too long in the oven, a situation that has befallen even the most conscientious Thanksgiving cooks.

Mashed potatoes, stuffing and gravy: I consider this one of Thanksgiving’s greatest achievements. The smooth, buttery potatoes and herbaceous stuffing are excellent together, and a generous spoonful of gravy makes the whole arrangement even better. There’s nothing particularly subtle about it, but Thanksgiving dinner isn’t generally celebrated for its subtlety.

Sweet potatoes and turkey: The natural sweetness of roasted sweet potatoes makes a lovely contrast to savory turkey, particularly the richer dark meat. A traditional sweet potato casserole can work, too, although I draw the line somewhere around the marshmallow topping. I have nothing against marshmallows, but I prefer to encounter them after dinner.

Sources indicate that green bean casserole and mashed potatoes: These are two of my Thanksgiving essentials, and I see no reason they shouldn’t share a fork. The creamy green beans and buttery potatoes are lovely together, but it’s the crispy fried onions that really make the combination work. That salty crunch is a welcome addition to the softer textures.

Turkey, stuffing and cranberry sauce: This is probably my favorite combo of the bunch. The savory turkey and seasoned stuffing are practically made for one another, while a little cranberry sauce cuts through the richness. It’s essentially a Thanksgiving leftover sandwich without the bread, although I wouldn’t object to adding a dinner roll to the equation.

Macaroni and cheese and cranberry sauce: I realize this combination may call my culinary credentials into question, but hear me out. Rich, creamy mac and cheese practically begs for something sharp and acidic to cut through it, and cranberry sauce does exactly that. It’s not so different from serving a sharp cheddar with fruit preserves on a charcuterie board, although I concede that putting cranberry sauce on macaroni and cheese is a considerably harder sell. But that’s half the fun of Thanksgiving dinner: discovering which combinations you’re willing to defend.
