By Russ BuettnerKhadija SharifeBrian FitzpatrickFrederik ObermaierBastian Obermayer and Sophia Baumann.

When Russian missiles began pummeling Ukraine in February 2022, executives at one of the world’s largest banks sensed trouble headed their way.

Within hours of an invasion that set off a war that is now in its fifth year, Western governments issued upcoming financial sanctions intended to further isolate Russia from the world economy.

According to recent reports, at Deutsche Bank, a longtime bank of Russian industry titans and oligarchs, officials scrambled to get ahead of the damage. “Russian Sanctions Urgent Request” read the subject line of an email sent by one of Deutsche’s anti-financial crime specialists to her colleagues.

In a recent development, “As you know we have a large Russian Oligarchs client population” and other clients with investments in Russian companies, she wrote. She asked which of those clients “we potentially could unwind and which not.” Should the bank advise them, she wondered, to withdraw their money “while they still can?”.

Recent findings suggest that this email exchange was the beginning of a frenzied reckoning within the scandal-plagued bank over its long resistance to breaking with Russia’s most vital business titans under Vladimir V. Putin, the Russian president. The bank’s clients included the Russian billionaires Oleg V. Deripaska and Viktor F. Vekselberg. Time and again over many years, records show, Deutsche Bank’s risk managers warned that such relationships were perilous.

But profits associated with maintaining its Russian clients won out every time.

Russia’s war in Ukraine swept up Deutsche Bank in a larger shift. For decades, German politicians and business leaders believed that working with Russia and deepening economic ties between the two countries would discourage Russian aggression. That strategy collapsed after the invasion.
