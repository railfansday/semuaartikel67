6 Plants That Need Mulching in November to Keep Roots Cozy as Temperatures Fall – and 4 You Should Never Cover

Late fall is an key time to prep a garden for winter, and knowing which plants to mulch in November is a vital part of the plan. As weather worsens and the ground begins to freeze, adding an insulating layer of mulch keeps soil temperatures more stable, helping plants to survive winter better. Protected from freeze-thaw cycles, frost heaving and low temperatures that could cause root damage, mulched plants will wake up in spring ready for abundant growth.

Because many plants have died down by late fall, mulching in November is a speedy and satisfying job on a crisp sunny day. All you need to do is spread a layer of mulch on top of the soil, keeping it away from the plant stems, which is easy with a hand rake such as this from Lowe's. For best results, choose a mulch made of natural materials like compost, shredded leaves or bark, as it will start breaking down over the winter to supply the soil with nutrients, ready to fuel that strong spring growth.

Many folk like to mulch all their borders when winterizing a garden in fall because it not only protects plants but helps to suppress weeds and keeps beds looking smart over winter. However, while most plants will get through winter better if treated to a fall mulch, there are a few which don't appreciate this extra layer so it's vital to know which to leave be. There are a few plants that seriously benefit from a late fall mulch, too, so even if you're not planning on protecting your whole yard, it's well worth mulching these garden heroes. Here's everything you need to know to get great results.

If you’ve planted upcoming perennials this fall, whether you bought them from a garden center or had success creating free plants by dividing your perennials, they're likely not fully established in their new home yet. This means they're more vulnerable than your other perennials to the cold temperatures and fluctuations of winter weather.

Sources indicate that mulching now is a smart idea for all newly planted perennials, but especially any that are borderline hardy in your growing region. Loosely apply three to four inches (8-10cm) of mulch around these plants as the soil begins to freeze.

Many roses benefit from winter mulching, especially less hardy varieties and those that are newly planted. Fluctuating temperatures can damage plants and cause frost heaving, pushing the roots out of the ground. Mulch roses with 8 to 12 inches (20-30cm) of compost or organic mulch, or even loose, light soil around their bases, keeping the mulching material a couple inches away from the stems of the plants to prevent moisture collection that can trigger rot.

Recent findings suggest that reliably hardy, well-established hydrangeas may not need mulching but younger plants will benefit from a protective layer this month. Hydrangeas have shallow roots that can be damaged by the cold and the frost heaving that often results from freeze-thaw cycles. Winter damage can stunt spring growth, so a layer of mulch can give your plants a head start next season.

Recent findings suggest that mulch hydrangeas with two to six inches (5-15cm) of organic material, depending on how cold your winters are. Keep in mind that mulch only protects the roots, so you may also want to protect buds growing on old wood with a burlap wrap during cold snaps.

Your spring bulbs – tulips, crocuses, daffodils and hyacinths – need time to establish roots before winter, and fluctuating soil temperatures can disrupt this process by freezing and thawing repeatedly. The solution is to apply two to three inches of light, loose mulch as the soil begins to freeze. This keeps soil temperatures more consistent, and can also prevent premature emergence during periods of milder winter weather.

If you're adding spring-flowering bulbs that can still be planted in November to your garden – and there are plenty that are fine to go into the soil this late in fall – then mulch straight after planting.

In a recent development, strawberries are particularly vulnerable to damage caused by cold temperatures but also by the cycles of freezing and thawing that many gardens experience. Cover your strawberry patch with a loose layer of three to four inches of an organic mulch. Weed-free straw such as HealthiStraw GardenStraw, available from Amazon, is a good choice for protecting without compacting and weighing down delicate strawberry plants. Pull the straw back in spring as you see new growth, but leave it around the edges or between rows of plants to keep berries clean and off the ground as they develop.

While many people use mums as fall annuals, there are hardy chrysanthemums that can be grown as perennials with the right care. To get them to come back next year, though, you’ll need to add some mulch for winter protection. They have shallow roots, so the extra insulation helps prevent cold damage. To protect your new garden mums, leave the foliage intact and spread a loose layer of a few inches of organic mulch under and around the leaves.

In a recent development, always choose a mulch made from natural plant materials, as that will improve your soil as well as protect your plants. Choose undyed mulch to avoid adding unnecessary chemicals to the soil, and there's no need to use mulch binder or glue to keep it in place.

It has been revealed that you can buy purpose-made mulch like like Timberline Pine Bark Mulch, stocked by Lowe's, but there are plenty of other options to choose from to suit your garden style and budget. You could use homemade compost or a store-bought compost like Back to the Roots Organic Compost, available from Amazon. Fall leaves that you've shredded by running the lawn mower over a few times make an excellent, free mulch, while bark chips like Golden Trophy Pine Bark Nuggets from Walmartadd texture to your beds.

Coco coir chips such as Riare Organic Coco Husk Chips, available from Amazon, are a sustainable option as they're made from outer coconut husks, are a sustainable option. Because they're packaged in a dehydrated form, the bales are compact and light to carry. While coco coir is a very effective mulch, it's not the best option for an exposed garden as after a long dry spell the chips can become light enough to be blown around by strong winds.

All these natural mulches will break down to enrich your soil and improve its structure so it drains freely yet holds onto valuable moisture.

November is the right time to mulch in many gardens, but it’s important to follow your local conditions when deciding on exact timing, depending on your USDA zone. Applying mulch early might feel like you’re getting ahead, but this can warm the soil too much and delay plants going into dormancy. So, wait until plants are dormant and the ground is beginning to freeze, which typically happens after several hard frosts.

For all the plants that will thank you for the cozy winter coverage mulch provides, a few can be damaged by it. These plants are vulnerable to excessive moisture, poor airflow, or having their crowns and growth points covered so, while they may benefit from a very light layer of mulch, be careful not to smother them.

Winter mulching is all about strategic protection, not simply covering everything in a thick layer of insulation. But get it right and employ a few insulating frost jackets to zip up vulnerable plants and keep them cozy, and your garden will be protected from the worst of the winter cold, ready to flourish once spring arrives.
