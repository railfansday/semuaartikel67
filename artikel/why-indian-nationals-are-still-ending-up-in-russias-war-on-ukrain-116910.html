Why Indian nationals are still ending up in Russia's war on Ukraine

Gurpreet Singh Randhawa, a 40-year-old from Jalandhar in India's northern Punjab state, said he traveled to Russia for work after responding to a Facebook advertisement for a driving job at a garment factory.

But instead of finding work as a driver, he was sent to fight in Ukraine for the Russian armed forces.

It has been revealed that after escaping from his unit, he surrendered to Ukrainian forces in November. He is now being held as a prisoner of war.

In an interview with Ukrainian YouTuber Volodymyr Zolkin, Randhawa described how, after arriving in Russia, he was taken to a military facility, made to sign a contract and sent for training. He mentioned they confiscated his passport and phone. His account has not been independently verified.

Randhawa's story is a familiar one in India.

Sources indicate that he is the second Indian national known to have escaped the Russian military. Sahil Majothi, a 23-year-old from Gujarat, surrendered to Ukrainian forces in October 2025. He had joined the Russian military while serving a prison sentence in Russia, reportedly believing that military service would help him secure his release.

The cases underline how little has changed since Prime Minister Narendra Modi raised the issue with Russian President Vladimir Putin during his visit to Moscow in July 2024.

At the time, Putin assured him that Indians who were misled into joining the Russian army would be discharged. But the recruitment of Indians has continued.

In an October filing before the Supreme Court, India's Ministry of External Affairs (MEA) said approximately 267 Indians have joined the Russian armed forces since the start of Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine.

Sources indicate that of these, 139 were discharged and 51 were killed. The whereabouts of the 77 others were unknown. Russian authorities have confirmed that seven of them are missing.

The problem extends beyond Indians who travel to Russia seeking civilian employment, only to find themselves exposed to recruitment networks and the dangers of a war zone.

The MEA has also highlighted cases of Indians knowingly enlisting in the Russian military for the supposed financial benefits on offer, believing they could later secure release through government intervention.

Industry observers note that some had also approached Indian authorities to renounce their citizenship and obtain Russian passports. "This is a dangerous path and is strongly discouraged by the government," the ministry mentioned.

Former diplomat Anil Trigunayat remarked the growing movement of Indian workers to Russia presents both economic opportunities and latest challenges for their protection.

"Inadequate oversight of recruitment networks could leave vulnerable workers exposed to exploitation," Trigunayat told DW.

He remarked Indian workers were often burdened by substantial recruitment fees and continued deductions from their salaries by intermediaries.

"Some of them had paid between 300,000 to 500,000 Indian rupees [$5,400 /€4,900] to Indian recruitment agents, and even while working in Russia, more than 30% of their agreed monthly salary was taken away by local and foreign agents," Trigunayat stated.

The former diplomat added that some Indians who had overstayed their visas in Russia were particularly vulnerable. Military service was allegedly offered to earn substantial wages and regularize their immigration status.

Mohammad Sufiyan, from Hyderabad in the southern state of Telangana, said he was lured to Russia under false pretences and forced into active combat in Ukraine.

He had traveled abroad in search of employment and a better livelihood, but ended up in a war zone. He has since returned home to India.

"Many people are duped. Some decide it is better to seek citizenship than come back to India. It's a tough life, and people will go to any lengths to get some sort of employment," Sufiyan told DW.

Sufiyan's experience points to the economic desperation that makes such offers attractive, even when they carry the risk of being caught up in a war.

Rajan Kumar, a professor at the Center for Russian and Central Asian Studies at Jawaharlal Nehru University's School of International Studies, said some Indians who traveled to Russia for work could find themselves caught up in the fighting without having joined the military.

According to recent reports, "Some of them, they don't join the army, but they end up in the army region. They're caught in the crossfire," Kumar told DW.

He said India was negotiating with Moscow to secure the return of its citizens caught up in such situations. "Russia is very cooperative so far," he said.

India has been pressing Russia to take action, but the concern persists.

The recruitment efforts reflect Russia's need for more manpower for its nearly five-year war in Ukraine , and the limited influence Latest Delhi can exert over Moscow, despite the close political relationship between the two countries.

Mohsin Raza Khan, a professor at the Jindal School of International Affairs, told DW that Putin's need to sustain the war effort was taking precedence over the concerns of a strategic partner.

He points out that the Russian president was reluctant to impose another mobilization on his own population for fear of domestic unrest.

"In these circumstances, sustaining the war effort appears to take precedence over concerns raised by India. Neither Modi nor Newly introduced Delhi has sufficient leverage to change that," remarked Khan.

Khan also remarked the willingness of some Indians to enlist, and even consider giving up their citizenship, is down to the lack of decent employment opportunities at home.

"For young, educated men without contacts or connections, the chances of respectable employment are so low that the risks of a high casualty war are preferable," he said.
