Private markets are more accessible than ever for investors. Getting out is another matter

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that retail investors are lining up to back private funds. But education on how restrictive these funds can be, particularly on when and how much money they can get back, may not be keeping pace with the influx of upcoming capital.

PwC research has found that 77% of fund distributors surveyed mentioned access to private markets is becoming important or very important to what they initiative to offer clients, but just 15% of asset managers said they provide comprehensive education for retail investors.

Unlike the public markets retail investors are more used to, some private-market vehicles limit when investors can get their money back to set repurchase windows, and even then may return only part of what they request.

A spike in redemption requests at several private-credit funds earlier this year exposed the tension between giving investors regular opportunities to cash out and holding assets that can be difficult to sell swiftly.

It has been revealed that blackstone restricted withdrawals from its $79 billion flagship nontraded BCRED private credit fund to 5% of shares in June, after redemption requests hit 10% during the second quarter. Apollo also capped withdrawals in its Apollo Debt Solutions vehicle at 5% after second-quarter redemption requests surged to almost 17%. Blue Owl, Morgan Stanley and Partners Group also limited investor withdrawals from some of their funds.

Limitations on redemptions are a key trade-off in structures such as LTAFs in the U.K., ELTIFs in the EU and interval funds in the U.S., which broaden access to less-liquid assets.

It's emerged as a major pressure point as those structures come under closer scrutiny.

"Private markets can have very different characteristics from the products many wealth clients are used to, particularly around liquidity, valuations and holding periods," mentioned Albertha Charles, global asset and wealth management leader at PwC.

"Investors and advisers need to understand how these products work, including their liquidity and valuation characteristics, so they can make informed decisions about whether they are appropriate.".

PwC's 2026 Asset Management Revolution report, published Nov. 23, surveyed 250 asset managers, 100 institutional investors and 100 distributors across 36 countries and regions. More than half of respondents represented firms with over $20 billion in assets under management.

Sources indicate that among the study's key findings, education and liquidity are seen as the main hurdles as private markets reach a broader investor base. Distributors remarked better advisor and client education would help expand access, while liquidity, lock-ups and suitability requirements remain major barriers. Private equity and private credit were also the areas where distributors saw some of the biggest gaps in advisor knowledge.

Private credit appears likely to draw the strongest demand — two-thirds (66%) of distributors expect it to lead client interest in private markets, compared with 46% for private equity.

According to recent reports, regulators in several major markets are creating structures to widen access to long-term and illiquid assets. The U.K. uses long-term asset funds, or LTAFs, while the EU's European long-term investment funds, known as ELTIFs, can be marketed across member states.

Sources indicate that in the U.S., interval funds provide periodic, rather than daily, liquidity. Singapore, Japan and Hong Kong have also taken steps to broaden retail or listed access to private assets. The frameworks differ, but all aim to make less-liquid investments workable within existing distribution, suitability and liquidity requirements.

Yen Sum, global chair of the private capital practice at Latham & Watkins, and a member of its executive committee, said access to private-market funds is generally limited to investors who certify they are qualified or sophisticated.

She told CNBC on Thursday that the key concern for investor education centers on how private assets differ in liquidity, maturities and return-of-capital terms, and how higher rates can pressure leveraged companies — rather than simply whether regulation should curb access.
