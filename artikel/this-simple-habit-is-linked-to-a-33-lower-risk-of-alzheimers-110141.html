Reading for enjoyment may offer far more than entertainment. Whether someone prefers a classic Jane Austen novel or a Heartstopper graphic novel, research suggests that reading for pleasure is associated with better mental health, stronger empathy, and a lower risk of dementia.

Researchers at the University of Cambridge are working with The Queen's Reading Room, a charity established by Her Majesty The Queen, to raise awareness of these benefits across all age groups. Their collaboration is part of the National Year of Reading, an initiative encouraging more people to make reading part of their lives.

According to recent reports, cambridge psychiatry researchers Professor Barbara Sahakian and Dr. Christelle Langley began exploring the subject while writing their 2025 book, Brain Boost: Healthy Habits for a Happier Life. They discovered that reading for enjoyment was connected to a remarkably wide range of positive outcomes, yet the amount of research specifically examining these benefits was surprisingly limited.

In a review published in Psychological Medicine, Sahakian and Langley joined Vicki Perrin, CEO of The Queen's Reading Room, to examine the available evidence. Their findings suggest that the format and type of reading matter less than the enjoyment people get from it. They also highlight how reading together, whether through book clubs or parents sharing stories with children, can provide additional benefits.

Recent findings suggest that professor Sahakian noted: "Reading is a great way to support our brain health, develop important cognitive skills and boost our well-being. It's something we ought to be encouraging from an early age, given the increasing evidence of its benefits, particularly to children, but really, it's never too late to start, and it can bring benefits throughout our lives and into old age.".

Reading Early in Life Is Linked to Better Brain Development.

According to recent reports, despite the growing evidence of its advantages, reading for enjoyment has become less common among young people. Surveys indicate that reading rates among children and teenagers have fallen to their lowest recorded levels, with particularly steep challenges among teenage boys and children from disadvantaged backgrounds.

Even children who are least enthusiastic about reading recognize that it can help them academically. Nearly half acknowledge that books and other reading materials can introduce them to unfamiliar words or teach them something newly introduced.

It has been revealed that one of the most compelling studies examined more than 10,000 young people. Conducted by Sahakian, Langley, and their colleagues, the research found that children who started reading for pleasure at an early age tended to perform better in several key areas. These included attention, memory, and executive functioning, the mental abilities involved in planning, decision-making, and managing behavior.

In a recent development, early readers also showed stronger academic performance, experienced fewer mental health problems, and were more likely to develop healthier daily routines. In particular, they tended to get more sleep and spend less time using screens.

Dr. Langley said: "Given how beneficial reading for pleasure is at a young age, we should be doing everything we can to introduce children and young people to reading. The notable thing is to find something they'd really enjoy reading about - it might be a TV program or a game - and use this as a way in. Reading comics and graphic novels could also be a helpful introduction as they might be seen as less intimidating, maybe less boring, than novels.".

Sources indicate that the academic advantages may extend beyond language and literacy. Studies have also connected reading with stronger mathematical performance. Researchers suggest this relationship may arise because both activities rely on some of the same mental abilities, including language processing, memory, and executive function.

Just Five Minutes of Reading May Reduce Stress.

The benefits of reading do not disappear after childhood. Among adults, surveys have associated reading for enjoyment with improved emotional well-being, greater relaxation, and reduced stress. Readers also tend to report more empathy, a deeper understanding of other people, and greater openness to viewpoints different from their own.

For many people, books are an especially appealing way to unwind. Reading ranks as the most popular approach to stress relief, with nearly two in five adults (38%) identifying it as their preferred way to relax.

A neuroscience study commissioned by The Queen's Reading Room in 2024 found that spending just five minutes reading fiction could lower stress by as much as 20%. The study also reported improvements in concentration and problem-solving abilities, along with a reduced sense of loneliness.

Recent findings suggest that books may even provide more comfort than some traditional relaxation habits. In one survey, more respondents said they turned to reading (35%) for comfort than to drinking wine (31%) or taking a hot bath (10%).

Reading Linked to Lower Dementia and Alzheimer's Risk.

As people get older, the potential advantages of reading may become especially meaningful. Research has associated mentally stimulating leisure activities, including reading, with slower cognitive decline and a reduced likelihood of developing dementia.

According to recent reports, one study followed nearly 5,500 adults aged 55 and older for five years. Participants who regularly took part in activities such as reading were significantly less likely to develop cognitive impairment during the study period.

Another investigation reported an even more striking association. People who read newspapers, magazines, and books had a 33% lower risk of Alzheimer's disease.

These findings suggest that keeping the mind engaged through reading may support cognitive health in later life. However, the researchers emphasize that most of the available studies identify associations rather than proving that reading itself prevents dementia.

How Reading May Change the Structure of the Brain.

In a recent development, what could explain the many benefits associated with reading? Brain imaging research offers some intriguing clues.

It has been revealed that studies have identified differences in brain structure associated with reading, particularly in regions responsible for language, learning, emotional regulation, and executive control. These differences include greater brain volume and larger cortical areas in certain regions, as well as thicker cortex in areas notable for language processing.

Researchers have also observed stronger connections within the brain's white matter, which helps different regions communicate and supports the processing of language.

Although much of this evidence is correlational, some research suggests that reading-related training can directly influence the brain's physical structure.

In one small study involving 11 children with dyslexia between the ages of 7 and 11, researchers tested an eight-week training program designed to strengthen reading abilities. The exercises involved mental imagery, articulation, and tracing letters, combinations of letters, and words.

In a recent development, following the program, the children demonstrated significant improvements in reading skills. Brain scans also revealed increases in gray matter volume in key regions, providing evidence that reading interventions can be accompanied by measurable structural changes in the brain.

Why Reading Together May Offer Even Greater Benefits.

Industry observers note that reading does not have to be a solitary activity. Researchers suggest that sharing books with other people can add a valuable social dimension, creating opportunities for connection and potentially improving both mental and physical well-being.

The Queen's Reading Room has made this idea a central part of its mission. Released on social media in January 2021, the initiative originally provided a way for people to exchange book recommendations and stay connected during pandemic lockdowns.

In a recent development, it has since expanded into an international community reaching more than 12 million people across 184 countries. Through its flagship festival, neuroscience research, and other programs, the charity works to make reading a more prominent part of everyday life.

Its activities also extend to homeless shelters and domestic violence refuges, where shared reading is used as a therapeutic resource to help people during recovery.

The social connections created through reading groups may be particularly valuable for older adults, who can face increased isolation and reduced opportunities to participate in physically demanding activities.

Recent findings suggest that professor Sahakian said: "Shared reading groups and book clubs appear to provide additional benefits through social interaction. They can help people feel less isolated and more connected to others, which has been shown to reduce the risk of dementia.

"While these clubs can be fun and beneficial at any age, they're perfect for older people who may not be so mobile - they might not be able to go out as an exercise group or a cycling group, but a reading group is something most people can enjoy.".

The researchers' overall message is that people can benefit from reading at any stage of life. Whether someone enjoys novels, newspapers, comics, or stories shared with friends, making time for reading may help support cognitive health, emotional well-being, and social connection.

Vicki Perrin, CEO of The Queen's Reading Room, added: "At The Queen's Reading Room, we have seen first-hand the transformative power of individual and shared reading to change lives. Books truly do make life better, and we're excited to do our bit to help everyone, everywhere, make more room for reading.".
