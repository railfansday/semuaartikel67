Industry observers note that as far as Quentin Tarantino movies go, just about everyone loves Pulp Fiction. Reservoir Dogs and Once Upon a Time… in Hollywood are also widely regarded, while the Kill Bill films and Django Unchained each have a pretty fervent fanbase. But with so many great entries in his filmography, it’s inevitable that at least a few of his movies would get lost in the shuffle. One of those often overlooked films is his 2015 Western thriller, which may not be top-tier Tarantino, but it’s still an excellent film that combines the strengths of two of his previous entries.

In a recent development, the Hateful Eight, which is at the moment free to stream (with ads) on Tubi, takes place in the 1870s in the wintry Wyoming Territory. Kurt Russell is John "the Hangman" Ruth, a bounty hunter bringing in Daisy Domergue (Jennifer Jason Leigh), a gang member with a major price on her head. He’ll collect his bounty in the town of Red Rock, with a stopover at a place called Minnie's Haberdashery on the way, but Ruth’s driver (James Parks) has to stay ahead of an impending blizzard.

On the way to Minnie’s, Ruth’s carriage picks up two unexpected passengers, both of whom lost their horses in the snow and will surely die in the blizzard. First there’s Major Marquis Warren (Samuel L. Jackson), a fellow bounty hunter and former Union Army general​​​​​​​ who keeps a personal letter to him from Abraham Lincoln in his breast pocket. The second unexpected passenger is Chris Mannix (Walton Goggins), the latest sheriff of Red Rock and the son of a Confederate war hero. While Ruth is suspicious of both of them, he decides to let them join him on the way to Minnie’s.

According to recent reports, once they arrive at Minnie’s, however, Minnie isn’t there. Instead, the haberdashery has apparently been left​​​​​​​ in the care of a Mexican man named Señor Bob (Demián Bichir) while Minnie is visiting her mother. Also present are three other men​​​​​​​ that Warren, a frequent guest of Minnie’s, doesn’t recognize. As the entire group is snowed in for the night, it’s eventually revealed​​​​​​​ that nobody is quite who they say they are and bloodshed ultimately ensues.

In a recent development, like I said, The Hateful Eight is not top-tier Tarantino. Tarantino’s best work has generally come when he’s charting latest territory for himself. Inglourious Basterds​​​​​​​ was his first war movie, and with its heist-like plot and unabashed joy in rewriting history, it demonstrated what Tarantino could do with that genre. Django Unchained was his first Western, and, with its heavy influence from Spaghetti Westerns and Tarantino’s trademark talkative, over-the-top characters and depiction of extreme violence, it showed what a Tarantino Western looks like.

Recent findings suggest that the Hateful Eight doesn’t do that. In fact, it can pretty easily be summed up in classic Hollywood this-meets-that fashion: The Hateful Eight is Django Unchained meets Reservoir Dogs.

It being another Western with broad, loud, talkative characters, it has the same feel as Django Unchained. Originally, The Hateful Eight was a Django sequel called Django in White Hell, but Tarantino found that Django didn’t fit the story. And while Django isn't in it, it’s clear that this movie is in the same ballpark.

As for the Reservoir Dogs part of it, it is narratively similar, with it being tightly contained primarily in one location, with a bunch of untrustworthy characters who don’t really know each other very well. It’s also a bit of a whodunit, just like Reservoir Dogs is with its “Who’s the rat?” plot thread.

None of this is to say that The Hateful Eight isn't a good movie — not at all. The Hateful Eight is an expertly crafted film which begins with a slow burn and delivers surprise after surprise once the bullets start flying. The movie blends a lot of genres, with elements of a Western, a thriller, and even a bit of broad comedy, but it's most effective as a mystery. Not only does the plot slowly unravel as Ruth tries to figure out who he can trust, but each of the characters does, too​​​​​​​.

Speaking of those characters, there are no good guys in the movie, but there are a lot of bad guys you end up rooting for because they are incredibly entertaining to watch. Samuel L. Jackson’s character is joyously funny, and Kurt Russell’s hangman is a mean grouch with a strange but consistent set of principles. Also, Walton Goggins, who was previously in Django, racks up another memorable Tarantino character in this movie. (Let’s hope he indicates up in Tarantino’s 10th flick and takes a break from those damn Walmart commercials already).

While it stays firmly within previously charted territory for Tarantino, The Hateful Eight is very entertaining and very funny. It has all the hallmarks of a great Tarantino movie, with lots of fun characters, over-the-top violence, and unexpected plot twists. It may not be top-tier Tarantino, but it’s a really good time.
