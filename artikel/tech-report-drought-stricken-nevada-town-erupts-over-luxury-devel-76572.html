Tech Report: Drought-Stricken Nevada Town Erupts Over Luxury Development’s Plan for ‘Firefighter Pond’

Should a private developer have the ability to build a human-made pond in the middle of a drought-stricken region? What if that developer states that firefighters can access the pond to fight wildfires?

That’s the question at the center of a growing debate in Clark County, NV, where water is scarce and locals are skeptical about a luxury development's plan to create a massive water feature.

Joel Laub and his partner, Randall Jones, began developing the Reserve at Red Rock Canyon in 2022, aiming to create a high-end luxury community amid the Nevada wilderness. The Reserve is being built on 65 acres that were once home to the Bonnie Springs Ranch, a historic, Old West-themed family resort that once featured horseback riding and a small zoo.

Two-acre empty plots in the community start at $7.5 million. But that’s not what has the community up in arms. Instead, it’s what the Reserve is touting on its website: a large human-made pond in the middle of the property.

To outsiders, it seems like just one feature in a long list of amenities. But for some locals in Clark County, which was declared a natural disaster area due to drought earlier this year, the pond seems like a slap in the face—even as the developers attempt to position it as a fire safety "dip pond.".

“This little dip pond here is really fire safety as an afterthought, and I think that’s pretty obvious in terms of even their planning,” mentioned Sarah J. McCrea-Dameron, a Blue Diamond, NV, resident and former deputy fire chief from Las Vegas Fire & Rescue, at a zoning commission meeting this past week. “It’s not in an area that’s necessary, and it’s not the best use of that water and that precious resource for our community.“.

The Reserve at Red Rock Canyon development sits within the Red Rock Canyon Conservation Area, an expansive 195,000-acre area within the Mojave Desert. Aztec sandstone foundations dating back to 180 million years can be found in the area, along with sea fossils and hundreds of local bird species. More than 3 million people visit the area each year.

Water for the area is supplied by the Colorado River. In recent years, the river and the several lakes it feeds have been pushed to the limit, as upcoming development, coupled with more frequent droughts, have strained resources.

The river doesn’t just supply necessary water to communities across the Southwest: It also produces much-needed hydropower for the region.

Earlier this year, Lake Powell, one of the lakes fed by the Colorado River, dropped to its lowest level in history, threatening to shut down the Glen Canyon Dam, which supplies power to Wyoming, Utah, Colorado, Latest Mexico, Arizona, Nevada, and Nebraska.

In a recent development, communities around the Red Rock Canyon Conservation Area take the mantle of water conservation seriously. In fact, in 2020, the nearby community of Blue Diamond issued a temporary moratorium on “the issuance of additional water commitments” that remains in place.

Now, as the developers Laub and Jones offer unbuilt lots in their new development for $7.5 million, they are pitching the proposed pond as a neighborhood amenity.

“Paths and roads will meander past water features fed by the canyon’s natural springs,” the development’s website states.

But those types of features are typically prohibited by Clark County’s Title 30, which governs building and development in the area. Title 30 currently prohibits the creation of human-made lakes of any size and water features greater than 10 square feet of surface area. So the developers stressed that the human-made pond would be fed via private water rights, and therefore not impede on community resources. Instead of using Colorado River resources, the project would be fed using groundwater.

Bob Gronauer, the developers' lawyer, noted the water feature would be a “dip pond”—a water source for use by firefighting helicopters. Wildfires are a frequent problem in the region. In 2025 alone, there were 619 fires in the state, which destroyed more than 368,000 acres.

Industry observers note that in fact, the developers have already signed a use agreement with the Nevada Division of Forestry. The developers claimed the NDF came to them to ask them to create the pond, but the NDF mentioned the developers approached them about the partnership, the Nevada Independent reported.

According to recent reports, "We reached out to the Bureau of Land Management Red Rock management staff at the visitor center, Save Red Rock, Friends of Red Rock, as well as the Nature Conservancy Nevada immediately upon purchasing the property to work with them to make this development compatible with the Red Rock Canyon National Conservation Area," Laub tells Realtor.com. (Laub is the chairman of the Nature Conservancy board.).

Laub indicates the idea for the dip pond actually came from the BLM.

Recent findings suggest that "They asked if we could work with them on fire management in Red Rock Canyon, and would we consider providing water for firefighting. Of course our answer was yes. We have a 220,000-gallon water tank designed into our water system for supplying water to our fire hydrants, and we agreed to provide a fire hydrant for the BLM water tankers to fill up in the event of a wildfire," Laub continues.

"The ponds are designed to fit into the canyon ecosystem and look natural but also to be compatible with helicopter firefighting.".

Laub tells Realtor.com that they've already been testing the wells in the canyon, and "none of the testing results from these wells has indicated a draw down of significance from our use of the water system.".

He also notes that they purchased the water rights from Bonnie Springs Ranch, which had been in use for more than 60 years.

"The property has had ponds, streams, pools, and springs from the original ranch in 1952," he explains.

To expedite the creation of the dip pools, the developers proposed an amendment to Title 30 that would add language permitting “a body of water, served from standalone private water rights, within 1,000 feet of a National Conservation Area, intended for fire suppression and life safety purposes as agreed upon with a State or Federal Agency.”In October, Clark County Planning Commission's professional staff recommended denying the amendment.

"As proposed, there is no indication of how these water features would be designed, the capacity of these water features, or other potential uses for the stored water. The proliferation of non-essential water features will place an additional burden on the existing water supply," the staff recommendation read. "Furthermore, this request may encourage additional non-essential water features within residential and nonresidential development.".

According to recent reports, however, the Planning Commission approved the amendment by a vote of 6-1. It then went to the Clark County Zoning Board for approval. On Wednesday night, during the public comment period of the meeting, around a dozen community members spoke out against the planned feature.

It has been revealed that “You can design a decorative water feature and then call it a dip pond. You can offer to let the Forest Service use it knowing that they never will because of the way it’s designed and location, but that doesn’t make that decorative water feature a piece of critical fire infrastructure," stated former Clark County Fire Capt. Erik Klausen.

“They’re trying to put a plastic fire helmet on this water feature and tell us it’s about public safety,” he continued.

At the end of the public comment period, all seven commissioners voted in favor of the proposed amendment, though, as Commissioner Justin Jones noted, the approval of the amendment does not mean the dip pond project has been approved.

Furthermore, noted Commissioner Marilyn Kirkpatrick, the project needs to be fit for purpose.

"If it's a fire-suppression pond, then it has to be a fire-suppression pond in my mind," said Kirkpatrick. "It should stay as a fire-supression pond, not as a recreational venue, not as irrigation, because that water is supposed to be there for a reason.".

Realtor.com has reached out to developer Jones and lawyer Gronauer for comment.
