Industry observers note that companies don't usually announce good news in a compensation filing on the Friday after Thanksgiving. But a regulatory filing that Carnival (CCL +0.54%) released on Friday contains one of the clearest signals yet about the cruise operator's recovery.

In August 2022, Carnival's board gave CEO Josh Weinstein a special grant of 500,000 restricted stock units. The award could pay out anywhere from zero to twice that amount in February 2028, depending on Carnival's average adjusted return on invested capital (ROIC) in fiscal 2026 and 2027. ROIC measures how much profit a organization squeezes out of the money tied up in its business, which for a cruise line means a lot of very expensive ships.

Sources indicate that when the board set those goals, Carnival was still working its way back from the pandemic shutdown that had idled its fleet. According to the filing, the award was nonetheless projected to pay out at the maximum level of 200% under its original terms.

Industry observers note that on Nov. 24, the compensation committee changed the deal. The performance period now ends this Nov. 30 instead of a year later, and the award will vest on Dec. 21. At the same time, the committee raised the bar: the ROIC goal is now tougher than originally set, two other targets tied to 2026 earnings and emissions were added, and the maximum payout was cut to 180%, or 900,000 shares. That's worth about $25 million at Friday's price of $28.26. The filing doesn't explain why the cap was lowered, but the effect is that Weinstein gives up as many as 100,000 shares in exchange for being paid more than a year earlier. He also has to hold the shares he receives after taxes until December 2028 and must return them if he quits before the original 2028 vesting date.

The details of executive pay can be a red flag. Here, the more telling point is the one buried in the middle: four years ago, the board set return targets for 2026 and 2027, and Carnival has been on track to beat them by enough to max out the award.

In a recent development, carnival's latest results back that up. In its fiscal third quarter, which ended Aug. 31, the manufacturer reported record revenue of $8.4 billion and record net income of $1.9 billion. Customer deposits, a sign of future demand, hit a third-quarter record of $7.6 billion. Management said bookings for 2027 are at record levels for both occupancy and pricing.

Industry observers note that the organization is also spending its cash on shareholders and on its balance sheet. Carnival had repurchased about $1.2 billion of stock through late September this year and paid out $618 million in dividends in the first nine months of the fiscal year, after paying none in the same period a year earlier. Total debt fell to $24.6 billion as of Aug. 31, down from $27.4 billion at the end of the prior fiscal year, and S&P upgraded the company to investment-grade during the quarter.

It has been revealed that for the full fiscal year ending this month, Carnival expects adjusted earnings of about $2.24 per share. At $28.15, the stock trades at roughly 13 times that figure, below its 52-week high of $34.03.

There are real risks. Higher fuel prices and currency moves cut about $0.10 per share from third-quarter adjusted earnings, and the company cited additional costs due to disruptions related to the Middle East conflict. Debt is falling but still large, and cruising is the kind of discretionary spending families cut first in a downturn.

Still, the compensation filing adds something you don't often get: evidence that Carnival's own targets, set when the outlook was far murkier, turned out to be easy. For investors looking at travel stocks, a company delivering record profits, shrinking its debt, and paying a dividend again at about 13 times earnings looks like a reasonable buy. Investors focused on income can monitor whether the payout continues to grow before treating it as one of their core dividend stocks.
