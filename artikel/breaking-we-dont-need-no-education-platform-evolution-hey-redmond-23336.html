📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Microsoft pulls the plug on free student email, but people are learning fast.

Industry observers note that "Give me the child until he is seven, and I will give you the man." This saying about the importance of influence in early education is variously attributed either to St Ignatius  of Loyola, founder of the Jesuits, or Aristotle, depending on whether you wanted to entrust your very young son to Catholic priests or ancient Greeks. Fortunately for both groups, perhaps, neither of them ever said that, even though both certainly believed it.

According to recent reports, another group that certainly believes it is the cadre of tech organization policy makers, which goes a long way to explain the cultural and legislative backlash to what happens to young people online. The least pernicious and longest established example of the catch 'em young principle is the educational discount for hardware and software. Apple has been the highest profile company to do this, with hardware discounts and aggressively positioned complete service packages for educational institutions, often integrated with funding authorities.

It has been revealed that if you grow up using a package or platform, it becomes your first tech language and you're likely to become a lifetime customer. Microsoft has also played this long game, seasoned where it can with curriculum integration. Its latest wheeze, described by the tech giant as educational platform evolution is a response to institutions' changing needs, with clearer pathways to adopting and enabling new technologies. Yada, as we say, yada.

The evolved platform removes email and Teams from the free student tier, while imposing the requirement for Windows hardware to qualify, along with comprehensive restructuring of higher tiers and storage not necessarily to the advantage of the educators.

Recent findings suggest that this is the same sort of evolution to evolving needs as the British Empire brought to natives of other lands. Hello, India. Greetings, Africa. Whassup, America! These warships packed with gun-toting Brits are here to evolve your changing needs in local resource management.

Industry observers note that microsoft has realized what Imperial Great Britain adopted as its primary principle. If you can get your future, ahem, customers over a barrel, then not only can you imprint your brand on their minds, you can take their money. You have to come up with some balderdash to cover it, whether it's about civilization and progress or enabling educators to… oh God, these poor fingers can't even type this stuff.

Missing completely is the idea that educators' evolving needs may not include spending even more money on basic necessary services when budgets are stretched tight. Moreover, while existing uses of the old tier can stay on it until the end of next year, new sign-ups have to be on the Enhanced Speedy looks.

Keen students of history, especially those in the former colonial lands of Eastern North America, may care to refresh their memories of events some 250 years ago. It is quite natural, when being bent over a barrel ceases to be an amusing pastime, to seek a less barrel-shaped position.

Industry observers note that in this case, the pressure to find alternatives has many elements. As well as the cost,  support for Macs and Chromebooks becomes problematic. Both platforms are heavily represented in education at various levels, with the Macbook Neo thundering onto the battlefield with colors flying. Add to that the pressure towards digital sovereignty in Microsoft's own colonial outposts, the accelerants for an Old World version of the Boston Tea Party are only building. We even have a Boston of our very own. It's very nice.

It has been revealed that microsoft isn't threatening anyone with guns and rapine, not yet anyway, and the whipping away of free student access to email and Teams only feels like extortion because of the misery it is causing across the sector. It is perfectly legal for Microsoft to cause as much suffering coated in balderdash as it likes. It is also perfectly legal to return the favor by actively seeking a sector-wide migration strategy. There are pioneers to learn from, especially huge multi-state, multi-supply chain organizations like Airbus and the various European governmental entities that are some way down that path already.

This growing pool of expertise may be the most important factor in being able to react to Microsoft's efforts to turn education into a science fair experiment in high velocity frog boiling — one where the frog most definitely feels the heat — that and the growing distaste for the antics of American substantial tech.

It's not just the forcing of unwanted technology that makes the barrel increasingly unbearable, nor using that as an excuse to charge more, it's what the companies are actually trying to do in Europe. Google Chromebooks are nice. Google wolves invading your villages because the company illegally erased their ecosystem are not nice. You thought those Wi-Fi snooping Maps cars were bad?

According to recent reports, the Microsoft educational platform "evolution" is foolhardy, counterproductive, and hugely tone deaf. It will not make it new lifetime customers, it will increase the speed at which its already hostile constituency grows resentment and appetite for radical action. Colonies revolt when the time is right, and empires are lost when arrogance and under-estimation of the situation combine with mismanagement and strategic blindness.

The US midterms revealed how that storm is blowing in. To choose this time to provoke an entire global sector is functional illiteracy writ large. Perhaps Microsoft should get an education. ®.
