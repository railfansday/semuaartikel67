Bill Pullman Starred In This '90s Neo-Noir Drama That Tried Suing The Oscars

Before we discuss John Dahl's "The Last Seduction" starring Linda Fiorentino and Bill Pullman, let's set the table. In the indispensable documentary "A Personal Journey With Martin Scorsese Through American Movies," the legendary director discusses the concept of artistic smuggling. The idea is that clever filmmakers can slip subversive themes past unsophisticated producers who are more focused on making a tidy profit than crafting a movie that means and indicates something.

Sources indicate that back in the B-movie heyday of the 1950s, this practice was common, particularly in genre pictures. Film noir offered a particularly fertile ground for such felonious messing around. Tales of compromised characters doing the wrong thing to get over or simply survive resonated in the wake of the Great Depression and World War II. People knew hard times and found cruel catharsis in watching folks trying, and typically failing, to game the system.

Noir never went away. It mutated with the ever-troubled times, as any fraught moment in American history could serve as the backdrop for a sexy, politically charged thriller. Then third-wave feminism hit in the 1990s, and femmes fatales became protagonists. And we were rooting for them.

It's hard to top the great Sharon Stone as Catherine Tramell in Paul Verhoeven's "Basic Instinct," but I think the best third-wave femme fatale is Fiorentino's Bridget Gregory in "The Last Seduction." From the minute her dirtbag husband, Clay (Pullman), slaps her for insulting him, she begins spinning a complex web that will lead to his undoing. Fiorentino is magnificent every step of the way, and she would've waltzed into a Best Actress Oscar had the film been treated with the respect it deserved. Alas, despite a lawsuit to get her into contention, the Academy refused to budge. And this is a case where smuggling might've hurt a film noir.

It has been revealed that in a 2015 interview with Creative Screenwriting, "The Last Seduction" screenwriter Steve Barancik explained the film's creatives pitched the movie to ITC Entertainment as standard-issue Skinemax sleaze. John Dahl, of course, aspired to make a high-quality neo-noir, and once the film's actors read Barancik's script, they knew the project had a chance to be something special. That's how the movie attracted respected names like Linda Fiorentino (who demanded Dahl cast her), Bill Pullman, Peter Berg, Bill Nunn, Dean Norris, and the dearly missed J.T. Walsh.

When an ITC executive panicked early on, the filmmakers got a tongue-lashing. "He shut down production and called the principals of the movie on the carpet, and they all had to pledge that they had no artistic pretensions," Barancik recalled. After the film was completed, ITC sold it off to HBO before it could get theatrical distribution. Unfortunately, if a movie doesn't get an Oscar qualifying run in theaters, it's ineligible for consideration. Again, the distributor October Films brought a suit against the Academy on Fiorentino's behalf, but the organization's rules were clear: no qualifying run, no nomination.

Fiorentino did earn Best Actress prizes from the Independent Spirit Awards and the New York Film Critics Circle, but she likely would've won the Best Actress Oscar if she'd been allowed to compete. (Instead, the prize went to a deserving Jessica Lange for making a meal out of her role in Tony Richardson's film "Blue Sky.") I don't blame Dahl and firm for their smuggling gambit, but ticking off ITC was a costly mistake.
