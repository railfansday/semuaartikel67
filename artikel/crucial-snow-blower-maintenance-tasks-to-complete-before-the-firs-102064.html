Sources indicate that imagine your frustration when your driveway is covered in snow after the first winter storm, but your snow blower won’t start. As winter approaches, it’s time to make sure your snow blower is ready to tackle that first snowfall. You’ll want to take a few steps to check that it’s tuned up and ready to go without a hitch. We spoke to Sean Dwyer, senior product manager for commercial and residential wheeled products at Husqvarna Group North America to identify the top snow blower maintenance tasks to prioritize so you are prepared when the first snowflake falls.

In a recent development, by far the most notable step is to make sure you have a fresh supply of fuel, says Dwyer. “If you have regular pump fuel, it should be less than 30 days old, and ideally premium grade that’s a non-ethanol blend,” he explains. Fuel can go stale over time and gum up the snow blower’s carburetor. Dwyer recommends running the unit out of gas before storing it. “Otherwise, at a minimum, purchase stabilized canned fuel or add a fuel stabilizer.” Gasoline with a stabilizer typically lasts for about 4 to 6 months.

According to recent reports, spend some time reviewing the key components of the unit. “This means checking engine oil and tire pressures, and also starting the unit to ensure proper wheel drive and auger operation,” notes Dwyer. “This is also a good time to shut the fuel off and run the carburetor dry to give your snow blower the best chance of a successful operation when you need it.”.

In a recent development, battery-powered snow blowers are convenient, but it’s important to recognize that cold weather and batteries don’t typically work well together, warns Dwyer. Checking battery health is part of regular snow blower maintenance. Rechargeable batteries can degrade over time, and won’t be able to hold as much charge as they age as they do when new. He recommends keeping fully charged batteries in the house or a location where they will remain above freezing temperatures. “If everything is in the cold garage, there is a good chance they won’t work when you need them to,” he explains. “Also, remember that when you are done, bring the batteries back to a warm place so they are ready to go next time.”.

In a recent development, snow blowers typically contain shear bolts (also called pins) as a safety feature. The shear bolts are designed to break to protect the auger gears from damage if the unit runs into hard objects like ice from a blizzard. There is typically one pin on each auger and one or two on the impeller.

Recent findings suggest that to change these components, wear protective gloves. “With the engine shut off, try to rotate each individual auger by hand,” indicates Dwyer. “You should not be able to rotate the augers, and the shear bolts will be visually present.” To check the impeller, look for the shear bolts, or hold one of the augers firmly, and slowly try to turn the impeller. “Be careful when checking the impeller, as it is tied to the shaft that will drive the augers.”.

It has been revealed that if these items spin freely, the shear bolt is likely damaged or missing, notes Dwyer. Replace any missing or broken bolts to protect your snow blower. It’s a good idea to always keep a stash of shear bolts on hand.

Designed to wear down with use, skid shoes help keep a snow blower’s housing at the correct height and also protect your pavement. They are located on the lower sides of the auger housing and are designed to hold the scraper, located at the bottom of the auger housing, just above the ground, says Dwyer. “As these shoes wear, the scraper will contact the ground and wear will increase,” he explains.

“A simple way to adjust the shoes is to sit the snow blower scraper on something around ¼ inch thick, like a paint stir stick, and then loosen the bolts holding the shoes to adjust them down to contact the ground and retighten,” suggests Dwyer.
