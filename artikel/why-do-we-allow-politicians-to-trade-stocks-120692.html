Why Do We Allow Politicians to Trade Stocks?

Earlier this year, before coming on to Rolling Stone’s staff full time, I sold some stock. Back in the early 2000s, my grandmother had willed me a handful of shares of Disney stock, which sat around appreciating value and were worth a few grand. I told myself I was selling off the Disney stock for ethical reasons — many places I wrote for as a freelancer asked employees to sign contracts that attest that they won’t buy, sell, or hold stock in individual companies they cover. Really, though, I just needed the cash.

Industry observers note that donald Trump, however, has no such qualms. Nor does he really need a little extra cash. In his time as president, he has made billions of dollars, some of it through the sale of stock in companies that his policies have directly affected.

Recent findings suggest that this week, after exerting a considerable amount of political pressure, Trump convinced the board of Nvidia, the most valuable firm in the world, to reinstate its disgraced CEO Jensen Huang after an absurd scandal that revealed Huang was potentially complicit in helping one of his mistresses buy up advanced Nvidia computer chips on behalf of the Chinese government. Huang’s ouster — and the whole selling super computer parts to a Chinese spy thing — had tanked Nvidia’s stock in recent weeks, dragging most of Wall Street down with it, which was not something that Trump wanted. But now, Huang is back as CEO, and the numbers are once again going up — including Trump’s own. According to financial disclosures from September, Trump owns hundreds of thousands of shares in Nvidia — which could have gained as much as $15 million in value in the past 48 hours alone, since Huang’s reinstatement.

The scale of Trump’s corruption and grift in office is something we have truly never seen before, but elected officials making big money off the stock market is nothing latest. There are no provisions prohibiting politicians from investing in private companies — only limp guidelines passed in 2012 that force them to disclose their positions online and promise not to use privileged information. Shockingly, that hasn’t stopped many of them from taking advantage of their position for years. The numbers don’t lie: There are even indexes that often out-perform the S&P 500 and multiple major hedge funds just by mirroring the exact trades that former House Speaker Nancy Pelosi makes. Recent findings suggest that trump, of course, has taken this to its extreme end, at times appearing to manipulate markets on behalf of his friends and political allies, while enriching himself through everything from stock sales to pump-and-dump crypto scams. In 2025 alone, the president’s annual earnings were more than $2.2 billion, which makes Pelosi’s millions look like the take from a podunk coin laundromat. But ultimately, the discrepancy is beside the point. Why do we let any of these people trade stocks at all? Why is a cub reporter at a newspaper held to a higher standard than someone holding federal elected office? Why are members of Congress allowed to pass legislation pertaining to companies they have a financial stake in? Why is the president of the United States allowed to wheel and deal on Wall Street as he makes decisions that can turn the global economy on a dime?

According to recent reports, in a functioning democracy, this would all be banned. And I mean all of it: a total ban on owning individual stock for any elected representative. Hell, ban their staff and consultants too, but start with the electeds. The point of representative democracy is for the public to appoint someone to serve as their avatar in government. That person’s job, then, becomes representing their constituents. They should have no other occupation, no other outside interests. I’d go as far as to say that they should have to sell off any equity in businesses they themselves started (and get rid of this nonsense of “blind trusts”). If they must hold stocks, they should have to dump their portfolio into a mutual fund or market index, and be prohibited from buying or selling at all until they leave public office. These people are supposed to work for us. That doesn’t mean the job is easy. Freshmen members of Congress who don’t come from money often have to get roommates when they come to D.C., because their base salary of $174,00 a year (in the House) can’t keep up with the cost of living their new life in politics. So increase their pay! I have no problem with every member of Congress, every major elected official, making a salary of a million dollars a year. That’s barely more than the league minimum for a rookie in the NFL, and I’m pretty comfortable saying that more people depend on the job performance of the former Speaker of the House than they do on the quality of the third-string offensive lineman for the San Francisco 49ers (no offense to Enrique Cruz Jr., who has a bright career ahead of him). Pay them all a million bucks, two million bucks, whatever — but strip them of everything else.

Recent findings suggest that it’s clear, too, that the American people are fed up with this. In exit polls following the 2026 midterms, 90 percent of voters remarked that corruption was a crucial issue to them. Many of the country’s political failings stem from a lack of trust — in leaders, in institutions, in the building blocks of society and social contract. It’s hard to rebuild those with leaders who are so blatantly helping themselves to a bigger share of every pie that comes across their table. Democrats, newly poised to take charge of the House and Senate, have pledged to bring their investigative might down on Trump and his children, but my fear is that the rot will remain even if the most corrupt president in history is taken down. Trump is a particularly egregious offender, but he didn’t create the system he exploited. There are 15 members of Congress with net worths in the hundreds of millions, and dozens more with millions in play. As the system is at the moment constructed, they’re all going to continue trading stock — an act that benefits no one but themselves. Hakeem Jeffries, Pelosi’s assumed successor as House Speaker, has pledged to make banning stock trades a priority next year, but it’s unclear how the push will play out with Trump unlikely to sign such a ban — at least as long as it includes the president — and if Democrats will be as motivated to pass it when he leaves office.

In a recent development, running for office should be a selfless act. The point should be to subsume your own sense of self into the collective will of the people who elected you. If you want to get rich in America, go ahead and get rich, but know that we elect our leaders to serve, not rule. It’s time we started making sure they do so with both clear eyes and empty pockets.
