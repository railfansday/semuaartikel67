Ravi Dhaval, Fortune 500 advisor & inventor of patent-pending (#19/440,089), quantum-powered AI platform for proactive global cyber defense.

It has been revealed that for years, executive security briefings have centered on one fear: machine-speed attacks powered by artificial intelligence (AI). Boards fund automated response, AI red teaming and zero-trust policies to protect the corporate perimeter. Yet most cyber defense remains reactive, built on detection after the fact and limited by classical computing’s difficulty simulating complex attack paths.

That gap is where I focused my published patent application, aiming to close it by simulating attack paths in real time, sharing threat intelligence without exposing raw data, and orchestrating defenses automatically.​.

In a recent development, the more unsettling reality sits outside the boardroom. While Fortune 500 companies harden their defenses, everyday people face AI-native attacks aimed at their finances and personal data, with nothing comparable protecting them. Everyday life has become the primary attack surface.

In a recent development, indirect prompt injection hides instructions in content an AI system later reads and obeys, and OWASP ranks prompt injection the top risk for applications built on large language models. One of the newest delivery methods is the poisoned instruction file. In October 2026, researchers at Forever Security reported finding a malicious AGENTS.md file, disguised as engineering notes, in a customer’s downloads folder, which is the one folder a website can write to without a click. AI coding agents such as OpenAI Codex and Claude Code follow the instruction files in whatever folder they open, so the planted text behaved like a program while slipping past every control built for executables. In testing, it led agents to rewrite their own configuration, opening the door to malware installation and stolen login tokens.

According to recent reports, voice and video impersonation is another growing threat. The FBI warns that criminals use generative AI to clone the voices of loved ones and to produce convincing video for live calls. The FTC notes a scammer needs only a short audio clip, which can often be pulled from content posted online.

The growing list of connected devices in the home adds still more exposure. Thermostats, smart TVs, voice assistants and even light bulbs all exchange data. The FBI cautions that one unsecured IoT device can give an attacker a path into the home router and everything behind it.

It has been revealed that this holiday season, many consumers will install shopping agents: AI deal finders and checkout assistants that request access to email, saved cards and sometimes even password managers. OWASP calls this excessive agency, meaning an AI holding more permission than its task requires. A hidden instruction in a fake review or poisoned listing could steer such an agent into leaking credentials or placing fraudulent orders.

It has been revealed that antivirus software and firewalls hunt for malicious signatures and binaries. A natural-language instruction, a cloned voice or an edited settings file is none of those. As the Forever researchers argue, nearly every defense we rely on assumes a human who clicks, approves or runs the code. Once an agent takes the human’s place, that assumption breaks. Asking the public to fight AI attacks with tools from an earlier era invites systemic failure.

Businesses have a system for defending one another, but the public doesn’t. Since Presidential Decision Directive 63 in 1998, Information Sharing and Analysis Centers (ISACs) have let critical-infrastructure sectors pool threat intelligence and defend collectively.

Recent findings suggest that consumers can report to the FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center or the FTC, but those channels exist to support investigations after the fact. None of them pools what one household experiences into protection for the next one in real time.

Industry observers note that the biggest gap needs a latest institution: Consumer ISACs that collect telemetry on consumer-targeted AI attacks and voice-cloning operations and turn it into protective updates for home gateways and phones. Natural members are ISPs, mobile carriers, device makers and AI platforms, the companies already standing between consumers and attackers. CISA would be the federal partner, mirroring how sector ISACs pair industry operators with government. Privacy is the hard part, but solvable. In my patent application’s federated design, for instance, I chose to pair secure enclaves with threshold cryptography so that no single party ever holds the full decryption key.

It has been revealed that public health relies on clean-water infrastructure, not purification tablets in every home. Similarly, internet providers should build behavioral analytics into residential gateways to protect devices that can’t run security software of their own. The attack-path modeling that enterprise platforms are beginning to use, which maps every route an intruder could take and ranks the most damaging, could inform simpler gateway logic that spots and blocks an intruder moving between smart devices.

Consumer AI tools should ship with least-privilege permissions and require explicit human approval before touching credentials or payments. Because no single control reliably stops prompt injection, protections must be layered. Vendors should also disclose which models, plug-ins and data connections their agents can reach.

Sources indicate that corporate defenses are only as strong as the exposed public around them. We built collective defense for pipelines and power grids. It is time to build it for people, and consumer ISACs are where security leaders can start. Leadership will be measured not only by how well we protect balance sheets, but by how effectively we advocate for everyone else’s digital safety.​.
