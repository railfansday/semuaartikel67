Timothée Chalamet Came Over for Lunch—and a Cover Story

The history of actors appearing in Vanity Fair goes back about as far as the movies themselves. Making a studio-orchestrated, Edward Steichen–captured debut in the pages of this magazine was a milestone in the careers of Gary Cooper, Gloria Swanson, Anna May Wong, and dozens of others, many of whom never made it to Technicolor.

In a recent development, on more than one occasion, the idea of Timothée Chalamet as the last male movie star has been a debate here at the office, usually ending in uneasy agreement: He is one of very few left, maybe the only one of his generation. He is in the tradition of Marlon Brando and Leonardo DiCaprio in that his knowability runs inverse to his fame. Like other endangered species, his extreme rarity makes him celebrated and isolated in equal measure. And with each of these stars, one rule usually holds true: The bigger they get, the less they open their mouth.

All of which is why we are thrilled to have Chalamet on our cover as he sets out to open the final, breathtaking installment of Denis Villeneuve’s Dune trilogy. He and our team, led by photographer Aidan Zamiri, fashion editor Taylor McNeill (with whom I was an assistant at this magazine in 2010), and VF global creative director Jennifer Pastore, trekked to Iceland for a multiday shoot to capture his cover story “A Star on Planet Earth.” Everyone involved—including and especially Timothée—approached it more like a film, or maybe a music video, than a typical cover, and there is a spiritual quality that runs throughout the pictures. “I love that being on the edge of a cliff in Iceland,” Timothée tells me, “you get the same feeling as being in Grand Central.”.

Sources indicate that timothée also had the idea that I write the cover story myself, and while I was reluctant, I eventually thought, Why not mix it up? He came over to my apartment in early October for a conversation about Dune; his career; the careers of other actors, musicians, and painters; and especially the careers of the Upcoming York Knicks. I took away from our meeting that he may be a movie star, sure, but he approaches everything as an artist. The writing process is a humbling one, though—okay, it’s agony—and since filing the cover story, I have come to believe that every editor really should write a feature now and again to make us more understanding of our writers. Perhaps once a year. Consider it a new rule at Vanity Fair.

Timothée and I were both reading Kevin Roose’s The AGI Chronicles when we met up, and he pointed out that the first several chapters of the book might as well have been the source material for Artificial, Luca Guadagnino’s newly introduced film starring Andrew Garfield and Yura Borisov. The film presents a villain origin story of OpenAI’s cofounders, particularly CEO Sam Altman. It is the natural inheritor to David Fincher and Aaron ­Sorkin’s 2010 film, The Social Network, which similarly helped answer the how-did-we-get-here question for another quite recent era of technology.

It has been revealed that in case you missed it, Altman was recently on the cover of Vanity Fair, sitting atop our revived Upcoming Establishment list. It inspired a torrent of furious reader feedback, including a few death threats. I understand that those messages were rooted in genuine fear of this technology and ire toward its Dr. Frankenstein founders (and me, by extension, for covering them). But reporting on the powerful—whether power is granted by elections or equities or zettaflops of compute—has always been the mandate of this magazine and will continue to be. We’ll keep the movie stars too.
