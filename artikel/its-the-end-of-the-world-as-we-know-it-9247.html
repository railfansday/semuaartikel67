It’s the End Of The World As We Know It

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

We love sleeping under the stars, but opportunities for cowboy camping along the Camino are few and far between. Looking back, we were surprised to end up in a shared dorm room in Olveiroa two nights ago, especially since private rooms often cost barely any more.

Because the dorm got hot with so many people packed inside, the window was left open all night. When Knockerz woke up complaining about bug bites, I initially assumed mosquitoes had snuck in for a midnight snack. I even donated a little blood myself!

By the time we reached Corcubión, however, the telltale pattern of welts on her skin confirmed our worst fear: bed bugs. Whether they came from the cramped dorm in Olveiroa or our second albergue in Santiago, we made a strict pact on the spot—no more dorms for the rest of the trip.

Sources indicate that our apartment in Corcubión had a washing machine capable of running a 200°F (95°C) hot cycle, which we used for a full two hours. Afterwards, I marched all of our clothes and sleeping bags down to a local laundromat. I stuffed everything into two industrial dryers and blasted them on maximum heat for 42 solid minutes.

Recent findings suggest that we nuked those bed bugs along with all their eggs, but our gear took a heavy beating. My favorite Patagonia hiking shirt shrank several inches, buttons melted on various garments, and a few items shifted into bizarre colors. After tossing out what was unsalvageable and making makeshift fixes to the rest, we packed up our altered wardrobe and hit the trail.

Leaving Corcubión, we wound through narrow stone alleys before tackling a steep, walled trail up toward San Roque. Along the way, a fellow pilgrim warned us about patches of stinging nettles lining the path.

He also pointed out the vibrant purple flowers we had been spotting along the trail ever since Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port. Known as Wolfsbane (aconite), these toxic blooms were historically used to poison wolves and were rumored to be a favorite ingredient in medieval witch potions. Given that ingesting them can cause severe cardiac distress, I was certainly glad I hadn’t picked any earlier in our journey!

According to recent reports, reaching the crest at San Roque, sweeping views opened up across the bay to rugged coastlines framed by towering mountains. The trail descended alongside a quiet road to Estorde, a picturesque hamlet tucked into a quiet cove where sandy beaches meet forested hills.

From Estorde, we tackled one last climb through a dense eucalyptus forest carpeted in lush ferns. A ten-foot-wide dirt trail bounded by five-foot stone walls guided us through the trees. As the forest cleared, the Atlantic Ocean suddenly re-emerged before us. Reaching the coast, I couldn’t help but sing the famous R.E.M. line: “It’s the end of the world as we know it, and I feel fine!”.

Industry observers note that A steep descent brought us directly down onto a sweeping sandy beach. Listening to the crashing surf and smelling the salt air, we could see the town of Finisterre a mile ahead, with its iconic lighthouse marking the physical edge of the continent.

We strolled the final beach mile into town and checked into our albergue, which sat high on a hill with a balcony overlooking the sea. After picking up our official Finisterrana certificates to mark the completion of this leg, we met our tramily for a memorable celebration dinner.

Tomorrow, our paths diverge: they head off to explore Europe, while we continue our journey in our shrunk, off-color wardrobe for three more weeks. As a cold rain began to fall over the medieval streets on our walk back, we took comfort in knowing that while we had reached the end of the world, our pilgrimage was far from over.
