Sources indicate that A pause in conversation can signal hesitation, uncertainty, or simply that someone is thinking. A transcript may not record the pause at all. Interhuman AI’s Social Signal Bench, publicized on November 24, asks AI models to identify observable signals like these from video and audio, then scores them against the judgments of trained human raters. Its most useful feature is how it handles an awkward problem: two trained observers can watch the same clip and reasonably come away with different labels.

Sources indicate that the research preprint describes a video track with ten signals, including interest, skepticism, and confusion, and an audio track with six. A model can predict more than one signal in a clip. The held-out test sets contain 2,372 video clips and 1,094 audio clips. Interhuman has made the dataset card and training data publicly available, while keeping the expert-labeled test material separate for hosted evaluation.

Sources indicate that in a conventional exact-label score, confusing hesitation with uncertainty is simply an error. So is confusing hesitation with agreement. Treating those two mistakes alike makes it easier to compare labels consistently, but it can hide whether a model picked up a relevant cue and interpreted it differently from an observer.

Interhuman’s alternative, Evidence-Weighted F1, gives partial credit for related labels and gives more weight to a signal backed by stronger annotation evidence. Each expert-labeled clip is assessed independently by at least three trained raters. Their labels capture when a behavior was visible or audible, how strong it appeared, and how certain the observer was about the label. Agreement among raters is useful, but disagreement isn’t automatically discarded. If observers had different reasonable readings, the scoring method can retain evidence for each.

Recent findings suggest that that does not mean a model can earn an easy score by listing everything it might have seen. The benchmark pairs predictions with reference labels one to one and penalizes unmatched predictions. The choice of how closely different labels are related still comes from the authors’ taxonomy. A different definition of which mistakes matter most could change what a good score looks like, which is one reason exact-label scores remain useful alongside the fresh metric.

Sources indicate that the benchmark fits into Interhuman’s broader work on models that process conversations as audio and video. The company’s technical write-up describes outputs that include a predicted signal, a confidence level, and a short account of the evidence. A keyword search would miss an example like a speaker agreeing verbally while slowing down or pausing; the benchmark asks whether models can recognize behavioral cues rather than rely solely on the literal words.

Sources indicate that but the human labels reflect what observers perceived. They cannot establish whether a speaker really felt uncertain or why they paused. Culture, speaking style, the setting, and the short length of the clips all affect interpretation. The public dataset is primarily English online video with one clearly visible speaker; the held-out sets broaden that selection but do not establish that results transfer to every language, community, or interaction. The dataset terms also rule out unconsented surveillance and high-stakes uses such as hiring or workplace monitoring.

In a recent development, the immediate value is a better way to test how multimodal models respond to ambiguous evidence, particularly when a system might otherwise treat a transcript as the entire conversation. It also clarifies what a high score cannot tell us: a model may be good at matching trained observers on selected clips without reliably knowing what a person intended. That gap is precisely what makes a carefully bounded benchmark useful.
