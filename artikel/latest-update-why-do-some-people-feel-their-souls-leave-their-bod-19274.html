📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a recent development, when you have what is known as an out-of-body experience, you may feel as if your mind has left your body, though it hasn’t actually gone anywhere. Maybe you feel like you’re floating or flying. On the darker side, it might be an unsettling experience that makes you feel as if your body no longer belongs to you, or a nightmarish bout of sleep paralysis that leaves you panting and in a cold sweat. How can the brain conjure experiences that sound straight out of science fiction but are really nothing more than illusions?

Industry observers note that to make sense of these intangible phenomena, neuroscientist Teresa Campillo-Ferrer of Radboud University in the Netherlands recruited participants for an experiment designed to capture what happens in the brain when the mind seems to wander from the body during sleep. Most of her subjects had already had an out-of-body episode or some other kind of unusual bodily experience (UBE). Some were lucid dreamers, people who can become aware that they are dreaming. Others had dealt with sleep paralysis, which is waking up while the body is still paralyzed as it is during dreaming sleep. Some had even felt that they left their physical body behind and passed through a portal to another realm. Although UBEs can occur during waking hours, Campillo-Ferrer reasoned that she would probably capture more of them in sleeping brains.

In a recent development, “Studying UBEs is essential for understanding the mechanisms underlying bodily awareness, self-perception, and the sense of ‘having a body’ (embodiment) or ‘owning a body’ (body ownership),” she wrote in a study recently published in Consciousness and Cognition. “As such, UBE research has the potential to expand current theories of human self-consciousness and multisensory perception.”.

Sources indicate that the participants’ main job was simply to relax. Before they drifted off to dreamland, they were fitted with EEG electrodes, practiced pre-sleep meditation, and were given visual stimulation that could help induce lucid dreams and UBEs. Researchers also asked them to move their eyes left and right several times if they noticed anything strange happening, whether or not they were lucid dreaming. This works because even in a semiconscious state, the brain can remember instructions and respond to something unusual. Participants then slept for three hours in soundproof chambers. If the recordings showed the left-right eye signal, participants were either spoken to over an intercom or woken up to describe their experiences. After the sleep session ended, participants also discussed their dreams with the researchers.

The reported UBEs fell into several categories. In vestibular-motor experiences, subjects felt as if they were moving or being moved, such as levitating, spinning, or falling. These sensations can also show up in the opposite way, as heaviness or a feeling of sinking deeper and deeper, like the harrowing scene in A Nightmare on Elm Street where Glen is sucked into his own bed. Flying and other movements that defy the laws of physics also belong in this category. In tactile UBEs, the person feels touch or vibrations from an outside source even though nothing is touching them. In UBEs involving distorted body boundaries, the outer limits of the body blur, so that the body seems to merge with the bed (another very Freddy Krueger moment), shapeshift into another form, or gain a duplicate.

Other UBEs are defined by a lack of bodily sensation or sense of position. These are often associated with feeling detached from the physical body or not knowing where the body actually is. They are similar, though not identical, to out-of-body UBEs, in which participants reported leaving their physical body or realizing, at least while dreaming, that they felt separate from it. Because out-of-body experiences often involve a feeling of levitation, they tend to overlap with the vestibular-motor category, though some involve no sense of movement at all. Sleep paralysis made up another category, and it may be the most frightening. The person is fully aware but can’t move, and sometimes it feels as if they can’t breathe. Still other UBEs stemmed from hallucinations or lucid dreams.

Industry observers note that exactly where UBEs arise in the brain is still unknown. After analyzing EEG patterns, however, the researchers suggest that they happen somewhere in the liminal space between waking and sleeping. In participants whose UBEs were confirmed by the eye-movement signal, slower brainwaves known as delta and theta waves decreased while faster beta and gamma waves surged, especially in the temporal lobes on both sides of the brain. These are the regions that process the sensory information that gives us a sense of existing in our bodies. What makes this pattern striking is that slower waves are linked to sleep, while faster waves are more typical of being awake. It is as if the sleeping and waking brain overlap.

Campillo-Ferrer explained the significance of these findings: “Specific EEG features associated with UBEs appear to reflect the reactivation of EEG activity from levels observed in baseline brain states in which UBEs emerge. [Our] models allowed for precise analysis of neural activity and individual variability, providing newly introduced insights into the neural correlates associated with UBEs and their role in bodily self-perception and consciousness.”.
