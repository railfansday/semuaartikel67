India's religious make-up - what a new Pew study says about Hindus and Muslims

India will remain overwhelmingly Hindu for decades to come, but its religious composition is projected to shift gradually.

A new study by the Pew Research Center, projecting the world's religious populations to 2070, estimates that Hindus will make up 76% of India's population by then, down from 79% in 2020, while the Muslim share will rise from 15% to 20%.

The shift is rooted largely in differences in age and fertility: Indian Muslims are younger than Hindus and, on average, have more children.

The Pew projections place India at the centre of a wider demographic shift across South Asia.

Pakistan is expected to overtake Indonesia as the country with the world's largest Muslim-majority population, with India moving into second place. Bangladesh, too, is projected to see substantial growth in its Muslim population.

The study projects the religious composition of the world over the next 50-year period, drawing on data from 201 countries and territories, covering virtually the entire global population.

Muslims offer prayer at the historic Jama Masjid in Delhi.

It tracks seven religious groups between 2020 and 2070, assuming recent demographic trends continue. Using census and survey data, it establishes each group's size and age profile in 2020, and factors in differences in fertility and religious switching.

The findings place India at the heart of a changing global religious landscape. The country is expected to have the world's second-largest Muslim population by 2070, after Pakistan, with about 334 million Muslims. Pakistan itself will overtake Indonesia as the country with the largest Muslim population, with an estimated 461 million.

Yet the projections offer little support for the more dramatic narratives sometimes attached to India's changing religious composition.

If current demographic trends continue, the Hindu population in the country is expected to grow by 176 million between 2020 and 2070, the report says. It will continue to account for more than three-quarters of the country's population.

According to recent reports, despite a sharp fall in India's overall fertility, Muslim women still have more children, on average, than Hindu women. Pew attributes the shift primarily to demographic momentum.

Indian Muslims had a median age of 24 in 2020, compared with 29 for Hindus.

Between 2025 and 2030, Pew estimates Muslim women in India will have an average of 2.3 children, against 1.8 among Hindu women.

"We expect religious change in India to be driven almost entirely by long-standing demographic differences between Hindus and Muslims: Muslims are younger and have higher fertility rates," Stephanie Kramer of the Pew Research Center told the BBC.

"But Hindu fertility is already below the replacement level, while Muslim fertility is projected to remain above it for another two decades." (A replacement level is one at which new births are sufficient to maintain a stable population.).

The projections are hardly surprising, claims KS James, an Indian demographer at Princeton University.

Recent findings suggest that "The fertility gap between Muslims and Hindus is well established and it means Muslim population growth is likely to remain faster for the next few decades. But fertility is declining across all religious communities and the gap is expected to narrow further," he told the BBC.

"Ultimately, they will converge," he notes, adding that the projections do not point to anything dramatically different in India's demographic trajectory.

In a recent development, demographers push back against claims, often amplified by Hindu right-wing groups, that Muslims will eventually outnumber Hindus. There is no evidence of any significant religious conversion driving population change in India, they say, and the projections do not support such "alarmist scenarios".

Muslim children dressed in festive attire in India.

The wider South Asian picture is equally revealing, according to the report.

Pakistan's Muslim population is projected to more than double to 461 million by 2070, overtaking Indonesia as the world's largest. India will rank second, with 334 million Muslims, while Bangladesh's Muslim population will rise to 218 million. India and Nepal, meanwhile, will remain the world's only Hindu-majority countries.

There appears to be a global story behind these regional shifts.

Industry observers note that by 2035, Muslims are projected to overtake Christians as the world's largest religious group, according to the report. By 2070, their numbers are expected to reach 3.64 billion, up from 2.02 billion in 2020.

In a recent development, christianity, meanwhile, will have just under three billion followers. Muslims will account for 36% of humanity, compared with 26% today.

But the most dramatic transformation may be taking place thousands of miles west of South Asia, in Africa.

Christianity's centre of gravity is moving steadily away from Europe and the Americas towards sub-Saharan Africa.

Recent findings suggest that the report states that if recent trends continue, more than half of the world's Christians are expected to live there by 2070, compared with 31% in 2020. Pew forecasts that the Democratic Republic of Congo will overtake the United States as the country with the largest Christian population.

With more than 1.4 billion people, India overtook China in 2023 in terms of population, according to the UN.

Sources indicate that the steepest decline is expected to occur in Europe. If current trends continue, by 2070 Europe will be home to 11% of the world's Christian population, down from 22% in 2020, the report indicates.

The US is projected to lose its Christian majority by 2065, although Christians will remain the country's largest religious group in 2070.

One reason for this changing religious landscape is the growing number of people identifying with no religion.

In UK and France, the "religiously unaffiliated" are projected to become the majority by 2070. South Korea is expected to follow suit.

Yet their share of the world's population is forecast to fall from 24% to 17%, largely because of a decline in China's population.

Recent findings suggest that china accounts for about two-thirds of the world's religiously unaffiliated population. Its expected population decline will more than offset growth in secular populations elsewhere, the report says.

By 2070, there are other unexpected shifts.

The US is projected to become the world's fifth-largest Hindu population, with immigration driving much of its growth.

And Australia's unaffiliated population will overtake Christians as the country's largest group.

Buddhism, meanwhile, faces a demographic squeeze. Its population is expected to fall by more than half in China, Japan and South Korea, reflecting the ageing and shrinking populations of East Asia. Laos and Mongolia are projected to lose their Buddhist majorities.

Muslims offer Eid prayers in Pakistan.

According to recent reports, there are, of course, caveats. These are demographic projections, not predictions of everything that might happen. Pew assumes that recently observed patterns of fertility, migration and religious switching - or conversions - will continue.

"There are many events that could affect religious group populations (including wars, natural disasters, epidemics and changes in economic or social circumstances) that are not analysed here," the report admits.

India's baseline also required projecting the 2020 religious composition from the 2011 census, because the subsequent census had been delayed.

Still, the broad direction is unmistakable.

South Asia will remain indispensable to the world's religious story, not just because of India's enormous Hindu population but because of the growing weight of its Muslim populations. Africa will increasingly shape Christianity's future, while Europe and parts of East Asia become more secular.

Clearly, by 2070, the world's religious landscape may look markedly different, shaped less by conversion than by the uneven arithmetic of births, deaths and migration.
