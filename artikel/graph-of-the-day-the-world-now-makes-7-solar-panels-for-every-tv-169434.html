It has been revealed that in 2015, the world made six solar panels a second. In 2026, it’s on track to make ten times as many.

In a recent development, at an Australian clean-energy conference in 2015 hosted by Renew Economy Disruption and the Energy Industry conference, I posed a simple test for the solar boom. If the world was still making more panels that consumed electricity — TVs — than panels that generated it, I reckoned we hadn’t quite seen the real boom yet.

On the updated chart (Figure 1), solar overtook televisions in 2016.

By May 2018, when I spoke at Power Philippines in Manila, the crossover had become a line in journalist Lala Rimando’s live coverage of my talk: more solar panels were now being made than TV sets.

Passing televisions was an crucial milestone, but it’s really the least interesting part of the story.

According to recent reports, in 2021, the world made an estimated 360 million solar panels. A year later, it made about 790 million. By 2025, annual production had reached about 1.4 billion panels, against just over 200 million televisions. The gap opened not because the world stopped making TVs, but because it started making solar panels on a much bigger scale.

Put another way, the world went from making about six solar panels a second in 2015 to about 45 in 2025, averaged across every second of the year. My full-year estimate for 2026 puts the rate at about 62 panels a second — nearly two billion panels in a year (Figure 2).

In a recent development, and the panel count tells only part of the story. The average module rating I used to estimate those counts rose from 150 watts in 2008 to 475 watts in 2025. At that 2025 rating, 1.4 billion panels represent roughly 665 gigawatts of rated generating capacity coming out of factories in a single year.

Televisions offered me a familiar yardstick for what mass production for a consumer market looked like. A decade later, the question isn’t whether solar can match that scale. It’s how quickly we can put all those panels to work. After all, it is the fastest shift in energy in human history.
