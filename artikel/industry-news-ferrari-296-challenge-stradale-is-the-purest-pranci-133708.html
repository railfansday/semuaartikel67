Recent findings suggest that nostalgia must be in the air in Maranello. A few months ago, Ferrari unveiled the 12Cilindri Manuale, reintroducing a (simulated) six-speed manual transmission to its model lineup purely for the sake of driver involvement. And now, the Challenge Stradale badge returns to the rear engine mesh of a Prancing Horse for the first time since the 360 CS was discontinued in 2004. Simply put, it's a good year to be a sentimental Ferrarista.

In the same vein as its predecessor, the 296 Challenge Stradale is a road car that shares its fundamental philosophy with the new 296 Challenge Evo racecar, which will debut in the single-marque Ferrari Challenge racing series next year. Parked side by side at their reveal at the Circuit de Barcelona-Catalunya today, we would excuse a gentleman driver for confusing the CS with his new track-only machinery.

There have been several limited-edition, track-focused mid-engine Ferraris since the 360 Challenge Stradale—including the F430 Scuderia, 458 Speciale, and 488 Pista—but the 296 Challenge Stradale goes the furthest of all of them in emulating its racecar counterpart, both aesthetically and mechanically.

While previous track-centric Ferraris have been somewhat demurely styled, the 296 CS puts its aerodynamic elements on full display. The carbon-fiber rear wing, dive planes, adjustable front splitter, and vented hood are essentially a copy-paste job from the Challenge Evo, with concessions made for road legality. Up front, twin vents positioned in the hood just before the windshield channel hot air expelled from two under-hood radiators over the windshield and towards the rear of the car, reducing the aerodynamic variations caused by changing ground clearance.

Industry observers note that other aero features are hidden out of sight. The CS's underbody has been smoothed to better feed the rear diffuser and work with three pairs of vortex generators at the front and two at the rear to generate additional downforce. With all of the aerodynamic elements colluding, the 296 Challenge Stradale makes 1360 pounds of downforce at 155 mph. That's a 72-percent increase over the base 296GTB's peak figure.

Significant changes have been made to the CS's powertrain. You won't find any orange high-voltage wiring snaking around under the glass of its mid-mounted hood like you would in a 296GTB or 296 Speciale, as the 5-kWh battery pack and rear-mounted axial-flux electric motor have been removed entirely. The twin-turbo 3.0-liter V-6 operates unassisted and supposedly sounds better than ever courtesy of a reworked twin-exit exhaust system.

Overall firepower has decreased to 670 horsepower—149 ponies less than the standard 296GTB's. The upside is a notable decrease in mass, with Ferrari claiming that the removal of the plug-in-hybrid componentry played a major role in shedding 328 pounds from the 296GTB's dry weight. A lightened transmission flex plate, driveshaft, and timing cover were also developed to make the CS's powertrain as featherweight as possible. Without an electric motor present to fill in any thrust gaps left by turbo lag, the V-6 has been retuned to increase low-end grunt, although peak torque remains 546 pound-feet delivered at 6250 rpm.

As to be expected, yanking out an integrated powertrain system comes with some unique challenges that affect more than just the engine itself. For example, Ferrari was forced to enlarge the 296 CS's rear carbon-ceramic brake rotors and the ventilation ducts that cool them to compensate for the lack of regenerative braking. The decreased weight in the rear also shifted its weight distribution 1.5 percent to the front, which was addressed with suspension design and geometry changes.

The CS's newly developed suspension is derived directly from the 296 Challenge Evo and features semi-active dampers and a dual-spring design that improves rebound response. The result is reduced body roll, which helps the performance of the specially developed Pirelli P Zero R and P Zero Trofeo RS tires, measuring 245/35ZR-20 in the front and 305/35ZR-20 in the rear. The 20-inch forged wheels can be ordered in Matte Grigio Corsa, Matte Nero with a diamond-cut finish, or Matte Bianco Fuji.

The 296 Challenge Stradale's cockpit is very similar to the one found in the 296 Speciale. While niceties like climate control and a stereo remain, carbon-fiber door panels and a lack of armrests and storage pockets are a clear indication of the CS's weight-saving mission. For true gluttons for punishment, an even further stripped interior spec is available that removes the trim from the area beneath the seats and the lower section of the carpets beneath the center console, leaving part of the chassis exposed.

Ferrari is adamant that the 296 CS isn't a replacement for the 296 Speciale and will not cannibalize that model's customer base, as the two are intended for different buyers. Besides being geared less towards outright track duty, the 296 Speciale is a part of Ferrari's special series, meaning that customers need to have an "existing relationship" with the brand in order to get an allocation. The 296 Challenge Stradale is a standard range model, meaning anyone can walk into a dealer and order one, existing relationship or not. Ferrari says that it wants to court fresh Ferrari buyers interested in experiencing the purest driving experience that the prancing horse has to offer, and the 296 CS is the first step in that direction.

U.S. pricing has not been confirmed, but in its home market, the 296 Challenge Stradale costs 291,000 euros, or the equivalent of about $320,000. Stateside deliveries of the fresh CS are expected to begin sometime in late 2027. As a non-Speciale model, CS production numbers aren’t limited, which presents a unique opportunity to buy a special new Ferrari without already owning one.
