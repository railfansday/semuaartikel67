Smith & Diction rebuilt the PHS brand identity around a Bloom logo, with three plants growing from the letters and Philadelphia grid underneath.

According to recent reports, the old mark was a tree in a circle, and it said too little about an organization that does far more than plant trees. The Pennsylvania Horticultural Society tends gardens and parks across the Philadelphia region and runs the Flower Show. The new mark keeps the letters PHS as the foundation and grows three plants out of them, each drawn by Summer McClure to fit a Philadelphia city block. In deep green and burgundy on cream, mint, and sky blue, with dozens of colors in the palette, the identity looks like a garden because it behaves like one: every visit indicates something new.

Recent findings suggest that the wordmark is custom, built to hold the plants like roots hold a stem. The supporting type comes from the societyâs own archive: old exhibition posters that were beautiful and hardworking, no frills, just type. Caslon Doric leads, with the confidence and clean legibility of classic newspaper headlines, and ES Park backs it up, a slab serif with geometric moments and measured softness.

Underneath it all sits William Pennâs original city grid. The studio baked the grid into the logo itself, then extended it through every print layout, matching the grid to the width of the flowers. Like beds in a garden, the structure keeps everything in place while each layout keeps its own personality. The mark never sits still: Henrique Barone hand-drew every frame of the plants growing, and Justin Lawes made a version that sways with the breeze.

Recent findings suggest that this is a brand made to be held, not just seen. The studio printed a 42-page brand book, made a matte gold lapel pin of the mark, and built a flip book that reveals the logo growing frame by frame. They matched paper tones to nearly every brand color, so the team can print in-house and stay on brand. The book cover carries the Bloom logo in dark green on mint, with an orange seal.

Industry observers note that the floral illustrations do more than decorate. Each one stands for a part of the organization: a flower in a cocktail glass for the Pop Up Gardens, a leafy radish for the community gardens, an oak leaf for the Healthy Neighborhoods program, a branching tree for the McLean Library.
