Latest Update: Mom and Dad Survived 1980s Mortgage Rates. Here Is Why Buying a Home Today Is Still Much Harder.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It now takes the typical household 10.5 years to save for a down payment on a home—more than triple the time it took in the 1980s, according to a newly introduced analysis from Realtor.com®.

According to recent reports, it's a stark generational divide that may be hard to ignore this Thanksgiving. Roughly 1 in 3 young adults lived with their parents in 2025—a record 25.2 million Americans aged 18 to 34—according to separate research from Realtor.com. And the share rising the fastest is in regions long considered affordability havens.

For families sharing a roof, one seemingly innocent question could spark a much thornier conversation than intended: When are you going to buy a house?

For parents who bought their first homes in the 1980s, the question might seem fair. After all, mortgage rates reached a record 18.63% in 1981, while inflation averaged 13.5% in 1980—compared to 7.28% and 3.7%, respectively, today.

But while older generations certainly faced obstacles of their own, those war stories don't fully capture the economics of today's market, says Hannah Jones, senior economist at Realtor.com.

Recent findings suggest that “The reality is that today's aspiring buyers face a different housing market than generations before,” she explains. “While mortgage rates were significantly higher in the 1980s, home prices were far more attainable relative to household incomes, allowing buyers to save for a down payment in a fraction of the time it takes today.”.

In a recent development, the impulse to look back at the 1980s for perspective on today's housing market is certainly understandable. But to Jones' point, these comparisons often overlook how dramatically home prices have climbed since "Back to the Future" first hit theaters.

Sources indicate that in the 1980s, the typical home cost about 3.2 times the median annual household income. Today, that ratio has climbed to 4.8, according to the Realtor.com analysis.

So even with mortgage rates well below their 1980s highs, today's households have approximately $150,000 less homebuying power than their counterparts four decades ago, according to the analysis.

And much of that divide has emerged within the past decade, giving individual and societal expectations little time to adjust.

As recently as 2019, the typical household needed 5.7 years to save for a 10% down payment. Today, that timeline has nearly doubled to 10.5 years.

According to recent reports, "Envision running in a race when your parents begin halfway to the finish. Everyone still expects you to reach the finish at the same time as they did," indicates Max Doshay, a clinical psychologist and co-founder and CEO of KMN Psych. “This is what comparisons of finances can be like for younger generations.”.

Recent findings suggest that and the finish line has moved in more ways than one. Even after households save enough for a down payment, the resulting mortgage can remain out of reach.

In 43 of the nation's 50 largest metros, the estimated monthly mortgage payment on a median-priced home would consume more than 30% of median household income, even after putting 10% down, according to the metro-level calculations in the report.

Consider San Diego, where Doshay is based. There, the typical household would need an estimated 18.8 years to save for a 10% down payment of roughly $91,200.

Yet at today's prices and mortgage rates, the resulting $5,563 monthly payment would consume nearly 65% of median household income—more than twice the conventional 30% housing cost benchmark, even before accounting for property taxes or insurance.

Of course, it's hardly unreasonable for parents to want their children to enjoy the same security homeownership afforded them.

According to recent reports, and the advantages of such security are well documented. Purchasing a home around age 30 was associated with 22.5% greater net worth by age 50—roughly $119,000 more—than buying in one's 40s, according to a March 2026 Realtor.com analysis.

The trouble begins when homeownership becomes more than a financial milestone, and the inability to reach it starts to feel like a symbol of personal failure.

“Homeownership has become such an integral part of what many consider to be successful in this country,” explains Bonnie Lambert, a board-certified psychiatric mental health nurse practitioner and licensed professional counselor at Crestview Recovery Center. “People buy houses so much because it makes them believe they are independent, responsible, and have accomplished something.”.

Those expectations can weigh on parents, too.

A whopping 71% of parents believe their adult children's successes and failures reflect at least a fair amount on the job they've done as parents, according to a 2024 Pew Research Center survey.

So, a child's struggle to buy a home may feel personal to parents, too. But what one generation expresses as concern, the other may hear as criticism.

It has been revealed that "Financial shame typically starts when someone simply thinks, 'I'm behind everyone else,'" Lambert claims. "Whether it be because of seeing friends buying houses or hearing parents talk about how much property they had at our age, this comparison will leave us feeling embarrassed.".

And when those comparisons overlook how dramatically the economics have changed, frustration can turn inward.

"People who mistakenly view different beginning lines as failures make their frustrations about money into feelings of shame," Doshay notes. "They stop asking questions like 'Why are houses so expensive?' and instead ask themselves 'What's wrong with me?'".

That shame, in turn, can become another obstacle to overcome.

A 2021 study published in Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, involving more than 9,000 participants across six studies, found evidence that financial shame can cause people to disengage from their finances, potentially reinforcing the difficulties that produced those feelings.

There's one more thing worth remembering before the conversation turns to homeownership this Thanksgiving: The struggle to buy a home isn't a reflection of how hard someone is trying.

According to recent reports, "For many younger Americans, the challenge isn't a lack of willingness to save. It's that the financial goalposts have moved," Jones says.

And while recognizing those differences doesn't mean families have to avoid discussing money, how they approach those conversations can make all the difference.

"Well-meaning inquiries can be perceived as critical," Lambert claims.

"No one should ever have to go through an annual holiday dinner as their personal/financial review time," Doshay adds.

Rather than comparing financial milestones, he encourages parents to listen to their adult children. For those on the receiving end of uncomfortable questions, that may mean establishing boundaries.

Doshay suggests a response such as: "I am working on my objectives; however, I would prefer it if we did not talk about money today.".

In a recent development, lambert, meanwhile, recommends shifting the focus away from homeownership as the ultimate measure of financial progress. Building savings or paying down debt can be accomplishments worth recognizing in their own right.

"No one else's timeline should ever dictate our deadlines for being happy," she claims.

After all, parents can want their children to enjoy the same financial security they did without expecting them to reach it on the same schedule.

As Doshay puts it: "Owning a home is a financial obligation—it’s not proof you’ve reached maturity.".
