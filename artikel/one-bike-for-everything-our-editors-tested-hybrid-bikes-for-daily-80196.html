Sources indicate that when I worked in bike shops, we sold tons of hybrid bikes. These bicycles are ideal for riders who say, “I need a bike that does a little of everything.” If you’re seeking versatility, a hybrid is often your best bike option. They’re a good choice for general fitness, commuting, running errands, and family rides.

Outside of cycling circles, the term “hybrid” often refers to vehicles that pair an electric motor with an internal combustion engine. However, in bike-speak, hybrids are a bicycle style that combines elements of road bikes and mountain bikes. Hybrids range from lightweight, sporty bikes designed for roads and bike paths to more rugged models suitable for light off-road use.

While they’re not ideal for die-hard mountain bikers or roadies seeking speed and the highest performance, hybrids work surprisingly well for many people on real-world rides. Typically, hybrids blend the lightweight frame, fast-rolling tires, and larger gears of road bikes with flat handlebars and the upright riding position of hardtail mountain bikes. Some hybrid models also feature suspension forks similar to those used on bikes designed for more aggressive, technical trails.

It has been revealed that the Cannondale Quick 3 is our overall favorite hybrid for its fast feel and smooth ride, comfortable upright riding position, and reasonable price. These qualities make it great for upcoming riders who may be hesitant about incorporating regular bike riding into their routine.

The Quick 3 features a comfortable Selle Royal saddle and Ergon grips. It also has mounts for adding a rear rack (the fork lacks rack mounts, however) and fenders to keep road spray out of your eyes and off your backside. While the pricier Quick 2 features a carbon fork and 1x9 Shimano drivetrain, the Quick 3 uses a steel fork and 1x8-speed Shimano system. These differences add a little weight, but the bike still has the gear range to tackle the hills on your commute or rides.

Industry observers note that cannondale also offers the Quick 3 with a step-through frame. It also sells the Quick CX 3 model with a suspension fork.

The CTY 1.1 (by REI’s Co-op Cycles house brand) is a capable, affordable hybrid. The CTY is a great choice for a wide range of riders—especially for budget-minded beginners and commuters. The bike has an upright riding position (versus the sporty fit of fitness-focused hybrids). This geometry, along with a comfortable memory foam saddle and gel grips, makes the CTY 1.1 more comfortable than many competitors.

Commuters will appreciate the CTY’s flat-resistant, wire-bead 40mm tires with reflective sidewalls; they let you roll over potholes, dirt, gravel, random road debris, and even the occasional curb. For all-weather stopping power, REI equips the bike with Tektro mechanical disc brakes, and the Shimano 3x8-speed drivetrain gives you enough gear range to get you most anywhere you want to go.

Based in Brooklyn’s Greenpoint neighborhood, Brooklyn Bike Co. uses firsthand knowledge to design well-priced bikes that can withstand the rigors of NYC commutes—and the brand’s Roebling model is a great example. While the Roebling’s design is simple and understated, it’s entirely practical for commuting, fitness, and leisurely rides.

Brooklyn builds the Roebling around a double-butted cromoly frame. Although its skinny tubes look retro compared to the shaped, formed ones used on alloy-framed hybrids, the bike is lightweight (approximately 30 pounds), durable, and easy to ride. The Roebling features a 1x9-speed drivetrain with an 11-41-tooth cassette and 42T chainring. This gives you an easy gear for hills and a significant enough gear for speed on flatter parts of your ride. Unlike many similarly priced hybrids, the Roebling uses V-style rim brakes. While not as flashy as disc brakes, V-brakes are lighterweight and easier to adjust.

If you’re 5’6” or shorter, also check out the Lorimer. It’s essentially the same bike as the Roebling, but in smaller sizes (and in two color options).

According to recent reports, want the easiest-to-maintain, rust-proof hybrid for commuting and riding around town? Then, Priority’s Eight hybrid is probably the best choice you can find. Weighing only 27 pounds, the Eight is lighter than many hybrids thanks to its aluminum frame and carbon fiber fork.

Industry observers note that priority originally introduced its iconic Eight commuter bike in 2016 but discontinued it after a few years. However, the New York City-based brand relaunched the model due to rider demand—and it’s a winner. The Eight features a Gates Carbon belt drive drivetrain instead of a standard chain; this system requires no lube or maintenance (other than an occasional wipe-down with a rag). For gearing to scale hills and overpasses, Priority equips the Eight with an eight-speed (hence its name) Shimano Alfine internally geared rear hub with a 307% gear range.

The Eight comes in traditional and step-through style frames. For all-weather commuters, Priority also offers the Continuum Onyx. It has fenders and dynamo lights that never need charging. Plus, it uses the Enviolo CVT (continuously variable transmission) rear hub. It has the same gear range as a traditional 21-speed drivetrain.

It has been revealed that test team member Tom Messina often rides the Cannondale Quick 2 for his daily commutes and on the local rail trail. He’s ridden it through snow squalls and on beautiful fall afternoons, and he’s impressed with the bike’s performance, versatility, and overall value.

“At $949, you’ll be pressed to find many better options on a commuter or introduction bike than this one, especially from a brand I trust as much as Cannondale,” he noted. Messina particularly likes the bike’s low weight, wide-range Shimano Cues 9-speed drivetrain, and sure-stopping hydraulic disc brakes. “It’s a top-tier bike for those getting back into riding after a long time off, or spinning the bike path on a sunny day.”.

According to recent reports, want a suspension fork? Cannondale also offers the Quick CX 2 for $1,099.

It has been revealed that most hybrids use a road-style frame with a mix of mountain bike and pavement components. Scott takes a slightly different approach with its Scale Gravel series; the result is a blend of mountain bike and gravel tech ideal for adventure riding, backpacking, and commutes in foul weather or worn-out roads.

Intended “to go the distance over any and all types of terrain,” this adventure-ready variant of Scott’s Scale XC racing hardtail features an aluminum frame and carbon fork with multiple attachment points for carrying gear. The Gravel 20 has a SRAM Eagle 70 1x12 drivetrain that provides extremely low gearing, and Shimano hydraulic disc brakes ensure strong stopping power.

Industry observers note that while the Gravel 20 is probably way too much bike for most people’s rides, it’s a perfect option if your gravel roads and rides are more extreme than most. However, if you like the look of this bike and want a lighterweight option, Scott offers a range of Scale Gravel models—some with carbon frames and electronic shifting—from $1,700 to $5,500.

In a recent development, this FX 2 earns high marks for its value. Its aluminum frame and fork have a look and feel of some of Trek’s more premium hybrids, without the added price. Internal cable routing keeps the frame looking clean, and like its electrified counterpart, it comes equipped with a Shimano Cues 1x9 drivetrain and Tektro hydraulic disc brakes. At just 26 pounds, it’s impressively light for a bike this capable. Keep the FX 2 as-is for a sleek, fast look, or use the rack and fender mounts for added utility.

Sources indicate that if you want substantial tires for soaking up rough terrain, this isn’t the bike for you. The FX 2 rolls on 35mm tires with a max clearance of 40mm, making it better suited for smooth roads and cinder trails. But if you like a fast, efficient ride, this is a great bike at a great price.

Even though hybrids typically blend road and mountain bike technology and features, many hybrids are biased towards riding pavement and gravel roads rather than trails. However, Polygon’s Heist 2 leans more heavily on its mountain bike DNA.

In a recent development, the Heist’s frame styling closely resembles hardtail mountain bikes, and its geometry isn’t too far from XC racing hardtails from only a decade ago. With a 63mm travel SR Suntour NEX fork and 1x8 Shimano Alivio/Essa drivetrain, you can take the Heist on light-duty singletrack. (Though you might want tires knobbier than the stock WTB Riddlers if you have anything but smooth trails.).

Industry observers note that specialized has sold its Sirrus platform for decades, and that experience shows in this bike’s details. This bike embodies the “I need it to do everything” ethos.

It has been revealed that the Sirrus features sporty geometry for a fast ride feel and a rigid fork to save some weight. Specialized equips the Sirrus X 2.0 with a wide-range Shimano Cues 9-speed drivetrain, hydraulic disc brakes, and 40mm semi-slick tires for some off-pavement use. The Sirrus frame also has mounts for racks (for commuting or touring) and fenders.

Many “women’s hybrids” use step-through frames, or are men’s bikes in a different color. As one of cycling’s only brands focused exclusively on women riders, Liv designs its frame geometries and touchpoints (such as saddles and grips) to better fit women’s bodies.

Alight is Liv’s hybrid platform; Alight models feature flat handlebars, rigid forks, 700x38c tires, and an upright riding position. This makes the Alight comfortable and stable without compromising too much speed. You can use the Alight for fitness rides and commutes since Liv equips the bike with mounts for a rack and fenders.

The Alight Disc 2 features a 1x9 MicroShift drivetrain, with an 11-46 tooth cassette and 40T chainring. This gives the Alight a low gear for climbing hills; however, some riders may find the gearing isn’t hard enough for flat sections of road.

Sources indicate that most hybrid models are traditional human-powered bicycles. However, some brands offer hybrid-style electric bikes. These hybrids feature lightweight electric motors and rechargeable batteries to provide pedal assistance up to 20 or 28 mph, depending on local regulations.

Sources indicate that this relatively affordable seven-speed model from Aventon delivers simplicity and style that is tough to beat—and now even more so with hydraulic disc brakes on this revamped version. Push the throttle, and the Soltera’s brushless hub motor kicks in, assisting you more gently as you begin pedaling.

The Soltera features disc brakes, an integrated light, and turn signals. The battery is hidden in the bike’s frame (a surprising feature compared to the bolt-on vibe of many bikes in this price range), which helps the bike blend in with non-electric bikes. Best for smoother roads and bike paths, the Soltera delivers the essential features a city rider needs in a 46-pound package.

In a recent development, many electric bikes exceed 60 or 80 pounds, so a sub-40-pound e-bike is rather impressive. Unfortunately, e-bikes this light are usually rather expensive. However, Velotric’s Tempo only costs $1,499 and boasts a 39 lb. (claimed) weight. Velotric achieves this weight reduction and low cost by ditching the bulky suspension fork and using a smaller battery.

Like most hybrids, and unlike many e-bikes, the Tempo features 700c gravel tires. These tires roll quicker than 26- or 27.5-inch tires on pavement while giving the Tempo improved traction on dirt roads and gravel paths. Velotric offers the Tempo in a traditional double diamond frame and a mid-step design. Both frames are available in two colors and two sizes.

Since riders use hybrids in different ways, brands make various styles of these bikes to meet their needs and budgets. Some focus on lightweight and speed, while others are more rugged and can even tackle some easier mountain bike trails. There are also hybrids with features such as racks, fenders, and lights for commuting and touring, as well as some e-bike models.

When shopping for a hybrid, I recommend first thinking about where you will ride it most often. If you will mostly ride on paved roads and bike paths, consider a hybrid that focuses on low weight and has slick tires. If you’re commuting, look for bikes with mounts for racks or fenders, and have heavy-duty tires and wheels. For gravel paths or light-duty trail riding, consider hybrid models with suspension forks and knobbier tires.

Industry observers note that lastly, I always remind hybrid shoppers to consider a bike’s long-term use. Look for hybrids that use common, universal-sized components and avoid those with proprietary features or gimmicks. Riders often own their hybrids for several years, sometimes decades. You’ll want to be able to service and repair the bike down the line, especially if you use it for commuting.
