Pennine Way Wrap-Up

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Although I only completed 120 miles of the Pennine Way’s 268 mile route, I learned so much. What went wrong; what went right? To finish up this blog, I thought I’d summarize my experiences and lessons learned for anyone else who might be considering this adventure.

In a recent development, compared to the major thru-hikes in the US, the Pennine Way sees only 1,500 to 3,500 thru-hikers each year. On the morning I started, there were only two other people leaving Edale: Owen (aka SST) and Michael.

Over the course of my journey, I crossed paths with others infrequently and briefly. Some days, I didn’t see another person except at the pub where I had breakfast or dinner. There is usually a village somewhere along the day’s route. However, there are some stretches at the beginning and the end where there is nothing for two or three days. The terrain feels vast and trackless. If you don’t enjoy time alone, bring someone with you.

Sources indicate that that being mentioned, the British people were universally friendly (except the Uber drivers). There were many times when I was saved by the kindness of strangers.

The trail is boggy and muddy in sections, rocky and treacherous in others. Total elevation gain is more than Mount Everest. The wind is incessant and can be powerful. Although the temperatures stayed between a mild mid-50s to mid-60s, the wind-chill kept me bundled up even on hard climbs.

Sources indicate that when the rain comes—and it will come—it can be anything from a light drizzle to a torrential downpour. It might be both within the same hour. Most of the trail provides no shelter, manmade or otherwise. Full rain gear is essential. A tent that can be pitched in exposed areas in rain and wind will make a big difference.

I trained for this adventure in inclement weather and on technical terrain with steep climbs. However, the weather was worse than I had experienced and this made the terrain more challenging. Mental toughness in the face of physical hardship is a must.

Sources indicate that I’m one of those travelers who tends to make minimal reservations and just goes where opportunity takes me. This did not work so well for me on the Pennine. The villages along the route are tiny and services are very limited. You might hike all day only to find that there is no place to stay. Campgrounds close due to weather. Inns in the smallest villages may have less than six rooms. Many pubs stop serving food after 6:00 pm. Shops may close by 5:00 or 5:30 pm, and don’t open again until 9:00 am.

Industry observers note that while the notion of wild camping is romantic, it was also more challenging than I thought. You have to be far enough away from farms and villages to remain unnoticed. However, many of the truly remote locations are on windy, barren hilltops or boggy moors. The mental toll of not knowing where I would end each day, especially when the weather was really bad, began to take a toll after a few days.

Sources indicate that in retrospect, I wish I had researched more camping options and committed to reaching those locations. Knowing that I was going to be some place I could depend on, even if it took a couple extra miles, would have helped me push through. I think that’s how Michael, and Graeme and Rachel succeeded. Michael did a lot of wild camping, but he always had a solid idea of where he would stop for the night.

It has been revealed that finding trail markers can be challenging. Markers can be hidden behind overgrown foliage, barely legible, or missing entirely due to wind and weather. If multiple trails share the same route, not all the pertinent trails are listed. Some areas across the moors are completely unmarked, the trail only revealed through the boot prints of earlier walkers. I used digital maps linked to GPS, a highly-recommended guidebook, and physical ordinance survey maps. At times, these three sources contradicted each other, and sometimes disagreed with the physical trail markers.

In a recent development, graeme told me that having a second person to spot the tiny “acorn” markers was a massive help. From my experience, I wandered off trail at least once every day. Every time I fell, it was because I was trudging across wild terrain trying to find my way back. I feel fortunate that I wasn’t injured sooner.

Sources indicate that the guidebook claimed that there is no reason to bring more than a 12 kilo (26 pound) pack. I think the book assumes you’re not camping. My pack was 30.3 pounds without food. With food, I think I was carrying around 34 pounds. With the wisdom of hindsight, I would have packed differently.

I wish I had spent the money to buy a lightweight pack in the 45 to 50 liter range, and a lighter sleeping bag, retaining the 20 degree rating. Together, my pack and sleeping bag came in at nearly 7 pounds, a big chunk of my base weight.

Sources indicate that my research indicated that if I packed 5 days of food, it would last me to the end of the trail. I should have scaled back my packed meals to 2 days. While accommodations can be hard to find in the villages, food is easier. The pubs and cafes are frequent enough, and generally close to campgrounds. “Honesty shops” (little kiosks of food and drink, paid on the honor system) and snack shops are a welcome treat and usually close to the trail. Hearty snacks like cheese, protein bars and trail mix are good enough when you’ve had either a full breakfast or dinner in a pub. While the villages don’t have grocery stores, the smaller towns always had at least a convenience store for resupply.

Between a lighter pack and sleeping bag, and less food, I could have easily shaved 5 pounds or more off my total weight. For me, 27 pounds total weight would be ideal.

In a recent development, regardless of how gloomy all of the above sounds, I truly enjoyed my time on the Pennine Way. In fact, I’m already making intends to try again! I see what went wrong and I’m more confident than ever that the next attempt will be successful.

Round trip airfare from the US cost me around $1,200 about 2 months before departure. When I was doing my research, that same flight 6 months before departure was around $850. Book early.

Sources indicate that I’ve you’re traveling from the US, an Electronic Travel Authorization (ETA) is required to enter the UK. It’s good for one year and cost me about $28.

Train fares from London to Edale, booked 30 days in advance, came to $170. This required multiple transfers across different transit companies. If you buy your tickets through an app like the Avanti West Coast, all the required tickets are sold as a bundle. This app was a lifesaver once I was forced off-trail and had to navigate by train for the remainder of my trip.

It has been revealed that bus and train fares from Kirk Yetholm to Edinburgh came to $40. Bus routes are managed by each local area, so there aren’t any apps. However, Google maps was accurate on routes and times and always helped me navigate the bus system. There is no advance ticket purchase, but I was able to tap a card or digital wallet on every bus I rode. Even the smallest villages and most remote bus routes accepted electronic payment on the bus.

Pre-booking my hotels 6 months early would have also saved me a lot. The difference between prices 6 months early and 2 months early was about 40 percent. Keep in mind that changing exchange rates will affect prices.

Recent findings suggest that A cheap hotel in Manchester and close to the train station, cost me $115 per night. I went straight from Heathrow Airport to Manchester, avoiding an expensive stay in London.

A major chain hotel in Edinburgh, close to the airport, costs me $255 per night.

It has been revealed that on the Pennine, the average price I paid for a double room with a private bathroom was $160 per night. Single rooms with a shared bathroom were often available at 20 to 30 percent less. Double rooms with only 1 guest were offered at a discount over the same room with 2 guests. If you’re traveling solo, make sure to adjust your reservation criteria on the inn websites.

Campground fees were generally $13 per night, sometimes discounted for Pennine Way walkers. I’ve since learned that this price can be per person. I wasn’t aware of this since I was alone.

My original plan was to camp for two nights and sleep indoors every third night. If I had kept to that plan, I estimate the trip would have cost me about $4,168. This excludes gear and airfare. Booking six months in advance would have brought this number down.

Industry observers note that my actual costs were quite a bit higher since I spent the second half of my trip off-trail shuttling between various towns and cities. I had additional transportation costs, and higher average lodging and meal costs for the latter half.

For my next attempt at the Pennine, I’m making several changes:.

As challenging as it was, I can’t wait to return and try again. With a bit of luck and a lot more foreknowledge, I’m going to make it all the way to Scotland!
