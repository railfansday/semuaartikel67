Kim Jong Un's communist regime is accused of brutal crackdowns on religious freedom in a latest report.

Living in North Korea, Kim Bo-bin got used to praying with her eyes open.

Recent findings suggest that she found ingenious ways to keep her Christian faith a secret in the Communist dictatorship, which tightly controls how and where its citizens worship. Unauthorised displays of faith could land people in prison, she tells the BBC.

Sources indicate that so Kim would silently mouth hymns and clap with just two of her fingers. "In North Korea, if there are three people in a room, you are being spied on. So I would only close my eyes [and pray] when I lay on the floor.".

Now 44, Kim lives just outside the South Korean capital Seoul, where she fled 15 years ago.

Her testimony - of how she prayed in secret for years, and was jailed and beaten for her faith - is part of a new report, external documenting the experiences of 570 North Korean escapees, most of whom say they experienced or witnessed anti-Christian persecution.

Sources indicate that spanning five years and featuring interviews with defectors from across the country, the report by British human rights group Korea Future accuses Kim Jong Un's regime of repeatedly violating religious freedom through a system of surveillance, fear and violence. It suggests little has changed after sustained international pressure, with multiple incidents of suspected Christians being beaten and tortured as recently as last year.

North Korea maintains that it allows religious freedom in spite of years of evidence heard by rights groups and the United Nations. While churches exist in Pyongyang, they are controlled by the state and widely seen as projecting an illusion of religious liberty.

"The North Korean leader's authority is god-like," notes Kang Dong-wan, professor at Dong-a University. "So they cannot allow gods whose authority is above him." Pyongyang fears that religious freedom may threaten the regime itself, he adds.

Kim Bo-bin has been living in South Korea for 15 years now.

First established in Korea in the late 1700s, Christianity exploded across the north after Protestant missionaries arrived in the 1880s. North Korea provides no figures on its Christian population but the UN has referred to estimates of 200,000 to 400,000 of them practising secretly, some of whom are detained in camps.

Surveillance and control over religion, like many other aspects of North Korean life, intensified under Kim Jong Un, who succeeded his father in 2011.

In a recent development, three South Korean missionaries operating in the Chinese border region were arrested between 2013-2014 and sentenced to life in prison on charges of espionage and plotting a coup. It's not clear if they are still alive, as Seoul says Pyongyang has refused to share any information about the detainees.

According to recent reports, pyongyang has also been cracking down on smuggled memory sticks containing South Korean-made videos of Christian sermons. In 2020, it passed a law - on Rejecting Reactionary Ideology and Culture - to block so-called "foreign influence". Owning Bibles and hosting services can now result in imprisonment, while distributing religious books or videos is punishable by death.

Lessons against religion begin as early as kindergarten for most North Koreans. Children must attend "self-criticism" sessions at school in which they are warned against "superstitions", widely understood to refer to Christianity and other faiths.

An image from 2010 showing the inside of a state-sanctioned church in Pyongyang.

This is what Kim Bo-bin recalls being taught. The faiths Koreans have long practised - Christianity since the 18th Century and shamanism, a millennia-old practice rooted in ancestral spirit worship - were dismissed as superstitions.

Her teachers never mentioned that Kim Il Sung himself was born into a fervently Christian family, or that Pyongyang had once been dubbed the "Jerusalem of the East.".

Industry observers note that rather, every child and adult is expected to know North Korea's foundational Ten Principles by heart. They state that citizens must hold the "firm perspective" they know of "nothing greater than the great comrades" Kim Il Sung, the founder of North Korea, his son Kim Jong Il and the ruling party. The "smallest attempt" to dent this authority must not be tolerated, it adds.

The report documents both the indoctrination as well as the constant surveillance, with police and a network of neighbourhood spies monitoring suspicious activities. A former police informant claims she was instructed to gather information on neighbours' worshipping, and handed a memo by an officer describing Christianity as an "anti-socialist activity".

North Korean authorities are also reported to hold intergenerational records of families' beliefs condemning Christians' children and grandchildren to a life without higher education or decent career prospects.

All of this illustrates how various North Korean institutions, including schools and the police, control its citizens' beliefs from "cradle to the grave," co-author James Burt states.

And yet many North Koreans turn to religion because their lives are so precarious, he adds. Faith offers them something that the state ideology "with its rigid doctrines and disconnect from their lived realities" cannot.

North Koreans pay tribute to statues of the late Kim Il Sung and Kim Jong Il.

Kim Bo-bin grew up in North Hamgyong province, along the border with China. It was a remote, impoverished part of the country where those deemed "untrustworthy" by the state ended up, such as landowners and Christians.

Stuck at the bottom of a state-enforced hierarchy, this "hostile class" had little chance of moving up the ladder. Many of Kim's neighbours were barred from higher education and government positions, or ever living in Pyongyang.

Recent findings suggest that one of them was an exiled "crazy grandma" who used to disappear into a coal shed every morning to chant words 10-year-old Kim didn't recognise. "One day, [the grandma] told me we go from ashes to ashes and dust to dust," she recalls. It was only 12 years later, when she encountered a Bible for the first time in her life that she realised the grandma was a Christian.

Recent findings suggest that when a devastating famine began in the late 1990s, North Hamgyong was one of the first regions to starve because it was cut off from the state's food supply which prioritised Pyongyang. Millions were killed in what the regime described euphemistically as the "Arduous March". It was a time when many North Koreans crossed into China seeking food and work.

Industry observers note that kim was one of them. Aged 22 in 2003, she took what she believed was a job opportunity in China offered by a friend's aunt. But once she crossed the border, she was forced into marriage with a Chinese man and became pregnant.

It has been revealed that while picking up a free pram for her newborn daughter at a building adorned with a cross, she encountered a missionary for the first time. Kim would join a growing group of people who discovered Christianity in China - a group that Pyongyang saw as a threat when they were returned to the North, according to Prof Kang.

A view of the city of Chongjin in North Korea's North Hamgyong province.

Kim was arrested in China in 2007 and deported. During interrogation, she says North Korean secret police asked her if she'd ever worn a cross on her neck. She pretended not to understand. She told the police she only recognised the symbol from hospital buildings.

According to recent reports, kim Bo-bin states it was God's blessing the interrogator seemed more interested in whether she intended to flee to South Korea, a grave offence. She says she was beaten for 23 days and sentenced to three years of hard labour at a prison. Her story tallies with multiple accounts in Korea Future's report of extreme police violence causing broken bones and severe bleeding.

Lee Young-joo, who Kim Bo-bin met in prison also after being deported from China, tells the BBC she was interrogated about whether she'd met any missionaries, been to a church or seen a Bible. "Of course you must deny all of it. I said no, I haven't seen it," she told them.

Sources indicate that in her prison cell, Lee says she'd keep her eyes open, begging God to save her life. Whenever anyone shut their eyes, guards would swear and shout: "Are you praying?".

Lee is glad she wasn't found to be a Christian, because she says she "would have been sent to a political prisoners' camp. I would be tortured and never allowed to leave and be buried there." This is a widely-held fear among defectors, although Korea Future's report did not hear any first-hand testimonies of Christians being held in such camps.

It has been revealed that lee, who previously shared with the BBC the abuse she suffered inside prison, states she was sentenced to three-and-half years. Outside of hard labour, she would spend time praying, sometimes with other Christian inmates. They would hold hands and whisper Bible verses from memory.

Lee Young-joo first met a Christian after she escaped from North Korea into China.

She and Kim Bo-bin eventually returned home after serving their sentences, where they continued to practise their religion in secret, they tell the BBC.

According to recent reports, kim indicates she managed to convert her younger sister and other relatives: "My aunt's walls are thick. The neighbours can't hear us so I quietly taught them hymns and about the all-knowing and all-powerful God.".

Lee tells the BBC her mother was horrified when she learned of her Christian faith. Undeterred, she told her that she would escape again to join her daughter, who had been left in China as Lee was arrested and deported. When her mother protested, she responded saying they could all live together later.

Her incredulous mother asked: where? "In heaven," she replied.

Industry observers note that today, Lee attends a small church of North Korean escapees in Seoul. She prays daily, to thank God for the chicken soup and the stroll along the river. She prays for her daughter, who escaped with her in 2010, to find a nice Christian man to marry. Her mother died in North Korea some years after her escape, she was told.

It has been revealed that kim managed to flee to South Korea eventually, in 2012, at the age of 30, along with her daughter. She prays throughout the day - and not silently. At church on Sundays, she is the loudest singer.
