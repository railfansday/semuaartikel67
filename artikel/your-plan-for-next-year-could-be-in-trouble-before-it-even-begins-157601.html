A large business unit was entering its annual planning cycle with three fresh priorities competing for specialist capacity. The leadership team had already reduced the list once, and the remaining proposals had strong business cases, clear owners and estimated resource needs.

The planning baseline also highlighted an older regulatory-remediation program that would continue into the new year. Nobody had forgotten it. The work was visible, funded and already staffed because the program had missed its original completion date.

During the planning session, one executive asked whether the remediation program would release its specialists early enough for the latest priorities to start as planned. The program lead mentioned the current end date still held unless the final regulatory review produced additional work. The room accepted that date and returned to ranking the new priorities, treating the remediation capacity as already committed until then.

What the planning discussion didn’t do was force the remediation work to compete again with the three latest priorities. It sat in the baseline as committed work, while leaders debated how to allocate what they believed was the remaining capacity.

The problem became visible in the first weeks of execution. One of the approved priorities needed several of the same risk, legal and data specialists who were still closing the remediation program.

The planning model counted those people as partially available because the older work was expected to wind down early in the year. Instead, a final set of regulatory findings required more analysis, and a small group of specialists stayed fully engaged.

The new priority still had its approved funding, executive sponsor and start date. The people needed to move it weren’t actually free.

The old work was visible, but its claim on the upcoming year’s capacity had been treated as fixed background rather than as a choice.

Sources indicate that managers began stretching the same specialists across both commitments. The newly introduced priority’s first milestone slipped as specialist reviews that should have happened early were pushed behind remediation work. Meetings multiplied, decisions slowed, and work that looked fully staffed on paper started moving in fragments.

The pressure spread beyond those specialists. Project leads started moving milestones to fit the people they could get, finance held back some spending because dependent work couldn’t start, and senior leaders spent more time resolving clashes between commitments they believed had already been reconciled during planning.

The interesting thing was that the approved blueprint hadn’t changed, but the amount of work that could move at the same time already had.

This is where the “Hidden Problems” execution leak appears.

A baseline records what’s already in motion, but it doesn’t force old work to justify its capacity against new work. Once carryover is treated as given, only the new initiatives are asked to compete.

That distinction matters because visibility can create false confidence.

Leaders can see every major piece of work on one page and still never face the question of whether an old commitment deserves to keep the people and budget it carried across the year boundary.

When execution starts slowly, leadership tightens prioritization. Leaders ask for fewer initiatives, sharper business cases and clearer ranking, then delay or reject new proposals that can’t justify scarce capacity.

In a recent development, those actions improve the choices among new priorities; however, they don’t reopen the older commitments already sitting in the baseline. That was the pattern in this business unit.

The remediation program wasn’t part of that debate because it had been approved in the prior year, and finishing it was treated as an “obligation” rather than as a competing use of scarce capacity.
