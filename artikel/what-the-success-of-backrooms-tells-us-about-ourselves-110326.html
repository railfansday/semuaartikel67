What the Success of "Backrooms" Tells Us About Ourselves

Recent findings suggest that the 2026 horror film Backrooms became one of the surprise hits of the year. Made for about $10 million, it has taken in close to $400 million worldwide, making it the biggest box-office success in the history of its distributor, A24. The film follows the troubled owner of a furniture store who discovers a doorway in his basement. It leads to another dimension: an endless maze of eerily distorted office rooms, apparently haunted by strange creatures.

It has been revealed that the film's backstory is at least as fascinating as the film itself. The Backrooms began life as a "creepypasta," a bit of digital horror that gets copy-pasted across the internet. In 2019, customers on a paranormal-themed board on the forum 4chan were sharing images that just felt "off." One highlighted an empty room with yellow wallpaper and a stained carpet.

An anonymous user added a caption, and the Backrooms were born:.

If you're not careful and you noclip out of reality in the wrong areas, you'll end up in the Backrooms, where it's nothing but the stink of old moist carpet, the madness of mono-yellow, the endless background noise of fluorescent lights at maximum hum-buzz, and approximately six hundred million square miles of randomly segmented empty rooms to be trapped in.God save you if you hear something wandering around nearby, because it sure as hell has heard you.

Sources indicate that "Noclipping" is a video-game cheat that lets the player pass through walls and floors. Fans went on to build the Backrooms into a sprawling mythology of "levels" and "entities," and in 2022, a 16-year-old YouTuber, Kane Parsons, turned it into a series of found-footage shorts. Four years later, he directed the feature film.

It has been revealed that here's the thing, though: The photo reveals nothing frightening. Online sleuths have traced it to a hobby store in Oshkosh, Wisconsin, photographed during a renovation in 2002. Everything frightening comes from the caption, and from what it invites us to imagine. That is a clue to how the Backrooms works on us.

Backrooms belongs to a strand of horror, sometimes called "elevated horror," that favors dread over the startles of jump scares and the gross-outs of broken bodies. My colleague Jens Kjeldgaard-Christiansen (2024) arranges horror's scares along a scale of psychological sophistication. At one end are jump scares, often dismissed as "dumb scares" because they rely on a simple startle reflex. Next are immediate, concrete threats—such as monsters—that produce fear. Further up the scale are threats whose presence we have to infer, which cause anxiety and apprehension, and at the far end are abstract threats that disorient us. Backrooms aims mainly at the upper end of that scale. Through sounds and glimpses, such as distant footsteps or a fleeting shadow, it keeps suggesting that something is out there, roaming those endless, yellow hallways.

Recent findings suggest that why is that so effective? The psychologists Francis McAndrew and Sara Koehnke (2016) found that creepiness is a response to ambiguous threat: We don't know if there is danger, or what kind of danger it is. But not every ambiguous threat is creepy. A coughing stranger is an ambiguous infection risk, but hardly a creep. Kjeldgaard-Christiansen and I have therefore proposed an amendment: Creepiness arises from disrupted mentalization (Kjeldgaard-Christiansen & Clasen, 2023). We get creeped out when we cannot read the mind of another being, so that it seems threateningly unpredictable.

Recent findings suggest that the Backrooms is full of such cues. Someone built these rooms, placed the furniture, switched on the lights. But who, and why? The caption ends on exactly this note: Something is wandering nearby, and it has heard you.

It has been revealed that the rooms themselves add to the effect. They are ordinary office décor gone subtly wrong, with odd proportions and endless repetition. The researchers Alexander Diel and Michael Lewis (2022) found that buildings that deviate slightly from what we expect are rated as eerier — an "uncanny valley" of places.

The film also taps into a primal fear of being lost in a bad place. According to prospect-refuge theory, proposed by the geographer Jay Appleton (1975), we prefer landscapes that offer both prospect and refuge: an open view and a place to hide. The Backrooms offer neither. There are no landmarks and no exit. Every corner may hide something, and there is always another corner. McAndrew (2020) lists poor visibility and restricted movement among the features that make places creepy.

Some critics read the film as a portrait of a generation's fear of spending eternity in a meaningless office landscape. But first and foremost, it targets something much older: a mind tuned to detect hidden agents and dangerous places. Like The Blair Witch Project before it, Backrooms did not succeed despite showing us almost nothing. It succeeded because our minds are so eager to fill the void, and so quick to fill it with danger. We are a fearful species, but also a fear-loving one—and that's why we flock to movies such as Backrooms to be scared.
