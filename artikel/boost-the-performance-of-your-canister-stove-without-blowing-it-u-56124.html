It has been revealed that I had just finished pitching my tent and inflating my sleeping pad on a recent trip, and I could not wait to tear open the packet of dried miso soup I’d packed. My fingers were chilled from fiddling with guylines, and I craved the feeling of a steaming cup cradled in my hands. I screwed my ultralight stove onto a fuel canister, squeezed some water from my filter bag into a pot, and lit up. And then I proceeded to wait for what felt like ages. The flame was low and weak. Had I packed a nearly empty canister? No. The feeble flame was a harbinger, much like the leaves littering the trail: Winter was coming.

Canister stoves, like the ones most backpackers use during the summer hiking season, perform poorly when the mercury drops. Cold temperatures lower the internal pressure of the canister, causing it to falter, sputter, or produce a smaller flame. Regular winter campers often solve this problem by packing liquid fuel stoves like the MSR Whisperlite, which perform well in below freezing temperatures. Some canister stoves have a built-in pressure regulator that helps them stay efficient in the cold. But not every backpacker wants to shell out for an entire quiver of camp stoves. That’s where a little innovation comes in: Keeping a canister warm, even when the ambient temperature is frigid, helps maintain pressure so you can boil water on a regular canister stove all year round. Do it the wrong way, however and you might end up scorching more than your food.

Recent findings suggest that when it comes to boosting the efficiency of your canister stove in winter, the key is gentle warming. A fuel canister’s contents are pressurized; when it is cold out, your goal is to increase that pressure ever so slightly. But overheating—and thus overpressurizing—can cause the canister to explode.

Warnings printed on the side of a canister tell this tale. Most manufacturers caution consumers to keep a canister below 120°F. Heating a canister by rapid means, like placing it in boiling water is a very bad idea—just ask the guy who punched a hole in his ceiling trying to heat a canister for a fuel transfer.

It has been revealed that how else can one warm a canister? One hiker on Reddit suggested sticking a chemical hand warmer next to the canister and enclosing it in an insulated pouch. But experts Backpacker spoke to did not recommend that method.

“You’re adding a continuous heat source while also insulating the canister, and there’s no particularly good way to monitor or control its temperature,” notes Adam Wilcox, senior mechanical design engineer at Jetboil.

Hothands, manufacturer of popular chemical warmers, claims that their products reach an average of 135°F, and warns that the maximum temperature can surpass 158°F. Granted, this doesn’t mean that placing a hand warmer beside your fuel canister will immediately warm the fuel contents to that same temperature, but it does heighten your risk of dangerously increasing  the pressure. Gentle warming is a safer bet.

“Body heat before use or a controlled warm-water bath are much more predictable ways of warming a cold canister,” says Wilcox. “The key with water is warm, not hot, and avoiding excessive heating of the canister.”.

For a quick boil, keeping the fuel canister inside your jacket for a few minutes prior to use may do the trick to increase internal pressure. Of course, the effects of this will diminish rather quickly as you run your stove and expose the canister to cold air. If you need to run your stove a little longer to cook a meal or melt snow, a warm water bath can provide safe, continuous warming. Place your fuel canister inside a dish or spare pot. (I like to carry a collapsible silicone bowl for this purpose.) Light your stove. As soon as the water begins to warm but before it boils, pour some into the dish surrounding your canister. You should immediately notice improved performance.

In a recent development, shopping for a new stove? Springing for one with a pressure regulator can help it perform more consistently across seasons. MSR claims that its Pocket Rocket Deluxe can run at full power at a fuel canister pressure of as little as 16.5 PSI. (A full canister would typically be at 45 PSI at room temperature.).
