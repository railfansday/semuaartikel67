DeepHealth announced on November 24, 2026 that the U.S. Food and Drug Administration had cleared Mammo Risk through the 510(k) pathway. The software estimates three-, four- and five-year breast cancer risk from an existing 3D mammogram and is commercially available to healthcare providers across the United States.

Recent findings suggest that the clearance is dated November 23, 2026. DeepHealth describes Mammo Risk as the first FDA-cleared image-based breast-cancer risk-prediction solution for 3D mammography, including combined 3D/2D imaging workflows. Its intended role is to flag patients with no apparent signs of cancer whose mammograms nevertheless suggest elevated future risk, giving clinicians information for personalized screening pathways and consideration of supplemental imaging. The output concerns future risk rather than detection of an existing tumor, and no additional scan is required.

Victoria Smart, senior vice president of mission at Susan G. Komen, said: “Everyone’s biology and breast cancer risk are different, yet too often we take a one-size-fits-all approach to screening and care.”.

DeepHealth indicates the AI was trained on 220,000 studies and derives personalized risk estimates directly from routine mammogram images, unlike conventional assessment approaches that rely on extensive patient questionnaires and clinical information. The software is compatible with supported systems from multiple vendors.

DeepHealth reports that an independent, multicenter pivotal study of more than 65,000 screening mammograms at 10 U.S. clinical sites demonstrated strong predictive performance, with consistent results across subgroups including Black women and women with dense breasts.

Mammo Risk builds on earlier DeepHealth technology designed to predict risk up to two years ahead. That technology was CE-marked and commercially available in Europe, with clinical validation across European and U.S. screening populations using both 2D and 3D mammography. The cleared version extends the prediction horizon to three, four and five years.

Sources indicate that the 2022 study in Science Translational Medicine, led by Karolinska Institutet researchers, documents the earlier model. It used a nested case-control design drawn from 154,200 multiethnic women aged 35 to 74 who underwent digital breast tomosynthesis, or 3D mammography, screening in the United States between 2014 and 2019. The analysis included 805 incident cancers and 5,173 matched healthy women.

In a recent development, the paper reports that the model was developed to predict late-stage and interval cancers after negative screening exams. Its one-year discrimination, a measure of how well it distinguished women who developed cancer from those who did not, was 0.82, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.79 to 0.85. Under the high-risk threshold used in the paper, 14% of women met the cutoff; that group contained 76% of stage II and III cancers and 59% of stage 0 cancers.

According to recent reports, those results concern the earlier research model, not Mammo Risk’s pivotal study. The paper also discloses iCAD funding and an image-based risk-patent license to iCAD.

In a recent development, mammo Risk joins DeepHealth’s Radiology AI Suite and complements Mammo Dx, pairing future-risk prediction with cancer-detection technology. DeepHealth, a wholly owned subsidiary of RadNet, looks to integrate Mammo Risk into RadNet’s mammography screening initiatives, including the Enhanced Breast Cancer Detection program. The integration is planned rather than completed.

The American Medical Association’s CPT Category III code descriptors list 1137T and 1138T for algorithm-derived five-year breast malignancy risk scores with automated results. Code 1137T applies to a screening-mammogram dataset acquired in a separate session, while 1138T applies to a dataset acquired in the same session. Both were released to the AMA website on October 2, 2026, and take effect January 1, 2027.

According to recent reports, the AMA explains that Category III codes are temporary tools for collecting utilization and outcomes data on emerging services. Inclusion does not establish clinical efficacy, payer coverage or reimbursement, and payments depend on payer policies rather than a yearly fee schedule.

Recent findings suggest that the clearance puts a future-risk tool into the U.S. market, while the AMA’s coding framework separates tracking from payment. The central practical question is whether the scores become part of screening decisions and whether the resulting utilization and outcomes evidence supports reimbursement by payers.
