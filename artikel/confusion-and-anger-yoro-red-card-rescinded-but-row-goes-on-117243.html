Confusion and anger - Yoro red card rescinded, but row goes on

Leny Yoro will be available for Manchester United's game against Brentford on Saturday after his one-match ban was rescinded.

Leny Yoro's overturned red card against Liverpool appears to have turned into an unseemly difference of opinion between Manchester United and referees' body Pro Ref.

Let's recap what happened on Sunday.

Yoro was sent off after bringing down Liverpool striker Alexander Isak in the eighth minute of the 4-0 defeat at Anfield.

Referee Sam Barrott sent off the France international, and the decision was backed up by video assistant referee (VAR) John Brooks.

Recent findings suggest that it split opinion among pundits and fans. Would Isak have got to the ball before goalkeeper Senne Lammens? Was it really a clear goalscoring chance?

Then came the events of Tuesday.

First, a Football Association regulatory commission upheld United's appeal and cancelled the red card.

Then Pro Ref boss Howard Webb mentioned the red card was not "clearly wrong" - just moments before United held a pre-match news conference.

Over to Red Devils head coach Mauricio Pochettino, who was fiercely critical of the decision, and in particular the VAR.

But how can Pro Ref still believe the decision was right when an FA commission stated it was wrong?

BBC Sport looks at how the system works.

In a recent development, so United won their appeal. Pro Ref would disagree. Could the two sides not just agree to differ?

"You ask me a question and I need to defend our situation and our club and our players," Pochettino said about Yoro's red card.

It continued a theme from his post-match news conference.

You can see why Pochettino would be keen to talk about the red card, rather than the performance of his players. After all, United had just lost the first game of his tenure. Was the Argentine keen to create a siege mentality?

You can also understand United's frustration - two of their biggest games of the season so far have been defined by refereeing decisions.

The first was the VAR error to allow Erling Haaland's match-winning goal in the Manchester derby, when Enzo Fernandez clearly committed an offside offence.

Recent findings suggest that as for the Yoro incident, Pochettino reserved much his criticism for the VAR. United felt he only took a cursory look at where the ball would run.

It should be noted that Match Officials Mic'd up, on which Webb gave his views, was filmed on Monday evening - before the result of the appeal.

Sources indicate that would that change his opinion? Almost certainly not.

It has been revealed that webb acknowledged the argument against a red card, which was Isak would not get the ball. But he was adamant it was not such an egregious error that it required a VAR intervention.

So are we back to arguing about when VAR should get involved?

It has been revealed that the VAR threshold is too high, until it is too low. Rinse and repeat.

It is the Premier League clubs, and not Pro Ref nor the FA, who set where the bar sits.

Take the last stakeholders' survey, which helps to shape how the game is officiated.

It found 65% wanted to maintain the current threshold for VAR intervention, 20% to increase it, and just 15% to lower it.

But, of course, where the Yoro incident sits is still a matter of opinion.

'And the three points?' - Pochettino after Yoro red overturned.

How confusing it must be for a supporter just watching on, hoping to understand.

Recent findings suggest that an FA regulatory commission has mentioned the red card decision was wrong. Case closed? Not so fast.

Pro Ref will not always agree with the decision of an appeal board, nor will they use it as a barometer for future decisions.

And the burden of proof for a VAR intervention is considered higher than for an FA commission.

Pro Ref places far more stock upon the judgements of the Premier League's Key Match Incidents Panel.

The KMI Panel creates the merit table for referees, and helps shape how decisions are made in the future - especially for the VARs.

But surely the FA is the higher body? Not quite.

The FA is responsible purely for disciplinary measures and has no say on how games at the professional level are refereed.

If the Premier League tells Pro Ref the decision is right or wrong, that counts far more than any vote by an FA commission.

So how did United make their case?

BBC Sport understands the club argued that if Isak had maintained his top recorded speed, he would not have got to the ball first.

Sources indicate that the club say that is objectively provable, within minutes, using data. This, they say, was not a subjective decision.

To argue it was not subjective goes completely against the nature of the decision - it required a referee's judgement.

It has been revealed that whatever this data was, it most definitely was not available to the referees. The VAR cannot call upon a 3D animation of each player, detailing their running speed and providing an objective result.

How much stock did the commission place on this evidence? We will not know until the written reasons are published in a few days' time.

But United are not going to let this lie, and Pro Ref is not going to bend.

Such is the way FA commissions work, you can never be sure who will win.

There are three panel members who are usually former players, but occasionally you might get a retired referee.

The role of the commission is not to "usurp the referee by re-refereeing the incident".

A designated referee delegate is present to answer questions on the laws of the game, but they cannot offer an opinion on the incident.

Industry observers note that the only input Pro Ref has is the official's report - a short entry by the referee that notes which law was used for the red card. There is no other context.

It will read something like this: "I have to report that I, as the referee, sent off Yoro, Leny of Manchester United FC Under Law 12 section: S5.".

The burden is on the club to prove its case by submitting a body of evidence - videos, images and testimony.

There is no right of reply. The evidence is weighed up by the panel, a vote is cast and the majority wins.

It makes predicting the outcome notoriously difficult, as the system is an opinion poll based upon a club trying to persuade at least two people to agree with them.

Industry observers note that take a look at Aston Villa's successful appeal against Douglas Luiz's red card for violent conduct four years ago. Luiz had been sent off via a VAR review for a headbutt on Aleksandar Mitrovic.

The panel's written reasons stated the appeal was won partly because Luiz was "a few inches shorter than Mr Mitrovic so his forehead is naturally at the same height as Mr Mitrovic's nose/face as the two players come together".

So, when the two players bumped chests the headbutt was just an accident. Appeal upheld because Luiz was a bit shorter.

Some might think anything is worth trying.
