I've been inspired to share in this letter about my first encounter with The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time following the feature you ran on this very topic, because it's a touching, unique story.

I was about 11 years old in 1998 and had been avidly following OoT in the magazines, absorbing every screenshot and article about this sword and shield weilding greened tuniced hero.

According to recent reports, bizarrely I'd never played a Zelda game before but something about it looked magical and exciting. So I did what any 11 year old would do. I asked for the game for Christmas.

Recent findings suggest that so, Christmas morning arrives and I excitedly tumble down the stairs to the presents under the tree. I open them all but.... There's a pang of absence. No Zelda game. Where is it I exclaim!? And my mum sheepishly informs me that it didn't arrive in time for Christmas.

Sources indicate that I cry. Real tears. I'm distraught. Inconsolable. For months I've waited for this amazing looking game and I can't play it.

What happened next is... I think... wonderful parenting.

Industry observers note that while I was busy in my bedroom creating my own water temple with more tears than a kingdom (see what I did there?), my mum phones every parent of my school friends she knows until she finds someone with a copy of Zelda. And eventually, she does. She creeps up to knock on my door and says "Hey, Listen! I'll take you to your friends place, he has a copy".

And so, my tears stop and off I go to watch my pal play the game and suddenly a terrible Christmas becomes a brilliant one. The game is everything I imagine it to be and more and my mate and I spend hours solving Deku Tree puzzles and gasping at Hyrule Field's majesty.

So as we all enjoy the remake of Ocarina of Time decades later, I just have one thing to say: Thanks mum. You understood the stakes and you cared enough to help me get my Zelda fix that fateful Christmas of 1998.

Sources indicate that and ever since that day, my life has been met with the least terrible of fates: I became a huge fan of Zelda and have bought every single one since on day one - no more painful Christmas mornings! YoshiTails.

Industry observers note that that is some above-and-beyond parenting, Mrs YoshiTails! We salute you.

Sources indicate that I'm imagining what my mum would have remarked to some random mother ringing her on Christmas morning, asking if their kid could come over to play a video game. I assume she would have reacted in the classic British manner: agreeing to it with bemusement while silently seething, resenting the interloper for every surrendered mince pie.

I joke! I had no friends. I did have Zelda, though. - Ed.

We seem to have sleepwalked into an era of walking simulators.

I’m not talking about artsy indie games emphasising investigation over action, but massive budget AAA titles starring main “characters” who look like they’ve walked out of a Hollywood movie. There are too many humans in games and it’s stifling the medium.

Back when the mascot character was king, protagonists felt properly differentiated both aesthetically and mechanically: Mario jumps, Sonic runs, Spyro flies, Crash bangs and wallops. Of these, only Mario could be called ‘human’, and even he has about as much in common with a real plumber as Sonic does with a real hedgehog.

Sources indicate that vibrant colours and exaggerated movements helped make the games legible on small screens when pixels, polygons and processing power came at a premium, and this gave games a unique vibe and feel. Now that we can better render humans in all their hairy, fleshy complexity, video game aesthetics have moved from ‘cartoonish’ to ‘cinematic’, and physics have followed suit.

Industry observers note that thing is, if I wanted to control a human, I’d put the controller down and go for a walk. (…Maybe not a bad idea actually). We’re meandering, clunky lumps of meat who navigate the world in a most pedestrian manner. Fine for a trip to the shops. Not the most inspiring thing to control in a video game.

It should be said that the ‘mascot character’ isn’t completely dead. The House of Mario was built by pixelated protags who carry the forward the flame of the 8-bit era. The portly purported plumber is still as satisfyingly springy as ever. DK has swapped pixels for vowels but continues to dish out delightful destruction like a primate possessed. Even the fresh squid-kids on the block - Splatoon’s Inklings - are unashamedly ‘gamey’, slipping slickly through their own mercurial fluids.

In a recent development, it’s a pleasant reminder that video games let us be anything we want: a bird (Untitled Goose Game), a plane (Star Fox) or even a kickflipping train (Denshattack). With that in mind, it seems a shame to be stuck as the same humdrum human that AAA titles insist on lumbering us with.

Industry observers note that as film studios conglomerate and cinematic universes bleed into each other, I wish video games would embrace what makes them unique rather than dissolving into the same great multimedia melange. I’ve had enough of the homogeneous Homo sapiens-centric pseudo-cinema now synonymous with AAA game development and wish that more creative energies would be put into games that actually push the mechanical possibilities of the medium.

Yours misanthropically, GameMechanixz.

I disagree, purely because there are just so many games of all flavours out there. If you want to be a squirrel or a crab or an insect or anything else, there's never been a better time. Significant, small, there's just so much incredible variety. If AAA isn't doing it for you, the other As have got you covered.

I get the sense perhaps you're reacting to the priority of animation over gamefeel in some cases - lumpy realistic animation versus snappy response? Pushing right on the stick and a moment later a soupy animation cycle transitions into the movement you've input (as opposed to Mario or Link responding instantly, if unrealistically).

If that's the case, I agree that Nintendo's approach is my preference, whatever form of animal, vegetable or mineral you're controlling. The animation of an Assassin's Creed or a GTA is incredibly impressive, but it's interminably slow if you're coming to it with a Mario mindset. I find I have to be invested in the cinema and 'show' of those games if they're going to click. My all-time favourites tend to have much more responsive controls, though. - Ed.

When Nintendo announced Pikmin Day this year for November 1st, it seemed to me that it was met with mixed reactions. People remarked that saying 11/1 looks like Pikmin is a bit of a stretch, and they didn't really announce anything particularly interesting. While this is true, I found it positively delightful, specifically the second half of the direct when Fuka Koshiba was revealed to be hired by Nintendo. While their conversation was a bit awkward (as Nintendo Direct dialogue always is), I thought it was super cool that they hired her just from seeing her posts on Instagram. And I've been loving the charming shorts that they've been releasing recently. And I'm not even a significant Pikmin fan!

It has been revealed that the direct was weird, certainly unnecessary, and a bit awkward, but that's what made it amazing. It was delightfully quirky, dumb, delightful, and wholly Nintendo.
