It has been revealed that when startup founder Safouen (Saf) Rabah decided to get in on the AI boom, he didn’t start by coding or by training any models. He got in touch with urban planners at Bellevue City Hall, and together they combed through nearly 300 land use permit applications to find common mistakes and bottlenecks.

Bellevue began using Rabah’s platform, Govstream.AI, in the summer of 2025, starting with an AI assistant for city staff. In a test using past applications, about three times as many were judged complete with Govstream’s tool. Rabah indicates that change alone has the potential to alter the housing stock of the city.

Sources indicate that “Why can’t we build more housing? All the macro economic reasons, inflation, material costs, labor, land scarcity, are all exacerbated by the slow permitting process,” Rabah said. “That’s what we want to change.”.

So, how did Rabah, a B2B software engineer by training, find his way to land use permitting?

Recent findings suggest that rabah hadn’t heard of the government technology sector, aka GovTech, until mid-career, but looking back, the signs were all there. He was born in Sfax, Tunisia, and grew up in Tunis. Throughout his childhood, he dreamed of running for office.

Industry observers note that “I come from a small developing country, so there’s always this idea of, what could a small developing country become? How can it prosper and grow?” Rabah mentioned. “Building has always been a major part of what I imagined myself doing.”.

In a recent development, as a teenager in the 1980s, he attended a USAID-funded boarding school in Tunis. The school was part of Cold War efforts to expand American soft power around the Middle East; classes were conducted in English and many of the students went on to university in North America.

“The school had the first Macintosh lab in all of Africa,” Rabah said. “I can still picture the USAID logo on the back of each computer.”.

Rabah got a scholarship to University of British Columbia, where he studied electrical engineering while running what he calls his “first entrepreneurial venture”: a service that built and hosted websites for small businesses.

That led to a job at a startup called Pivotal, a customer relationship management platform where Rabah led a team responsible for e-business applications. After Pivotal went public, Rabah left for a job at TELUS, one of Canada’s “substantial three” telecommunications corporations, but swiftly felt frustrated by the dynamics at such a large organization.

It has been revealed that “Even though I worked on nascent products, it was very, very bureaucratic,” Rabah remarked. “There were committees, a hundred thousand employees. I missed building things.”.

Rabah began to look for a upcoming job that would not only allow him to build but also to make a difference.

“Being an immigrant, it’s always tempting to stay content with having a good job, good status,” Rabah said. “But I’ve always enjoyed the ‘what if?’ mindset, the clean slate. It’s this way today, but could we imagine it to be something different?”.

That vision led him to Socrata, which in 2010 was just a nascent government technology startup.

“I really connected with the founder,” Rabah noted. “It had a really cool mission, it combined the ‘do-well’ with ‘do-good.’”.

Socrata was one of the first platforms to host data portals for local governments and federal agencies, allowing citizens to access and map public data sets. Many U.S. cities, including Seattle, still use Socrata’s software today to publish information including crime logs, permits, and budgets.

“We were a substantial part of pioneering the open data movement,” Rabah said. “At one point almost every major city was our customer, and half the states.”.

Socrata was acquired by Tyler Technologies in 2018, and Rabah stayed on until 2024.

According to recent reports, “The funny part is today we use that data quite a lot in our work because one of the most popular data sets that any city publishes is its permit data,” Rabah said. “We’ve even built a connector between Socrata and Govstream.”.

But once Socrata was acquired, Rabah began to feel the same way he had at TELUS: boxed in by bureaucracy.

It has been revealed that “Tyler was a really good company, but it is a big company,” Rabah said. “Tech was getting repetitive, and I was thinking about leaving it altogether.”.

It has been revealed that “The thing that pushed me over the edge is that AI happened,” Rabah said. “It was one of those moments where once you start working with it, you really can’t help but think, ‘I can revisit virtually every assumption I’ve made over the last 20 years, every constraint.’”.

Immediately, he thought of his passion for building—building a startup, building a better country, and literally building a building.

“I’ve been fascinated by the built environment since my Lego days: What makes some cities livable and others not?” Rabah remarked. “Then, living in Seattle, you see the lack of housing, affordability, and the homelessness problem we have, and you’re like, why can’t we build more housing? I started believing that these problems are in fact solvable if we simply speed things up and simplify things with AI.”.

In July of 2024, Rabah began working with his “design partner,” Bellevue’s Development Services Department, to learn more about common permitting problems.

“We started with something visionary and high-level, but you can only get there if you understand the granular problems of the day-to-day,” Rabah noted. “So we talked with developers and builders and we talked with the people on the other side of the counter who are processing these permits and guiding these builders.”.

In a recent development, he learned that most permitting mistakes are avoidable, like applying for the wrong zone or forgetting to include a north arrow on an architectural strategy. Those oversights can send an application back to square one while clogging up the permitting process for all other projects.

“The wait time disproportionately affects lower margin projects,” Rabah mentioned. “It really constrains the supply of housing, especially the housing we need more of, which is the more affordable kind.”.

In a recent development, govstream’s AI assistants are designed to help first-time builders avoid those errors and make permitting faster for everyone. Rabah and his team began by building what they call an “intelligent intake” platform for a city permitting portal, which guided users through preparing to submit their applications.

When Bellevue first tested Govstream’s intake tool on 276 past residential building permit applications, the city reported a completeness rate of 67%, compared with 22% when those applications were originally filed. According to the company, the latest testing has yielded an 88% completeness rate.

More recently, they’ve begun to build out an AI assistant for the backend, which assists city permit techs with the initial review and reduces what Rabah calls “toil,” like answering unnecessary pre-application emails and tracking down missing documents.

Bellevue staff estimated that Govstream’s tools saved them 152 hours over one month this summer by helping them research and respond to pre-application inquiries.

“For a developer, time is money, so the type of housing that can be built in Bellevue will become more affordable,” remarked Jake Hesselgesser, Bellevue’s development services director. “This technology has a direct and material impact on the cost of housing.”.

Eventually, Govstream.AI will be an end-to-end service, assisting in later stages of the permitting process, too. But Rabah is emphatic that Govstream will not give AI the final say.

“We always put our AI systems in the role of assistants that suggest stuff, but never decide stuff,” Rabah mentioned.

Rabah says Govstream uses preset rules, outside the AI model, to check whether a portion of the application is complete, whether a site needs a geotechnical report, or whether an architect is properly licensed. In Bellevue, city staff retain final authority.

Hesselgesser said that Rabah’s conservative approach to AI is what made the city of Bellevue want to work with his startup.

Recent findings suggest that “Our core principle around Bellevue’s use of AI is to retain human decision making, and we wanted to ensure that we were aligned with a company with the same core value,” Hesselgesser said. “Saf took that holistic approach.”.

Recent findings suggest that around the office, Rabah has become known for his eagerness to build. According to Mariia Derevianko, head of marketing, he spends his weekends building out a product or an idea in order to workshop it with the team.

It has been revealed that “He’s very hands-on with the product, with design, marketing, all of the aspects,” Derevianko said. “He never waits for the engineers to build something.”.

Most recently, he built a lead tracker to research which cities Govstream should partner with, and previously, he vibe-coded one of the research assistants in the Govstream platform as a template for his software engineers.

Derevianko, who joined the team last year after Rabah poached her from AWS, remarked he’s also known for his kindness.

“I originally didn’t want to join a start-up,” Derevianko noted. “I only said yes because I saw he is a genuinely good person who really cares about this project.”.

Govstream.AI has already won at least 12 contracts around the Puget Sound, including King County and the City of Everett, as well as cities across the country, such as Louisville, Kentucky.

Industry observers note that ultimately, Rabah has his eyes set on Seattle, where construction and development has been on the decline. Last December, the city council passed permanent “middle housing” rules to upzone residential neighborhoods and encourage the construction of ADUs, all of which will need a permit.

It has been revealed that seattle tested a competing AI tool from CivCheck in 2025. In June, city staff recommended a pilot that would let applicants check their submissions for completeness before filing.

“We need affordable housing in Seattle,” Rabah stated. “We would really, really love to help our home city streamline permitting and remove it as a barrier.”.

In founding Govstream.AI, Rabah has found the job he has been looking for: one in which he can build things without the bureaucracy of a large company, all while “doing good.”.

“This has become my obsession,” Rabah said. “I can point to things in a city and say, maybe Govstream was a part of what allowed this project to get built.”.
