Latest Update: Americans worry capitalism won’t work for them in the AI era. Here are 3 ways for companies to show that it can

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that according to Just Capital’s 2026 Americans’ Views on Business survey, three in four Americans believe the nation’s best days of innovation are happening now or are still to come. Yet only half say our current form of capitalism gives people like them a fair shot at getting ahead financially. The percentage of people who believe they will be financially worse off this time next year is at its highest since 1960. In other words, Americans believe that—in the age of AI and related innovation—our economic system is working for someone else, not them.

Bridging this divide is the defining leadership challenge of our time. And as the recent midterm elections highlighted, when people feel strongly enough about something they are apt to seek change.

Americans haven’t given up on business as a driver of solutions—78% say large companies have a role to play in solving important societal problems. The key is to show Americans they have a place in the AI-powered economy. Our assessment of the 500 largest American employers highlights that so far approximately 180 companies have publicly reported AI-related actions affecting their own employees or the broader workforce since December 2025. 161 of those companies announced actions that specifically aim to support their workers and the American workforce at large.

Sources indicate that these companies are primarily in industries with large frontline workforces like Personal Care, Drug, & Grocery; and Telecommunications. Companies in industries with close ties to the AI buildout such as Energy; Construction & Materials; and Utilities were less likely to have declared an action in our analysis. Of the twenty industries we examined, six had a higher percentage of companies taking action than the Technology industry, which sits at the center of the AI transition. However, the Tech companies that did announce an action took roughly twice as many as peers in other industries.

The first movers can offer lessons for leaders in every industry: redefine roles rather than eliminate them, open pathways into skilled trades, and equip the next generation of innovators.

According to recent reports, many experts now predict AI will reshape jobs rather than lead to a wholesale disruption of the workforce. Our data reflects the same trend. In the Fall 2026 edition of Just Capital’s quarterly survey of the American public, investors, and corporate leaders, 52% of corporate leaders say they have redefined or transitioned existing roles over the past 12 months compared to 15% who say they have conducted layoffs. Several companies show how this could work in practice.

Recent findings suggest that at General Dynamics Information Technology (GDIT), 12,000 employees have moved into new roles inside the company since 2023, and more than 9,000 have earned certifications in AI, cyber and other technologies. Kimberly-Clark takes a similar approach to training. Its “IT Digital University” covers 17 skill areas, and employees are assessed before and after, with completers averaging 60% skill growth. ServiceNow put AI agents to work on its own operations, and they now resolve 90% of IT service desk tickets. Instead of laying off the service desk team, the company assessed everyone’s skills, provided role-based training and held one-on-one career conversations before moving anyone. As a result, 85% of the service desk moved into new roles in security, AI operations and executive briefing, and the other 15% now supervise the AI agents.

While the dominant narrative has focused on the negative impacts AI may have on the American workforce or on communities across the country, the real story may be a revolution of reskilling and internal mobility.

According to recent reports, our polling shows 47% of Americans aged 18 to 25 worry AI will make it harder to land an entry-level job in their field, yet only 20% of recent grads are considering a trade or licensed profession despite the U.S. facing a historic skilled trades labor shortage.

In a recent development, several companies are tackling the issue head on. Caterpillar is investing $100 million over five years in manufacturing and industry technician jobs in Illinois and Indiana, building a pipeline in the communities where it operates. Lowe’s has gone further by convening the nation’s largest skilled trades coalition, with NVIDIA, AT&T, Bank of America, Carrier, General Motors, DEWALT and Duke Energy, and a goal of training 1 million people. Beyond the founding corporate partners, Lowe’s also brought on over 75 educators, businesses, workforce organizations, industry vendors and stakeholders, including the National Association of Home Builders (NAHB), The National Center for Construction Education and Research (NCCER), SkillsUSA and Carhartt.

The message here is that workforce readiness is not a problem to be owned by any one tech giant, and the firms with the most to gain from solving it are well placed to lead.

24% of Americans in the Americans’ Views on Business survey say that individual entrepreneurs and innovators are the primary driver of American innovation, more than large companies R&D investments (23%), venture capital and private investment (14%), government funding (13%), and universities and academic research (13%).

Several companies stand out for cultivating entrepreneurial capacity. Boeing put $250,000 behind Technovation, pairing its engineers as mentors with 25,000 girls building AI-powered solutions worldwide. DoorDash offers a one-day Local Business Workshop that gives small business owners training, tools, local connections and advertising credits. Duke Energy sponsored the third annual high school AI design contest at the University of Florida, where student teams used AI tools to design a smart power grid for their own communities.

Recent findings suggest that investments like these, aimed at people early in their careers or businesses, can widen who gets to participate in what comes next. Investing early in entrepreneurs is also an investment in the customers, suppliers and talent pools companies will depend on later.

Just Capital’s monthly National AI Monitor asks 1,000 Americans how they are experiencing AI as workers, customers, community members, investors and stewards of the environment. Americans are net positive about AI today (+37 overall, +44 at work). But their outlook for the next few years falls to +13, with job loss among the main concerns for the future.

The examples of corporate action outlined here illustrate the path forward for leadership to address these concerns in a fresh era. By redefining roles, creating internal mobility, and fostering the skills of the future, including a focus on entrepreneurship, these companies are showing how to broaden access to opportunity rather than narrow it.

What’s needed next is to measure the success of these initiatives, scale those that work, and continue to explore other pathways. Leaders willing to make meaningful investments in and test new solutions for the American workforce will be repaid in the form of greater trust, goodwill, growth, and social license to operate; all crucial determinants of success in the AI-powered economy. They will also strengthen Americans’ faith in capitalism in the process.
