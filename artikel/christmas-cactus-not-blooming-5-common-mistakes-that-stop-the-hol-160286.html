Christmas Cactus Not Blooming? 5 Common Mistakes That Stop the Holiday Flower Show

A Christmas cactus not blooming is a common holiday letdown, and it stings more when the plant seems perfectly healthy with plump green stems. And you ask yourself, why won't my Christmas cactus flower when the plant looks just fine? A Christmas cactus not showing buds by the end of November is usually reacting to light, warmth, water or a move at the wrong moment. And, once you've worked out what the cause is, there's still time to fix some mistakes so you can enjoy the holiday flower show.

Industry observers note that A healthy Christmas cactus doesn't automatically mean you have a holiday houseplant that's ready to flower. Buds form in response to fall conditions – long dark nights and cooler temperatures – and many folk create the exact environment to make sure a Christmas cactus blooms right on time for the holidays. So, unless it gets those triggers to start forming buds, the plant won't flower even if you give your Christmas cactus all the care it needs in November to keep it healthy. Another common problem with a Christmas cactus not flowering is that it does form buds, but drops them before they open.

Sources indicate that there are five common mistakes that lead to zero holiday flowers. Which of these concerns is the reason why your Christmas cactus doesn't have buds yet?

Industry observers note that holiday cacti set flower buds when nights grow long. They need about 13 to 14 hours of uninterrupted darkness each night for six weeks or more, and that dark period must be truly dark. Even a table lamp switched on in the evening, a street light shining through the window or a hallway light left on overnight can interrupt the cycle, causing the plant to keep making stems instead of buds. This mistake demonstrates up as no buds at all.

If you suspect this is the reason your Schlumbergera doesn't have buds yet, place your plant in complete darkness after dinner then bring it back to bright, indirect light in the morning. A dark room that isn't used is perfect, and you can block any artificial light coming through the window with blackout curtains like Sun Zero Curtain Panels, available from Walmart. Another option is simply to cover the plant in an upturned cardboard box overnight.

Once buds have set, night length stops mattering as much, so evening light isn't a problem.

Cool nights are the other half of the trigger to get a Christmas cactus to flower. Holiday cacti need nights between 50°F and 59°F (10–15°C) to set buds, so keep a living room warm all evening with the furnace running and that signal disappears.

Industry observers note that move your Christmas cactus to a cooler spot at night, not a cold one. An unheated bedroom or a sunroom that cools down after dark often works, as long as temperature doesn't dip below 50°F (10°C). It's hard to guess how cool a room gets at three o'clock in the morning so a cheap indoor thermometer that records the maximum and minimum temperatures, like the TempPro digital indoor thermometer on Amazon, is handy to check the overnight number.

Once your plant has formed buds, it'' still very sensitive to environmental conditions, so be sure to avoid the common spots that cause a Christmas cactus to lose its buds.

Holiday cacti aren't desert cacti. They grow on rainforest trees in Brazil so don't like to bake dry the way a barrel cactus loves. Let the potting mix dry out hard while buds are forming and the plant may make fewer of them, or wilt and drop the ones it has. Keep the mix soggy and the roots can rot, which ends the same way – limp stems and buds on the floor.

In a recent development, once buds appear, keep the potting mix evenly moist but never wet. An easy way to check if your Christmas cactus needs watering is to push a long wooden skewer like these bamboo skewers from Walmart to the bottom of the pot and pull it out: if damp soil clings to the wood, wait a few days before watering again. An indoor watering can with a long goose-neck spout like this houseplant can from Walmart makes it much easier to be precise about how much water you're giving your plant. Empty the saucer after every watering too.

Once buds have formed, moving your plant is one of the easiest ways to lose them. Any sudden shift in light, temperature or humidity can trigger bud loss. Christmas cacti are so sensitive to their environment, it's not just moving the plant from a cool back bedroom to a warm, dim spot beside the tree that causes bud drop. Even moving your holiday houseplant to a different spot in the same room or rotating the pot can be enough to lose those precious buds.

Choose the spot where your plant will stay through the holidays as soon as buds appear, then leave it there. Check that spot for heat and drafts first. Buds also drop on plants near heating vents, a gas fireplace or a bowl of ripening fruit which gives off ethylene gas. The same care applies if you buy a plant with buds already set, such as this pack of 2 Holiday Christmas Cactus from Costa Farms, via The Home Depot. Ask for the plant to be wrapped against the cold for the trip home and put it in its final spot as soon as it arrives. A few buds will fall while it settles in but most will stay put as long as you don't move the plant again.

Sources indicate that holiday cacti should only be fed during active growth from spring through late summer, so don't fertilize after September. That break from fertilizer helps plants set more flower buds in fall. November feeding won't help a plant that already lacks buds and, on a stressed plant, extra salts in the pot do more harm than good.

Recent findings suggest that wait until the flowers finish and new growth starts in spring, then use a balanced food for indoor flowering plants such as Espoma Organic Violet! plant food, available on Amazon, once a month at half strength through summer.

If you don't see any signs of buds on your holiday houseplant by late November, it's worth trying the banana bag trick to trigger Christmas cactus blooms. However, flowers will likely open in early January rather than on Christmas morning.

Sources indicate that next year, start the dark nights in late September or early October so the timing lines up. Buds usually show up three to four weeks after long nights begin, and the flowers open after roughly six to eight weeks of short days.

A plant that already has small buds at its stem tips in mid-November has done the hard part, however, so as long as you keep your Christmas cactus in one spot that's not too warm at night, and evenly moist, those flowers should open on schedule.
