Judge denies motion to dismiss lawsuit accusing Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron of worsening RAM crisis by fixing memory prices and supply

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A judge has denied a motion to dismiss a US lawsuit against chip makers Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron. The suit acccuses the companies of fixing RAM prices and supply, thereby contributing to worsening the effects of the ongoing memory crisis.

The class action suit was filed at the United States District Court for the Northern District of California in June this year. It accuses Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron of moving in lockstep away from production of consumer-focused DDR3 and DDR4 RAM and towards HBM memory more typically used in AI datacenters. The suit claims that "in a competitive commodity market", "at least one of three large producers, seeing record prices, would expand output of the product whose price was rising", thereby forcing their competitors to either follow suit or risk losing out on customers.

Sources indicate that the trio of companies deny having colluded, arguing in a upcoming motion to have the case dismissed (thanks, IGN) that this suit simply retreads old ground covered in a previous antitrust case and quoting deceased judge Richard Posner. "[C]ompetitors in concentrated markets watch each other like hawks," was the Posner quote they wheeled out, which I mention because otherwise the response I'm about to lay out will sound like a very weird tangent.

"But Plaintiffs’ allegations go beyond hawks watching each other," judge Noël Wise wrote in her order to deny the dismissal motion. "Their contentions are more akin to hawks flying in formation – an activity that hawks ordinarily do not do.".

Recent findings suggest that you know a case is getting serious when facts about Avian behaviour start being wheeled out in response to similes. Bird chatter aside, all this decision means is that the case will rumble on, with both sides still having a chance to present more evidence or make points in their favour before any eventual decision is made by the courts.

So, shelve any hopes you might have of a rapid solution to the memory crisis, which presently looks likely to last until at least 2028.
