In a recent development, last Friday, Will Baude wrote a guest essay in the Upcoming York Times attempting to explain why the Supreme Court deserves our celebration. I responded that Will's only gestured reason is that a few of the Trump appointees demonstrate "independence" by ruling against Trump--not a very compelling reason. Apparently, Steve Vladeck offered a lengthy to Baude, which I didn't read. Baude, in turn, took the time to write a response to Vladeck:.

In a recent development, the [NYT] piece has been met with a large mix of outrage and ridicule, but I knew what I was getting in to. It has also prompted some more thoughtful responses. Chief among them, as usual, are two recent posts from Steve Vladeck . . .

In a recent development, I suppose my post falls into the "outrage and ridicule" category. Buckle up, because more unthoughtful outrage and ridicule is coming.

Recent findings suggest that today, the Upcoming York Times published a "News Analysis" by Carl Hulse titled "Supreme Court Rulings Cheered by Republicans Wound Up Hurting Them." For those who don't read the Times, "News Analysis" allow reporters to write op-eds under the guise of journalism. I usually don't see much of a difference between standard left-leaning news coverage and "News Analysis." Reporters can arrange quotes and select sources to convey a particular narrative. At least with the "News Analysis" label, reporters don't have to pretend to be neutral.

In any event, this piece unintentionally offers the real reason why the Roberts Court deserves our celebration: the decisions allows the people to prove they can govern themselves.

It has been revealed that let's start with the Voting Rights Act. For generations, elites told us that black and hispanic voters simply lacked the political power to organize and elect the candidates of their choice. As a result, platonic guardians in the federal government and benighted federal judges would have to superintend all facets of the democratic process to ensure democrats were elected. Even the most modest changes to voting procedures were attacked as a return to segregation. I am exaggerating slightly, but you get the gist.

The conservatives on the Court, however, dismantled that infrastructure. Justice Kagan and others warned that we would return to Jim Crow. Instead, we saw what happened when the people are able to govern themselves: the people do not need the Voting Rights Act anymore. If there was ever a need for that statute, that moment passed decades ago. Shelby County was right: times have changed.

Moreover, the political backlash to gerrymandering the districts amped up black voter turnout even more. Nothing happens in a vacuum. The Times reports:.

It has been revealed that the decision [Callais] enraged and energized Black voters in a region where the history of past attempts to restrict minority voting rights still runs deep. It spurred a backlash that amped up turnout, helping propel Democrats to big newly introduced majorities in the House and Senate.

In a recent development, "I went into 14 states, and it was everywhere," Representative James E. Clyburn, Democrat of South Carolina, mentioned of the deep resentment to the ruling seen as a final step in the court's dismantling of the Voting Rights Act, a premiere achievement of the civil rights movement. "Even in states that were not necessarily what you might call targets.".

In a recent development, "The Supreme Court was absolutely the motivator in all of this," added Mr. Clyburn, a civil rights activist first elected in 1992 when the court initially opened the door to "majority-minority" congressional districts to offset an ugly history of voter suppression.

In Shelby County, Justice Ginsburg wrote that striking down the coverage formulas was like "throwing away your umbrella in a rainstorm because you are not getting wet." But sometimes the only way to know if you still need the umbrella is to put it down, hold out your hand, and see if there is actually still a rainstorm. Black voters in the south, standing without an umbrella, didn't get wet. They triggered a blue Tsunami.

It has been revealed that the Times demonstrates another reason to celebrate the Supreme Court: they are not partisan in the common use of the term. In other words, they may matter rulings that conservatives like, but do not advance conservative politics. Dobbs is perhaps the greatest example. That decision led to significant electoral defeats across the country. Moreover, many states enacted constitutional amendments guaranteeing rights to abortion. Nearly five years after Dobbs, abortion is more available. And given the loss in the Texas AG's race, I think the Mifepristone battle is just about over.

It has been revealed that what about Trump's immigration tactics? The Supreme Court upheld the searches in Los Angeles based  on immigration status. Those raids triggered an electoral backlash among Hispanic voters who supported Trump in 2024. Indeed, the entire Texas gerrymander was premised on Hispanic voters in the valley supporting Republicans. The Supreme Court's smackdown of Judge Brown's ruling does not seem to have helped conservatives at all. The Times writes:.

Rahm Emanuel, the former House Democratic strategist who ran his party's successful effort to capture the majority in 2006, mentioned a trio of court decisions reverberated for Democrats. He pointed to the voting rights outcome; the grant of immunity from prosecution to President Trump that helped fuel a narrative of runaway corruption; and a decision by the court that allowed ethnicity to be used as justification for stops and searches by immigration agents.

Recent findings suggest that together, he noted, the rulings alienated Black Americans, Hispanics, more moderate Republicans and independents — all groups that had been crucial to the success of Mr. Trump and congressional Republicans in the last election.

According to recent reports, "It broke up their 2024 coalition," Mr. Emanuel stated.

In a recent development, even Mr. Trump seemed to concede the point on immigration enforcement, saying after his party's midterm drubbing that his federal crackdown might have been "too tough.".

Was the Roberts Court in the pocket of the Republican Party? Hardly. The Los Angeles case proved to be so unpopular that Trump scaled back the raids. Once again, the Roberts Court proves that not all problems need to be solved through contortions of the Constitution. The political process works.

The Times and liberals draws all the wrong lessons.

"John Roberts, perhaps unwittingly, undercut the Republican cause as much as anyone else except for Donald Trump," mentioned Senator Richard Blumenthal, Democrat of Connecticut, who served as a Supreme Court clerk in the mid-1970s. "He has tried to function as a politician in robes completely unsuccessfully because he has no intuitive grasp or experience in the political world.".

Recent findings suggest that the Court's conservatives are not trying to help or hurt Trump. For the most part, they issue rulings fully aware that the republican party may suffer. But they do so anyway. That is the definition of independence. Indeed, I wonder at some level whether John Roberts was trying to save the GOP by upholding Roe v. Wade. The real cause for celebration is there for all to see.

Recent findings suggest that my advice to the members of the Supreme Court: don't try "harder." Instead, keep doing what you're doing without regard to what the chattering class says. Your real legacy cannot be written in the moment a case is decided, or even at the next midterm election, but only when all of the current political moments have passed. Baude's op-ed will look quaint in a decade.
