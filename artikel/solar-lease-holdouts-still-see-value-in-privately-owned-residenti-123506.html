Solar lease holdouts still see value in privately-owned residential projects

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, the One Significant Beautiful Bill Act drastically reduced the lifespan of federal subsidies available to certain segments of the solar market, and among the first to go was the investment tax credit for residential projects. The ITC is a historic renewable energy subsidy that provides a 30% tax credit against the total cost of an eligible project, and it is right now available to the commercial and utility-scale solar segments.

A residential solar project in Upcoming York installed by Nickels Energy Solutions. Credit: Nickels Energy Solutions.

Recent findings suggest that after 2025, homeowners hoping to install solar on their house couldn’t subsidize the project’s cost with this federal subsidy if it was privately owned. However, a loophole in the tax credit language has allowed leased or “third party-owned” (TPO) solar projects to still receive the ITC (25D) on residential solar projects. In this scenario, it matters who owns the solar project, not necessarily where it’s built. The project can be subsidized because it is owned by a commercial tech giant, and not the homeowner.

Recent findings suggest that some residential solar installers are still strictly pursuing and installing privately-owned solar projects. Nickels Energy Solutions (NES), a residential solar contractor working in Central New York believes a privately-owned PV system is still the better deal for the customer.

It has been revealed that “We don’t have the same offers as some of the bigger companies,” mentioned Kevin Nickels, VP of sales with NES. “If you can offer leases, it’s like the ‘haves and have-nots.’ I don’t have that loophole or that trick up my sleeve, so it forces me to get the project sold, while I’m as sharp as I can be with margins to make it somewhat competitive.”.

Industry observers note that TPO solar transactions are typically arranged at a lower up-front cost than a wholly owned solar project. Lease contract lengths vary by provider, and financiers have surety in these transactions because the projects will be built with products from an approved vendor list (AVL). Products on an AVL can qualify for a domestic content subsidy, another tax credit in addition to the ITC, because they are manufactured using a certain percentage of domestic components.

These are premium products vetted for AVLs. But it can also mean that installers are paying premium prices for components in building TPO solar projects.

Nickels believes that, while a privately owned residential solar project will cost more at the start, the combination of expensive components and interest rates for financing over a lease term — with factors like rate increases — can cost a customer more in the long run.

“I don’t think all leases are bad,” he said. “I just don’t think it’s what’s best for the industry or the homeowner.”.

Selling residential solar in 2026.

Industry observers note that rudy Wright, owner of Wright-Way Solar of Tyler, Texas, has shifted the organization’s focus from building newly introduced projects to servicing existing systems. As the ITC exited the residential solar market, so did many of the lenders financing these projects. Without lending options, Wright-Way’s installation output has slowed down, and those clients who can afford solar are paying with cash or using a home equity loan to cover the cost.

Despite that, Wright noted he isn’t very interested in building leased solar projects.

Industry observers note that wright-Way Solar built a solar plus storage project at a Ranch in Texas featuring arrays that are facing east, west and south. Credit: Wright-Way Solar.

In a recent development, “I do think it cost me some of those entry level clients, but every time I analyzed the financing end of it, I felt like I was benefitting my firm and the industry more than the customer,” Wright mentioned. “I haven’t seen the angle where bringing a pre-purchase agreement or bringing a lease agreement has enough ironclad return for the customer.”.

Industry observers note that tyler is a unique market for solar because many of Wright-Way’s customers are trying to make their properties more resilient, and less reliant on the grid, by installing solar and storage, and not just seeking cost savings for their energy use. Wright has installed solar plus storage systems on Texan ranches at a scale close a small commercial project.

That promise of resilience is still enough reason for some people to commission a solar project in Texas, where the state’s independent electrical grid operator has faltered and experienced dangerous outages in recent years.

“Folks realize money in the bank doesn’t do you any good if the power goes out,” Wright said.

NES is still building a steady pipeline of residential solar projects in Latest York. Nickels stated that has been possible in part because many of its clients are upper-middle class; but New York also maintains an incentive program for solar projects, NY-Sun, which includes a 25% state tax credit for residential systems.

“The OBBBA caught a lot of states flat-footed,” Nickels mentioned. “Without federal support, it puts a lot of pressure on what the state is offering.”.

Recent findings suggest that some states’ solar subsidy programs have stepped in where federal support has diminished since the OBBBA was passed. The North Carolina Clean Energy Technology Center maintains the Database of State Incentives for Renewables & Efficiency, which analyzes which states presently have policies and incentives supporting clean energy developments. Every state has policy enabling solar projects, but not necessarily cost incentives for individual projects.

Sources indicate that while traditional residential solar projects have lost a significant subsidy, the reality is that energy costs have risen in response to newly introduced grid demands from hyperscale data centers and oil scarcity from the United States’ war in Iran. Sure, a privately owned residential solar project won’t receive the ITC, but it can still stabilize energy costs without them.

“We lost a big incentive, but the rate increase is proportional,” Nickels noted.
