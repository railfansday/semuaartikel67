📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

An American Airlines frequent flyer complained about a $91 first class upgrade offer and received a response asking him to delete his post. That might sound like an airline trying to suppress criticism, but there is a much more sensible explanation: his screenshot exposed his reservation details. As for the inexpensive upgrade itself, I see a pragmatic opportunity rather than a scandal.

This hit my algorithm on X (and apparently VFTW too), but an AAdvantage Executive Platinum member took to the platform to express frustration that American was offering paid first class upgrades for $91 while his complimentary upgrade had not cleared (this was on a short 551-mile flight from Dallas to El Paso).

American acknowledged his frustration, then added:.

“Please delete this from public view and join us in DM.”.

Industry observers note that the response attracted attention, but the screenshot included the passenger’s reservation confirmation number. That is a good reason to remove it and not a sign of AA trying to hide from its transactional upgrade policy!

We can understand why this feels frustrating. Your loyalty is genuinely appreciated, and we're sorry this experience left you feeling overlooked. Please delete this from public view and join us in DM.

— americanair (@AmericanAir) November 18, 2026.

It seems obvious to me that American was trying to protect the passenger here.

Industry observers note that A reservation confirmation number, often called a record locator or PNR, is something you should keep private. Combined with your last name, it can potentially allow someone to retrieve your booking. Depending on the airline and its security controls, that could expose information about your trip or allow someone to interfere with it. At least with AA you also need your birthdate in order to access the record online or on the AA app.

Posting a screenshot to make a point about upgrades should not mean giving strangers an opportunity to tamper with your reservation…and some losers have far too much on their hand (which I’ve found out the hard way and why even when I “live blog” I never truly live blog).

American could have explained this much better. A simple request to remove the screenshot because it contained private booking information would have avoided the ambiguity. The passenger could then have reposted his complaint with those details obscured.

As to the underlying complaint, I understand why an Executive Platinum member would be frustrated. If you have directed substantial business to an airline and are waiting for an upgrade, watching the airline offer that seat for a relatively small payment can feel like your loyalty counts for very little.

But I am not going to attack American for selling inexpensive upgrades. This is the business model now, and I use it to my advantage when I fly AA. If the economy fare is attractive and American subsequently offers a reasonably priced upgrade, I can evaluate the total cost and decide whether first class is worth it. Sometimes it is. Sometimes I am perfectly happy staying where I am, like on a recent flight where I ended up with an entire row to myself. Oddly, sometimes the cost of the upgrade is more than buying the ticket outright…I have no explanation for that.

That is a much more straightforward transaction than spending more throughout the year in the hope that an airline will eventually reward me with a seat it would still prefer to sell.

Fundamentally, a complimentary upgrade that has not cleared, even at the gate, is not a seat that already belongs to the elite member. We can debate all day long about how prominently or aggressively airlines market upgrades when frequent flyers are waiting when upgrades are dangled like a carrot on a stick, but passengers can respond to that change by reconsidering how much they are willing to spend pursuing status.

I am a free agent, and it is liberating. More people should try it. Two years in, I’m more happy than ever (and flying in first class more than ever on domestic flights). I  choose a flight because the schedule works or because the price makes sense, without worrying that I am depriving my preferred airline of another qualifying flight or dollar toward next year’s status.

Industry observers note that if American offers a good fare and an inexpensive first class upgrade, great. I can take advantage of it. I do not need to be an Executive Platinum member to recognize a good deal (as much as I miss the oneworld emerald lounge access when traveling internationally).

According to recent reports, that does not mean status has become worthless. I still receive occasional complimentary upgrades as both an American Airlines Gold and United Airlines Gold member. I appreciate those upgrades very much, especially when I am not expecting them. But I do not build my travel plans around them. Hoping for a free upgrade seems futile in many cases even with top-tier status, particularly when the airline business model now centers on actively trying to sell the remaining seats.

For me, an occasional upgrade is a pleasant bonus on a flight I was otherwise willing to fly in economy class on. But upgrades are not a compelling reason to keep spending more with one airline when another offers a better flight….sorry, but the value proposition is gone.

American Airlines could have been clearer about why it wanted this passenger to delete his post. Still, the exposed reservation details provide a sensible explanation, and I would not characterize the request as an attempt to silence criticism.

According to recent reports, as for the $91 upgrade, I am pragmatically happy those offers exist. They give me another way to sit up front without organizing my travel around the pursuit of top-tier status. I still enjoy the occasional complimentary upgrade, but choosing flights on my own terms, and paying for a better seat when the price is right, has been far more satisfying than chasing an upgrade that may never clear. Try it, you’ll like it.
