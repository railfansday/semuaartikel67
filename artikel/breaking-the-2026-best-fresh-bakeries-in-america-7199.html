It has been revealed that precision pastries are sweeping the nation. These bakers are leading the charge.

If there’s one thing we’ve noticed over the past several years, it’s how much bakeries have stepped up their game.

In a recent development, some embody a personal expression of a baker’s identity, while others push the envelope beyond expected flavor combinations; all excel at a time when “little treat” culture is at its peak. — Missy Frederick, editorial director.

If one newly introduced place in Chicago needs no further hype, it is Bad Butter. You’ll know you’re there when you spot the line cascading out of the whitewashed storefront along West Cortland Street, a queue that doesn’t let up until the pastry case is empty. Formerly a head baker at Soho House Chicago, Dan Koester was stuck at home like the rest of us in 2020, handing off excess home-baked sourdough loaves to friends and family and making some available for free pickup. After he was officially laid off that summer, he released his own online bakery business, earning a massive following along the way.

One residency at the Emily Hotel later, Koester debuted his Bucktown bakery, demonstrating the full range of his skill. Still warm, charred, and dimpled flats of focaccia showered with scallion furikake rest on its shelves. Impossibly crisp and exquisitely laminated twice-baked almond croissants burst with subtle, aromatic almond paste. A spicy harissa monkey bread with a cloudlike texture is cooled by a pool of creamy labneh drizzled with silky olive oil.

Industry observers note that the bakery’s menu has breadth and also manages to arc with the season. Customers snake along the floor waiting their turn to claim a towering cruffin, dusted in fine grains of sugar and piped full of ginger plum jam, or a seasonal tart piled with plump, lacquered blueberries. A pastry box filled with these confections is well worth the wait. — Brenna Houck, dining editorial manager.

Born as an underground dinner party operated out of Karla Subero Pittol’s Echo Park garage, Chainsaw finally got its own digs in November 2025 in the form of a lively cafe in Melrose Hill, an unquestionably gentrifying neighborhood that has become home to some of Los Angeles’s best independent restaurants. At the cafe, Subero Pittol channels her Venezuelan heritage through a menu of arepas plump with beans, limey guasacaca, and cheese; a Venezuelan hot dog (“perro caliente”) buoyed by an acidic slaw; and her bracingly tart passionfruit-lime icebox pie, which commands a cult following all its own.

According to recent reports, stepping into Chainsaw feels like you could be in Subero Pittol’s own kitchen as she greets regulars and newcomers as if they are old friends. Customers study a handwritten menu that changes daily — it doesn’t help to come in too attached to any one dish (save the ever-present icebox pie) when you may get tempted by another. The cafe’s impact on Los Angeles feels clear in the near-constant crowds that hover outside, reading their newspapers over an espresso or tucking into the latest of Subero Pittol’s ideas, such as a full English breakfast served for a single day. In a city lacking in untamed Venezuelan cooking, Chainsaw offers a home for Subero Pittol to celebrate the cuisine in a way that feels distinctly Los Angeles. — Rebecca Roland, deputy editor, Eater Southern California/Southwest.

Sources indicate that with both Afghan and Latin American influences, Diljān gives New Yorkers a bakery with a point of view and innumerable reasons to become a regular. Ali Zaman and Mohamed Ghiasi, owners of Astoria’s Little Flower Cafe, teamed up with Bryan Ford, Honduran American baker and cookbook author, to conceptualize the bakery, now perched on a corner near Atlantic Avenue in Brooklyn Heights.

Their combined talent makes for some of the city’s most compelling baked goods. For the Saffron Shah, laminated pastry holds delicate saffron cream in the shape of a crescent, an key Islamic symbol. Halwa tops the sticky bun, while a swirl of samosa pastry wraps halal lamb under a dusting of powdered sugar. Don’t miss the enormous Afghan flatbread crowned by toppings such as halal beef bacon with scallions or sour cherry cream cheese. The team has also branched into pizzas topped with cheese and Afghan cumin, kofta kebabs, or beef pepperoni. Look for specials like the flan with cardamom-poached apricots and conchas stuffed with firni, a rice pudding. — Nadia Chaudhury, deputy editor, Eater New York.

Sources indicate that san Francisco’s pastry game is stronger and more competitive than ever before, and Parachute Bakery steps to the forefront of a crowded field with a mix of patisserie standards and seasonal standouts. At his Ferry Building bakery, pastry chef Nasir Armar marries his fine dining training, honed at Michelin-starred Sorrel and Saison, with flavors that make everyone sit up and take notice (and snap a food photo or two for their socials).

In a recent development, vegetables and savory complements are given their own footing on the menu. Stalks of grilled asparagus shine in a baton-shaped croissant shell, with fennel jam, sheep’s milk ricotta, and ramp pesto alongside them; one brief, bordering-on-gimmicky croissant got piped with clam chowder, a reference to the soup in a bread bowl famously served on the city’s wharf. Even potato-laden samosas and bright tomato shakshuka get interpreted into pastry form. Parachute succeeds with its experimental vision, a hype well-earned that leaves fans clamoring for what they’ll come up with next. — Dianne de Guzman, editor, Eater SF.
