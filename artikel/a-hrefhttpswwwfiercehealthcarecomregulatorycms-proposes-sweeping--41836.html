The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services rolled out a latest proposed rule it said could save $90 billion over ten years through increased oversight and prevention of fraud and improper payments.

The Comprehensive Regulations to Uncover Suspicious Healthcare (CRUSH) proposed rule, shared late Monday, spans Medicare, Medicaid, the Children’s Health Insurance Program (CHIP) and the Health Insurance Marketplace. It follows a request for information the administration circulated back in February. CMS it intends to conduct similar program integrity rulemaking “on an annual basis going forward.”.

Among the highlights: stiffer provider screening and safeguards for Medicare enrollment, a new regulatory framework for reporting and returning self-identified Medicaid fee-for-service overpayments similar to those in other programs, and requirements that Medicaid managed care prepares report beneficiary deaths or address changes within 30 days to help CMS better spot improper payments.

In a recent development, “When President Trump called upon the federal government to crush fraud, waste, and abuse, CMS got right to work, using every tool at its disposal to achieve historic savings,” CMS Administrator Mehmet Oz, M.D., remarked in the agency’s announcement of the proposed rule. “CRUSH would build on those results by giving us stronger tools to prevent fraud, remove bad actors, and protect the Americans who rely on these programs. Whether criminals are billing Medicare for nonexistent canes and wheelchairs, fraudulently enrolling healthy seniors in Medicare-funded hospice care, or signing people up for Marketplace looks without their consent, the CRUSH proposed rule would give CMS stronger authority to stop these schemes and hold those responsible accountable.”.

The additional guardrails are expected to cost the government about $378.5 million over the same 10-year period CMS expects to save nearly $90 billion, according to the proposed rule.

CMS, in its release and a lengthy fact sheet outlining the proposed changes, remarked it wants to upgrade Medicare provisions to tighten Medicare Advantage (MA) Adjustment Data Validation audits so that it can better spot cases in which diagnoses are not supported by medical documentation, thereby bumping up risk scores and payments. It also wants to increase suspicious activity reporting requirements for MA organizations and Part D blueprint sponsors, require some MA plan providers and suppliers to also enroll in Original Medicare for credential verification and guard seniors from unsolicited outreach from medical supply companies.

Sources indicate that in Medicaid and CHIP, the agency wants to make it easier for states to join in on suspected schemes while fleshing out their data collection and reporting. CMS also wants to add provider enrollment termination grounds similar to those in Medicare.

Recent findings suggest that within the marketplace, CMS remarked it wants to begin restricting access for agents and brokers with certain felony convictions or other healthcare program exclusions on their record. The agency is also looking to bolster its authority to suspend and terminate, and to do so more promptly.

Sources indicate that “President Trump directed us to stop fraud, waste, and abuse, and the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services is taking action to protect taxpayer dollars,” HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy, Jr. noted in the announcement. “CRUSH would give CMS stronger tools to block fraudulent payments, remove dishonest providers, and protect Americans from schemes that exploit their healthcare coverage.

According to recent reports, public comment on the proposed rule, which includes more potential changes and a breakdown of the expected savings and costs, will be open through Jan. 19, 2027.

The program integrity proposed rule landed the same day CMS released its estimates on improper payments during fiscal year 2026.

Improper payments are those that do not meet the agency’s program requirements, and can include overpayments or underpayments alike. They are not necessarily a measure of fraud, though “some improper payments may involve fraud or abuse” alongside administrative errors, CMS noted.
