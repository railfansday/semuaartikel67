Nine months of the year, I rarely struggle to get myself out the door for a run. Then winter arrives, the temperatures drop, and suddenly it’s three straight months of daily prerun procrastination: I look for any excuse, task, or chore to delay heading out into the cold or stepping on the treadmill.

Recent findings suggest that while this does have occasional productivity benefits—my kitchen floor shines and my email inbox stays at zero—I’ll find myself more than an hour later no closer to starting my run. Except by then, I’m crunched for time, it’s pitch-black outside, and the whole process has drained enough of my mental energy that I’d rather push those miles to tomorrow.

It has been revealed that I know I’m not alone: Even professional marathoners fall into the “run-crastination” spiral. Previously, Des Linden and Kara Goucher discussed their own experiences on their podcast, Nobody Asked Us with Des & Kara. Still, I’d much rather find a way to avoid getting sucked in so I can get on with my run and my day, and enjoy both a little more as a result.

So, I asked a sports psychologist what actually works to get you out the door in the winter when anything sounds better than a run—plus when it’s actually a better idea to skip the miles.

The first step to beating prerun procrastination? Determine the reason you’re doing it. “Procrastination is usually one of two things,” Justin Ross, Psy.D., a clinical sports psychologist and UESCA-certified run coach, tells Runner’s World. “One: It’s an avoidance strategy, because what you’re about to do is uncomfortable, unpleasant, hard, or challenging. But, it can also signal toward a different need that’s not being met.”.

In the winter months—especially for driven runners coming off a fall marathon season—that other need can be rest. “True athletic wisdom is knowing the difference between when procrastination is avoidance, and when it’s a sign that you need something other than a run that day,” Ross explains.

Gaining that wisdom as an athlete takes time, practice, and some trial and error. The best way to start? Focus on creating “systems” that make procrastination less likely so you can more easily identify the action that aligns with your needs, goals, and values, Ross states. Most times, these systems (which we outline below) lead to better outcomes than trying to rely on willpower alone or following an impulse in the moment.

According to recent reports, if you find yourself avoiding a run, remove as many obstacles as possible that stand between you and getting out the door or to the gym. For Ross, that means setting out all his running clothes and fuel ahead of time so he doesn’t need to hunt for that one pair of gloves he hasn’t seen since last January.

“Without preparation, procrastination becomes a whole lot easier because you have all these extra hurdles,” he explains. “When it’s dark and cold in the winter, you need to problem-solve in ways that you don’t need to when it’s beautiful out and you’re excited to run.”.

For me, that means making sure my headlamp actually has working batteries if I’m planning to run outside in the evenings. If I’m hitting the treadmill, I cue up a playlist, TV show, or podcast on my phone and stock my gym bag with a postrun snack and a full water bottle.

In a recent development, when you’re procrastinating a run, try shifting your focus from what you’re doing to why you’re doing it. “Remind yourself why this run is key to you or how it helps you,” Ross says. For example, you might think about your values or current goals and consider how this workout brings you closer to them.

I’ve found I’m motivated simply by the fact that running makes me feel like my favorite version of myself—and that boosts my mood more than any race-specific or fitness goal. But you might be more excited by the idea that staying consistent with your training over the winter will set you up for a stronger chance at a marathon PR in the spring.

It has been revealed that consider what might add a little bit more spark to your routine or make it more enticing. You might decide to meet up with a friend or local run club for a few miles. You might look forward to your treadmill run as “the 30 minutes of my day where I don’t have to be bothered and I can just watch my favorite show [alone],” claims Ross.

The reward mindset helps you refocus on what brings you joy, instead of just relieving the pressure of needing to get in a workout.

Recent findings suggest that another helpful strategy? Doing the absolute bare minimum. “Runners often fall into this trap of having rigid rules around their exercise or their training,” indicates Ross. Try letting go of whatever mileage or workout you had planned for the day, and tell yourself that you’ll just run for 10 minutes and then reassess how you feel.

You may even want to adjust your whole training standard ahead of the winter season, Ross claims. For example, you might reduce the length, intensity, or frequency of the workouts you strategy to do each week. Or, instead of targeting a specific pace or mileage requirement, run or cross-train solely based on time. “Think about being more flexible in your approach during the winter months. Maybe you aim for the minimal effective dose to get the benefit you’re looking for, like just running for 20 minutes if your usual standard is 60,” Ross suggests.

Ross explains that winter can be a meaningful time for runners to examine what rules they have around their training that might be adding pressure. For example, I used to follow a silly rule from an old college teammate who mentioned that any run shorter than three miles “didn’t count” as a true run. Once I finally let go of that, I found it much easier to get moving—even if it was just for an easy walk in the park near my house.

“This time of year, see how you can approach exercise from an overall health or mood-boost perspective instead of a training perspective,” suggests Ross.

Also, question any rigid rules you’ve established for yourself around volume, pace, or mileage—like making sure to hit a specific number of miles every week or setting a minimum pace requirement for all your runs—because they may backfire and lead to mental burnout over the long term, he indicates.

If you’ve done all the above and you still can’t get yourself out the door, a run may not actually be what you need. “If you keep hitting the moment to head out and that intuitive voice notes, ‘I just really don’t want to,’ try listening to that voice. Listen to your body,” Ross suggests. “It’s okay to give yourself permission for rest and recovery—especially during this time of year.”.
