Why So Many Upcoming Moms Are Told to Wait to Exercise Again—and What the Research Really Notes

According to recent reports, earlier this year, trainer and WH advisory board member Sohee Carpenter, CSCS, posted an Instagram Reel challenging some conventional postpartum advice: Don’t work out until your ob-gyn clears you at your six-week follow-up appointment. Her point wasn’t that fresh moms should rush back to the gym. It was that one timeline can’t fit everyone. Some women are ready to move sooner, and others need more time.

The Reel ruffled some feathers.

Sources indicate that “I feel like so many people get angry because they assume we mean you're going to be doing a one-rep max back squat a week after you have a baby,” Carpenter claims. “But that's definitely not what we're saying.” (By “we,” she means trainers who keep up with the latest research on pregnancy and postpartum exercise.).

The problem, Carpenter explains, is that the six-week rule isn’t based on evidence. On the contrary, research suggests that this type of blanket advice doesn’t make sense for something as personal as postpartum recovery. When you return to exercise after childbirth should depend on your own symptoms, not a date on the calendar.

“There's really nothing magical about the six-week time point,” indicates Margie Davenport, PhD, a professor of kinesiology at the University of Alberta and chair of the 2025 Canadian Guidelines for Physical Activity for Pregnancy and Postpartum—the first-ever exercise guidelines to specifically focus on evidence-based suggestions for that first year postpartum. In the U.S., the main guidance from the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) covers pregnancy and the postpartum period together. ACOG’s guidance points in the same direction, though: It says many women can start exercising again within days of an uncomplicated birth. In fact, “there is no identified ideal time to return,” Davenport says.

In a recent development, based on the wide range of comments on Carpenter’s post—some supportive, some hypercritical, and still others asking more questions to try to better understand this shift away from the advice they’ve always heard—there’s still a lot of confusion around postpartum exercise. So we asked experts to break down what the research actually states, and how to approach working out in this fresh chapter of life.

In a recent development, in many cultures, the first 30 or 40 days postpartum is traditionally considered a protected time to rest, relax, and recover, Davenport states. In Chinese culture, it’s called zuo yuezi, which translates to “sitting the month.” In many Latin American and Latino communities, it’s known as “la cuarentena,” a nod to its roughly 40-day length. In South Korea, it’s sanhujori, or “postpartum care.”.

In a recent development, these traditions carry deep cultural significance and are meant to offer real healing and support after birth from one’s community (they put “it takes a village” into practice). They’re rooted in caregiving and cultural custom, and they were never meant to serve as a medical timeline for when it’s safe to start exercising.

It has been revealed that in Western medicine, six weeks has become an key medical benchmark in postpartum recovery—and, over time, a default point for returning to exercise too. “At that point, your uterus should be back to its pre-pregnancy state, and so a lot of the gynecologic changes should be fairly reversed by that point,” indicates Rita Deering, DPT, PhD, associate professor of exercise science and director of the Pelvic Health, Exercise & Musculoskeletal (PHEM) Research Laboratory at Marquette University.

In a recent development, the first six weeks after birth are also when the risk of complications like high blood pressure and infection is highest, Deering says, which is part of why that window became an important medical checkpoint (even though some pregnancy-related changes take longer to resolve). But, she says, “it was never intended to be about exercise.”.

Recent findings suggest that so how did six weeks become the exercise rule? Before researchers knew much about how safe it is to exercise during pregnancy and after birth, working out was considered potentially risky, Deering notes. There wasn’t strong evidence that exercise wouldn’t slow healing or raise the odds of complications. So the most cautious advice was simply to wait until a doctor confirmed you’d healed enough to resume physical activity.

It has been revealed that to come up with the 2025 Canadian Guidelines, researchers did a deep dive of all the available research on the topic, which included 574 unique studies organized into seven systematic reviews based on 21 different outcomes of postpartum exercise—such as depression and anxiety, low-back pain, injuries, glucose and blood pressure levels, and more. They also assembled an international panel of experts to give their recommendations.

Industry observers note that their advice: Start light-intensity physical activity and mobilization (think: carrying your baby around your home, a slow walk down the block, pelvic floor physical therapy) right away. From there, progress as you feel ready to moderate-to-vigorous activity such as brisk walking, resistance training, and running at your own pace—as long as tears and incisions have sufficiently healed and you don’t experience additional bleeding with exercise. (As mentioned, ACOG similarly recommends that women can return to postnatal exercise within days after an uncomplicated birth.).

Recent findings suggest that based on the research, there are no “absolute contraindications” to exercise postpartum, Davenport says—meaning no condition would rule out exercise entirely. There are some relative contraindications, she explains, “where you might have to delay a little bit or modify the type of activity that you're doing.” Examples include severe abdominal pain, extreme fatigue, and C-section incision pain that worsens with activity. All warrant a conversation with your doctor before starting.

Recent findings suggest that pelvic floor symptoms, like leaking urine or a feeling of heaviness in the vagina, aren’t a reason to stop exercising altogether. If you notice signs of pelvic floor dysfunction, the guidelines recommend starting slowly and building up gradually instead. “Some leakage is okay,” Davenport states. “It means that the training that you’re doing is just overloading the muscle.”.

Many people, including plenty of commenters on Carpenter’s Reel, worry that exercising after birth will damage the pelvic floor and cause dysfunction. But the evidence doesn’t support that. In fact, those muscles will likely be better off if you resume training, especially if you do exercises to target them in particular. “Engaging in pelvic floor muscle training reduces your risk of urinary incontinence by around 37 percent,” Davenport claims. Additionally, if you stop all exercise because you're leaking a little bit, “this can actually mean that you're missing out on those other benefits.”.

One caveat: If you’re leaking a lot, or it’s getting worse over time, “you probably need to pull it back,” Davenport states. That means being more selective about the moves you choose. For example, you might skip long runs and high-impact moves like jump squats for now, adds Sheridan Skye, a registered nurse, pre- and postnatal fitness coach, and founder of The Female Fitness Formula. Stick with lower-impact exercises, then build up gradually as you get stronger and your symptoms level off or improve.

It has been revealed that at the end of the day, every body, birth, and recovery is vastly different. “Some people have a very uncomplicated labor and delivery and pregnancy; for others, it's quite complex,” notes Davenport. “It only makes sense that the way that they're returning and what time they're actually returning is also going to be quite individualized.”.

For some new moms, six weeks is too soon, and a blanket deadline only adds stress. “Maybe they aren't sleeping or they don't have the support to be able to return [to exercise] at six weeks,” Davenport notes. On the flip side, for someone who had a straightforward delivery and is itching to get moving again, “telling them to wait for six weeks with no evidence to support it is also inappropriate.”.

Sources indicate that in fact, exercise can offer significant benefits during postpartum recovery, even before the six-week mark. “It improves muscle and bone density that we lose throughout pregnancy,” Skye notes. Postpartum exercise is associated with a 45 percent reduction in the odds of developing postpartum depression, and starting before 12 weeks is even more beneficial.

It’s also associated with a 28 percent reduction in the risk of developing diabetes postpartum. Exercise even improves sleep quality, Skye notes. “Women tend to feel more energized and like they've experienced more rest, even if they haven't,” she says. (Anyone who’s survived a 3 a.m. feeding stretch knows that’s no small thing!).

There is also evidence that exercise in general can help to decrease pain, says Deering, and research links sedentary behavior to lumbopelvic (the areas of the lower back and pelvis) pain. “So if you are having pain, movement might be what you need to help with that.”.

For many people, it may be difficult to know if you’re truly ready. “Especially when you're postpartum, it can feel like you are super out of touch with your body sensations, especially if you're not sleeping well,” Carpenter says.

It has been revealed that if you're at the point where you're thinking about it, but are nervous or unsure or just overwhelmed by having another decision to make, Davenport recommends taking the Get Active Questionnaire for Postpartum—a tool that her team designed as part of the 2025 guideline. It can help you rapidly understand if you’re ready to start from a medical perspective. That can help give you some reassurance.

Recent findings suggest that there can also be “a lot of fear involved,” Carpenter indicates, especially if you had a C-section or are healing from a vaginal tear. In that case, “you’re probably going to be a bit hesitant,” which is completely understandable. Starting slower and lighter than you think you need to is perfectly fine as you get reacquainted with your body. “The first several sessions are really about mentally trying to get that confidence back,” she says.

As you go, pay attention to how your body responds. Leaking, for instance? That’s not a reason to stop exercising, but you may want to adjust your workout to reduce it. More bleeding, new pain, or a sense that something just doesn’t feel right are signs to stop, reassess, and possibly scale back. If they don’t improve, check in with your doctor. “It’s really about setting your ego aside and being willing to modify, even if it’s one rep to the next, based on how you’re feeling right at that time,” Carpenter states.

In a recent development, and if you feel physically ready but aren’t quite there mentally? “It’s okay to honor those feelings,” Deering says, “and to recognize that it’s okay to give yourself some time.” Whether that’s two weeks, six weeks, or six months, the right moment to start moving is the one that works for you.
