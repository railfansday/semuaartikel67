How to Deal With Holiday Arguments and Guilt Trips

Sources indicate that ideally, the holidays are a time of happiness, cheer, and positive relationship interactions. In practice, however, we often need to manage some bad with the good. There are usually a few “bones to pick” over the Thanksgiving turkey and “elephants in the room” along with the Christmas tree….

So, to maximize your holiday joy (and minimize your angst), I have compiled some strategies to deal with dysfunctional games, family patterns, arguments, and emotional manipulation. By the end, you should have a good idea why Aunt Ruth keeps giving you a guilt trip, how Uncle Stu keeps making you lose your cool, and what exactly you can do about all of it. Then, maybe, you can enjoy yourself, too!

To start, according to Eric Berne in his book Games People Play (1964), people generally try to manipulate us because they want something from us—but they don’t believe they can get it honestly or fairly. These negative beliefs (called existential positions) come in two forms. On one hand, they can believe that you’re not OK and try to bully you because they think you don’t deserve their fair treatment. On the other hand, they can believe that they’re not OK (i.e., I’m not okay) and try to guilt trip you into feeling that you owe them something for nothing.

Thus, people play games, argue, and manipulate when they believe they are not OK, other people are not OK, or both. This allows them to get what they want without giving back or risking being fair and trusting. Such manipulations can have secondary benefits for them, too, like being excused from standards or responsibilities (I'm not OK), or feeling morally superior (You're not OK). Unfortunately, those bad beliefs and manipulative games also cause many of our hurt feelings and dysfunctional relationship patterns in the process as well.

How these game-playing relatives and friends push your emotional buttons was explored by Niven, Totterdell, and Holman (2009) and defined as negative affect regulation strategies. Put simply, they use emotional manipulation tactics to make you feel bad, so you give them what they want. There are four general tactics they can employ to emotionally manipulate, but they all share one common theme—they reject you and hurt your feelings, while also putting their feelings first.

In a recent development, beyond that, they might choose to emotionally bully you by being disrespectful and insulting or bragging and trying to make you feel bad by comparison. This would fall under the “You’re not OK” belief and allow them to feel superior at your expense. In contrast, they might decide to guilt-trip you by criticizing how you treat them or withdrawing until you feel bad and comply with their wishes. This would be part of an “I’m not OK” belief and allow them to be excused from standards and responsibilities—like doing something worthwhile for you back. In either case, unless you know how to defend yourself, you might be left trying to choke down negative emotions, while they munch on dessert!

It has been revealed that fortunately, there are things that you can do to defend yourself—and possibly even improve your relationship with that unruly relative, partner, or friend, too. To begin, though, you need to decide whether you want to deal with the manipulation indirectly by diverting attention and emotion away from the problem, or directly by engaging attention and emotion toward addressing the problem. In other words, can you make light of Cousin Bessie’s snotty comments and change the subject, or do you need to talk it out and come to a better arrangement?

Industry observers note that to help you decide, consider some indirect and direct softening techniques suggested by Overall, Simpson, and Struthers (2013). They were initially devised for addressing problems with emotionally avoidant or argumentative romantic partners—and serve as a foundation for dealing with relationship problems in my book Attraction Psychology (Nicholson, 2022). Beyond that, I have found them helpful with family and friends as well, especially when they believe one of us is not “OK” and are emotionally hurtful about it.

Sources indicate that dealing with complications indirectly requires that you “soften” by temporarily downplaying the severity of the problem, until their bullying or guilt-tripping lessens. To do so, it helps to build positive rapport by communicating your care, acceptance, and regard—while also minimizing their emotional manipulation attempts with positive feelings and humor (i.e., kill them with kindness). From there, you can redirect them toward other positive aspects about your relationship with them and points of agreement. Essentially, this is a lot like dealing with a toddler having a tantrum—make light of the outburst and find a more pleasant topic to change their mind and improve their behavior.

According to recent reports, dealing with complications directly, however, requires that you “soften” by acknowledging some aspect of their point of view as a base of agreement—without surrendering to the negative emotions from their bullying or guilt trip. Pretend you’re a cool TV lawyer who says, “We can agree that there is a problem here, but it would help us both to keep working on a better solution.” From there, you can show optimism about solving the disagreement and having a good relationship with them. Where you are able, also acknowledge some of their positive efforts in the past toward connecting and compromising with you. This can help improve their emotional outlook and motivate them to find healthier ways to interact with you in the future as well.

Whatever strategy you choose, I hope you have Happy Holidays and a Good Fresh Year, too!
