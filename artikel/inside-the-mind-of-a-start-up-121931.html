Inside the Mind of a Start-Up

Posted November 25, 2026 | Reviewed by Davia Sills.

According to recent reports, beginning in early 2024, I spent two years building a start-up as a co-founder and its Chief Content Officer. It was an interesting, intense, exciting, and frustrating experience. As I reflect on it, I feel an immense feeling of satisfaction at how much we accomplished. At the same time, I also feel a sense of lost opportunity. Why? Because in early 2026, I chose to leave the company.

Industry observers note that I left not because the mission wasn’t worthwhile or the work was no longer meaningful, but because the cumulative psychological demands crossed a line I was no longer willing to cross. The current start-up culture often frames departures like mine as a failure of sorts, whether a lack of vision or perseverance or resilience. I’m sure as a way to justify the psychological and emotional costs that are incurred for those who choose to pay those costs, regardless of how high they run. For me, however much time and psychological capital I had invested (not to mention a fair amount of financial capital), I could no longer continue to pay those costs, even though the financial ROI might have paid for those costs down the road.

This is the first in a series about what I learned. These articles reflect my experience and interpretation; my former co-founders may understand parts of our shared experience differently. This article introduces you to the seven psychological challenges that I experienced that build slowly, inside founders at first, and they compound, but, unfortunately, not like interest.

In a recent development, uncertainty is not an occasional stressor in a start-up. It is the steady drip, drip, drip that fills every waking hour. Questions about market fit, funding, and product decisions can remain unresolved for months. And every moment triggers an unending sense of urgency.

It has been revealed that our brains crave familiarity, predictability, and control. But there is nothing familiar, predictable, or controllable about a start-up. The constant mental activity, emotional rollercoaster, and cortisol bursts that founders experience due to this uncertainty can lead to persistent hypervigilance and, in time, the erosion of everything that is required to build a tech giant, including perspective, patience, resilience, and, for me, a basic enjoyment of the work.

In a recent development, research on entrepreneurship has reported a strong relationship between uncertainty and stress in the start-up process. And it’s not a byproduct of founding a start-up; rather, it’s baked fully into the experience.

Sources indicate that there’s a saying that “business is just business,” but in the start-up world, business is personal. Founders invest their identity in the business and risk their fortunes and futures, all on an opportunity that, statistically, has little chance of being realized. When the tech giant struggles, it can feel like you are struggling. When traction is slow, it can feel like it’s your fault. That creates a boiling cauldron of stress.

It has been revealed that I worked “pedal to the metal” for almost two years. As Chief Content Officer, I felt that if I slowed down, the manufacturer would grind to a halt. My co-founders did their part, but without content, our company was dead in the water. I loved creating and seeing tangible results. For a long time, I thrived on the pressure. Yet every morning, as other stressors related to the start-up accumulated, the weight on my shoulders got heavier and heavier.

Start-ups reward urgency and intensity. But intensity doesn’t always play nice with productivity. When exhaustion becomes your baseline, you lose the ability to notice how impaired you are. Sleep goes south. The ability to focus declines. Irritability becomes the rule, not the exception. Relationships get tense, and conflicts increase. Eventually, many founders feel emotionally flat, as if the battery ceases to get recharged.

Recent findings suggest that research on entrepreneurial burnout highlights the toll of sustained emotional demands and dwindling resources. From the inside, burnout rarely unveils itself. It disguises itself as dedication.

According to recent reports, when you’re self-funding, every expense feels like a part of you is being lost, because it is. Even if you can afford it, watching money go out month after month with little coming in wears on you.

Among the self-employed, financial concerns strongly shape psychological well-being. In a start-up, financial anxiety can disrupt sleep, intensify conflict, or narrow what you’re willing to try.

My private practice eased the financial pressure I felt and gave me the freedom to leave. My co-founders were at different stages of their careers and faced different financial pressures. My own financial security gave me options they didn’t have.

We were bootstrapping, trying to cover expenses each month while waiting for revenue. As the app fell further behind schedule, revenue slipped further into the future. The company was on the clock, and the clock was always ticking down. We all felt it acutely.

Recent findings suggest that under pressure, people change their behavior. They make things simpler even when complexity is necessary. They think less deliberately and fall back on impulse and habit. Time pressure also reduces creativity, precisely when innovation is most needed.

It has been revealed that start-ups are cognitively heavy because everything connects to everything else. Product, marketing, revenue, runway, and hiring also impact each other. It can feel like a Möbius strip.

In a recent development, my formal role was Chief Content Officer. In practice, because of my relevant expertise and experience, I was involved in nearly every aspect of the business—strategy, design, marketing, sales, and finance, including areas where I knew very little.

Role overload felt like wearing a weight vest all day. Research on entrepreneurial role stressors helped me understand why the work had become so unsatisfying. Over time, I felt I was carrying more than my share, and it became a witch’s brew of inequity and resentment.

Industry observers note that together, these pressures make it harder for founders to accurately judge how the start-up is doing and when change is needed. Chronic uncertainty, identity-linked stress, emerging burnout, financial threat, time pressure, and role overload create the perfect psychological storm for biased judgment.

Industry observers note that optimism bias starts with a belief every founder must have: Something unlikely—building a successful and sustainable company—will happen. The danger comes when that belief keeps you underestimating timelines, costs, and obstacles long after the evidence suggests otherwise.

Confirmation bias protects that optimism. Founders begin to give more weight to information that supports the current path. Delays become temporary setbacks and structural problems are seen as execution concerns.

It has been revealed that loss aversion makes walking away feel like accepting a personal loss. The research on this is robust. Time, money, reputation, and identity are already invested. The more you’ve put in, the harder it is to acknowledge that it’s time to cut your losses and get out.

Sources indicate that I also saw how easily confidence can outpace competence. A few articles, videos, and conversations could harden into strong opinions about complex decisions. That included areas where I was learning as I went. The Dunning-Kruger effect helps explain why people with limited expertise can overestimate what they know. I think of this as “YouTube expertise,” the tendency to mistake familiarity with a subject for actual expertise and experience. When that happens, overconfidence and rigidity of thinking become more likely.

In a recent development, from the inside, it feels like conviction, clarity, and leadership. From the outside, it just looks like poor judgment. Once those biases take hold, course correction becomes psychologically very difficult. Changing direction can mean admitting that the decisions you’ve defended for months may have been wrong. My Ph.D. in psychology certainly didn’t inoculate me against cognitive biases.

According to recent reports, I haven’t regretted co-founding the organization for a minute. I loved pursuing big goals. I enjoyed being pushed outside my comfort zone. I took immense pleasure in bringing ideas to life. What I regret is how easily I came to see the psychological cost as part of the job. I told myself the sacrifice was worth it, with that proverbial pot of gold at the end of the rainbow (aka unicorn status), and that pushing through was always the virtuous route to take.

Sources indicate that many start-ups don’t struggle because the idea is wrong or they lack funding. They falter because psychological and interpersonal costs build steadily and reach a tipping point where they far outweigh the benefits. Leaving after two years was the healthiest decision I could make. When I gave my Notice of Separation to my co-founders, the dominant emotion I felt was relief. That relief told me everything I needed to know. And what it told me was that walking away was the right decision for me.
