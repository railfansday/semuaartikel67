Sources indicate that I typically take a less-is-more approach when it comes to my beverage choices. Having been burned one too many times by the flavor-packed ‘ingenuity’ of a bar’s signature cocktail, I’ve come to the conclusion that a martini, Negroni, or gin and tonic are better suited to my spartan palate. But once the holidays roll around, well, my fidelity to simplicity goes out the window, and I am an enthusiastic proprietor of the cozy cocktail.

Sources indicate that what’s a cozy cocktail? Exactly what you think it is. A spirited sip that summons the mood of a crackling fireplace and the flavors of a Christmas kitchen. That transports you to a snowed-in cabin in Lapland, or a bustling holiday market in Germany. And I’m not talking about kitschy cocktails afflicted by crushed-up candy canes and artificial syrups—no, no. These are recipes that chase the outer limits of mixology, bringing together all that is merry and bright and elegantly transmuting it into your glass.

There are many paths a cold-weather cocktail may take. Traditional classics, like eggnog and hot toddies, are always welcome. For something theatrical, a smoked tipple does the trick (top marks for those performed tableside). For an off-the-charts cozy cocktail, those infused with seasonal spices will leave you deliriously jolly. And if you can’t bear to part ways with your old fashioned or espresso martini, take solace in knowing there are ways to ‘winter-fy’ these quintessential favorites.

In a recent development, if you’re ready to raise a glass of something cozy in the coming weeks, let the guide below inform your order. And for those who wish to whip up cocktails for a festive fête at home, don’t miss the recipes at the end. Bar carts at the ready!

It has been revealed that the holidays present a decadent array of creamy cocktails calibrated specifically for the season. Take eggnog, for example. This rich tipple originated from a hot, milky, ale-adjacent drink called ‘posset’ that was enjoyed during Britain’s medieval era. Somewhere along the timeline, eggs joined the party, and several hundred years later, we’ve arrived at our modern-day recipes that consist of milk, heavy whipping cream, sugar, eggs, and nutmeg (spiked with rum, bourbon, or brandy). A boozy holiday milkshake, if you like.

Industry observers note that over at Smithereens in the East Village, head bartender Jacob Dorman wanted to make an eggnog with their in-house label rum, but decided to do an egg flip variation to show the versatility of the rum. (For reference, the main difference between an egg flip and eggnog is that a traditional flip leaves the dairy out.) “People always think of rum as a tropical spirit, but it can be warming and comforting too,” Dorman claims. He uses a squash amaro for his flip, which “is like drinking pumpkin pie (in a good way).”.

According to recent reports, another creamy drink that foregoes the use of eggs is coquito. This Puerto Rican holiday cocktail typically uses coconut milk, cream of coconut, evaporated milk, sweetened condensed milk, vanilla extract, cinnamon, and ground nutmeg—spiked with rum. In a similar spirit, at The Wig Shop in Boston, beverage director Oscar Simoza is presently serving up a Venezuelan-inspired take on eggnog. It’s made with banana peel-infused rum, batido de cambur (ripe banana and milk), and arequipe (caramelized milk). Notes of anise and cinnamon, alongside a hint of amaro, come together for a cocktail that is sublimely silky.

The Brandy Alexander is another perennial classic. It’s made with brandy or cognac, dark crème de cacao, heavy cream, and freshly grated nutmeg for garnish. One of my favorite variations is at Bar Rêve in Cobble Hill. “Ours is an après-ski homage,” co-founder Victor Triebel says. They pair a base of cognac with a layer of Frangelico whipped cream, finished with freshly grated nutmeg. “There’s nothing more luxurious than a warming brandy cocktail to end the evening,” Triebel adds.

Recent findings suggest that during the chilliest months of the year, a cocktail encircled by fragrant smoke is just what the doctor ordered. Last winter, I found myself in the grand lobby of the Carlton Hotel St. Moritz, where drinks are served beside a stone fireplace and arrive via trolley cart. Their Piz Nair Mule—presented in a glass case—is inspired by a mountain that rises 3,057 meters above the Engadin Valley. Once the case is opened, a gentle cloud of smoke escapes, revealing the cocktail and miniature alpine landscape, complete with a moss-covered mountain and tiny mountaineers traversing the summit. The cocktail itself is a soothing blend of fresh thyme and rosemary, spicy ginger, and lime.

It has been revealed that if you can’t make it all the way to Switzerland this year, fret not, because San Ysidro Ranch in Montecito, California, has an entire menu dedicated to its tableside smoked cocktails. I’ve had the Smoking Pig Poke, which combines rye whiskey, averna, Luxardo maraschino, and a bacon-wrapped brandied cherry—all smoked with cherry wood. (They also serve cocktails smoked with oak, hickory, pecan, and pear woods—in case you’re picky about such things.) And if you’d like to impress your friends and try your hand at your own smoked cocktails, this smoke infuser would make an excellent holiday gift.

Industry observers note that distilling the holiday season’s rich baking spices into a cocktail is nothing short of alchemy. Cinnamon, nutmeg, clove, cardamom, allspice—each offers its own shade of warmth, and can be used as liquor infusions, spiced syrups, bitters, or simply garnishes.

At the newly opened Thai restaurant, Hommali, head bartender Patrick Cura created their Horchata Oolong cocktail, blending tequila, shochu, and sweet vermouth that’s been infused with oolong and umami bitters. “We torch a cinnamon stick over the glass for the garnish so you get an initial hit of baking spice at the first sip,” he shares. “It feels like a warm hug in a glass, which is particularly enjoyable when the temps start to drop.”.

Recent findings suggest that over at The Portrait Bar inside The Fifth Avenue Hotel, bar director Darryl Chan’s Marrakesh cocktail is inspired by the spice markets of Morocco. And further downtown at abcV in the Flatiron District, the Saffron Mylk rum cocktail is a blend of spices and saffron, heated with oat milk and honey, then finished with lashings of nutmeg. “The aroma from the nutmeg gives a wonderful warmth, with the heady spice evoking the holidays,” Chris Moore, director of beverage at Jean-Georges Restaurants, says.

It has been revealed that and if you’re searching for something a bit lighter, head for Williamsburg dive Shenanigans for their spiced vodka cran, featuring vodka, mulled wine with baking spices, lemon, and soda. (Starting December 2, Shenanigans will kick off their holiday pop-up serving festive cocktails through the fresh year—make haste.).

Sources indicate that while the holidays have spurred legendary cocktail creations aplenty, if you prefer to stick to what you know, look for seasonal riffs on the classics. Hot cocoa is a wonderful place to start. You can spike your beloved hot chocolate with everything from aged rum and bourbon to cointreau and Bailey’s Irish cream. At Limo Bar in the East Village, their Santa’s Coffee cocktail dials up the cozy by pairing Irish whiskey and coffee with maple syrup and salted maple cream.

In a recent development, on a recent visit to the newly opened Bar CC, I ordered the Vesuvio, which is their spin on a coffee Boulevardier. The cocktail layers coffee-infused bourbon with cacao, tonic bean, amontillado sherry, Barolo Chinato, Campari, and Cappelletti rosso. The cocktail is then clarified with heated milk that’s been enriched with mascarpone. (Professional mixology here; do not attempt at home.) But wait! I haven’t even arrived at the best part: an edible lace doily is delicately draped over the glass, resulting in the most elegant riff on a Boulevardier you or I have ever encountered.

For the espresso martini devout among us, head for Pinky Swear to order the Cloud 9, which is an espresso martini garnished with a torched marshmallow. Or if you’re an old fashioned fanatic, take note of the Outtake at New Orleans’s Cure bar, which is seasoned with warm walnut and crisp ginger. Even clear spirits can receive the holiday treatment—at Dandelion in the West Village, their Poire None cocktail swirls together tequila, mezcal, ginger, allspice, yellow Chartreuse, and pear cider for an autumnal riff on the timeless mule. And at Dean’s, rather than relying on brandy or whiskey to conjure the cozies, their winter-friendly take on a classic sbagliato features sloe gin and elderflower liqueur for a deeper, fruitier character.

When in doubt, if you have a go-to cocktail but want to get into the spirit of the season, ask your bartender what they’ve got up their sleeve.

Not drinking alcohol? There are plenty of cozy mocktails that still fit the prompt. Beverages like eggnog, coquito, hot toddies, and hot chocolate can all be created with as much depth and complexity as their boozy counterparts—just skip the liquor part at the end. The Pear of Aces at Golden Steer is an aromatic hot toddy that simmers whole cloves, cinnamon, and star anise with lush pear nectar and melted lemon sherbet.

Industry observers note that or there’s the spirit-free amaretto sour at The Lobby Bar inside Hotel Chelsea, which doubles down on roasted flavors. This cocktail uses a zero-proof Amaretto liqueur made of roasted almonds from Puglia, plus a roasted green tea that’s acidified with lemon juice. “The cocktail is served up with a floater of honey-miso whipped cream, to replicate the head of egg white foam from a traditional sour-style cocktail,” shares Brian Evans, the director of bars at Sunday Hospitality.

Toddy Sauce Directions: Toast eight cinnamon sticks and four grams of whole black pepper in a pot. Add 650 grams Lambic (Duchess de Bourgogne), 650 grams Maple syrup, 12 grams turmeric and nine grams black pepper. Bring to a quick boil then lower to a simmer for 10 minutes. Cool and strain into a deli.

“Fresh pressed green apples are some of my favorite things to work with because the texture is so frothy coming out of the Breville. Steaming them doubles down on the intensity of the technique. The spice blend of turmeric, black pepper, and cinnamon evokes strong winter memories without falling into ‘pumpkin spice’ or Angostura bitters. Everything remains both familiar and impossibly fresh.” - Owen Gibler, Beverage Director, Bar Roza.
