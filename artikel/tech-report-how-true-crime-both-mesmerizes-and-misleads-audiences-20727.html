It has been revealed that I had the honor of serving as a Criminal Profiler for more than a decade of the twenty-two years I spent as an FBI Special Agent. Before the FBI, I was a prosecutor for the New York City Law Department. While there, I tried cases from petty theft to sex crimes and extortion to attempted murder.

Industry observers note that then, as an FBI Agent, I investigated bank robberies, white collar crimes, civil rights violations, child sex crimes, serial murder and rape, child abductions, and terrorism. I traveled across the country and around the world chasing the worst of the worst that humanity has to offer. Some of those offenders were among the most notorious criminals of our time.

In the DC Sniper case, for example, I witnessed firsthand what it was like to know that anyone could be shot dead, at any place, at any time, in the greater DC area. That’s because people were being killed right in my own backyard.

It has been revealed that from the start that case presented differently from other “spree killer” cases. Typically in spree cases, as the offender moves from shooting scene to shooting scene he decompensates and his criminal sophistication erodes. In other words, his crimes become more and more sloppy.

But here, the shooter made one shot/one kill, six times in a row while moving from location to location in rapid succession and no one ever even saw him. He was a ghost….

Recent findings suggest that fortunately, though, his behavior leaked out enough information that we were able to build a profile of not one, but two snipers, working together for the first time in US history. One older with police or military training and experience, and the other a teen who had written the communications on a tarot card and on children’s note paper. In the end, shortly after our profile was released, the snipers were spotted by an alert trucker who immediately called 911 and ended their twenty-three-day reign of terror.

Another notorious case involved JonBenét Ramsey, a six-year-old beauty pageant queen—who was reportedly kidnapped but was later found dead in the basement of her home. The case still remains officially unsolved decades later.

In my opinion, this is not due to law enforcement incompetence, but because the family refused to be fully interviewed by law enforcement for several months, the father filed multiple law suits against investigators, and the prosecutor made a decision not to follow through with the murder indictments voted for by the grand jury.

Industry observers note that in 2009, when I retired from the FBI and transitioned into full-time network television writing and producing, it was only natural that I gravitate toward what I knew best—crime. I formed a development and production organization, XG Productions, with my brother Tim, also a retired FBI agent. Together, we endeavor to put crime victims first (not notorious offenders) and bring authenticity to the crime entertainment space.

It has been revealed that one of the most successful shows I worked on as a writer/producer was the popular ABC/CBS prime time crime drama, Criminal Minds. In fact, we just finished shooting our nineteenth season at the time of this writing. I also worked on FBI, FBI Most Wanted, Blind Spot, Sleepy Hollow, Quantico, and many more. And XG also produced The Case Of: JonBenét Ramsey, The Case Of: Caylee Anthony, The Forgotten West Memphis Three, and INDIVISIBLE: Healing Hate.

Recent findings suggest that I published my first true crime book, Without Consent, in 2014. And since 2020, XG Productions has produced and published twenty-five Audible Original Docuseries. These include: Evil Has A Name, Paul Holes’ story of his relentless pursuit and identification of the Golden State Killer, with the help of the FBI’s Steve Kramer and Steve Busch. In the process of solving this decades-old cold case, they created Forensic Genetic Genealogy—a monumental breakthrough that has changed law enforcement forever.

Recent findings suggest that where The Devil Belongs is the story of the most prolific serial bomber in US history, The Unabomber. And Call Me God, is the story of the DC Snipers. All of these titles are based on cases my colleagues at the BAU and I actually investigated and analyzed.

Sources indicate that in 2024, I also co-wrote and produced my bestselling audio drama, Bluebeard, starring Joseph Fiennes, Karen David, Holt McCallany, and Adrian Pasdar. Bluebeard is closely based on the true story of Los Angeles’s first serial killer, James “Bluebeard” Watson, who married thirty women and killed more than a dozen of them.

I’m pretty sure everyone reading this introduction has noticed the rapid expansion of true crime across all forms of media in recent years. In fact, the proliferation of true crime over the last decade has been nothing short of phenomenal.

In a recent development, during that time, I was fortunate enough to launch a couple of successful podcasts. The first, Real Crime Profile—in which my FBI Profiler colleague, Kathy Canning-Mello and I discuss and analyze current and historical criminal cases from a behavioral perspective. Then about a year later we launched Best Case Worst Case, in which my colleague and fellow FBI Agent Maureen O’Connell and I interview law enforcement professionals about the best and worst cases of their careers.

Over a hundred million downloads later, I’ve enjoyed educating listeners while they are being entertained. I’ve also noticed a concerning trend, though. People have asked why I chose the name “Real” Crime Profile instead of what might have been a more popular moniker, “True” Crime Profile.

In a recent development, the choice was intentional. The popularity of “True Crime” across all platforms has given rise to a number of podcasts, docudramas, fictional series, and, frankly, Tik-Tok and Instagram posts, with hosts that “love” True Crime but know absolutely nothing about real crime and actual criminal investigations.

Industry observers note that as a result, there are millions of listeners and viewers who are being misinformed and misled. Were it just for entertainment purposes, that might not be so bad. But that’s not the case. People actually believe a great deal of what they see and hear on TV, podcasts, and social media. Fortunately, the best true crime show runners, writers, and producers hire law enforcement professionals as tech advisors and/or writers—because they care enough to get it right.

In a recent development, A great example of this happened early in my writing career. It started while I was still in the Behavioral Analysis Unit, when we conducted a study on child abduction homicides in the US. Over the next decade I taught about 60,000 law enforcement professionals, citing the results of that study.

Later, I wrote my first scene of Criminal Minds, in which Dr. Reid spouts off those statistics as he magically morphs on screen into a playground with ten children happily running around. As he says, “Of those children who are abducted and killed, forty-four percent die within the first hour,” half of the kids on the playground disappear. “[S]eventy-three percent in the first three hours,” and three-quarters of the kids are gone.

Then there is one little girl left swinging on a swing as he states, “And ninety-nine percent in the first twenty-four hours,” and the girl disappears, leaving the swing, swinging back and forth…empty.

When I saw that scene on TV the hair immediately stood up on the back of my neck. It also caused a “fight or flight” reaction in me and I had to get up and walk around the room to burn off the excess energy. I was overwhelmed with the visual depiction of statistics I had repeated hundreds of times in person. That’s because I realized that in under two minutes I had just reached over 17 million people. And by the time that episode went through syndication, 163 million people saw it and were now educated about the urgency surrounding child abductions. Media is a huge force multiplier and I was hooked.

Industry observers note that as a profiler, people have often asked me what I think about the explosion of true crime entertainment and social media and the fact that up to eighty-five percent of the audience is made up of women. Many people think it is a result of a sick fascination with the macabre.

I have a very different view. I believe that since women are caring, nurturing, and protective by nature, they are drawn to true crime because they want to learn how bad things happen. This is so they can use that information to protect those they love. It’s the same principal that almost forces us to “rubberneck” as we pass by accidents on the road. We feel a very strong pull to see what happened to the people in the accident, so we can avoid having the same thing happen to us.

This is not a negative personality trait. It is an instinctual and effective survival mechanism.

If you want to get the most out of true crime, you might want to pay more attention to the professionals who are trained and experienced in investigating and prosecuting real criminals.
