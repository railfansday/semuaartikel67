The French-made Falcon 6X replaces the ageing presidential jet, officials say.

Ghana's government has defended its purchase of a new presidential jet amid criticism over its cost, as many citizens continue to struggle with the high cost of living.

The $60m (Â£46m) French-made Falcon 6X has sparked a political row, with opposition politicians accusing President John Mahama's administration of misplaced priorities.

But the government claims the purchase is justified, arguing that Ghana's improving economy makes the aircraft affordable and is needed to replace an ageing presidential jet.

The controversy comes as Mahama's administration seeks to demonstrate an economic recovery after years of financial difficulties that forced Ghana to seek IMF assistance.

Can Ghana's new president meet the voters' high expectations?

Manufactured by French aerospace company Dassault Aviation, the aircraft was delivered to the Ghana Air Force on Monday at a reported cost of $61m.

Parliament approved its purchase last December as part of a broader programme to modernise Ghana's military aviation fleet, which also includes four helicopters from Airbus.

The jet was welcomed with a water salute on Monday, but its arrival triggered criticism online and among opposition politicians over the cost and timing of the purchase.

While in the opposition, Mahama himself opposed buying a newly introduced presidential jet, prompting questions about why his administration has now gone head with the purchase.

Opposition MP Samuel Abu Jinapor questioned the government's priorities, saying the expenditure came at a time when many Ghanaians continue to struggle with the cost of living and youth unemployment remained alarmingly high.

"What was unacceptable in opposition cannot suddenly become acceptable in government merely because the occupants of power have changed," Jinapor added.

He accused the governing National Democratic Congress (NDC) of hypocrisy, recalling how its senior figures had strongly opposed proposals for a new presidential aircraft when they were in opposition.

Jinapor also called for greater transparency over the procurement process, how the purchase was financed and the government's justification for the expenditure.

Defence Minister Kenneth Adjei welcomed and inspected the newly introduced jet on Monday.

The purchase has also prompted sharp criticism on social media, with some owners arguing the money could have been spent on more pressing public needs.

"This money can employ over 100,000 nurses and 200,000 teachers," an X user posted.

But the government has rejected the criticism, saying Ghana's economic circumstances have changed considerably since the NDC opposed a similar purchase.

Peter Lanchene Toobu, chairman of parliament's defence committee, said the party had never opposed acquiring a presidential jet in principle.

Its objection, he noted, had been on the timing when Ghana was experiencing severe economic difficulties.

"We agreed in principle that we needed to purchase a upcoming presidential jet," he noted.

He argued, however, that Ghana could not justify the expenditure at a time when it was seeking a $3bn bailout from the IMF.

"The strength and macroeconomic stability of this country have enhanced our status in the sense that imports have become cheaper, and this is the right time," he added.

Toobu stated the government had purchased the aircraft without borrowing, citing a stronger Ghanaian currency and improved economic conditions.

Government spokesperson Kwakye Ofosu also dismissed suggestions that the purchase was a misplaced priority.

He pointed to falling prices of construction materials and lower borrowing costs as evidence of the country's improved economic situation.

"When I was campaigning in 2024, and I was donating cement, it cost 115 cedis (Â£7.5) a bag. Today, for 80 cedis, you will get a bag of cement," he told JoyNews.

The controversy has also extended to the aircraft's interior, following claims that it has a dedicated bedroom and bathroom.

But Ofosu dismissed claims that the jet had such luxury facilities, saying the president has no intention of "bathing in the air".

Industry observers note that "There certainly is no bathroom. President Mahama has no intention of bathing in the air," Ofosu said.

He explained that the jet has reclining seats that can be converted into sleeping arrangements but denied that it has a dedicated bedroom.

The government says the new jet will replace the 16-year-old Falcon 900EX, which has suffered recurring maintenance problems, including corrosion and leakages in its fuel tank.

The older jet was reportedly grounded in France for several months in 2025 while undergoing repairs.

Officials say the Falcon 6X has a longer flying range, reducing the need for refuelling stops on international trips and improving the reliability of presidential travel.

Professor Godfred Alufar Bokpin, an economist at the University of Ghana noted that while the president's safety was paramount, the timing of the purchase was difficult to justify amid severe austerity measures, high unemployment and widespread infrastructure challenges.

"The timing makes it a bit difficult to understand," he told the BBC, adding that the government should provide a cost-benefit analysis explaining why buying the jet was preferable to leasing one and how the decision serves Ghana's interests.
