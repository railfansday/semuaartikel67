Steven Camarota of the Center for Immigration Studies (CIS) discouraged an amnesty deal as President Donald Trump considers working with Democrats on immigration.

After President Donald Trump signaled in a "60 Minutes" interview that aired shortly after the midterm elections that he was willing to "work on a deal" with Democrats to allow some illegal immigrants to gain legal status, Fox News Digital spoke with several immigration experts about the potential ramifications of such an agreement.

The interview came just days after Republicans suffered crushing losses in the midterm elections, raising questions about whether Trump might be willing to compromise on immigration. But the president later ruled out amnesty, saying, "There won't be amnesty," while maintaining that his immigration policy had not changed.

While Trump has not outlined a specific proposal, his comments have raised questions about what a potential deal could look like and how granting legal status to some illegal immigrants could impact the rule of law, assimilation and fiscal policy.

In a recent development, president Donald Trump signed two executive orders on Sept. 19, 2025, establishing the "Trump Gold Card" and introducing a $100,000 fee for H-1B visas. The "Trump Gold Card" is a visa program that allows foreign nationals permanent residency and a pathway to U.S. citizenship for a $1 million investment in the United States. (Andrew Harnik/Getty Images).

Fox News Digital spoke with three immigration experts about what a potential agreement could mean for the country, including its impact on the rule of law, assimilation and taxpayers.

"I think one of the first things we have to always consider is the impact on the rule of law and does it undermine it. It's very hard to argue that the best way to restore the rule of law and deal with illegal immigration is to reward some large fraction of people who've broken the law," remarked Steven Camarota, the director of research at the Center for Immigration Studies (CIS).

Camarota believes that amnesty begs the question of basic fairness, and impacts how people who have come to the United States legally view the immigration system.

"It seems like it mocks them," he mentioned.

He used the example of a person who illegally overstays a visa to illustrate his point, saying that person shouldn't be rewarded with citizenship after overstaying a visa, especially when there are millions of people who have already followed the law and left the country when their visas expired.

Laura Collins, director of immigration policy at the George W. Bush Presidential Center, acknowledged those concerns but argued that the country's immigration laws are part of the problem.

AMERICA NEEDS WORKERS, BUT TRUMP'S POLICIES ARE SHARPLY CUTTING LEGAL IMMIGRATION.

In a recent development, "I understand people's concerns about the rule of law," remarked Collins. "I also think you have a set of immigration laws that are not working as intended. The reason we have so many people who are here undocumented is because we have an economy that pulls in a lot of people's work, but an immigration system that restricts that labor flow.".

According to recent reports, immigrants take the oath of citizenship to the United States during a naturalization ceremony at Liberty State Park on Sept. 19, 2014, in Jersey City, Latest Jersey. (John Moore/Getty Images).

She states the United States is at a crossroads: the country can choose a system where illegal immigrants cannot stay and fix their mistakes while also contributing to the economy, or they can stay and be offered a chance at restitution.

"Almost every single piece of legislation that has had a chance at passage on immigration that involves legalization program has had things like paying back taxes, going through a background check, paying some sort of fine or fee or restitution payment in order to make up for the fact that these people did something illegal by either overstaying a visa or crossing the border," she noted.

But Camarota questioned whether a future amnesty agreement would actually require immigrants to complete those restitution payments before obtaining citizenship.

Camarota also remarked that one of the main reasons for restricting immigration in the first place is to ensure that the United States isn't overwhelmed.

"We don't want the numbers to be so large that they overwhelm the assimilation process, where you can go to a school where everyone's from your background or you don't ever have to learn English, because there's enough people around you who you don't [and] who you speak your native tongue So that's one reason to limit immigration," he remarked.

"In effect, an amnesty throws that out the window and says, well, we have these limits, but we're not going to enforce them," he continued.

Drawing from the findings of a 2022 book called "Streets of Gold: America's Untold Story of Immigrant Success," Collins argued that today's immigrants are assimilating at a pace similar to that of immigrants of prior generations. America's current foreign-born population has matched a historic peak similar to late 1800s and early 1900s levels. About 15% of the current population is foreign-born.

The book, she said, compared people's memories of how their grandparents and great-grandparents assimilated when they came to the United States.

"And what they found was actually that immigrants are integrating it pretty much the same rates," she mentioned.

She also said that work helps people to assimilate faster, and thus, if illegal immigrants were given the opportunity to work legally, assimilation would be a smoother process.

"I do think that one of the things America really is good at is us taking newcomers and turning them into Americans, and so I worry less about that" she said. "But I understand why there is a scenario where people could be afraid of that. But the data doesn't support it.".

Industry observers note that camarota estimated that households headed by illegal immigrants receive approximately $50 billion annually in government benefits, often through programs for which their U.S.-born children are eligible. He cited Medicaid, Supplemental Security Income (SSI), Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF) and free school lunches as examples, although illegal immigrants themselves are generally barred from receiving many federal welfare benefits.

Illegal immigrants use welfare not because they're lazy, but because most of them do not have educations higher than 11th grade level, Camarota said.

"Legalizing folks doesn't change the fact that the illegal population is overwhelmingly less educated, and less educated people just don't pay enough in taxes to cover their consumption of public services, especially when you consider all the means-tested programs they get," he said.

Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) agents conduct immigration enforcement operations in Minneapolis on Jan. 28, 2026. (Madison Thorn/Anadolu via Getty Images).

Low-income people, he noted, simply don't pay much in taxes.

"Remember, half of all workers pay no federal income tax," he said. "So, yes, illegal immigrants do pay taxes, and actually we think more than half already work on the books, so there's no major boon to taxpayers anyway. But now, they themselves will become eligible for welfare programs.".

Industry observers note that collins conceded that while many immigrants have low education levels, he remarked that that doesn't mean all of them are working jobs that meet their education skills. She pointed to a 2013 Congressional Budget Office (CBO) analysis of the Gang of Eight's immigration bill, S. 744, which estimated that the legislation would increase federal tax revenues and reduce federal deficits by approximately $158 billion over 10 years, despite increased spending on government benefits.

In a recent development, "You get a little bit more tax revenue," she said. "They are potentially making higher wages, they're able to negotiate for different jobs and different wages because they have that legal work authorization," Collins said.

She argued that with legal work authorization, some illegal immigrants would be able to fulfill their full potential in higher-level jobs.

He states Democrats won't be willing to concede any restrictions on immigration, thus leaving Republicans with no incentive to make an amnesty deal for illegal immigrants.

Industry observers note that he used DACA as an example. Recently, Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin floated the possibility of a compromise involving DACA recipients — people who came to the United States as children and received temporary protections from deportation — that could give them permanent legal status.

"A DACA amnesty? Frankly, the DACAs already have amnesty. It's upgrading them from amnesty light to amnesty premium," he said.

He said the first Trump administration tried making a deal for tighter immigration measures using DACA recipients as a bargaining chip.

"That idea is valuable and could be productive, but there's no way that upgrading the DACA who already have Social Security numbers and work permits to regular green card status is something that the Democrats would give anything in exchange for," he said.

Sources indicate that but Krikorian warned that giving green cards to DACA recipients could eventually allow them to sponsor additional relatives, including their parents, once they become U.S. citizens, although existing immigration restrictions could prevent some relatives from obtaining legal status.

"And that would have some effects, because once you have a green card, you can petition for certain relatives, and the five-year clock for citizenship starts," he mentioned. "And when you become a citizen, you could then petition for your parents. There's no numerical caps for that.".
