When procrastination stems from anxiety or fear of failure, use this playbook to help your child get unstuck.

In a recent development, here’s a familiar scenario: Your student with ADHD sits at the kitchen table, supposedly doing homework. Twenty minutes go by, and you check on their status. Nothing has been accomplished.

In these moments, your child is invisibly, involuntarily grappling with the Wall of Awful, a term I coined to describe the emotional barrier people confront when faced with draining and/or daunting tasks (homework is a good example). Before your child can engage in work, they must get past this wall of emotions.

Recent findings suggest that many parents misinterpret the early warning signs of the Wall. They mistake difficulty starting a task for resistance or defiance. So they double down on being a taskmaster, or, alternatively, they hover and provide too much help. Neither tactic aids their child in the long run.

Recent findings suggest that instead, I recommend talking about the idea of a Wall of Awful that stands between students and certain tasks. Then, when red flags appear, the Wall becomes the problem, not the kid, enabling the parent and child to problem-solve together.

It has been revealed that the Wall of Awful can affect anyone, but kids with ADHD are especially susceptible because their weak executive functioning skills make completing tasks harder. These struggles, combined with previous failures or fears of failure, add extra emotional bricks to the Wall.
