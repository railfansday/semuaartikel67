📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Natural disasters, cosmic phenomena, and Biblical tales of destruction have long fascinated humankind and sometimes have been thought to convey messages from above. Christie’s London will offer a prominent example of a 16th-century German manuscript with 167 miniature renditions of phenomena such as comets, monsters, and the end of the world.

In the The Book of Miracles, also known as the Augsburger Wunderzeichenbuch, which bears an estimate of £700,000–£1 million (about $923,000–$1.3 million), readers see bloodred moons, fiery comets, floods, cities smashed by earthquakes, swarms of giant locusts, volcanic eruptions, dragons, and “monstrous births.” It comes to the auction block as part of Christie’s classic week sale of valuable books and manuscripts in London on December 10. The house bills it as “one of the most remarkable surviving picture chronicles” of its century. Recent findings suggest that the volume comes from the holdings of the widely respected American collector Mickey Cartin. Christie’s is offering up to $8 million worth of selections from his collection, ranging from Netherlandish painting to contemporary art and, of course, rare books. Upward of 80 objects will be spread over more than 10 auctions in London, Paris, and New York, from this November until July 2027.

According to recent reports, the Book of Miracles was completed in the city of Augsburg around 1552. Its geographical origins are known because some of the captions refer to events “here in Augsburg,” and the paper bears watermarks associated with that city and time period. The makers are thought to be Hans Burgkmair the Younger, Heinrich Vogtherr the Younger, and their respective workshops. Its imagery, says the auction house, draws on the work of some of the best-known artists of the German Renaissance, such as Albrecht Dürer and Hans Holbein the Younger.

Industry observers note that the book begins with the Creation described in the Christian Bible and moves chronologically through other events in that book as well as ancient and medieval history, and finally, the Apocalypse. Events that were relatively newsy at the time appear, including a celestial phenomenon observed from Augsburg in 1541 and a hailstorm in Dordrecht in 1552.

The book traces back to a private collection in Bavaria. Munich bookseller and auctioneer Hugo Ruef, describing it as coming from the library of a small South German Baroque castle, sold it in 2008 for €127,500 ($140,305 at today’s rate of exchange). By 2010, it was with London dealer James Faber, who specializes in European drawings, and then it went to Cartin.

Industry observers note that “The Book of Miracles is an extraordinary window onto the Renaissance imagination,” said Eugenio Donadoni, Christie’s international specialist in medieval and Renaissance manuscripts, in press materials. “Its 167 miniatures capture a world fascinated by signs, wonders and unexplained phenomena, bringing together comets, eclipses, floods, monstrous births and visions of the Apocalypse. What makes the manuscript particularly compelling is the way it sits between observation and belief: these images record real events and natural phenomena, while also revealing how people in the 16th century sought to understand what they were seeing and what these extraordinary occurrences might signify.”.

As the New York Review of Books noted in 2014, on the occasion of a new facsimile printing by Taschen, the 16th-century book came as part of a “boom” in compendia of miracles that grew out of a new focus on reading the Bible, and wrote that in it, “One astonishing, delicious, supersaturated picture follows another.” Apollo fittingly described the book as “a revelation,” notes Taschen.
