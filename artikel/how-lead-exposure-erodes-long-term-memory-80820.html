Source: China Medical University.

Environmental lead contamination, stemming from aging lead-based paint, polluted soils, and industrial runoff, remains a pervasive global neurotoxicant, well documented to trigger learning deficits, cognitive decline, and memory impairment, particularly in developing brains. Yet despite decades of clinical observation, the precise molecular chain reaction through which lead disrupts memory storage inside individual neurons has remained elusive.

Now, a research consortium in Taiwan involving Professor Ann-Shyn Chiang, President of China Medical University and Academician of Academia Sinica, alongside investigators from National Tsing Hua University, National Sun Yat-sen University, the National Health Research Institutes, and Kaohsiung Medical University, has mapped this neurotoxic pathway at cellular resolution.

Published online on November 4, 2026, in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (PNAS), the study demonstrates that lead exposure hijacks mitochondrial redox homeostasis inside memory-consolidation neurons, starving the cell of the newly synthesized proteins required to lock short-term experiences into long-term memory.

To dissect how lead alters neural circuits without the confounding variables of mammalian systemic toxicity, the team turned to the fruit fly (Drosophila melanogaster), a premier model organism whose memory-encoding circuits are mapped down to single identified neurons.

To observe what occurs inside the brain during memory consolidation, the researchers deployed genetically encoded redox biosensors paired with live fluorescence microscopy.

In a recent development, the imaging pinpointed a pair of dorsal-anterior-lateral (DAL) neurons, cells previously established by Chiang’s group as critical hubs for translating newly synthesized proteins during long-term memory formation. Within these DAL neurons, lead exposure severely disrupted redox homeostasis, tipping mitochondrial oxidation-reduction reactions into acute oxidative stress.

This mitochondrial redox imbalance directly suppressed de novo protein synthesis within the DAL neurons. Without fresh structural and synaptic proteins to remodel neural connections, newly formed memories failed to consolidate.

To verify whether mitochondrial oxidative stress was the primary driver of memory loss, and whether the deficit could be reversed, the investigators administered several antioxidant compounds to lead-exposed flies, including N-acetylcysteine (NAC), astaxanthin, resveratrol, and epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), a major catechin found in green tea.

Under controlled experimental conditions, these antioxidants significantly improved long-term memory performance. Deeper cellular imaging confirmed that EGCG reduced mitochondrial oxidative imbalance and restored normal protein synthesis inside the DAL neurons. Furthermore, administering either EGCG or the mitochondria-targeted antioxidant MitoTEMPO after a period of lead exposure restored the flies’ ability to form latest long-term memories.

The authors emphasize that while these results demonstrate functional reversibility in fruit flies, they do not show the recovery of previously lost memories, nor do they establish that green tea or over-the-counter supplements can treat human lead poisoning. Further mammalian and clinical studies are necessary to determine whether targeting mitochondrial redox pathways can protect human cognition against heavy metal neurotoxicity.

How does lead exposure specifically affect memory?

Industry observers note that in fruit fly experiments, researchers found that lead does not impair immediate learning or short-term memory. Instead, it selectively blocks the consolidation of long-term memories by preventing neurons from manufacturing the fresh proteins needed to stabilize those memories.

What role do mitochondria play in lead-induced memory loss?

Mitochondria regulate cellular energy and oxidation-reduction (redox) balance. Lead causes oxidative stress inside the mitochondria of memory-consolidation neurons (specifically DAL neurons), which shuts down local protein synthesis and prevents long-term memory storage.

Can antioxidants reverse memory damage caused by lead?

Recent findings suggest that in the fruit fly study, antioxidants such as EGCG (found in green tea), NAC, resveratrol, and mitochondria-targeted MitoTEMPO reduced mitochondrial stress, restored neuronal protein synthesis, and allowed lead-exposed flies to form upcoming long-term memories. However, researchers caution that more studies are needed before applying these findings to humans.

Lead impairs long-term memory by disrupting mitochondrial redox homeostasis in Drosophila.

Lead (Pb) exposure remains a major environmental risk factor for cognitive dysfunction, yet the cellular mechanisms underlying Pb-induced memory impairment are poorly understood.

Here, we show that both developmental and adult-onset Pb exposure selectively disrupts protein-synthesis-dependent long-term memory (LTM) in Drosophila melanogaster while sparing other memory forms.

It has been revealed that using a mitochondria-targeted redox biosensor (mito-roGFP2-Orp1), we found that Pb elevates mitochondrial oxidative stress and disrupts redox homeostasis in cholinergic neurons and in dorsal-anterior-lateral (DAL) neurons, a critical locus for LTM consolidation. Pb-induced redox imbalance suppresses training-induced de novo protein synthesis in DAL neurons, thereby impairing memory consolidation.

Recent findings suggest that antioxidant treatment with epigallocatechin gallate restores mitochondrial redox balance and rescues LTM by reinstating activity-dependent protein synthesis in DAL neurons. These findings identify mitochondrial redox dysregulation as a key mechanism linking environmental Pb exposure to impaired memory consolidation.

Our results further establish a genetically tractable platform for dissecting neurotoxic mechanisms and for evaluating antioxidant-based strategies to mitigate Pb-induced cognitive dysfunction.
