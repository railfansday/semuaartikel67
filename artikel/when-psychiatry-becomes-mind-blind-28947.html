When Psychiatry Becomes Mind-Blind

Posted November 18, 2026 | Reviewed by Lybi Ma.

I once sat in a room while a psychiatrist discussed with my parents his belief that I would face a lifetime of institutionalization. At 15, I was perfectly capable of understanding him. The conversation excluded me not because I couldn’t grasp it but because that doctor had uttered the word “schizophrenia.” Once he did, the sense that anything real might exist in my head, even the most basic sense, evaporated.

The people around me became mind-blind.

I borrow this term from biologist and natural philosopher Michael Levin: Mind-blindness is the inability to recognize the minds of others. Michael was gracious enough to chat with me about it. He believes humans exist in a world full of minds, with cognition a potential not just for other creatures, but for cells, large language models, even algorithms.

Industry observers note that “We tend to think of things as either dumb like rocks or smart like humans and angels,” Levin said. Instead, Levin believes that the sciences of cybernetics and the tools of behavior science now show mind exists on a continuum, involving testable traits like whether a being can learn and what its competencies are.

Mind-Blindness Begins With Other Humans.

As Levin puts it, it’s more a question of what kind of intelligence exists, and how much, than a yes-or-no distinction.

When I told Michael Levin I believed humans’ mind-blindness begins with other humans, he said, “You’re absolutely right.” And he added, “I think the problem is a scarcity mentality with respect to compassion. It’s as if people thought, if we allow too many into the club of cognition, there will not be enough care or love for me.”.

Industry observers note that I once spoke in sign language with an orangutan named Chantek, who indicated he found his cage mate Sibu a bit messy. Remembering the thoughtfulness of his eyes still makes seeing a caged animal unbearable to me. I live with two cats who often let me know, with headbutts and meows, that I’m about to get sick before I know it; we overlook the capacities of the beings around us.

And humans can be highly mind-blind to each other.

In a recent development, white Europeans have historically demonstrated mind-blindness to other racial groups, to the point of enslaving them. Jeffrey Epstein was mind-blind to women, which no doubt made him feel better about his abuse. And based on their testimony, Lindsay Clancy’s caregivers remained mind-blind to the actual thoughts she was having. They slotted her pain into symptom categories, missing the awful acts her mind pushed her toward.

Industry observers note that psychiatry, and the culture that accepts its premises, has been mind-blind. This isn’t only true of my early doctor. Autistic people have suffered with the stigma that they lack “theory of mind,” or an understanding that the people around them have minds and feelings.

When I taught, fellow professors often shared their conviction that students who bothered them must be “on the spectrum.” Though “spectrum” has gotten to be a shorthand for a jumble of traits that don’t even fit together: insensitivity, oversensitivity, bluntness, shyness. These biases still lead to real discrimination, though they’re based largely on misunderstandings of subtle differences.

Sources indicate that then there is the child I was, turned to vapor by the word “schizophrenia.” My diagnosis changed ultimately. But I would be who I am with or without a word from the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual next to my name.

In a recent development, mental states, which DSM terms try and fail to pin down, are less hardwired, binary differences between people than they are part of the human condition, one piece of all our potential. They reflect inborn flaws less than they show the tumult of living a life in this human body.

A good share of the population—as many as 15 percent or more of adults—will at some point in their lives hear voices. Up to 38 percent will experience visual hallucinations, a high enough number to suggest this alone shouldn’t be enough to diagnose psychosis, though it very well could be. And one-quarter to one-third of people who have what is diagnosed as a full-blown schizophrenic episode will never have another one. Those whose conditions become chronic may be impaired as much or more by the treatment as the underlying mental state.

According to recent reports, trauma makes the experience of psychosis far more likely, certainly a factor for me as a girl. Difficult life experiences can also plant the seeds of creativity and novel thought. This was true of mind pioneer Carl Jung. An early exposure to death, and later sexual abuse, traumatized Jung, and as an adult he had an extended period of hallucinations.

Jung described “incessant visions” in his Red Book and compared their impact to thunderstorms and floods. Though this period wasn’t a pointless bout of “illness” in Jung’s life—he called that time the root of his most important ideas, such as the existence of archetypes and the collective unconscious.

Industry observers note that michael Levin has more to offer the field of psychiatry than the helpful idea of mind-blindness. He believes that in the future we might practice a somatic psychiatry that exploits bioelectricity, the “cognitive glue” aligning cells toward organ-scale anatomical goals, using bioelectrical signaling as an interface through which to communicate desired healthy form and function.

Levin also told me, “Our cells provide another level of intelligence in the body,” and communicating at these other levels might offer pathways to mind care.

Before we fully understand the many ways we can help ourselves, we need to drop narrow beliefs about minds.

Carl Jung wrote that our inner worlds are “truly infinite, in no way poorer than the outer one,” so powerful and individual that “humans live in two worlds.” We share the former world with a dizzying array of other beings. Perhaps, in ways that are not always easy to understand, we share the inner world as well.

“What I hope is that when we can acknowledge that the mind can belong to things that don’t look anything like us,” Michael Levin told me, “our differences become laughably small.”.
