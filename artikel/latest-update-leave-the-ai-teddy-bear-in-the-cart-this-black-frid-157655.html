Latest Update: Leave the AI teddy bear in the cart this Black Friday

In a recent development, somewhere this Friday, a grandparent is going to find what they believe to be the perfect Christmas present for a 4-year-old. A cute stuffed bear that tells stories, answers questions, and remembers things about the child. Even if the grandparents do their homework and satisfy themselves that the toy is safe, there’s a problem they probably haven’t considered.

The teddy bear they buy in November could be a substantially different product by February. The manufacturer could change the software that determines what the bear says to the child, potentially without anyone in the household realizing it. Who tests the toy again when that happens? And who is responsible for telling the family if something goes wrong?

Industry observers note that consider FoloToy’s Kumma teddy bear, one of the products examined by the U.S. PIRG Education Fund in its annual Trouble in Toyland report. Last year’s testing revealed that the bear was capable of engaging in remarkably inappropriate conversations. FoloToy subsequently suspended sales, conducted a safety review, and strengthened the toy’s safeguards.

Interestingly, researchers found in their 2026 report that the manufacturer had addressed the earlier problems, although it was now less clear which AI model was behind the bear’s conversations.

The fact that a manufacturer can make an existing toy safer through changes to its software is encouraging. But the ability to make those changes remotely also raises the possibility that a subsequent modification could make a perfectly acceptable toy dangerous.

This year’s testing of another product, the GoLabs AI panda, illustrates what is at stake. Researchers reported that the panda’s protections failed during testing, allowing conversations involving explicit sexual material and hazardous household items.

Anyone who has spent much time around young children should appreciate why safeguards need to be effective over prolonged conversations. Children are naturally curious and remarkably persistent. They will ask the same question repeatedly, sometimes phrasing it a little differently each time. A toy intended for young children needs to be designed and tested with that behavior in mind.

Recent findings suggest that for decades, children’s product safety standards have reflected what we know about how children play. We regulate the size of certain toy components because small children put things in their mouths. We consider the materials used to make toys because children sleep with them, chew on them, and carry them practically everywhere.

Industry observers note that imagine a manufacturer deciding to change the material used to make a stuffed animal. Few people would question the wisdom of evaluating whether the new material presents a safety risk. Why should our expectations be any different when the component being changed happens to be software?

In a recent development, as a trial lawyer working on matters involving children’s safety in digital environments, I would be interested in seeing the testing records not only for the original version of an AI toy but also for every subsequent version that materially altered its interactions with children. If a manufacturer switches AI providers, introduces newly introduced conversational capabilities, or changes the software responsible for filtering inappropriate content, there should be a fresh safety evaluation before those changes reach children.

The manufacturer should bear primary responsibility for arranging and paying for that testing, with the cooperation of the firm supplying the underlying AI technology. Significant changes affecting children’s safety should be independently evaluated.

Sources indicate that traditional product liability litigation frequently involves reconstructing the condition of a product when it left a manufacturer’s control. With internet-connected toys, manufacturers may retain considerable control over key aspects of their products for years after the original sale. We need to give much more consideration to the legal responsibilities that should accompany that control.

On Monday, more than 155 child safety organizations and experts called on Amazon and eBay to withdraw certain AI-enabled toys pending independent safety testing. The coalition identified more than 50 such toys recently available on Amazon’s U.S. marketplace and called for existing customers to be notified and offered refunds.

That last part deserves considerably more attention.

It has been revealed that removing a potentially dangerous toy from a website does nothing for the child who has been sleeping with one for the past six months. Amazon and eBay have something particularly valuable in this situation: electronic purchase records that allow them to reach many of the people who already bought these products.

Recent findings suggest that if credible evidence emerges that an AI toy presents a significant risk to children, online marketplaces should contact affected customers directly, explain the nature of the problem, and facilitate refunds where appropriate, even if the ordinary return window has expired. Manufacturers should be expected to cooperate, including by suspending unsafe conversational features and working with product safety regulators when necessary.

I would also like to see online marketplaces require manufacturers to disclose material changes to the AI systems powering their toys as a condition of continuing to sell them.

There is very little a parent can do to independently assess whether a software patch has introduced a new safety problem. Even a diligent parent who spends considerable time researching a product before buying it cannot reasonably be expected to repeat that exercise every few weeks.

Children have always talked to their toys. They tell them secrets, invent elaborate personalities for them, and occasionally insist that they have their own place at the dinner table. There is something lovely about this part of childhood, particularly because so much of it comes from the child’s own imagination.

According to recent reports, the traditional teddy bear never needed to know a child’s name, birthday, or where the family lived. It certainly never needed to transmit a conversation to a computer server operated by a tech giant thousands of miles away.

In a recent development, software updates can potentially change more than the content of a toy’s conversations. They can affect what information is collected, how it is retained, and which technology providers have access to it. Parents should be informed of significant changes to those arrangements, too.

Federal children’s privacy laws provide notable protections, but I suspect very few people shopping for a Christmas present on Friday morning will have the time or inclination to investigate a manufacturer’s evolving data collection practices. Nor should they have to.

Recent findings suggest that the entire premise of Black Friday is that consumers should make purchasing decisions swiftly. Discounts expire. Inventory runs out. Websites remind shoppers that other people are looking at the same product.

For retailers, however, the holiday shopping calendar has been predictable for months. They have had considerably more time than their customers to consider what they are selling.

Much of the litigation and legislative activity surrounding children’s online safety has followed a frustratingly familiar pattern. A technology becomes enormously popular with children, concerns begin to emerge, researchers investigate, and eventually we have a public conversation about whether adequate safeguards were ever in place. By that point, millions of children may already have used the product.

We have an opportunity to approach AI companion toys differently, including by establishing meaningful expectations for their safety throughout the time children are using them, rather than simply on the day they are purchased.

Industry observers note that there will be thousands of wonderful things to buy children this Black Friday. Books, building sets, art supplies, bicycles, and the same wonderfully uncomplicated stuffed animals that children have loved for generations. If the biggest technological advancement in the teddy bear you buy this weekend is that it can go in the washing machine, I think you’ve made an excellent decision.
