Tech Report: Eating a balanced diet that changes with your age is the real secret to a longer life

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A review links longevity to food quality and nutrient balance for people at different stages of life; the proposed diets by age remain untested.

In laboratory animals, eating less reliably lengthens life, but people find such restricted diets hard to maintain. A recent review suggests that people may be able to live longer without eating less, by changing the mix of what they eat.

Across large human studies, the balance of carbohydrates, protein, fat, and fiber in the diet was linked to the risk of death, regardless of how many calories people ate. The review also proposes that the best balance is different at different ages.

Industry observers note that more people are reaching old age. Scientists project that the number of adults aged 80 and older will more than triple between 2017 and 2050, reaching 425 million.

Recent findings suggest that jintao Huang and Ziyun Wu of Shanghai Jiao Tong University wrote the review. Their co-author, Dejian Huang, is at the National University of Singapore.

Industry observers note that the three authors ran no newly introduced experiments. They drew on animal research and on more than thirty large studies involving humans; they pooled the analyses, some covering more than a million people.

Most of the human studies tracked people’s diets and deaths over many years. Such studies can link a diet to a lower risk of death, but they can’t show that the diet was the cause.

People who got about 50 to 55 percent of their daily calories from carbohydrates had the lowest risk of death. The risk was higher for people who got less than 40 percent of their calories from carbohydrates, and for those who got more than 70 percent.

Neither a very low-carb diet nor a very high-carb diet looked best in these studies.

Recent findings suggest that what people ate in place of carbohydrates also mattered. Research has consistently linked low-carb diets based on animal foods to a higher risk of death, while it has linked those based on plant foods to a lower risk.

According to recent reports, people who ate more fiber had a 10 to 23 percent lower risk of death than people who ate less. The link was stronger for fiber from whole grains and vegetables than for fiber from fruit alone.

In a recent development, for protein, the findings depended on a person’s age. In middle-aged adults, eating more protein – especially protein from animals – often correlated with a modestly higher risk of death.

In adults about 65 and older, eating a moderate or higher amount of protein appeared to be protective. Protein at that age counters muscle loss and frailty, the authors wrote.

One study followed 6,381 adults for about 18 years. The researchers linked high protein intake to a higher risk of death in midlife and a lower risk in older adults.

Plant protein was consistently linked to a lower risk. Statistical comparisons also showed an advantage for replacing animal protein, particularly processed red meat, with plant protein.

The authors attributed the midlife risk to growth-promoting signals in cells, which protein activates. In animal studies, reducing those signals has been tied to longer life.

Recent findings suggest that with fat, the authors wrote, the type mattered more than the amount. People who had diets higher in trans fat or in saturated fat, the kind in butter and lard, had a modestly higher risk of death.

The unsaturated fats in nuts, olive oil, and fatty fish were linked to a lower risk. People who obtained most of the fat in their diet from those sources, did not show a higher risk of death.

Plant-rich eating patterns such as the Mediterranean diet were linked to a roughly 10 to 25 percent lower risk of death.

The reverse was true of ultra-processed foods, industrial products made mostly from food extracts and additives. People who ate the most of those foods had a 15 to 30 percent higher risk of death than people who ate the least.

Ultra-processed foods now supply more than half of the calories eaten in many high-income countries. The authors recommended limiting them at every age.

Lifespan gains seen in animal experiments can’t be applied directly to people, the authors wrote.

One example involves methionine, a building block of protein. Rats and mice fed a diet low in methionine lived 30 to 45 percent longer than animals on a standard diet.

Industry observers note that the evidence in people is more limited. Trials of similar diets have typically lasted 2 to 16 weeks.

According to recent reports, in those trials, levels of a growth signal in the blood called IGF-1 fell by 7 to 14 percent. A DNA-based estimate of how old the body is biologically didn’t measurably change.

Sources indicate that the authors found a pattern across such comparisons. The longer a mammal normally lives, the smaller the gain in lifespan tended to be.

In a recent development, A second example is the ketogenic diet, which is very low in carbohydrates and high in fat. A long-term ketogenic diet lengthened the lives of mice.

Human studies, though, have linked very-low-carb diets to either no difference in the risk of death or a modestly higher risk, particularly when the diets emphasized animal foods.

The authors concluded that the ketogenic diet seems best suited to short-term use under medical supervision, and less suited to years of use for a longer life.

Existing guidelines, including the Dietary Guidelines for Americans, mainly aim to prevent chronic disease, the authors wrote, and weren’t designed with a long life as the central goal.

The authors proposed daily ranges for protein, carbohydrates, fat, and fiber at each stage of life. For adults aged 36 to 64, protein is set at 10 to 15 percent of daily calories, with more of it from plants.

Later in life, muscle loss and frailty become the main concerns. From age 65, the proposal raises protein to between 15 and 20 percent of daily calories, mainly from fish and plant foods.

In a recent development, for fiber, the midlife range is 40 to 60 grams a day, which is higher than the 25 to 30 grams in current dietary guidelines. The authors wrote that the higher range comes from animal experiments and isn’t established guidance.

Sources indicate that the authors also graded their own evidence. They rated it as high for adults under 65 and as moderate for older adults. For older adults, the evidence rests mainly on measures such as muscle loss and frailty and on general dietary guidance.

A food pyramid, in the review, has water, sleep, and regular exercise at its base, beneath any food.

Industry observers note that the authors named several gaps in the evidence behind their proposal. Three groups remain understudied: adults 75 and older, people of non-European ancestry, and people in low- and middle-income countries.

Recent findings suggest that males and females may also need different advice. In mice, a lifelong high-fat diet shortened the lives of males by about 144 days on average and didn’t significantly change the lifespan of females.

Diets may one day be personalized, the authors wrote, using data such as blood sugar readings from wearable sensors.

In one study they cited, blood sugar and blood fat responses to identical meals varied up to tenfold from person to person.

It has been revealed that the next question is whether such personalized advice works better than standard advice. Does it keep people healthy for longer?

The authors wrote that future trials should last at least three to five years, long enough to capture changes in estimates of biological age.

Until those trials are done, personalized diet advice can’t be given “with the same confidence as recommendations for tobacco avoidance or physical activity,” the authors wrote.

As for the age-based ranges, the authors described them as testable hypotheses.

The full study was published in the journal The Innovation Nutrition.
