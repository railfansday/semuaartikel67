Latest Update: Can the EU make it harder to trick consumers online?

🌐 Source: Dw • 📅 12 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 4 min read (811 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Under the European Commission's proposed Digital Fairness Act (DFA), a organization would have to remind you before the free trial ends and get your explicit agreement before it can start charging you. The proposed law would also make it easier to cancel a subscription online and require reminders before certain contracts renew automatically.

Sources indicate that the Commission presented the proposal on Wednesday. It wants to give consumers more control over how much time and money they spend online.

"We haven't seen that kind of reform in consumer protection in the last 20 years," Agustin Reyna, director general of the European Consumer Organisation (BEUC), told DW.

According to recent reports, his organization pushed for stronger EU rules against manipulative online practices. He sees the proposal as a good starting point for tackling problems that frustrate consumers every day.

The proposal also includes a ban on manipulative marketing tactics, so-called "dark patterns." A concrete example: You are trying to reserve a hotel room and the website claims that only three are left, a potentially false claim to pressure you into buying.

A concert ticket that gets more expensive while you're waiting in an online queue raises a different problem. The price could still go up in the process, but the seller would have to show the current price while you wait and tell you the maximum you could be asked to pay.

Online games that encourage owners to spend their money to advance are also targeted. Children would no longer be able to pay to win or get to the next level of their online game.

Industry observers note that marketing and personalized ads would need to be clearly marked and restricted in the case of children. Consumers should get the option to switch off doomscrolling functions such as the autoplay of videos, showing one clip after the other. With these newly introduced rules, the EU wants to make apps and websites less addictive.

For Christoph Busch, an academic with a focus on consumer law and digital services based at Osnabrück University in Germany, the option to opt out doesn't go far enough. He argues that a version in which consumers actively have to choose to opt into those functions would be more effective.

In a recent development, "But then there is always the question of proportionality," Busch said. "And I think here the European legislators are trying to balance different interests.".

These competing interests are particularly visible in the gaming industry, where interest groups have warned that the proposed EU restrictions could hurt developers and leave players with fewer options to choose from.

Video Games Europe and the European Games Developer Federation called the proposal "unworkable, unjustified and disproportionate," arguing that a ban on selling children features that help them win or advance is worded so broadly that it "could cover almost any purchase in a game.".

In a recent development, for Busch, this argument doesn't hold. "If your business model is based on addictive design features, then maybe your business model has to be reviewed," he remarked.

Catalina Goanta, an associate professor of private law and technology at Utrecht University, emphasized in an interview with DW that "regulation can very easily slide into overregulation.".

Even though she believes that the DFA is a solid and reasonable compromise, she argues that prohibiting dark patterns and doomscrolling altogether would be too prohibitive. Many of the issues consumers right now face could be resolved by enforcing existing regulation more effectively, she said.

She points to the fact that the EU has made a name for itself in the past by adding fresh rules and regulations that have made it more difficult to access the European market, especially for small online businesses.

BEUC director Agustin Reyna disagrees.

"Instead of having 27 different regulations to adhere to, companies will now only get one," Reyna noted.

For him, this speaks for simplification, especially when the responsibility for compliance is shared between online influencers and the platforms they publish on.

According to the upcoming legislation, the EU would have the possibility to impose fines of up to six percent of a company's global annual revenue should the company violate the law.

The European Parliament and the Council, which represents member state governments, still have to agree on the text and formally adopt it. All experts agree that in that process, changes are still very likely.

"It will be a difficult trialogue, especially in this climate of simplification and deregulation with a focus on strengthening European competitiveness," said Christoph Busch. In that climate, it is not easy to add consumer rights, he adds.

It has been revealed that agustin Reyna is more optimistic. "The parliament is a divided house, much more than in the past," he indicates. "But consumer protection is a subject that brings parties together because it brings tangible outcomes to people.".
