Jade plant care this month comes down to balancing your own comfort against your plant’s natural seasonal rhythm. While you turn up the thermostat and pull the curtains against the dark, your jade craves bright, crisp days, cool nights, and dry feet. Even innocently shifting a pot aside to make room for holiday decor can plunge your sun-loving succulent into stressful gloom or into the path of a radiator blast.

Industry observers note that scaling back your watering routine is the single most impactful step you can take for a jade plant in November. As daylight dwindles and growth slows to a crawl, jade roots absorb moisture more slowly than they did in summer. Leaving roots sitting in a damp potting mix for weeks at a time invites fungal root rot. Before reaching for the watering can, push your index finger 1-2 inches (2.5-5cm) down into the soil, insert a plain wooden chopstick to the bottom. Alternatively, use a probe like the inexpensive XLUX Plant Soil Moisture Meter from Amazon.

If any dampness or dark soil clings to it, wait another week. When the mix is dry, water thoroughly until excess drains out the bottom hole. Empty the saucer immediately. Keep plant age and variety in mind. Compact varieties like Hobbit and Gollum, or young plants in small 4-inch (10cm) pots, dry out faster than massive specimens in deep tubs. Avoid over-correcting: if lower leaves feel thin, soft, or wrinkled, your jade plant is ready for a drink.

Sources indicate that by November, the sun sits lower in the sky and a jade that did fine in an east or west window all summer may only catch a sliver of the light it needs now. Jade plants require at least 4-6 hours of bright, direct light daily to keep stems sturdy and leaves compact. This is especially true of variegated cultivars like Tricolor or Variegata, which have less chlorophyll and lose their creamy-pink margins in dim rooms. Relocate your jade to an unobstructed south-facing window or a bright west-facing sill. Make the move gradually over 3-4 days so leaves do not scorch.

Watch for early signs of light starvation, such as pale fresh leaves or elongated stems with wide gaps between leaf pairs (known as etiolation). If natural winter light falls short in northern latitudes, supplement with a full-spectrum LED grow bulb positioned 12-18 inches (30-45cm) above the canopy for 10-12 hours daily. Jade has zero tolerance for frost or abrupt temperature shifts. Leaves pressed against a freezing pane can get damaged even indoors. Pull your potted jade back 3-6 inches (8-15cm) from cold windowpanes, and slide a piece of insulating cork or folded cloth beneath the pot on chilly stone or marble sills.

Also keep jades clear of exterior entry doors that blast icy air every time someone walks in, as well as radiators and heaters: neither extreme is a good neighbor for a jade, really. Aim for steady daytime temperatures between 60-75°F (16-24°C) and nights around 50-55°F (10-13°C), which keeps growth compact and encourages winter flower buds. In a recent development, in November, the best thing you can do with your bottle of houseplant fertilizer is tuck it away in a cupboard until spring. Jade plants grow very little in the dark months: feeding them won't change that. They enter a natural dormancy phase during short, low-light winter days, and their roots can’t process the extra nutrients. Feeding a dormant succulent just forces out weak, pale shoots that stretch awkwardly toward the window and snap easily under their own weight.

Unused fertilizer salts can also accumulate rapidly in dry winter potting mix, burning delicate roots and leaving crusty mineral deposits. Whether you tend a classic green jade plant or a trailing Ripple Jade, hold off on all feeding until February. If you did feed in October, that's fine – just don't follow it up! Once bright spring sunlight sparks fresh leaf tips, you can resume feeding every 6-8 weeks through summer. At that point, use a balanced liquid succulent or houseplant fertilizer diluted to half strength.

Sources indicate that warm, dry indoor heating creates prime breeding conditions for sap-sucking houseplant pests, making a thorough inspection essential. Tip your pot gently toward bright window light and check the undersides of leaves and stem junctions. Look for tufts of white fuzz or sticky leaves: these are the telltale signs of citrus mealybugs (Planococcus citri). Any flat, brown, shell-like bumps are scale (Coccus hesperidum).

Dropping a few older bottom leaves after a fall move is normal. However, dark, mushy stems signal rot, requiring immediate action.

When a jade plant looks tired in November, resist the urge to try to fix everything at once.
