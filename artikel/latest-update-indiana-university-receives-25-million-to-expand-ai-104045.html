Sources indicate that indiana University revealed on November 23 that it will receive $25 million from Lilly Endowment Inc. to bring AI into undergraduate education and student support across its nine campuses. The university aims to use the funding through 2030 to teach AI skills within degree programs, help faculty redesign assessments and expand the capacity of staff who advise and support students.

The award is part of a $291.71 million round of implementation grants for 35 Indiana colleges and universities. Those awards range from $2.5 million to $25 million. The endowment has allocated up to $500 million for the broader initiative, which also covers planning and collaborative projects. Lilly Endowment is a private foundation separate from the pharmaceutical manufacturer Eli Lilly and Company.

It has been revealed that IU calls its program the Human + AI Learning Ecosystem, or HALE. The implementation grant follows a $300,000 planning grant that funded a university-wide effort to identify where AI could help and where it could undermine learning. IU indicates more than 120 faculty members, staff and students participated in four planning groups. The two investigators leading the implementation grant are Rahul Shrivastav, vice president for student success, and Anne Leftwich, chief academic and AI innovation officer.

The program aims to teach AI literacy in the context of each discipline. That distinction matters: checking an AI-generated cost projection calls for different evidence than checking an AI-generated interpretation of a historical document. Faculty also have to decide where students can use AI and where assignments should demonstrate what they can do independently. IU has a faculty group on student learning in the age of AI to bring perspectives from different campuses and disciplines into that work.

IU also plans to use AI to help staff spot barriers to student success earlier and spend more time directly supporting students. The announcement names academic advising, financial aid, career exploration and student communications as possible areas for improved workflows. IU says it wants to improve retention and graduation. It did not report measured gains from HALE or specify which systems would flag cases and how staff would review them.

Sources indicate that the university already operates several AI services, and its data-management guidance shows why the choice of tool matters. An institution-managed chatbot workspace, a cloud service for application developers and models hosted in the IU data center have different approved uses and data restrictions. IU separately warns that public AI services are not approved for institutional data, even when that data has been anonymized. An advising workflow that uses student records has to meet a different standard from a classroom exercise that uses public material. Availability of a tool alone does not determine what information it can process or who should act on the result.

The grant gives IU several years to test whether its approach works across programs with different student populations and staffing needs. Useful measures would include whether students can identify errors in AI output, whether redesigned assessments still test the skills a degree is supposed to certify and whether earlier outreach gets students to the support they need. Usage statistics can show how widely a tool was adopted, but they cannot answer those questions on their own.

The wider Lilly Endowment initiative will give other Indiana institutions room to try different approaches. For IU, the test through 2030 is whether AI can improve access to support and prepare students for the workplace while preserving the independent judgment the university wants graduates to develop.
