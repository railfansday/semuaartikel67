“The Secrecy Was Insane:” The Making of ‘The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping,’ From Locked Safes to Original Cast’s Return

Industry observers note that some stories refuse to stay on the page. The Hollywood Reporter’s Beyond the Book column explores what happens when books make the leap to screen and beyond — unpacking what stayed, changed, how it was done and why it matters with the creatives who made it.

Before the odds could be in their favor, the creative team behind The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping had to survive a Games of their own, one played entirely in secret.

Sources indicate that when Hunger Games author Suzanne Collins called her trusted adapters, director Francis Lawrence, producer Nina Jacobson and original Hunger Games screenwriter Billy Ray, they were eager to read her newly introduced story. The catch: it wasn’t fully finished, and it hadn’t been announced to the public. That meant caution was essential.

According to recent reports, take Ray’s process for example. The screenwriter didn’t write his latest Hunger Games script on his own computer. Each day, he drove onto the Fox lot to Jacobson’s office and sat down in a small room with a safe that guarded Collins’ story. An assistant opened the safe, took out a laptop that had never been connected to the internet and set it in front of him. He wrote all day, mentioned “Done,” and the laptop went back into the safe.

“That’s how the script got written, which of course I had never done before,” Ray tells The Hollywood Reporter.

According to recent reports, it was an unusual way to adapt a novel that almost no one outside that small circle knew existed, yet maintaining strict confidentiality was imperative given the phenomenon Collins’ franchise has become. The original four films — The Hunger Games, Catching Fire and Mockingjay Part 1 and 2 — grossed roughly $2.98 billion worldwide. Debuting with its first book in 2008, the dystopian saga following teenagers forced into fatal combat has grown into one of the most successful young adult publishing franchises in history. Across its five installments, the series has sold more than 100 million copies globally.

In 2020, Collins released her first prequel, The Ballad of Songbirds & Snakes, which chronicled the rise of the tyrant Coriolanus Snow. The film was adapted for feature film starring Tom Blyth and Rachel Zegler. Now with Sunrise on the Reaping, released on Nov. 20, Collins has returned to another familiar face: Haymitch Abernathy, the District 12 mentor Woody Harrelson played in the films.

Industry observers note that the prequel revisits the world of Panem 24 years before the events of The Hunger Games, starting on the morning of the reaping of the 50th Hunger Games, also known as the Second Quarter Quell, when a younger Haymitch (portrayed by Joseph Zada) competes. Sunrise on the Reaping opens on Haymitch’s 16th birthday, which coincides with the second Quarter Quell, where the traditional choice of one male and one female from a district has been doubled to two of each.

Though initially not among the four selected tributes for District 12, Haymitch is automatically volunteered as punishment for trying to protect his girlfriend Lenore (Whitney Peak) who reacts after one of the chosen boys tries to make a run for it and is shot by Capitol soldiers known as “Peacekeepers.”.

The Haymitch origin story answers a question fans have had since the first film: how the hopeful, mischievous kid became the bitter, drunken mentor to Jennifer Lawrence‘s Katniss Everdeen and Josh Hutcherson‘s Peeta Mellark that Harrelson played.

Ahead of the film’s release, Ray, Jacobson and director Francis Lawrence spoke with THR about working under an NDA bubble, finding their latest Haymitch and reuniting franchise alums Harrelson, Lawrence and Hutcherson.

When The Hunger Games was first adapted for the screen, screenwriter Ray and director Gary Ross launched what would grow into a global success. Since both Ray and Ross parted ways with the franchise following the initial installment, Ray’s comeback to craft the backstory for a character he helped popularize represented a complete full-circle experience.

Industry observers note that “Having introduced that franchise, The Hunger Games has been such a blessing in my career,” Ray tells THR. “I can’t begin to enumerate all the ways it’s been great for me.”.

Industry observers note that “When the opportunity came to come back, I said yes before I read the book,” he claims of his return. “It was something I wanted to do. I wanted to work with Francis, and I wanted to be back under the hood with Suzanne and with Nina.”.

Ray has stayed close with Collins, who read an early draft of his debut novel, Burn the Water, which was released earlier this year. “It was as if we had spoken the day before,” he indicates of reteaming with Collins and others for Sunrise on the Reaping. “There was just no falloff at all.”.

According to recent reports, when Collins calls, producer Jacobson, director Lawrence and Ray answer, ready to find out what the author has created this time. But her first question is always the same: “Why don’t you guys take a look and let me know if you think it’s a movie?”.

“This is always kind of hilarious… We read this one and stated, ‘Yeah, it’s a movie, Suzanne,'” Lawrence claims, laughing. Jacobson’s answer was just as immediate: “My first response was, ‘Hell yes.'”.

According to recent reports, collins’ question is a sincere one, though, Lawrence explains. “She’s very serious when she claims it’s OK to tell her it’s not a movie. When you create anything, you’re vulnerable, and when she hands something to us, it’s usually not completely finished. She feels vulnerable about it and wants some reassurance that it really is worthy of being on the screen.”.

Industry observers note that getting that far took some doing. When the team read an early draft of The Ballad of Songbirds & Snakes, the world was in lockdown, so keeping it secret was easier, Lawrence indicates. Sunrise on the Reaping brought “a different kind of tension, because the book had not yet been published, so the secrecy and security around the project was pretty extreme,” Ray says.

According to recent reports, “For us, the secrecy was insane,” Jacobson indicates. “We had one hard copy of the book. It had to be mailed or messengered from New York to L.A. in sections. First it lived at her [Collins’] agent’s house and then eventually we got a safe for my office and it moved into the safe. There was one copy, and people could make an appointment if they needed to read it.”.

The secrecy extended to casting, too. “Even once we had our script, we couldn’t release sides, or even use a scene from the book instead of sides, because if we read an actor, people would know what scenes and characters were in the book besides Haymitch,” Jacobson says. “So we had the young actors do improvs, just so we could get acquainted with this generation of actors and start to collect knowledge about who they are.”.

“Having to keep it locked down in this NDA bubble was exciting,” she adds. “It was sort of stressful, and I was incredibly relieved when we could finally talk about it!”.

“We knew Haymitch was going to be the trickiest person to cast, because we’re anchored with him,” Lawrence claims.

Joseph Zada, who starred in Prime Video’s We Were Liars and had filmed Netflix’s East of Eden series, caught the attention of casting directors Debra Zane and Dylan Jury, who wanted Lawrence and Jacobson to meet him.

“He had sent in an improv tape. We had many, many people do improv tapes just to see who was out in the world, and his was pretty special,” Lawrence recalls.

From there, the audition process became its own Hunger Games. “We really put him through the wringer in the audition process,” the director admits. “We tested and tested to make sure he would be emotionally capable of going through everything he needed to go through, that he had the sense of mischief and irreverence Woody has, and that he could embody all those characteristics without feeling like he was doing an impression. That was the tricky thing. But he was definitely the guy.”.

In a recent development, “We needed to know he had the range to experience all of the loss in the movie, and to do it in different ways: sometimes shame, guilt or anger, or just complete loss and grief,” Lawrence adds. “We needed somebody who could bring all of that.”.

It has been revealed that for Jacobson, the deciding quality was mischief. “You needed somebody who had mischief in them, and that’s a surprisingly hard thing to find,” she notes. “There are some wonderful, likable actors who are very capable leading men, but who don’t necessarily have that mischief in them, that troublemaker vibe. And yet at his young age, it can’t be that dark, twisted kind of mischief-maker or trickster. It’s a boyish quality.”.

Zada was someone who “had a hopeful, sunny disposition” and the mischief they wanted Haymitch to embody.

“When you first meet him in the original movies, you don’t understand why he’s so angry, drunken, broken and bitter. You assume, well, it can’t be easy being a mentor for the poorest, most disadvantaged district out there,” she continues. “What all of us wanted to see in this one, and what Joseph captured so beautifully, is that he was bright, funny, hopeful and very apolitical.”.

Sources indicate that zada’s chemistry with Peak, cast as his love interest, Lenore Dove, sealed it. “You just felt it. You felt like this woman is the woman he loves. She’s his best friend. She’s the person who makes it all make sense for him,” Jacobson states of their chemistry reads together.

“There’s a quality about Woody, and we had to find someone who had that quality but wasn’t going to get burdened by it,” she indicates. “With all of our actors, we always said, ‘We don’t want impersonation. We want interpretation.'”.

That philosophy extended well beyond Haymitch. From Kieran Culkin and Elle Fanning to Glenn Close, Jesse Plemons and Ralph Fiennes, Sunrise wasn’t short on A-listers joining its younger, newer cast members that also included Mckenna Grace, Ben Wang, Molly McCann and Percy Daggs IV.

Sources indicate that but joining an established franchise isn’t always an immediate yes, Jacobson says. “There are definitely some people who just don’t know the series that well, so it’s about educating them about it, and making sure they know we’re not looking for them to impersonate,” she explains. “We want their interpretation of the character. If they don’t know the series, oftentimes they have to go ask around a bit and talk to people in their lives.”.

Culkin and Fanning, who play Caesar Flickerman and Effie Trinket, have been candid about finding their own interpretations while respecting the characters Stanley Tucci and Elizabeth Banks originated.

“What we tell people who are upcoming to it, and I really do believe this quite earnestly, is that it’s one of the very few franchises where, for each of the renowned actors we cast, it is actually all about performance. It’s never about prosthetics or visual effects,” Jacobson states. (Well, almost never: “Of course we use those things sparingly here and there. Those are not Glenn’s teeth,” she notes.).

The appeal, she states, is the creative freedom that comes with everything being “rooted in performance.” “You’re going to be collaborating closely with Francis and the department heads on the creation, building and design of your character, and it’s going to come down to meaty character work. You’re very rarely against a green screen. You’re almost always in a practical location with, hopefully, some other amazing actors in the scenes with you. I think these movies give each actor a real showcase of their talents, in a way that isn’t always true in a lot of other franchises.”.

Sources indicate that jacobson recalls Sunrise on the Reaping being an “incredibly emotional” read. “I cried quite a few times, and then cried quite a lot at the end. I found it so fully moving and satisfying on so many levels.”.

Industry observers note that but how do you adapt a book fans haven’t been able to read yet? The creative team relied on being fans themselves.

“Nina and I consider ourselves fans, so there are a lot of things we fight to keep from the book, details we love ourselves,” Lawrence indicates. “And Suzanne is obviously going to fight for certain things: making sure her themes, ideas, the spirit of the characters and their emotional arcs are there. So there’s a good group of us there to protect what we think fans are going to love.”.

“The funny thing is, it’s incredibly easy for me to know how fans will feel, because I’m the OG fan,” Jacobson indicates. “I’m as obsessed with the books as any diehard fan, and so is Francis.”.

In a recent development, still, some things had to go. “If I put everything that’s in the book into the movie, the movie would be six hours long. You can’t do that,” Ray states.

Sunrise doubles the number of tributes competing in the Games, and the team knew they couldn’t explore every one in depth. “Getting to know the tributes and understanding potential allies and enemies is one of the parts of these movies people really enjoy and are invested in. So figuring out how to balance the 48, and making sure we gave time and attention to the core characters whose stories are connected to Haymitch’s journey, was a definite challenge,” Jacobson indicates.

Recent findings suggest that the book is also told entirely from Haymitch’s point of view, which posed its own challenge. “What we found even in the original movies with Katniss was that if she wasn’t in a scene, the scene still needed to be about her in some way,” Jacobson says. “But when you’re only ever with that one person, there’s notable context and opportunities you might miss. So figuring out where to break with his point of view, when to show something he can’t see or know, was a major decision in the adaptation process.”.

As for every other decision, the guiding principle was Collins.

“At the very beginning of the process, I asked Suzanne, ‘What is this book about for you?'” Ray recalls. “She talked about the philosophy of the book: the philosophy of propaganda and implicit submission, the philosophy of Thomas Hobbes, David Hume and John Locke. And I said, ‘Suzanne, what’s the book about for you emotionally?’ She said, ‘It’s about love and loss.’ I said, ‘Great, that’s what I’m writing.’ So anything that didn’t stick to that had to go.”.

Sources indicate that the Haymitch audiences meet in Sunrise is a far cry from the broken alcoholic of the original films. In Sunrise, audiences encounter a teen initially easygoing and unconcerned with politics. But his life transforms when he is forced into the 50th Hunger Games. Refusing to simply comply, he attempts to disrupt the Capitol’s system and oppose President Snow, altering both his own destiny and the world around him.

Recent findings suggest that jacobson likens the young Haymitch to the “kind of guy who’s probably not very serious about school and is just waiting for the end of the day to run down to the beach with his surfboard and his girlfriend, hang out and catch some waves.” His attitude, as she sees it: “I can’t do anything about this broken world that I live in, but I’ve got a girl I love and a family I love, and that’s enough for me. Who am I? What difference would I make anyway?”.

Jacobson sees Haymitch’s arc as similar to Katniss’, with a priority on family that grows to include their community and district. “Your circle of concern grows in each movie, and there’s a cost to expanding that circle, a price to pay… But it’s the things he hears and brings with him from Lenore Dove. He indicates the Capitol has all the power and always will, and she says that doesn’t mean they always have, and it doesn’t mean they always will. It’s this notion that it’s not a foregone conclusion that oppressive power structures can’t be disrupted and ultimately destroyed.”.

In a recent development, the Haymitch of the film faces a dilemma: “What am I willing to give up for the people I care about? And what am I willing to give up to right these really profound wrongs and try to end the Games?”.

“Seeing him get radicalized, and seeing him decide, even with all this loss, that the way to honor these people is to continue to fight: that was what was really notable to me, and what I found really interesting and moving,” Lawrence states.

Recent findings suggest that “You see Haymitch in the other movies, and Woody does an amazing job portraying him, but people laugh with him,” the director adds. “He’s a drunk, and you just don’t know the history. Now you follow this movie and see all that he went through, how long and hard he fought, all the years he had to escort tributes to the arena and watch them die. But he continued to fight and kept his promise to his girlfriend, his mother, his brother, to Maysilee (portrayed by Grace) and all of them.”.

Recent findings suggest that for Ray, filling in that history was the point. “In that first movie, obviously Haymitch is just broken, with a real serious drinking problem and a lot of pain. This was the movie that explained all that, and it was great to do that sort of history retroactively, to say, ‘This explains the behavior you’ve been seeing through all those movies,'” he says. “People know what happened, and the trick is saying, ‘Here’s the part you didn’t know.’ That’s what we were going for here.”.

Industry observers note that collins doesn’t write unless she has something to say, and themes have been the foundation of each Hunger Games book. With the original trilogy, she explored the consequences of war for teens. With the prequels, she continues to examine the state of the world.

Industry observers note that “Look at the themes she’s dealing with now and how relevant they are: there’s a bit of a call to action, this idea of implicit submission, and diving into the use of propaganda to get people to submit. So there are fresh ideas that feel very relevant. There’s meat on the bone,” Lawrence says. “Take this idea of implicit submission. You can look at any government, whether it’s authoritarian, fascist or one that calls itself democratic: as humans, we always agree to be ruled by the few. It’s a bit of a call to action: don’t be apathetic, stand up, voice your beliefs, and don’t expect instant gratification, because change may take time. I feel that’s very relevant now, but it’s also timeless, because people are constantly fighting for their rights. You see it in Hungary, with the ousting of a regime, and in Brazil, where people actually make change. That’s happening now, but it’s also something that’s been happening for thousands of years.”.

Ray indicates he approached both the original film and Sunrise as if they were “true stories.”.

“And they’re getting closer and closer to being that, which is the bad news for us as a culture, and the great news for Suzanne Collins, because she has her finger on the pulse of something, and it does feel like a true story. If you think of it that way, you can write it as a screenwriter from a place of complete outrage,” he says.

Haymitch’s journey matters now even more given it’s “a time when so many people feel like, ‘What’s the point of trying to make a difference?'” Jacobson indicates. “It’s a story that is so emotional about how hard it is to try to make a difference. It’s also about how what you set in motion today might not be realized for another generation, and how hard it is to continue to resist and to endure, knowing that the changes you hope for could take 25 years to accomplish.”.

Anyone who knows The Hunger Games knows the brutality of the story. “I think an audience that has bought into the concept that a government forces children to kill each other knows they’re coming to see something dark,” Ray says. “I don’t think anybody is going to come away saying, ‘Wow, I was expecting a musical. What the hell is this?'”.

Even so, certain sequences in Sunrise had to be toned down to keep the film’s PG-13 rating.

“There’s a moment in the book where a character is holding the head of a child, and I thought, ‘We’re never going to be able to do that.’ There were probably three moments in the book where I went, ‘Come on, Suzanne, this is never going to happen.’ And she understands that when we get into the process,” Lawrence indicates.

The story’s emotional darkness stayed intact, and Lawrence says it was “toughest” on Zada, who had to ride that roller coaster on set.

“Whenever we led up to certain days, whether it was a Lenore Dove scene or a Maysilee scene, it was always a little scary for him, because he knew he’d have to sit in those feelings for 10 or 12 hours, basically all day,” he says.

It has been revealed that the Games were filmed first, with the District 12 scenes shot later. “He was so happy to just be happy: going to work convinced he’d never get called in the reaping, having a nice scene in the meadow with Lenore Dove, being in the candy shop. He looked forward to those days,” Lawrence notes.

But the darker turns were never far off. “We also had to do things like shoot his and Lenore Dove’s first scene one day and then, because we were in that location, jump to the end the very next day and do that whole scene (readers know the one.) That was tough on Joe. Even when he was happy to be happy, he knew what was coming tomorrow. That weighed on him more than it did on me,” the director says.

Though the book and film follow a younger Haymitch, the book’s epilogue, featuring Katniss, Peeta and an older Haymitch, was in the script from the start. “It was such a profound honor to get to write those characters again. It was just pure joy,” Ray says.

“I remember reading the epilogue in the manuscript and thinking, ‘Wow, this is fantastic.’ I found it really moving,” Lawrence says of the trio’s return in the book.

“The hope was always to bring them back,” the director indicates of Harrelson, Hutcherson and Jennifer Lawrence. But because of the secrecy surrounding the book and the adaptation, “We didn’t reach out to them until, I want to say, the day before the announcement. That’s when I called the three of them.”.

While the team hoped for an immediate yes, they prepared in case scheduling conflicts or other circumstances kept the actors from returning. “We had already tasked ourselves with figuring out how to make the story as fulfilling as possible if it didn’t happen,” Lawrence claims.

In a recent development, but there was no need to rethink the epilogue: the actors were “all very excited to return,” he claims. “Then it was really just a scheduling question: when can we get the three of them together and out to Germany?” the director says.

In a recent development, francis Lawrence has stayed in touch with the cast, but working together again in the world of The Hunger Games made for an especially meaningful reunion. “I think it was a Wednesday night when they arrived, and I got a text from Jen that she and Josh were having a beer in the hotel bar. I was coming back from set, so I joined, then Nina joined, and our cinematographer, the still photographer, the sound mixer… It’s the same group of people who have been making these movies for 16 years, so we had this great reunion of about 30 of us before costume fittings and all of that.”.

According to recent reports, with two prequel novels and films now out, the question is always whether there are more stories to tell in Panem. The creative team admits they’re just as in the dark as everyone else.

“She only really writes when she has something to say, so I hope she has something she wants to write about in this world, because I would miss it a lot,” Jacobson says.

Fans have been quick to name the characters they’d love to follow in their early years, with Finnick (portrayed by Sam Claflin) and Johanna (Jena Malone) at the top of the list, along with the first Hunger Games. After Sunrise, Jacobson’s own fascination is Plutarch. “When I realized from this book that Plutarch has been playing a double agent for 25 years by the time you meet him in Catching Fire, that to me is pretty fascinating. How do you pull that off? How could you possibly live that close to such danger? And what compromises did you have to make? He is not a morally clean character at all, so I have a total fascination with him,” she notes.

If the prequels have proved anything, it’s to never say never, a lesson Jacobson says the team learned early. “When we finished the original trilogy of books, we really thought we were done. We’ve never tried to make a movie without a book of hers. We only make her books. We never tried to develop anything that wasn’t based on something she had given us as a book she was going to publish. So when we first got Ballad, all of a sudden it was, ‘Oh my god, we’re coming back to this world.'”.

“I was so wowed by the way she’s taken us on this journey, and by the amount of things that come together in this movie from all of the books and all of the movies. On the other hand, honestly, you could make this [Sunrise] the first book you’ve ever read, or the first movie you’ve seen. You could start here, make it your jumping-off point, and then go in any order you wanted. So there’s accessibility for everyone.”.

As for future stories, Lawrence and Jacobson are hopeful but note only Collins will know the answer.

Industry observers note that “I don’t know if ‘superstitious’ is the right word, but she really does not let on whether or not she’s writing,” Lawrence says of Collins. “If she gets an idea and works on it, she keeps quiet, then turns it in to her publisher, who she trusts immensely, and waits to hear that it’s a publishable book. Only then does she tell us she’s written something.”.

“I have not heard anything. Would I love her to write another book? Yes, I sure would, because I still love the world so much, and I still find there’s so much to learn,” Jacobson notes. “Because she digs so deep in terms of both history and philosophy, I hope she still has so much more to say, and I hope she’ll decide to say it in Panem. But I don’t know. We haven’t gotten any winks or nods yet. I hope I get a wink and a nod, but I have gotten no winks, no nods so far.”.

The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping is now in theaters.
