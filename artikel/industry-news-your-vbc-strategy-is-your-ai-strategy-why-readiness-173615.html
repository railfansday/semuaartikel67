Industry News: Your VBC Strategy Is Your AI Strategy: Why Readiness for Both Is Aligning

🌐 Source: Unite • 📅 27 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 8 min read (1544 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways
  • Healthcare has been treating AI readiness and value-based care (VBC) readiness as separate journeys, but they are the same one.
  • Many healthcare organizations have been on a multi-year shift away from a system where profits are tied to care volume to one where incentives align with outcomes.
  • According to recent reports, AI promises better care, lower costs, and improved outcomes.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Healthcare has been treating AI readiness and value-based care (VBC) readiness as separate journeys, but they are the same one. The data foundation, governance, and cross-organization collaboration necessary to succeed in VBC are exactly what AI needs to deliver results, and the organizations that recognize this will move faster than those that do not.

Many healthcare organizations have been on a multi-year shift away from a system where profits are tied to care volume to one where incentives align with outcomes. In 2024, 45% of all U.S. healthcare payments flowed through an alternative payment model (APM) contract, and nearly 30% were part of a downside risk contract. This shift will continue to accelerate as cost pressures mount and technology offers more opportunities to provide higher-quality care at a lower cost.

According to recent reports, AI promises better care, lower costs, and improved outcomes. The global AI in healthcare market is valued at $36.7 billion in 2025 and projected to grow to $505.6 billion in 2033, with a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of nearly 40%. Yet most organizations are struggling to scale it. But there is also good news, because organizations that have already built capabilities to support VBC are uniquely positioned to realize meaningful results and ROI on their AI initiatives.

Recent findings suggest that AI holds promise to improve U.S. healthcare in multiple ways, from streamlining manual workflows and repeatable tasks to recommending appropriate treatment pathways based on unique patient needs. But not all the things these new technologies can provide will require the same capabilities. Administrative AI tools can dramatically improve efficiency and produce measurable benefits within an organization, with capabilities like summarizing and managing data, automating processes like prior authorizations, and assisting in simple or repeatable tasks (for example, IT support or notetaking during patient encounters).

Outcome-focused tools—the ones essential for VBC success—need more advanced capabilities to predict outcomes, intervene earlier to reduce preventable clinical encounters or expenses, anticipate and stratify risk in patient populations, and improve both clinical and financial results. This type of AI requires an ecosystem built to support data sharing, interoperability, and collaboration. While many healthcare organizations have made significant progress in areas of interoperability and collaboration, 2023 data from the Office of the National Coordinator for Health Information Technology (ONC) reveals that less than half (42%) of hospitals report clinicians routinely using clinical information from outside providers when treating patients, despite more than 70% having access to that information. Progress toward full interoperability remains disjointed and slow.

While many payers and providers have launched AI pilot programs, evaluated vendors, and explored point solutions and large language models, there is still only limited enterprise-scale deployment among organizations.

At the IndustryDive AI in Healthcare virtual event, Mutaz Shegwei, Senior Research Director, shared findings from IDC’s Worldwide Healthcare Provider AI and IT Investment Survey (September 2026) showing that only 12.6% of healthcare organizations describe their AI strategy as fully scaled. Nearly half remain in ad-hoc or emerging stages of adoption, particularly when it comes to measuring success. Three capabilities consistently emerged as notable to the ability to scale AI in the future: a comprehensive data platform, enterprise-wide governance, and formal measurement of outcomes.

In a recent development, the IDC research found that 43.4% of providers either have or are planning comprehensive data platforms that integrate internal and external information, while a similar percentage have established or intend to establish AI governance councils. Fewer than one in four organizations (22.7%) measure AI’s impact through a formal enterprise-wide program, and without measurement there is no “proof of value” for these initiatives. The gap isn’t in ambition or technology—it’s in the foundation.

Sources indicate that historically, healthcare organizations have treated AI transformation and value-based care initiatives as separate and parallel efforts, with different budgets, leadership structures, and objectives. That approach is now holding both efforts back. By aligning AI and VBC data readiness efforts, payers and providers will have the foundation necessary to optimize organizational efficiency, clinical care delivery, and patient outcomes.

A recent survey of payer executives found that 66% do not have the data infrastructure and technology in place to support AI-driven decision making. Additionally, 92% said that not having a single, connected data source is a major barrier to scaling AI in VBC initiatives.

In a recent development, healthcare organizations often possess vast amounts of information, including claims, pharmacy, laboratory, eligibility, financial, and provider data. The challenge is not data availability but transforming that information into a trusted, usable asset. Rather than fixing these complications, AI systems amplify underlying data strengths and weaknesses. If datasets are incomplete, inconsistent, delayed, or fragmented, AI models bring out those shortcomings rather than resolve them.

This dynamic closely mirrors the requirements of value-based care, where success depends on the ability to track quality, cost, utilization, and risk through data. Organizations that have already invested in population health analytics, provider performance measurement, longitudinal patient records, and care management already have much of the foundation to support advanced AI applications.

An intertwined strategic approach to AI and VBC also improves specific operational workflows. Care coordination, risk stratification, and prior authorization are all areas where both providers and payers are actively deploying AI capabilities. Outcomes in these domains depend heavily on shared information and aligned incentives between organizations—the ability to collaborate and share clean, enriched data from a single source of truth.

Stop funding AI and VBC as separate programs. Align planning, governance, and budget under one strategy.

Recent findings suggest that if the goal of deploying AI is simply to comb through volumes of data and interpret it in a report, healthcare organizations may have dozens of options from which to choose. Many AI tools can successfully identify patterns in a dataset. But for organizations that want their AI to take the next step toward identifying risks and opportunities, and suggesting options for intervention, the AI must have appropriate context.

A broad understanding of organizational goals, program incentives, population health and risks, and operational realities will ensure that an organization gets appropriate outputs from its AI. For example, if two separate employers are examining their diabetes-related risk and looking at interventions for their employees that will help meet VBC goals, it’s critical that the AI they use understands factors unique to their organization.

An employer with a highly transient workforce would be more interested in diabetes interventions that produce measurable short-term results, while a second employer with a long-tenured workforce may benefit from diabetes programs that take longer but produce better long-term health results. Organizations that are already optimizing for VBC—accounting for variables like patient populations, provider relationships, and incentive structures—would understand these contextual nuances and could apply a similar structure to their AI. The same model, deployed without context, gives both employers the wrong answer.

Administrative efficiency is a critical part of improving U.S. healthcare, but the true promise for AI in the industry lies in its ability to anticipate disease, mitigate preventable utilization, predict risk, and provide a path forward to improve patient health while reducing costs. That’s also the central premise for pursuing VBC programs and contracts. AI and VBC programs both require a strategic approach to ensure appropriate human oversight that can optimize outcomes, monitor results, and improve performance. Rather than choosing between an AI or a VBC roadmap, or pursuing them as separate strategic priorities, leaders can start by:.

The healthcare organizations that can succeed in their AI efforts won’t necessarily be the ones with the most AI pilot programs or the most advanced models and technology. It will be the organizations that align their AI and VBC efforts to ensure both are built around strong governance, interconnected systems, and a trusted data foundation.
