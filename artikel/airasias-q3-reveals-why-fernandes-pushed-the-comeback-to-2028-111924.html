AirAsia’s Q3 Reveals Why Fernandes Pushed the Comeback to 2028

According to recent reports, airAsia has shown it can charge more. It hasn't shown it can make money at its new, smaller size. Until the $1 billion debt financing lands, the balance sheet, not demand, decides when the comeback starts.

AirAsia Group posted a smaller third-quarter loss on Monday as higher fares made up for most of the jump in fuel costs, but the results show why Tony Fernandes pushed the comeback to 2028: a smaller AirAsia costs more to run, and it is still losing money.

Industry observers note that airAsia Group, the listed tech giant that now houses all of AirAsia’s airlines, posted a loss attributable to shareholders of RM175 million ($43 million), according to its third-quarter results. That’s about a third of its second-quarter loss. Most of the improvement came from charging more, helped by jet fuel easing from its second-quarter peak. Planes stayed nearly as full as fares rose sharply, and that won back much of what the jet fuel spike had taken earlier in the year.

Including minority interests, the loss after tax was RM381.3 million ($93 million). Revenue fell 11% from a year earlier to RM4.66 billion, and 8% from the second quarter.

“Our third quarter performance suggests that the worst is behind us,” CEO Bo Lingam said in a statement. AirAsia is a smaller airline than it was a year ago, and a smaller low-cost airline is an expensive one to run. It still spends more to fly each seat than it earns from it, and its equity cushion is thin, with shareholders’ equity of RM362 million. The refinancing it needs to steady the balance sheet, which AirAsia targeted for this quarter and as recently as last week expected by December or January, is now due to conclude in the fourth quarter or early 2027.

According to recent reports, the results came the same day Thai Airways reported its own third-quarter loss on fuel costs. If jet fuel eases as AirAsia expects in 2027, higher fares could start turning into profit across the region. For AirAsia, the question is whether that comes fast enough.

AirAsia consolidated its Malaysian, Thai, Indonesian, Philippine and Cambodian airlines with AirAsia X in January. This was the first quarter in which the merged group had almost no seats left that were sold before the fuel shock, which let it reprice. Average fares rose by almost a quarter from a year earlier, while the share of filled seats barely moved.

The group suspended 76 routes in the third quarter, mostly fuel-heavy international flights to India, China and Australia, and kept most of its flying close to home, where demand held up best. The retreat has cost it ground. Malaysia Airlines this month overtook the AirAsia group as the largest operator between Malaysia and India for the first time since 2021, though Fernandes has dismissed suggestions that rivals could take the lead for internal Malaysia flights.

According to recent reports, airAsia cut what it spends on staff, airport charges and leases, but its remaining fixed costs were spread over far fewer seats, so the cost of flying each one rose. Higher fares more than covered the extra fuel bill. The rise in other costs per passenger then took back part of that gain.

Sources indicate that not every market pulled its weight. Malaysia and Cambodia were profitable at the operating level, Indonesia and the Philippines lost less than a year earlier, and Thailand had a weak quarter. Currency losses added to the damage further down the income statement.

It has been revealed that fuel is squeezing AirAsia’s regional rivals too. Cebu Pacific’s management noted this month it has pretty much no chance of making money this year, and Thai Airways’ finance chief warned that if current oil prices become the upcoming normal then its third-quarter loss could be a guide to performance in the fourth.

Industry observers note that airAsia is handing back older, more expensive aircraft: 25 of them this year and most of them early. And it says it may return more in 2027. That move cuts lease bills but leaves less room to grow, six months after the group placed a record order for 150 Airbus A220s. Many of the planes it keeps aren’t flying either. About 31% of the 234 aircraft in the group’s main fleet were out of service at the end of September, mostly for scheduled maintenance, with engine problems among the other causes.

That trade-off worries some analysts. Handing back jets now could slow AirAsia’s eventual comeback, analysts told Skift this month, because fresh narrowbody delivery slots are scarce until around 2033.

The pressure point is funding. AirAsia ended June with far less cash than it owed within the year. It is working on up to $1 billion in debt financing and states talks with creditors are progressing.

The strain has been visible for months. In September, Endau Analytics’ Shukor Yusof questioned the airline’s financial resilience, citing its balance sheet and reports of delayed rental payments. Fernandes insisted that no aircraft had been grounded over nonpayment, and he has denied reports of a government-backed contingency plan. Malaysia’s transport and finance ministries remain in talks with the airline.

Industry observers note that for now, management is betting on the year-end peak. It plans to add back capacity in the fourth quarter, restore some routes and keep pushing fares higher — and states forward bookings are running in line with last year, at elevated yields. The Q3 presentation called 2026 and 2027 a “reset” and 2028 the “rebuild,” when fresh fuel-efficient jets begin arriving. It gave no passenger targets for 2027.

Fernandes has been somewhat blunter. In a LinkedIn post last week, he called 2027 “a period of consolidation” and warned of “more painful decisions” on costs, without repeating his September target of 80 million passengers next year. Expansion elsewhere is on hold too: the group’s Bahrain hub is now targeted for March 2027, should conditions in the Gulf allow.
