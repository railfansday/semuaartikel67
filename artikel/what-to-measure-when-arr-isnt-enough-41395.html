What To Measure When ARR Isn’t Enough

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In October, reports that OpenAI‘s annualized revenue was running well below earlier estimates sent AI-linked stocks sharply lower and prompted a fresh round of scrutiny over AI valuations. It also reopened a question investors have been circling for a while: Is ARR still the right way to describe an AI business?

The limitations have been understood for some time. ARR was built for software companies selling predictable annual contracts, which is only part of how most AI companies make money. Through the early part of the generative AI boom, growth was steep enough that few people pressed the point. The OpenAI episode has made it harder to leave alone, partly because it showed how much weight public markets now place on a single private manufacturer’s numbers.

Recent findings suggest that for founders, this is worth paying attention to now rather than later. A cohort of AI companies is reaching the growth-capital stage, and the questions they face in diligence are changing.

According to recent reports, ARR became standard in the mid- to late-2000s, when software moved from licence fees to recurring subscriptions. A customer signed for 12 months, revenue became predictable and annualizing it was arithmetic rather than forecasting.

The first generation of post-ChatGPT companies inherited it without much examination, despite earning money in a different way. Most now run hybrid models: a subscription base with consumption charges on top, billed against API calls, tokens processed or compute used. A growing number charge per outcome. An annualized figure blends revenue a customer has committed to with revenue that depends on what they use next month.

Recent findings suggest that what gets presented as ARR is usually a revenue run rate: recent revenue multiplied out to an annual figure. That is a reasonable way to describe momentum, and a less reliable guide to the year ahead. The first question an investor tends to ask is what the figure actually counts.

Industry observers note that companies that sell partly through partners can report either the full value of those sales or only the share they keep, and the two conventions produce very different numbers for the same business. Both are legitimate, but the two are not necessarily comparable.

Take two companies both reporting a $10 million annualized run rate. In the first, most of that revenue is contracted, and comes from customers whose usage has expanded steadily over 12 months. In the second, it rests on a handful of customers whose consumption spiked in the last quarter.

These are not similar businesses. The first has demonstrated that customers keep coming back and spending more. Nothing in the headline figure tells you that.

Sources indicate that if a single figure cannot separate those two businesses, the question is what can. The answer depends on the stage. At pre-seed and seed, the number matters. A steep trajectory can point to market demand, to customers willing to pay, and to a team beginning to crack go-to-market. A run rate isn’t generally expected to predict the year at that size.

Sources indicate that later you need more. What matters is the revenue behind the number: customer concentration, how much is contracted rather than consumed, how predictable that revenue is, and whether the gross margins hold as volume rises.

Industry observers note that retention and expansion are the centerpiece. They tell you how essential a product has become and whether it will still be in use in a few years. Churn is diagnostic in a way a growth figure never is. It can point to demand the product is not answering, pricing that does not match the value, a need that was never really recurring, or a market shallower than it first looked.

ARR has been a staple of how high-growth tech companies demonstrate momentum for such a long time that it’s not likely to disappear anytime soon.

According to recent reports, what is changing is how much weight that single number can carry on its own. Expect more investors to ask what a figure actually counts, how much of it is committed revenue, and what retention looks like by cohort. The founders who have those answers ready will tell a much more convincing story than a single number ever could.
