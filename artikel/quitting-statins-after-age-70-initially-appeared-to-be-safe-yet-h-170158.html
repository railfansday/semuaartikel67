Quitting statins after age 70 initially appeared to be safe, yet heart attacks, strokes, and deaths increased after four years off those meds

A trial of adults aged 70 and older without cardiovascular disease found that stopping preventive statins may carry greater long-term risks.

When adults aged 70 and older stopped taking statins for preventing a first heart attack or stroke, they did not see a significant jump in heart attacks, strokes, or deaths two years later.

A research team followed participants for a median of 103 weeks, and up to four years for some.

It has been revealed that during that time, 12.7 percent of the group that stopped taking statins suffered a heart attack, a stroke, or death. That compares with 9.2 percent of the group that continued taking them.

It has been revealed that the results were presented on Nov. 7, 2026, at the American Heart Association (AHA) Scientific Sessions in Chicago and published the same day.

Named STREAM, the trial enrolled 1,880 adults aged 70 or older with no known cardiovascular disease. The name stands for statin discontinuation as prevention among the elderly and multimorbid.

Industry observers note that every participant was taking a statin to lower cholesterol as primary prevention. That term means the drug is meant to head off a first such event, not a second one.

Recent findings suggest that each also had at least two chronic conditions, such as a past cancer or lung disease. The average age was 77, and 47 percent of the group were women.

Of those enrolled, 938 were randomly assigned to stop the drug and 942 to carry on.

Sources indicate that STREAM ran from November 2021 to April 2026 at 24 clinical centers in Switzerland, France and the Netherlands. Hospitals in Bern, Lausanne, Geneva, Zurich and Basel were among the Swiss sites.

The main outcome combined three events: a nonfatal heart attack, a nonfatal ischemic stroke, or death from any cause.

Industry observers note that during the first two years, 100 people in the group that stopped and 77 in the group that continued had one of them. That works out to 10.7 percent against 8.2 percent.

STREAM was built as a noninferiority trial, a design that asks whether stopping is not meaningfully worse than continuing. The two-year difference stayed within the limit set in advance.

Outcomes were judged by reviewers who did not know which group a patient belonged to, according to the University of Bern, which led the project.

Patients and their doctors knew the assignment, because STREAM was run open-label.

Its rationale was that the value of these drugs is uncertain after age 70 in people with no heart disease. Side effects and risky combinations of drugs are also common in that age group.

In a recent development, across the full follow-up, 119 events occurred among those who had stopped, against 87 among those who had continued. The counts come from the AHA’s late-breaking science report.

The design centered on the two-year comparison.

According to recent reports, nicolas Rodondi led the work. He is professor of primary care and internal medicine and director of the Institute of Primary Health Care at the University of Bern.

He estimated that over two years, one additional major cardiovascular event or death would occur for every 40 people who stopped their statin.

The association’s report notes that the estimate requires further investigation.

A second set of measures looked at how people felt. Pain, quality of life, fall risk and muscle weakness indicated no difference between the two groups.

Sources indicate that sarcopenia, the loss of muscle mass and strength that comes with age, was also no more common in one group than the other. Reports of muscle pain were no different either.

In a recent development, roughly 14 percent of participants crossed over along the way. Some who had stopped restarted, and some who had continued quit.

Extra analyses that took those switches into account did not change the result.

“Our findings provide information for shared decision-making about cautious statin discontinuation when statins are used for primary prevention only,” Rodondi mentioned.

Recent findings suggest that the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) advises a statin for some adults aged 40 to 75. They must have at least one risk factor and a 10-year cardiovascular risk of 10 percent or more.

When that risk falls between 7.5 percent and 10 percent, it tells clinicians to offer the drug selectively, because the likely benefit is smaller.

Recent findings suggest that for adults 76 and older, the task force concluded that the evidence is insufficient to weigh the benefits and harms of starting one. The recommendation dates from August 2022 and replaced a 2016 version.

Its advice turns on a patient’s overall risk, not on LDL cholesterol alone, and it addresses starting a statin. STREAM tested the opposite question: what happens when older adults already on a statin stop.

“Although deprescribing inappropriate drugs in older adults appears to be safe, the results of our trial suggest that additional randomized trials of discontinuation are needed for specific drugs,” Rodondi noted.

The study was published in the journal The Upcoming England Journal of Medicine.
