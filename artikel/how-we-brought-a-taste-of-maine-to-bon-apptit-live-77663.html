How We Brought a Taste of Maine to Bon Appétit LIVE

Attendees of Bon Appétit Live experienced a taste of Maine through a hands-on lobster cooking class and a demonstration of wild blueberry cornmeal shortcakes, both hosted by Visit Maine.

Industry observers note that the class, Lobster 101, was led by Bon Appétit manager of audience strategy Carly Westerfield and Krista Tripp, founder of Aphrodite Oysters in South Thomaston, Maine. Attendees learned how to break down a whole, steamed two-pound Maine lobster and transform the meat into a rich, creamy lobster dip—ideal for holiday hosting. Guests then enjoyed the fruits of their labor, sampling the freshly made dip with Ritz crackers before tasting oysters.

Tripp explained that her oysters, cultivated in the cold tidal waters of the Lower Weskeag River, take three years to reach maturity. Known for their distinctive fluted shells, they offer a briny, mineral-rich flavor balanced by a mellow sweetness.

According to recent reports, after years as a lobster harvester, Tripp started Aphrodite Oysters to diversify her lobster business and has since shifted her focus to oyster cultivation full-time. She’s come to appreciate the many environmental benefits of farming and consuming them. Her oysters filter up to 50 gallons of water a day, taking in nitrogen and sequestering some for their growing shells. “Eating an oyster is contributing to the environment,” she declared. Indeed, water test scores have improved at her farm, demonstrating that bivalves help create a healthier ecosystem.

In a recent development, before the end of the class, a few brave attendees volunteered to shuck their first oysters. Krista guided them through the process, demonstrating how to safely do it at home by protecting the hand holding the shellfish with a towel and gently working the knife into the hinge until the shell released. The well-earned reward was another taste of the Penobscot Bay, as fresh and pristine as the waters themselves.

It has been revealed that later in the day, guests in the Demo Kitchen were treated to Cornmeal Shortcakes with Blueberry Compote, prepared by Bon Appétit senior Test Kitchen editor Jesse Szewczyk. The compote featured wild Maine blueberries from Brodis Blueberries, a multigenerational family farm in Hope, Maine, stewarded by Ron Howard and his family. Howard joined Szewczyk for the demo, where he shared that 99% of the wild blueberries grown and harvested in the United States come from Maine. Attendees learned that they have been growing since the end of the Ice Age, when the tenacious plants, fortified by ericoid mycorrhizal fungi, began to grow and colonize the newly exposed land as glaciers retreated. An notable food source for Native Americans, wild blueberries were valued by Indigenous communities for their nutritional benefits well before the modern superfood movement.

Speaking passionately, Howard discussed the many challenges facing Maine growers, including tariffs and currency exchange fluctuations, particularly the impact of a stronger Canadian dollar. Like many Maine farmers, the family has had to get creative. Howard invested in new equipment, including a more efficient type of harvester that allowed the business to scale up and sell to retailers. They created a shelf-stable, freeze-dried product with no added ingredients, allowing them to ship their product beyond the local area. Howard and his son started a distillery, where they use wild blueberries to make an eau de vie and vodka. They have also begun hosting a Wild Blueberry Weekend, opening the doors to the farm and educating the public about the unique benefits of their crop and their growing process.

In a recent development, carolann Ouellette, director of the Maine Office of Tourism, emphasized that visiting Maine and engaging with local growers and producers such as Howard and Tripp was the best way to experience the state’s diverse bounty. “The relationship between people and nature is inseparable, and stewardship is an notable part of the state's identity,” she reflected. Ouellette grew up in New Jersey but relocated to Maine over 40 years ago. Her family used to visit Maine when she was a child, and she recalls those experiences vividly: “As a kid, I remember, when you get to Maine, you can smell the pie or smell the spruce.” It’s those potent memories that drew her to the state permanently, a place she described as “filled with hardworking, welcoming communities that depend on one another.”.

Sources indicate that A few years ago, Visit Maine began placing greater emphasis on the state’s culinary identity. Ouellette remarked that awareness of Maine as a food destination continues to grow, “Sea to table with local ingredients is what we were doing before it was a thing.” Beyond the lobster, Visit Maine has been intentional about showcasing the breadth of the state’s agricultural, fishing, and heritage industries. Apples, potatoes, kelp, seaweed, and grains are just a few of the local ingredients featured in Visit Maine’s newsletter and Bountiful Plate video series, which highlights food and beverage producers throughout the state. Ouellette noted that Maine’s food culture is more diverse than many people expect, and its abundance of local products has attracted an interesting mix of exceptional chefs, many of whom are deeply embedded in their communities. Ouellette cited Tripp’s practice of personally delivering her oysters to local restaurants as an example of how the hospitality ecosystem is interconnected.

Most key, Ouellette emphasized that sustainability and stewardship are central to the state’s approach to its food systems and relationship with the landscape. “You can’t get much fresher than food in Maine,” she concluded.
