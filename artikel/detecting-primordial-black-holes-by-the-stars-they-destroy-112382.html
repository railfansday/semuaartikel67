Industry observers note that dark matter is hypothetical matter that makes up the large majority of the matter in the Universe, but we can't see it because it doesn't interact with light. Scientists put their minds into hyperdrive trying to understand it and figure out what it is. One of the proposals is that primordial black holes (PBH) account for some or all of dark matter.

Primordial black holes are hypothesized to have formed in the first fractions of a second after the Massive Bang, from the direct collapse of pockets of dense matter. Really small PBHs wouldn't have survived this long because of Hawking Radiation, but more massive ones could have. Upcoming research focuses on asteroid-mass PBHs and how they could be detected, if they exist.

According to recent reports, the research is "The Life and Death of Stars that Capture Primordial Black Holes," and it's published in The Astrophysical Journal. The lead author is Ore Gottlieb, an assistant professor of physics at MIT. The authors focus on what are called Hawking stars, stars that have engulfed a microscopic PBH. They're named after renowned physicist Stephen Hawking, who first proposed them in the 1970s.

Recent findings suggest that "Primordial black holes (PBHs) in the asteroid mass window (1017â1023 g) remain viable dark matter candidates and can be captured by stars," the authors write. Though it's all theoretical at this point, the researchers developed a framework for what would happen to stars that capture these PBH. They used stellar evolution models, simulations, and other tools to try to understand what would happen.

It has been revealed that first of all, a single star on its own is unlikely to capture a PBH. But a star with a stellar or planetary companion would have enough gravitational interactions to trap a PBH in one of the stars. It could take thousands or even millions of year for a PBH to work its way into a star's center.

According to this work, there are two outcomes for stars that suffer this misfortune, and both spell doom. "We find that the fate of these systems bifurcates: PBHs that form an accretion disk before consuming the host drive explosive disruption, whereas PBHs captured too late or growing too slowly consume the star quietly," the authors write.

Sources indicate that once a PBH finds its way into the center of a star, it takes only a few million years before it consumes about 1% of the star's mass. Then, the rotation rate comes into play. If it's fast enough, the star's gas can't fall directly into the PBH. Instead, an accretion disk forms around the PBH, inside the star. This is where the two outcomes diverge.

It has been revealed that in the second case, the star's destruction is less cinematic. "The system dies quietly," the authors write, "leaving a PBH with mass of the order of the consumed stellar mass and little or no bright disk-powered emission." It's basically a stellar-mass black hole indistinguishable from others of its kind.

These two paths to destruction create different prospects for observing PBHs engulfed by stars.

"Our results carry important observational implications across both electromagnetic and GW channels," the authors explain. "If a PBH has grown sufficiently to form an accretion disk, the associated disk winds and relativistic jets can inject enough energy and momentum into the stellar envelope to unbind the star and power an explosive transient.".

Here's where dark matter comes in. These Hawking-stars, if they exist and can be detected individually, could be used to calculate their population.
