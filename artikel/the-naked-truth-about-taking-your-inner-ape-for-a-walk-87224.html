Walking is a key milestone for human development, an activity done for recreation, and can be an Olympic sport. Walking is a critical and integral behavior across the animal kingdom. It's also something that can be leveraged with deliberate practice. And it's been going on for millions of years since our proto-human ancestors were learning to stand upright, walk bipedally, and land on their heels.

Sources indicate that A key characteristic of human gait is that we land on our heels at the end of swing—something found also in African apes. My research career included diving deep into the neuromechanics of human walking and especially the way sensory feedback and spinal cord motor control vary functionally while we move. I was fascinated by the fact that the most critically controlled part of the step cycle is heel strike—just when your foot lands and weight begins to shift onto the new stance leg.

Sources indicate that so my eye was drawn to the recent work, "Heel-strike Mechanics Reveal Evolutionary Trade-offs in Hominin Bipedalism" by Nicholas B. Holowka et al.

These researchers studied bipedal and quadrupedal locomotion of chimpanzees and humans and examined forces and angles of the foot at the beginning of stance. They found that chimpanzees had much larger ranges of foot-strike angles than humans and often did not land heel-first.

Industry observers note that impact forces and loading rates are much higher with heel-striking, but metabolic cost is lower. This work suggests that the primitive leg anatomy of early hominins meant either high impact forces or poor walking economy. A convergent solution for less mechanically stressful, more economical, and larger travel ranges arrived via "evolution of larger heels and lower limb joints.".

According to recent reports, but can we turn heel-strike walking into striking with our heels? The timing of when you put your foot down at heel strike is the most dangerous part of the entire step cycle. If we make a mistake, or we slip on something, or the ground gives when we put our heel down and our body begins to shift weight onto that leg, we can have a catastrophic failure and a fall. Which is the ultimate failure for the locomotive system.

Sources indicate that so it's critical to avoid a catastrophe, and evolution of bipedal locomotion has incorporated neurological connections that assist in controlling this. But the opposite side of that is that it's also an opportunity. And that's where self-protection and martial arts training factor in.

Recent findings suggest that that critical part of the step cycle is an excellent time to take that heel and stomp it onto an attacker's instep or the top of their foot. You can jam all your weight that's already moving forward to normally be accepted by your stance leg. But now it's to destabilize your opponent.

Sources indicate that this is something I teach when guiding martial arts practice: harnessing the natural movement of the body as the base layer for tactical reaction. Martial arts like Bagua Zhang center core practice on circle walking, where locomotion is central to the performance of discrete actions. We can connect our evolutionary advantage in walking to become a powerful application of self-protective action. This allows us to fully express the functional connectivity within the nervous system, mostly subliminal and automatic, in deliberate mindful action.
