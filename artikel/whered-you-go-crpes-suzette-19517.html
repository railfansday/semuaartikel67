Where’d You Go, Crêpes Suzette?

In a recent development, there’s something simultaneously comforting and dazzling about setting a dessert on fire: Candles atop a birthday cake; the meringue encrusting a baked Alaska; the caramelized fruit in a classic bananas Foster. But chief among them is one whose very name promises something different, something with French sophistication and an air of mystery that demands a moniker all its own: crêpes Suzette.

In a recent development, crêpes Suzette is a surprisingly simple dish considering its decadent, theatrical reputation. The drama comes from the flames—thin, traditional French crêpes are drenched in a sauce made of orange liqueur, which is then set ablaze (typically tableside in many restaurants), causing the spirits to simmer and the sugars to caramelize. French chef Andrea Calstier, of Michelin-starred La Bastide in North Salem, Newly introduced York, grew up eating them with his family, and in the brasseries of his home country.

Recent findings suggest that for such a famous dish, though, its appearance on restaurant menus is increasingly rare. And for such an iconic French dessert, well, it may not technically be French at all. So where did these flaming pancakes come from? Where did they go? And isn’t it about time we bring them back?

While there are conflicting reports about the original creator of crêpes Suzette, the top candidate is Henri Charpentier, chef at the Café de Paris in Monte Carlo, Monaco, in the late 1800s. But while multiple roads lead back to Charpentier, the path gets murky, contradicted by both general plausibility and Charpentier himself.

Originally, Charpentier claimed credit for the iconic dish in his autobiography, Life à la Henri: Being the Memoirs of Henri Charpentier. In this 1934 volume, he suggested that the dish was a happy accident, tinged with drama. He alleged that as a young man working at the Café de Paris, he had been carrying a platter of crêpes to the Prince of Wales (the future King Edward VII) and stepped too near a chafing dish, setting the crêpes on fire, to the shock and delight of the prince, who proposed naming the newly created dish after his dining companion, a young woman named Suzette. (Frustratingly little information is available on who this Suzette may have been, if she was a real person at all.).

The story has the right theatrical touches for the dish, but it is almost certainly apocryphal, not least because Charpentier himself, in an interview with American writer Elsie Lee for the 1950s “Compleat Imbiber” series, stated that he had developed the dish by taking a beloved childhood fruit crêpe and adding the then-fashionable touches of a liquor-based sauce and, presumably, a flame.

Wherever it came from, though, and whatever Charpentier had to say about it, Suzette’s unwitting legacy has endured for more than a century—her crêpes have remained a household name, even if they have fallen somewhat out of style. While chef Calstier notes that they’re still “served in many restaurants” in his native Marseille and that “French customers…are accustomed to seeing crêpes Suzette in any brasserie,” he admitted that “here in the U.S., they are more difficult to find.”.

Sources indicate that part of the reason may be that, as with many dishes that predate the Edwardian era, it simply went out of fashion. But according to Lucile Plaza, executive chef at Benoit Newly introduced York, it’s a result of the dish’s inherent service requirements: “True tableside service requires high staffing levels and precise choreography that a busy modern kitchen can’t always support daily,” she states. In London’s West End, fine-dining heavyweights Savoy Grill and The Ritz take this showmanship to the extreme, with portable burners and bottles of Grand Marnier on wooden trolleys.

But while there are complications to adding an edible spectacle to a restaurant menu, there are benefits, too. Chef Plaza notes that for the kitchen, “Suzette is brilliant because it is the ultimate way to use leftover crêpes. The intense bathing in the hot, rich citrus sauce revives the starch perfectly.” But not every restaurant that serves crêpes Suzette does so with a tableside flourish: In London’s St. John’s Wood, Oslo Court Restaurant, which is famous for its retro menu of classics like steak Diane and sole Veronique (two more of Edward VII's girlfriends, perhaps?), crêpes Suzette are brûléed with a torch in the kitchen rather than set aflame at the table. Adding crêpes Suzette to a menu may only be as hard as a restaurant wants it to be.

In a recent development, A burst of blue-tinged flame may not be necessary for the dish to achieve its signature caramelization, but it is key to the show—and a major point in favor of the dish’s present-day revival. As the old saying goes, “we eat with our eyes first,” and it’s truer than ever today. In fact, for many diners in the age of social media, it’s the phone that eats first, and that’s where crêpes Suzette has real potential for a modern renaissance. Nearly 400,000 viewers have watched a YouTube video of the tableside crêpes Suzette service at the London Ritz, and more than a million have viewed a TikTok of crêpes Suzette being prepared tableside at Switzerland’s five-star Suvretta House in Saint Moritz.

Spectacle is cool again, and whether dining out or cooking at home, we want to feel astonished and excited by the foods we eat. While a tableside flambé may prove logistically complicated at a restaurant scale, the feat is significantly easier to pull off at home—all it takes is a lighter or a match and a little confidence. Chef Calstier, for one, says that crêpes Suzette are a favorite of his wife, Elena, adding, “You can prepare everything in advance and do the flambé in front of your guests to wow them.” Plus, he says, “they don’t require many ingredients, beyond that bottle of Grand Marnier that we all have stashed away somewhere. They’re quite simple to make.”.

It has been revealed that moreover, as delectable as classic crêpes Suzette are, they lend themselves well to upcoming takes and customizations. Chef Plaza’s version at Benoit has a cherry twist: “Replacing Grand Marnier with kirsch is a natural match,” she says. “The dry cherry distillate brings an incredible aroma to the tableside without adding cloying sugar.” Meanwhile, at La Bastide, chef Calstier dishes up a twist that uses French toast instead of crêpes while sticking to the classic flavors and flambé. We asked one of our favorite pastry chefs, Caroline Schiff, author of Daily Desserts: Treats on Repeat, to develop a savory version for us, which she describes as “a cheeky little twist” on the classic. In her take, inspired by French onion soup, crêpes layered with caramelized onions and nutty gruyère are set ablaze with a splash of sherry. “They are at once a novelty and a nod to the classic,” she says.

Charpentier, one imagines, would approve.
