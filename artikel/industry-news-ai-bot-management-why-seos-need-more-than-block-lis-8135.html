📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that for years, bot management in SEO was relatively straightforward. You either allowed crawlers to access your website or blocked them. That model was never perfect, but it was generally sufficient for what we needed.

Image from author, November 2026.

AI search has made that approach increasingly inadequate.

It has been revealed that today, websites are accessed by traditional search crawlers, AI training crawlers, retrieval bots, user-initiated fetches, and autonomous agents. These serve very different purposes, yet we regularly lump them together under the term “AI bots.”.

Blocking a crawler used for model training has different consequences from blocking one retrieving content for search answers – this distinction really matters!

Robots.txt remains useful, but as it’s voluntary actual enforcement happens through CDNs, web application firewalls and bot mitigation services, which can contradict your robots.txt rules without the SEO team even knowing.

Image from author, November 2026.

Agents complicate matters further. They can browse websites like real users, trigger analytics tracking, and become increasingly difficult to distinguish from human visitors. Block an agent performing a product comparison, and you might not simply lose a ranking. You could disappear from the answer entirely.

Sources indicate that my argument is that we need to move from block lists to deliberate bot policies. Policies that consider who’s accessing our content, why they’re doing it and the consequences of allowing or blocking them.

Recent findings suggest that these decisions increasingly involve SEO, engineering, security, legal, and commercial teams. They need to be coordinated, consistently enforced and regularly reviewed.
