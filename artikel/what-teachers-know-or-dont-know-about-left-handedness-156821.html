What Teachers Know (Or Don’t Know) About Left-Handedness

All over the world, teachers play a crucial role in their pupils' learning experience. This is especially true for left-handed children, who, for a long time, have been forced to write with their right hand against their will in many countries. Thankfully, the awful practice of handedness relearning is now a thing of the past in the US and many other countries, and today, both left-handed and right-handed pupils are encouraged to write with their naturally dominant hand in school. This makes it key that teachers have some basic knowledge about handedness and also the ergonomic needs of left-handed kids in learning environments. As handedness typically is not a main focus in teacher education, it is, however, largely unknown what knowledge the average teacher has about handedness and the specific learning needs of left-handed children. Thus, more psychological research on this important topic is needed.

A new study entitled “Handedness literacy in teachers: Data from Greece, Serbia, and Belgium”, just published in the scientific journal Laterality, aimed at closing this gap in the psychological literature by focusing on teachers’ handedness literacy (Papadatou-Pastou and co-workers, 2026). Handedness literacy refers to a person’s general knowledge and understanding of handedness.

The study team, led by renowned handedness researcher Marietta Papadatou-Pastou, asked more than 800 prospective and in-service teachers to fill out a questionnaire with various questions about their knowledge of handedness and about themselves. The teachers came from three different European countries: Greece, Serbia, and Belgium.

According to recent reports, the questionnaire the teachers had to fill out consisted of four main parts. The first part consisted of open-ended questions about left-handedness, such as “Do left-handed students differ from right-handed students?”. The teachers had to write answers to these questions. The second part of the questionnaire consisted of 18 questions quizzing the teachers about their knowledge of handedness. The third part of the questionnaire contained further questions about handedness, and the fourth part contained some questions about the teachers themselves, such as their own handedness and their age, among others.

The analysis of the data yielded several interesting results about teachers’ handedness literacy.

Sources indicate that the analyses of the open-ended questions about handedness in the first part of the questionnaire highlighted that teachers generally had a supportive attitude towards left-handed pupils. The teachers wrote about different aspects of handedness, such as the underlying neurobiology or specific challenges left-handed pupils face, or their ergonomic and classroom needs. In general, they were in favor of letting children choose which hand they like to write with.

It has been revealed that the analysis of the handedness literacy scores revealed that teachers knew some things about handedness, but also had quite a lot of knowledge gaps. As there were 18 questions and the scientists gave 1 point per correct question, the maximum score for handedness literacy was 18. Teachers from Serbia had an average score of 8.11 (45% correct answers). Teachers from Belgium were slightly better as they had an average score of 8.85 (49% correct answers), as were teachers from Greece, who had an average score of 8.90 (49% correct answers).

Sources indicate that so, in all three countries, teachers had less than 50% correct answers- if there were a left-handedness test, they would probably fail! One thing that many teachers got wrong was that they thought that left-handed pupils were more intelligent than right-handed pupils. Also, many teachers thought that there were more left-handers in the population than the actual percentage (10.6%). Interestingly, left-handed teachers did not show higher handedness literacy than right-handed teachers.

Many of the teachers who participated in the study stated that they thought that handedness literacy in teachers needs to be improved. The results of the study show that this is actually true. This suggests that teacher education should include more scientific information on left-handedness, so that teachers can optimally educate left-handed kids.

Industry observers note that papadatou-Pastou, M., Papadopoulou, A. K., Kovic, V., Gerrits, R., & Vingerhoets, G. (2026). Handedness literacy in teachers: Data from Greece, Serbia, and Belgium. Laterality, 1–43. Advance online publication. https://doi.org/10.1080/1357650X.2026.2753053.
