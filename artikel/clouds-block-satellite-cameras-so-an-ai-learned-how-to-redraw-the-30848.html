Clouds block satellite cameras, so an AI learned how to redraw the ground beneath them

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

GeoCore-9B learned how to recreate the ground that is hidden beneath cloud cover in satellite photos.

Industry observers note that A satellite picture of a flooded town is only worth something if the camera can see the town. On a cloudy day it can’t, and the picture highlights cloud where the streets should be. A fresh AI model fills those streets back in.

It was built to know where on Earth it is looking and how much ground each pixel covers.

In a recent development, jeonghyeok Do and Munchurl Kim of KAIST in South Korea built the model, called GeoCore-9B. Their paper is a preprint that has been accepted for presentation at NeurIPS 2026, an artificial intelligence conference. It has not yet been published in a journal.

Sources indicate that the ground the model draws under a cloud is a reconstruction, not a photograph. Do and Kim make that point themselves, and they warn that realistic fake satellite imagery could be used to spread disinformation about disasters or geopolitical events.

Ordinary satellite pictures work like a camera. They record sunlight bouncing off the ground, so thick cloud or nighttime leaves them with nothing to record.

That matters most during floods and wildfires, when the weather is often bad and the ground is exactly what people need to see.

A radar satellite sends radio waves down and measures the echoes that come back. The method is called synthetic aperture radar, and it works at night and through cloud.

But its images don’t look like photographs, because brightness and texture in them mean different things. Do and Kim wanted one model for both problems.

It has been revealed that earlier attempts started with an AI built to generate ordinary photographs and then taught it about satellites. Do and Kim wrote that this builds in biases.

Ordinary photos are taken from eye level with a subject in the middle, while a satellite looks straight down at a scene with no center.

So they trained their AI model from scratch on satellite views alone, adjusting about 9 billion internal settings, known as parameters, as it learned.

According to recent reports, the training set was 10 million satellite images, each paired with a sentence describing it. The note for a port mentioned ships and warehouses; the note for farmland mentioned a road between fields.

Each image also carried its latitude, longitude, and how much ground one pixel covers. A building looks very different when each pixel covers about 3 feet (1 meter) than when each covers about 105 feet (32 meters).

Without that number, a small building and a large industrial plant can have the same shape.

In a recent development, A model this large, trained from nothing, learns slowly and tends to produce scenes with no coherent layout. So Do and Kim brought in a second AI, already trained to recognize satellite imagery, as a teacher.

During training, the newly introduced model was scored on how well its internal picture of a scene agreed with the teacher’s picture of how roads connect and where field boundaries lie.

The teacher is switched off once training ends, so it adds no work when the model is in use. Without it, the team found, the model’s images had fragmented textures and distorted structures. With it, every score they tracked improved.

Recent findings suggest that the team also tested what the model had absorbed about geography. Given only a latitude and longitude, with no text at all, it drew dense skyscrapers for the coordinates of New York City and ice fields for Antarctica. It drew sand dunes for the Sahara and forest for the Amazon.

The team checked 500 of these coordinate-only images generated by the model against all 10.5 million real world photos that were used as training images.

They found none that closely matched the real-world satellite images, suggesting that the AI was not simply copying existing photographs. However, they wrote that the test can’t rule out every form of memorization used by the AI model.

It has been revealed that to put the model to work, Do and Kim froze it and trained a small set of extra settings for each task. This was far cheaper than retraining it.

According to recent reports, on a public test set of cloudy and clear image pairs, GeoCore-9B outscored every program built specifically for cloud removal on two of three standard measures. Those were cover brightness and color accuracy, and how closely the structure matches the clear image.

On the third, how natural the image looks, it scored about the same as the best specialist. The roads and field boundaries in its reconstructions stayed where the clear images put them.

It has been revealed that on a second public dataset, the model turned radar images into photo-like images. It scored best on the two measures of realism and fine detail, and it roughly matched the best specialist on structural similarity.

“By training the AI on where each satellite image was taken and the actual size of the terrain it demonstrates, we made it possible to use a single model for multiple tasks such as cloud removal and image conversion,” mentioned Kim, a professor in KAIST’s School of Electrical Engineering.

He added that he expects the work to make weather-limited satellite data more usable for assessing disasters and monitoring changes in forests and cities.

Sources indicate that the model only produces ordinary color images. It can read a radar image but can’t generate one. In addition, it doesn’t handle the multispectral images, with light bands beyond visible color, that some Earth-observation satellites record.

According to recent reports, one piece, the component that compresses each image before the model works on it, came from ordinary photography. On the cloud-removal images, it kept the main features but lost some faint local detail.

By their own account, the experiments can’t say how much of the gain came from the model’s size, its training data, or the teacher.

Recent findings suggest that so far each task has been tested on a single public dataset. How the model performs on other satellites and regions is unknown, and that is the test Do and Kim plan next.

Industry observers note that do and Kim also want the model to predict changes on the ground: flooding, building damage, wildfire spread, and urban growth. The team intends to explore surveillance and reconnaissance as well.

The model’s code and weights are online, so other groups can run those tests.

The full study has been posted as a preprint on arXiv and accepted for presentation at NeurIPS 2026. It has not been published in a journal.
