📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Back in March, Facebook doubled down on its efforts to woo creators from other platforms by launching Creator Fast Track—a program that pays established creators up to $3,000 a month (for a max of three months) to start posting Reels on Facebook.

Sources indicate that now, the Meta-owned platform is expanding that initiative to include U.S. college students with as few as 1,000 followers on Instagram, TikTok, or YouTube. Among other perks, Creator Fast Track College Edition offers eligible creators “immediate access to Facebook Content Monetization” and “increased reach on eligible reels” (per Meta).

College students with larger followings can also access bonuses that provide “three months of guaranteed pay for sharing eligible reels.” According to Meta, “Creators with at least 20,000 followers on Instagram, TikTok, or YouTube can earn between $100-$450 per month, those with at least 100,000 followers can earn $1,000 per month, and those with more than one million followers can earn $3,000 per month.”.

Recent findings suggest that facebook’s creator payouts hit an all-time high last year at almost $3 billion—a 35% increase from 2024 (per Meta). Reels accounted for 60% of that total, while the rest went to Stories, photos, and text posts.

That growing investment in creators—especially short-form video creators—is clear evidence of Facebook’s determination to compete more directly with rivals like TikTok and YouTube.

And it’s not just targeting big-name creators, either; as Creator Fast Track’s lower entry point for college students reveals, Facebook is eager to attract young creators at the start of their careers.

In a recent development, the rising importance of NIL rights and trends like #RushTok and #DormTok makes that a smart move. Since 2021, student athletes have carved out a bigger and bigger space for themselves in the creator economy, attracting the attention of major brands and platforms.

Industry observers note that content creation has also become a strong Strategy B for NIL athletes who may not go pro after college. If Facebook can become a key platform for those creators while they’re still in school, it has a better shot at keeping them around after graduation—whether they go pro or pivot to full-time content creation.
