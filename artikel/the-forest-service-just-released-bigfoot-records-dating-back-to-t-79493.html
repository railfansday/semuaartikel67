In the 1940s, a man named Leo Kinney described in an informal written account a disturbing encounter along Petty Creek, a tributary of the Clark Fork River west of Missoula, Montana. At the time, he and three other hunters went into the woods to pack out a six-point bull elk they had shot down.

“On arriving where the elk was supposed to be lying, we found the entrails only, and in the snow coming to and leading away from the area was a large set of tracks,” Kinney wrote in his 1980 retelling.

At first, the men guessed a large bear had eaten the elk—but the tracks appeared in sets of two, and bears don’t tend to walk on their hind legs.

“Whatever it was had picked up the elk whole and clear off the ground,” Kinney continued.

Industry observers note that the men followed the tracks for about a half-mile and, according to Kinney’s account, came across a felled log measuring three feet in diameter. They could see where the elk’s horns had been dragged across the snow and over the timber.

“At this point we held a conference and decided that we were not particularly anxious to catch up to whatever it was,” Kinney wrote. “Though we didn’t confess it at this time, in later conversations we found out that we all had a very uneasy feeling and could feel chills running up and down our backs.”.

The following year, another person, W.W. Schmitz, was hunting in a nearby area called Bills Creek when he found the same type of tracks, smelled a similarly strong odor, and also heard grunting noises.

According to recent reports, kinney’s story is one of three historical files the U.S. Forest Service (USFS) released on October 15, describing Bigfoot encounters in Washington, Oregon, and Montana. In a world rife with sasquatch hunters and Bigfoot believers, the newly unveiled reports are shedding light on a long-held conspiracy theory. The public can access the files through the USFS website.

About 70 miles away in Plains, Montana, locals described an equally bizarre Bigfoot encounter in a January 15, 2003, article published by the Clark Fork Valley Press. According to the story, around January 3, hundreds of large, human-like footprints appeared in fresh snow outside Jim Rohweder’s sawmill shop. His neighbor, Bob Mesing, reportedly measured the prints at 16.5 inches long and 8.5 inches wide, with visible toes, an arch, and a stride that spanned about five feet.

Mesing and Dean Peterson, a former Kalispell policeman, followed the tracks about 100 yards into the woods, where the trail eventually faded.

Recent findings suggest that “I don’t know if I believe in the Bigfoot theory, but the tracks were right there,” Mesing reportedly said. “What else could it be?”.

According to the Clark Fork Valley Press, the men did not contact authorities but had planned to give a sample to wildlife officials.

In a recent development, mesing remarked he believed the visitor walked up to the shop and turned back, startled by a motion-sensor light. Peterson reportedly added that his dog barked around 3:40 A.M. that day, but he saw nothing.

Rohweder’s wife, Gladys, claimed to have seen a hairy, two-legged creature when the couple first moved to the area.

According to recent reports, A June 16, 1982, account published by the U.S. Department of Agriculture took place in Umatilla National Forest, which hugs the southern border of Washington State and northern Oregon. In it, wildlife biologist Rodney Johnson reports that the agency asked him to investigate three sets of Bigfoot tracks and record the details of his trips.

The tracks were recorded in three locations of the Mill Creek Watershed, a 36.5-mile tributary of the Walla Walla River about 262 miles from Portland.

Site A contained six identifiable tracks going down a muddy trail for about 25 feet. Similar tracks were also seen at a second location near a bog, Site B, this time 18 prints spanning about 40 feet.

Johnson took photos and plaster casts of both sites, but skipped the third, citing terrain challenges.

According to recent reports, but was it actually Bigfoot? Likely not.

“It is my opinion that the tracks observed at Site ‘A’ and Site ‘B’ along Low Creek were made by something other than a 300-pound-plus two-footed being,” Johnson wrote.

When a two-legged being walks, its stride changes with gradient—shorter uphill and longer downhill. According to Johnson, the distance between the tracks remained a consistent four feet and did not change with the slope. The tracks also had a clear beginning and end, rather than following an existing trail. Furthermore, Johnson added that the imprints did not resemble an authentic print—for example, a human footprint demonstrates a distinct significant toe pad and typically exhibits heel slippage on a steep gradient.

What’s more, at certain points, it appeared that pine needles had been intentionally brushed aside before the track was made.

Sources indicate that “It was obvious that the litter had been displaced sideways from the track area in an unnatural manner,” Johnson wrote. “If the tracks were faked, someone went to a great deal of trouble and detail.”.
