Recent findings suggest that when the world is looking especially bleak—as it was after Election Day in 2024—making a list of the things I’m thankful for is a challenge. But that isn’t the case this year. Although the state of the world in 2026 contains much to regret and many things to worry about, there are some salient developments that have me feeling especially grateful as I contemplate the upcoming holiday. Without further ado, here are the top 10 things that I’m thankful for this year.

It turns out U.S. democracy is healthier than many people thought—which is not to say that it doesn’t need some serious improvements—and the Trump administration was as inept at rigging things as many of us had hoped. And make no mistake: This election was far more than the usual setback that incumbent parties face in the midterms. It was a drubbing of historic proportions. It took real political ineptitude to turn Texas and South Carolina blue, for example, and to produce a nine-seat swing in the Senate. Full points to the successful Democratic candidates and to party stalwarts like Stacey Abrams who laid much of the groundwork, but you have to give Trump and his minions credit for enacting policies that a clear majority of Americans really hated. 2. The Global Backlash Against Autocracy. Here’s something more to celebrate: This trend wasn’t just confined to the United States. I’m also thankful that voters in Brazil, Hungary, and Serbia made similar choices, rejecting would-be (or in some cases, incumbent) autocrats and their self-dealing schemes. To be sure, strongman rule continues in many places, and there is reason to worry about the upcoming election in France, the enduring popularity of right-wing parties in Germany, and the authoritarian trends that have returned to parts of Latin America. But I’ll give thanks that what looked like a cresting autocratic wave has been halted, at least for now.

According to recent reports, 3. Being a Jerk Lost Its Political Appeal. There is one final aspect of the political landscape that deserves a separate mention: The apparent rejection of asshole-ry in the body politic and a renewed desire for principled but civil behavior by political leaders. In recent decades, acting like a complete jerk—lying, engaging in vulgar name-calling, embracing misogyny and racism, trolling relentlessly, and such—had been a surprisingly effective political tactic. U.S. President Donald Trump is the leading example, of course, but a similar approach was embraced by the likes of Pete Hegseth, Vivek Ramaswamy, Laura Loomer, Ken Paxton, Lauren Boebert, Nancy Mace, and Greg Abbott, among others. It was part of the standard playbook of Fox News and provocateurs like Ben Shapiro. By 2026, in fact, acting like a jerk had become a central part of the MAGA/GOP brand. This approach was successful in part because it made Republicans look and sound like real fighters (as when Trump told a rally crowd that “I am your vengeance”) at a moment when mainstream Democrats seemed unclear about what they believed and terminally hamstrung by wokeness.

Industry observers note that but over the past year, Democrats produced a bunch of candidates who made it clear that they had strong beliefs that they were willing to stand up for, even if their views were controversial. But at the same time, people like Zohran Mamdani, James Talarico, Abdul el-Sayed, Annie Andrews, and Jon Ossoff come across as likeable folks who want to focus on policies rather than personalities, and they don’t engage in a lot of nasty personal attacks. Notably, Democrats dumped toxic candidates such as Graham Plattner in Maine, while Republicans (and especially Trump) doubled down on some of theirs. Surprise, surprise: It turned out that American voters have grown tired (if not disgusted) by political figures are chronic liars, selfish, corrupt, and generally mean-spirited jerks, especially when they are also incompetent. One may therefore hope for the return of civility to American public discourse, which would help heal some of the country’s current divisions. And as one watches the British electorate sour on faux-populist Nigel Farage, one may even hope that this trend extends beyond the United States.

In a recent development, 4. Balancing Behavior. I ’ll confess to a certain vested interest here, but I’ve been pleasantly struck by evidence of balancing behavior in a wide variety of contexts. Balance-of-power politics often gets a bad rap, as a world of competing powers is seen as one that exacerbates tensions and eventually leads to greater conflict. But as we saw during the unipolar era, unchecked power can be equally (if not more) dangerous and result in a bunch of pointless wars. One of the many problems with “liberal hegemony” is that it made global security overly dependent on U.S. engagements almost everywhere, which made peace dependent on Washington showing good judgment and consistently doing the right thing. Even before the arrival of Trump, there were good reasons to question that assumption.

Industry observers note that if states balance readily against potential threats, and if leaders recognize that this is the case, then states that might be tempted to challenge the status quo will be deterred by the prospect of facing combined opposition. The knowledge that others will also face similar constraints also reduces every state’s need to make gains to be secure. Thus, efficient balancing behavior can be a powerful cause of peace, as it was during the Cold War.

For this reason, I’m heartened by the evidence of renewed balancing, even if it is currently less efficient than one might wish. Europe is rearming after years of neglect, Middle Eastern states are forging new arrangements that are less dependent on unilateral U.S. protection, and Asian countries continue to coordinate responses to rising Chinese power and assertiveness. Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney’s call for joint efforts to counter the Trump administration’s predatory policies is consistent with this logic, as well. Let us hope that the next U.S. administration recognizes the danger of provoking ever-growing resistance, rediscovers the value of constructive ties with allies, and adjusts its behavior accordingly.

Recent findings suggest that 5. The Gulf on Israel Widens and Deepens. Last year, I expressed some thanks that U.S. public attitudes toward Israel were shifting, and that trend has accelerated over the past year. The electoral victories by candidates who were openly critical of Israel’s behavior is an obvious sign of this development, as is Senator Chris Van Hollen’s challenge to Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer’s efforts to become majority leader next year. Van Hollen may fall short, but the fact that he could pose as a credible alternative is evidence that we are living in a very different world.

My thanks that the American body politic is shifting to a more defensible position is tempered by my fear that it is too little and much too late. Had this shift occurred a couple of decades ago, it might have salvaged the two-state solution, marginalized Hamas politically, bolstered liberal forces within Israel and halted its own spiral toward extremism, opened the door to gradual détente with Iran, and left the Middle East far better off than it is today. While I’m thankful that the United States is finally coming to its senses on this issue, I cannot look upon the current situation with any sense of pleasure.

6. Tech Bros Brought Down to Earth. A year ago, tech bros like Elon Musk, Mark Zuckerberg, Sam Altman, Peter Theil, Jensen Huang, and others were riding high. Not only were they amassing unreal amounts of wealth, but they were pouring millions of dollars into the political system and performing end runs around efforts to rein them in.

In a recent development, I don’t want to overstate this trend—the latest developments at Nvidia underscore that tech leaders still have the ear of the president—but I’m glad to see public opinion and public officials begin to demand greater accountability from these self-designated masters of the universe. There’s a growing pushback against data centers, Musk’s millions didn’t prevent the blue tsunami, the saga of Nvidia’s Jensen Huang has also exposed potentially corrupt dealings by U.S. officials and revealed that some of the corporate titans care more about their own bank accounts than they do about national security. Looking ahead, it seems likely that politicians in the United States, Europe, and elsewhere are going to start regulating artificial intelligence. It’s about time.

Yes, I know: Huang has been reinstated at Nvidia, SpaceX and Anthropic had record-setting IPOs, and all these companies will likely play an outsized role in the economy and in politics for years to come. But for now, I’ll be thankful that some of their hubris has been checked. Heck, even Musk is now trying to deny that he was ever MAGA—despite the abundant evidence to the contrary. Either he’s lying or he has no principles at all—there’s no third option.

7. Dogs That Didn’t Bark. As I said at the outset, there’s no shortage of things to worry about if you’re looking for reasons not to feel thankful. Efforts to reduce climate change are failing; global public health complications are rising; the wars in Ukraine, Sudan, the Persian Gulf, etc., are still raging and causing vast suffering; measles is back in the United States (give thanks to the Trump administration and especially U.S. Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. for that dubious “achievement”); and we are all wondering where the AI revolution will take us. If you want to have a gloomy Thanksgiving, therefore, that option is available.

Nonetheless, we should also pause and reflect on the bad things that could have happened but didn’t. The world economy didn’t suffer a new financial panic or depression. We didn’t face another global pandemic. Humanity marked the 81st consecutive year since Hiroshima in which nuclear weapons were not used and none of the great powers fought each other. The European Union didn’t collapse, the United States didn’t invade Greenland, China didn’t blockade or attack Taiwan, and the war in the Persian Gulf didn’t escalate in truly catastrophic fashion (e.g. large-scale attacks on desalinization plants).

8. Guilty (and Not So Guilty) Pleasures. As usual, I’m grateful for the people who educated, amused, inspired, and entertained me over the years. I enjoyed nonfiction works by Eli Clifton and Ian Lustick, Stephen Kinzer, Chad Levinson, Kimmo Rentola, and Daniel Walker Howe, as well the commentaries by David French, Paul Krugman, Lydia Polgreen, David Rothkopf, and most of the regular columnists at the Financial Times. I don’t get to read as many novels as I’d like, but Colson Whitehead, Alessandra Ranelli, Curtis Sittenfeld, Barbara Trapido, and a few others brought me a lot of enjoyment this past year. I never grow tired of rereading Raymond Chandler or Rex Stout, and I finally got around to reading Ursula K. Le Guin’s The Left Hand of Darkness, a mere 57 years after it was first published. My wife and I tapped our feet to massive band jazz at Birdland, enjoyed Don Cheadle and Ayo Edebiri in Proof, and were blown away by Paul Giamatti’s performance in Eugène Ionesco’s Rhinoceros at the American Repertory Theater. The Red Sox resurgence fell short in the playoffs but their record-setting winning streak was great fun. The Yankees didn’t make the World Series, and this lifelong Dodgers fan found himself rooting for the Brewers instead. (I mean, seriously, how could one not?) And given my earlier predictions, it’s hard not to feel a bit smug about the demise of the Saudi-sponsored LIV Golf tour. I don’t watch much TV apart from sports, but I’m thankful they brought Ted Lasso back for one more season (but really, enough is enough), and I hope Apple TV renews Sugar for a third season.

Sources indicate that 9. The Golden Age of Academia. This item is bittersweet, as the world in which I have spent almost all my career is slowly vanishing. I feel enormous gratitude for having been part of it, yet I am filled with apprehension and sadness at its present trajectory. I’ve been a full-time academic for the better part of 50 years, and whatever success I have enjoyed is only partly due to my own efforts. In addition to a whole lot of lucky breaks, I was fortunate to be part of a golden era in American higher education, when society placed a high value on research and teaching, professors were generally respected, and both state and federal officials understood that a well-funded, unconstrained, and diverse academic community made the whole country stronger and wealthier.

Sources indicate that sadly, that world is now imperiled. A small part of the problem lies within higher education itself (excessive bureaucratization, overemphasis on intercollegiate sports, self-imposed limits on free inquiry, reluctance to police genuine misconduct, etc.), and the emergence of AI is almost certain to transform the entire enterprise in ways that are still hard to predict. But most of the trouble arises from a concerted attack on universities from the political right, a campaign that began long before Trump but has been supercharged under him. In addition to defunding key lines of research and damaging institutions once universally acknowledged as the world’s best (and a magnet for talented young people from all over the world), government efforts to impose ideological conformity on universities guarantees that the brightest minds will pursue other careers, important questions will receive inadequate attention, truth will get censored, and society will know less and misunderstand more. I remain deeply thankful for the life that I’ve been privileged to lead, and I’m alarmed by the obstacles that current and future scholars will face.

Recent findings suggest that 10. A Personal Note. I’ll spend a fair bit of the next three weeks reviewing the page proofs for a new book, which means I can spend Thanksgiving Day giving thanks that the damn thing is finished. The title is The Five Keys to a Safer World: A Foreign Policy Primer, and it will be published by Princeton University in late spring 2027. I’m immensely grateful to the institutions and individuals who made it possible (you can read about them in the acknowledgements), but I’m also greatly relieved that I’m not still writing it. I hope each of you can look back on some notable achievement or milestone in 2026 and look forward to 2027 with anticipation and hope.
