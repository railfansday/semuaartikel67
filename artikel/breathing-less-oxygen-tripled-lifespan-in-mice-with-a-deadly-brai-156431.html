Breathing less oxygen tripled lifespan in mice with a deadly brain disorder

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, oxygen is essential for human survival and for most forms of life. Yet under certain conditions, it can become dangerously toxic, causing serious damage to the body.

In the brain, excessive oxygen levels have been linked to a rare and often fatal childhood disorder called 3-MGA, as well as Leigh syndrome (the most common pediatric mitochondrial disease), Parkinson's disease, and premature aging. Researchers at Gladstone Institutes are now investigating a promising approach that could potentially help treat these conditions by reducing the amount of oxygen available to the body, a strategy known as hypoxia therapy.

Industry observers note that for the past decade, Gladstone Investigator Isha Jain, PhD, has studied how oxygen levels influence health and disease. Her research has explored how the reduced oxygen availability at high altitudes can produce beneficial effects in conditions ranging from Leigh syndrome and diabetes to solid tumors.

Expanding the Potential of Hypoxia Therapy.

Jain and her colleagues wanted to determine whether lowering oxygen levels could also benefit people with other rare and common conditions involving mitochondrial dysfunction and neurological damage.

For a fresh study published in Nature Metabolism, the team partnered with James Shorter, PhD, professor of biochemistry and biophysics at the University of Pennsylvania, and Daniel Southworth, PhD, professor of biochemistry and biophysics at UC San Francisco.

The researchers discovered that a malfunctioning protein known as HTRA2 can cause oxygen to accumulate to dangerous levels in tissues. Remarkably, reducing the oxygen concentration in the air dramatically extended lifespan and improved brain function in mice experiencing motor neuron degeneration caused by defective HTRA2.

"This protein is linked to many other conditions, so our findings suggest that hypoxia therapy could be transformative for treating many neurological diseases," states Jain, who is also a core investigator at Arc Institute.

How Excess Oxygen Becomes Toxic to the Brain.

It has been revealed that most cells contain tiny structures called mitochondria, which use oxygen to generate the energy the body needs to function. One of the most important components of this energy production system is Complex 1, the largest molecular machine within the mitochondria's respiratory machinery.

"Every time we breathe, 90 percent of the oxygen we consume goes to our mitochondria," states Ankur Garg, PhD, postdoctoral fellow in Jain's lab and first author of the study. "But if Complex 1 malfunctions, the mitochondria can no longer burn off oxygen at normal rates.".

Industry observers note that when mitochondria lose their ability to consume oxygen efficiently, the unused oxygen can accumulate in surrounding tissues and eventually reach toxic levels. This process can contribute to the brain damage associated with certain mitochondrial and neurological disorders. The researchers suspected that reducing oxygen availability might help prevent some of this damage.

Identifying Genetic Causes of Oxygen Toxicity.

It has been revealed that to identify diseases that might respond to hypoxia therapy, the team revisited data from an earlier large experiment. They searched for genes whose absence impaired cell growth under normal oxygen conditions but allowed cells to grow normally when oxygen levels were reduced.

By comparing these results with a database of known genetic disorders, the researchers identified 75 genes directly associated with diseases that could potentially benefit from hypoxia therapy.

According to recent reports, among the most promising candidates was the gene encoding HTRA2. Further investigation revealed that HTRA2 works alongside another protein (CLPB) to maintain the structural integrity of Complex 1.

"Together, these two proteins act like a clean-up crew inside mitochondria, preventing the machinery from becoming clogged with clumps of misfolded proteins," says Jain.

According to recent reports, the team found that when HTRA2 and CLPB are absent or malfunctioning, this cellular maintenance system breaks down. As a result, a critical component of Complex 1 stops functioning properly.

Breathing Less Oxygen Triples Lifespan in Mice.

To determine whether reducing oxygen levels could provide therapeutic benefits in a living organism, the researchers conducted experiments on mice deficient in HTRA2.

Mice that breathed air containing less oxygen lived three times as long as those breathing normal atmospheric air, which contains approximately 21 percent oxygen. The treatment also reduced inflammation in the striatum, a region of the brain.

Industry observers note that "By showing these mice could be successfully treated with low oxygen, our study expands the potential of hypoxia therapy to a wide range of conditions that affect mitochondrial Complex 1, either directly or indirectly as in the case of HTRA2 deficiency," Garg says. "This will motivate a broader application of 'turning the oxygen dial' to conditions ranging from rare genetic diseases to common neurological conditions and beyond.".

Developing a Drug That Mimics Low Oxygen.

According to recent reports, although the experiments involved having mice breathe air with reduced oxygen concentrations, Jain and her colleagues are working toward a more practical approach. They are developing a drug called HypoxyStat that could potentially reproduce the therapeutic benefits of low oxygen through a pill or injection.

"Right now, no treatments are uniformly available for mitochondrial diseases, and hypoxia therapy offers the hope that we could treat not just one, but many of these genetic conditions," Jain notes. "We're working hard to make it a practical treatment for human patients in the clinic.".
