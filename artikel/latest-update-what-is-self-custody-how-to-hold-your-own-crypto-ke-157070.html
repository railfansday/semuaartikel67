Sources indicate that when you buy crypto on an exchange, the exchange usually holds it for you. Your account shows a balance, but the private keys that control the coins belong to the trading platform.

According to recent reports, the risk is that if an exchange collapses, is hacked or freezes withdrawals, its customers can lose access to their funds. The collapse of FTX in November 2022 left millions of consumers unable to withdraw, and turned the old crypto saying "not your keys, not your coins" into a rallying cry.

In a recent development, self-custody is the alternative: holding your crypto yourself, with no intermediary standing between you and your money. This guide explains what self-custody is, how it works, the tools available and the risks it brings.

Self-custody means you, and only you, control the private keys to your crypto.

On a blockchain, coins are not stored in a wallet in the way cash sits in a purse. They are recorded on a public ledger, and a private key, a long secret number, is what proves you have the right to spend them. Whoever holds the private key controls the coins.

It has been revealed that with a custodial service, such as a centralized exchange, the organization holds the keys on your behalf, and you rely on it to process your withdrawals. With a self-custodial, or non-custodial, wallet, the keys are generated and stored on your own device, and transactions only happen when you approve them.

According to recent reports, when you set up a self-custodial wallet, it generates a seed phrase, usually a list of 12 or 24 words. The seed phrase is a human-readable backup of your private keys: anyone who has it can recreate your wallet on another device and move your funds.

In a recent development, that makes the seed phrase the single most vital thing to protect. Most guides recommend writing it down on paper or stamping it into metal, storing it somewhere safe offline, and never typing it into a website, sharing it or saving it in a photo or cloud file.

Custodial services are easier to use. If you forget your password, you can reset it, and an exchange handles the security of its own systems. But you are trusting the company to stay solvent, keep your funds safe and let you withdraw when you want to.

Sources indicate that self-custody removes that counterparty risk, and means no firm can freeze your funds or block a transaction. The tradeoff is responsibility: there is no password reset. If you lose your seed phrase, or someone steals it, there is usually no way to get your crypto back.

Welshman James Howells says the keys to about 8,000 BTC are on a hard drive that was thrown out in 2013 and buried in a landfill in Newport, Wales.

Losing your keys is the most common risk, but not the only one.

In a recent development, hardware can have flaws. In July 2026, a firmware vulnerability in Coldcard, a Bitcoin-only air-gapped hardware wallet, let attackers reconstruct some people' seed phrases. Losses rose to nearly $114 million in Bitcoin within days, according to Galaxy Research. Even the most secure devices depend on their makers getting the code right.

Phishing and malware are a constant threat to software wallets, and scammers often pose as wallet support staff to trick users into handing over seed phrases.

In a recent development, holders also face physical danger. So-called "wrench attacks," in which criminals use violence to force victims to hand over crypto, have surged. Blockchain security firm CertiK verified 52 wrench attacks worldwide in the first half of 2026, up from 39 a year earlier, and Chainalysis mentioned such attacks were on pace for a record year.

The threat is one reason holders have pushed back against proposals to make them report large self-custody holdings to tax authorities, warning that such records could leak.

Recent findings suggest that regulators have long debated how to handle self-custodial, or "unhosted," wallets. In October 2026, the U.S. Treasury's Financial Crimes Enforcement Network withdrew a 2020 proposal that would have required banks and money services businesses to keep records on transactions with self-custodial wallets over $3,000 and report those over $10,000.

In France, the Senate rejected a proposal in November 2026 that would have required holders to declare self-custodied crypto worth more than €100,000.

Self-custody is starting to extend beyond humans. In 2026, MetaMask released Agent Wallet, a self-custodial wallet that lets AI agents execute on-chain trades within limits set by the user, while Coinbase introduced its own self-custodial wallets for AI agents.

Wallet makers are also working to make self-custody easier for newcomers, with features such as social logins and passkeys that reduce reliance on seed phrases. But the core principle remains the same: whoever controls the keys controls the crypto.
