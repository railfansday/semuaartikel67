High-Speed Nanoscale Brain Imaging Uncovers Hidden Synapses

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Researchers dramatically accelerated electron tomography to map large volumes of brain tissue at 15-nanometer 3D resolution. The breakthrough captures fine neural structures missed by conventional connectomes, including rapid-fire electrical synapses and uncharacterized protrusions where neurons appear to “nibble” neighboring glial cells.

In a recent development, to understand how billions of neurons orchestrate thoughts, memories, and movement, neuroscientists construct connectomes, detailed wiring diagrams mapping how brain cells connect and communicate. While macro-scale MRI scans chart major white-matter highways and standard electron microscopy traces individual chemical synapses, scaling up to large tissue volumes has historically forced a difficult compromise: sacrificing nanoscale structural detail for imaging speed.

As a result, even the most ambitious brain wiring maps have routinely missed ultra-fine cellular architectures, including electrical synapses (also called gap junctions), microscopic channels that enable direct, near-instantaneous electrical communication between adjacent neurons.

Now, in a study published November 19 in Nature Methods, researchers at Harvard Medical School (HMS) and the University of California San Diego have overcome that bottleneck by supercharging electron tomography—a 3D imaging technique that reconstructs nanoscale architecture by tilting tissue samples and capturing views from multiple angles.

Led by co-senior authors Dr. Wei-Chung Allen Lee (associate professor of neurobiology at HMS and associate professor of neurology at Boston Children’s Hospital) and Dr. Mark Ellisman (UC San Diego), alongside co-first authors Dr. Aaron T. Kuan and Dr. Sébastien Phan, the team demonstrated that large blocks of brain tissue can be mapped in exquisite 3D nanoscale detail without prohibitive imaging times.

In a recent development, conventional electron tomography is painstakingly slow because it typically requires capturing hundreds of tilted images across razor-thin tissue slices. By refining image acquisition and computational reconstruction, Lee, Ellisman, and colleagues discovered they could cut the required number of images by roughly 50-fold while imaging tissue sections 20 times thicker than standard electron microscopy protocols, preserving an extraordinary 3D resolution of 15 nanometers or finer.

“We can now see brain structures that we haven’t been able to see before in connectomics data,” explained Lee. “We hope this work will help us understand how nervous systems are built; what changes with age; and what goes awry in neurodevelopmental, cognitive, and neurodegenerative disorders.”.

When the researchers applied their high-speed tomography pipeline to tissue from the mouse cerebellum, the 3D reconstructions revealed both long-sought neural connections and surprising cellular behaviors:.

“We’re seeing uncharacterized structures, suggesting that neurons are ‘nibbling’ their neighboring cells,” Lee said.

In addition, the researchers traced delicate nerve fibers continuously through the thick 3D tissue block right down to their exact synaptic contact points with target neurons.

While scaling this accelerated tomography workflow to map an entire mammalian brain will require automated tissue-handling robotics, the breakthrough bridges a critical resolution gap in modern connectomics—equipping neuroscientists to examine how fine electrical and glial-neuronal connections form in early life and break down in brain disease.

Why do traditional brain wiring maps (connectomes) miss certain structures?

Mapping large volumes of brain tissue at high speed usually forces scientists to sacrifice ultra-fine resolution. As a result, critical microscopic features, such as tiny electrical synapses (gap junctions) that connect neurons directly, often blur out or go undetected in large-scale datasets.

How did researchers speed up nanoscale 3D brain imaging?

The team optimized electron tomography so that it requires about 50 times fewer multi-angle images on tissue slices 20 times thicker than usual, while still maintaining a 3D resolution of 15 nanometers or finer.

What surprising newly introduced structures did they see in the brain tissue?

Alongside hard-to-see electrical synapses in the mouse cerebellum, the researchers discovered tiny protrusions between neurons and neighboring support cells (glia), suggesting that neurons physically “nibble” or take up material from adjacent cells.

Scalable electron tomography for connectomics.

We demonstrate limited-tilt, serial-section electron tomography (ET), which can non-destructively map brain circuits over large 3D volumes and provide high-resolution, supramolecular details within subvolumes of interest.

Here we show accelerated ET imaging of semi-thick sections ( >500 nm) from the mouse cerebellar cortex, with 3D resolution <15 nm and machine learning-based image augmentation, providing the capacity to resolve key features of neuronal circuits including chemical synapses, endocytic structures and gap junctions.

Furthermore, we systematically assess how imaging parameters affect image quality and speed to enable connectomic-scale projects.
