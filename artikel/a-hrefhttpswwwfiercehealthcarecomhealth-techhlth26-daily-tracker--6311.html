It has been revealed that the Fierce Healthcare team is on the ground at HLTH 2026. Stick with this news tracker to catch all the highlights from the show floor, and click here for additional coverage from the conference.

Medicare Director Chris Klomp said the administration is focused on bringing in top tech talent as it looks to make the massive, but aging, government insurance system work better.

Klomp spoke on a panel Monday morning at HLTH, where he discussed the challenges in harvesting the data necessary to transform Medicare, Medicaid and other key government programs. Klomp was recently elevated to serve as a senior adviser to Department of Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy, Jr., and is a rising star in the White House.

Industry observers note that he mentioned that technology can play a critical role in accelerate processes that have been historically inefficient and time consuming. At the Food and Drug Administration, for example, tech is hastening the process of developing drugs and making approvals, a timeline that previously taken a decade or more.

But embracing technology in this way isn't compromising safety for these medications, Klomp said.

In addition, he mentioned the administration is leaning on the personal nature of healthcare to bring in workers with the tech know-how necessary to drive critical advancements and change.

"Most people don't care about healthcare unless you work in it," he mentioned. "People also start to care about healthcare when you lose your health, or someone you love loses their health. And than it promptly becomes the most notable thing to you.".

"We're using that as an opportunity to drive requirement, drive amazing talent," Klomp said.

Houston Methodist unveiled Monday it has expanded its advanced inpatient monitoring care to include rural hospitals throughout Kansas.

Participating hospitals will provide patients with Medtronic’s BioButton, which continuously monitors vital signs, while the Houston Methodist remote monitoring team will review the data and work with bedside nurses.

The vital monitoring devices are used at all eight acute-care hospitals in the Houston Methodist system, across more than 2,500 inpatient beds, according to the Nov. 11 announcement.

It has been revealed that “Innovation is most meaningful when we identify patient needs, take what works or doesn't work and find new ways to extend the positive impact,” stated Sara Loewy, Houston Methodist virtual care vice president, in a statement. “What we’ve built in Houston has the potential to reach much further. By thoughtfully extending this model into new care settings, our goal with this program is to demonstrate how our system’s existing technology and clinical expertise can strengthen access and improve outcomes in rural communities.”.

Loewy added the expansion is an opportunity to “explore how the capabilities of a major health system can be shared across geographic boundaries.”.

While 72% of health tech startups lead with “AI-powered” in their descriptors, zero surveyed venture capitalists in a new report from HLTH and SolComms remarked the phrase increased their confidence.

Independently surveyed health tech reporters also ranked “AI-powered” as one of the most overused phrases in pitching–in addition to “revolutionizing/transforming” and “disrupting.”.

Alongside these survey responses, researchers compared–and scored–the messaging of 72 U.S.-based, venture-backed health tech startups at series A to series C for the “Healthtech Attention Economy Report.” Startups’ messaging across their website and social media was scored on four main dimensions: clarity, differentiation, proof and policy awareness.

SolComms Vice President of HealthTech Isabella Morreale said in a statement the companies that will get attention within the next year “are leading with proof, not adjectives.”.

Recent findings suggest that “That means speaking to clinical data, measurable outcomes, and validation from third parties,” Morreale noted. “Founder-led storytelling will also continue to stand out. Among the data, one common through-thread for investor and reporter interest was founders with a real point of view on the industry.”.

Morreale continued, “Reporters don't need another manufacturer description; they need a source who can help them explain what's happening in healthcare.”.

Less than half (42.2%) of patients say communications from healthcare providers “often” or “always” feel relevant to their needs, a latest report from Lirio found.

Analysts at the behavioral science and AI-powered behavioral intelligence platform polled 1,500 adults across the U.S. for the survey. While 58% of respondents report communications not consistently feeling relevant to their needs, 55.6% rate overall communication experiences with healthcare organizations as “good” or “excellent.”.

When asked why healthcare communications are ignored altogether, respondents report generic or irrelevant content (24.4%), too many similar messages (21.8%) and confusing, jargon-filled messages (18.8%) as top reasons.

Nearly half of respondents (47.9%) report the potential value of more personalized, better-timed messages as an “8, 9 or 10” for how much it could help them make better care decisions and stay engaged.

Lirio Chief Behavioral Officer Amy Bucher, Ph.D., said in a statement patients “are not rejecting healthcare communications outright.”.

According to recent reports, “They are telling us, in specific and measurable terms, that the system is talking at them instead of to them,” Bucher stated. "The instinct in healthcare has long been to solve engagement with more outreach - more emails, more reminders, more portals. This data demonstrates that volume is not the problem. What is missing is relevance — content and timing designed for where a specific patient is in their own health journey, rather than a single message broadcast to an entire population.”.

Bucher added the healthcare organizations that close the communication gap will be “the ones that use behavioral data to determine the right message, the right channel, and the right moment for each individual patient, which is the model Lirio was built to deliver.".

Community-based specialty palliative care provider Vynca is expanding its reach into four more states.

Patients will now be able to access Vynca’s services in Georgia, Hawaii, Upcoming Jersey and Texas. Vynca is one of the first practices approved to provide services under New Jersey’s Community-Based Palliative Care Medicaid benefit, released in April, according to the Nov. 16 announcement.

Founded in 2013, the company’s hybrid care model combines in-home visits with virtual clinical support and predictive technology, and executives say it has shown 43% fewer emergency department visits and 52% less hospital stays within six months of enrollment.

According to recent reports, “Health plan and government program leaders understand the value of community-based palliative care for people with serious illness,” remarked Darren Schulte, M.D., Vynca CEO, in a statement. “Our expansion in four key states reflects growing momentum to expand specialty outpatient palliative care outside of hospices. The key is to broaden access and convenience for the entire eligible population by offering home-based options. Too often, patients are limited to seeking palliative care when available in the community, at hospital-based clinics and facilities.”.
