📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

OpenAI Group PBC, io Products, Chang Liu and Tang Yew Tan filed counterclaims against Apple on November 27, 2026, in California federal court, alleging that Apple misappropriated confidential hardware designs and manufacturing records. The accompanying answer denies Apple’s trade-secret and contract claims, while the counterclaims contain 12 causes of action.

Apple began the underlying case in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California on July 10, 2026, alleging trade-secret misappropriation and breaches of Intellectual Property Agreements. The defendants admit Liu’s and Tan’s employment histories and current OpenAI roles but deny stealing Apple’s trade secrets.

According to recent reports, the central new allegation concerns an operations employee identified only as Employee X, who OpenAI says joined from Apple on December 2, 2025. He initially handled camera and optics quality before his responsibilities expanded to enclosures, finishing, inspection and manufacturing quality; his last day at OpenAI was June 2, 2026.

Industry observers note that the counterclaims describe transfers to personal email accounts and phone numbers, including a 76-page supplier measurement and inspection procedure sent on February 5, 2026. The materials allegedly included design geometry, manufacturing measurements, finishing criteria, supplier-performance results and inspection software. On June 1, 2026, Employee X sent six archives to a personal Singapore number, including structure.zip and CPK_GRR.zip, containing unreleased-device geometry, enclosure measurements, process-capability records and gauge analyses.

Sources indicate that openAI alleges that Employee X rejoined Apple and, on information and belief, briefed senior Apple Asia-Pacific Operations personnel in July 2026 on its confidential hardware work and unreleased-product roadmap at Apple’s request. OpenAI sent Apple a cease-and-desist letter on October 9, 2026, requesting an investigation, preservation, containment and an accounting.

It has been revealed that openAI notes the records’ value comes from specific engineering and production work rather than ordinary supplier identities: access to designs, test results and failure analyses could let a competitor avoid unsuccessful trials, qualify manufacturing methods sooner and anticipate production problems. The federal claim targets Apple’s alleged knowing acquisition, use or disclosure of that information through Employee X.

Sources indicate that the filing separately alleges that Apple’s supply-management personnel pressured shared vendors to identify OpenAI projects and the names, locations and assignments of workers with Apple experience. Suppliers feared losing Apple allocations if they supported OpenAI. For many specialized functions, OpenAI has only one or two qualified suppliers, making interrupted capacity difficult to replace promptly.

It has been revealed that one finishing supplier allegedly moved OpenAI’s work on a general-purpose anodizing line, a shared metal-finishing process, to a separate overnight shift after Apple objected to OpenAI devices being processed in the same area or at the same time as Apple work. The change increased costs for the supplier and OpenAI. A second manufacturer allegedly withdrew from planned work because of threats from Apple, forcing OpenAI to revamp operations, staffing and contracting. The interference claim concerns actual disruption of existing purchasing and manufacturing commitments, rather than merely lost future orders.

Sources indicate that the counterclaims also challenge Apple’s broad contractual definition of proprietary information and a one-year restriction on soliciting Apple employees or contractors after departure. Liu and Tan argue that California’s protection against contracts restraining lawful work prevents those terms from reaching general skills, public information or lawful recruiting.

OpenAI indicates 400 Apple employees have left for OpenAI and that Apple’s account and enforcement practices deter further hiring. Tan, who worked at Apple for approximately 24 years and is now OpenAI’s Chief Hardware Officer for Consumer Products, seeks declarations that his agreement cannot bar use of public information, general skills or lawful recruiting.

Liu alleges that Apple encouraged employees to mix work files and communications with personal iCloud and iMessage accounts, then used the resulting access after departures. After leaving Apple on January 22, 2026, he allowed his personal iCloud account to remain signed in on a returned Apple device solely so his former manager could retrieve Apple work files. His permission did not extend to review of his personal data.

The filing says Apple and forensic consultant Charles River Associates reviewed Liu’s personal iMessages and other materials, including post-employment communications with non-Apple third parties. Those allegations support an invasion-of-privacy claim and claims under federal and California computer-access laws. They also underpin OpenAI’s unfair-competition claim that the prospect of post-departure monitoring deters engineers from moving to a competitor.

Industry observers note that the counterclaim plaintiffs request compensatory damages where permitted, reasonable royalties or statutory relief for trade-secret misappropriation, restitution, attorneys’ fees and narrowly tailored injunctions against established ongoing or threatened unlawful conduct. They also demand a jury trial.

Industry observers note that the counterclaims broaden the case beyond files retained during employee departures. They put the infrastructure behind OpenAI’s unreleased hardware effort—supplier capacity, recruiting and the boundary between work and personal accounts—into the same judicial examination of whether either firm unlawfully obtained the other’s information.
