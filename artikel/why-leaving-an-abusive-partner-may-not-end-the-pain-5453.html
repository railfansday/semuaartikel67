Why Leaving an Abusive Partner May Not End the Pain

It has been revealed that many people have left an abusive relationship feeling heartbroken and relieved at the same time. Whether the abuse was emotional, physical, or both, many victims experience a temporary sense of relief, realizing their mental health is no longer linked to a partner’s toxic behavior. Or is it? Because recognizing symptoms helps identify appropriate treatment, it is helpful to understand the potential fallout from a maladaptive relationship, even after the fact.

Shahida Arabi (2023) investigated the impact of partner narcissism and psychopathy on posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptomology. She explains that both dark triad traits share numerous common traits that may cause aggression within intimate partnerships, including grandiosity, manipulation, lack of empathy, and exploitation. Such personalities also engage in manipulative behavior, designed to keep partners insecure and emotionally unsteady. They shower romantic prospects with excessive attention and flattery commonly referred to as “love bombing,” shut down conversations or "stonewall," and “gaslight” partners by questioning their memory, reality, or perception.

Describing the characteristics of two narcissism subtypes, Arabi explains that grandiose narcissism is linked with higher amounts of self-esteem and self-confidence, while vulnerable narcissism is associated with fearfulness and hypersensitivity. Regarding the potential for victimization through aggression, Arabi recognizes the strong association between narcissistic and antisocial personality traits and perpetrating intimate partner violence, and the impact of intimate partner violence on the victim, including mental health problems and PTSD.

Of the dark personality traits, one type of narcissism may be uniquely damaging over time. Studying 1,294 subjects romantically involved with individuals expressing a high degree of narcissistic or psychopathic traits, Arabi found that grandiose and vulnerable narcissism were significant predictors of PTSD symptomology for ex-partners who had exited the relationship, even after factoring in the impact of previous abuse and manipulation.

It has been revealed that arabi found that grandiose narcissism expressed by a partner was the strongest predictor of PTSD, more so than frequency of abuse, childhood abuse, or physical abuse. It was also a stronger predictor than vulnerable narcissism or psychopathy, which Arabi opines may be due to the fact that grandiose narcissism is linked with a higher degree of self-aggrandizement, which could produce elevated self-blame and intrusive thoughts in victims who are abused by a more confident and ruthless person who has such traits.

Considering the frequency with which we learn about and discuss the potential impact of dark personalities on romantic relationships, the goal is to ferret out such characteristics sooner rather than later. And although we gain more information as we spend time with someone over time, many people detect cues as early as a first date.

Sources indicate that detecting grandiose narcissism involves more than recognizing elevated self-esteem and self-confidence, which are not inherently negative characteristics on their own. Instead, look past the confident veneer to the cunning, venomous behaviors emerging over time as dark personalities begin to reveal their true colors.

Be attentive to subtle tactics of emotional manipulation, consider whether flattery is authentic and appropriate, and recognize that healthy romantic attention makes you feel desirable, but also valuable, cared for, and comfortable. Getting to know a potential paramour at a pace that is slow and steady affords time to decide if your new love interest genuinely possesses the traits of a good partner and will be a good fit for you.
