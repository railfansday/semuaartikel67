Sources indicate that companies are giving AI a bigger role in decisions, but that does not mean their leaders find decisions easier. In a survey published by Körber, 76% of senior decision-makers stated decision-making had become more complex. The same share reported allowing AI to play a greater role. The answers describe two trends happening at the same time; they do not establish that one caused the other.

Recent findings suggest that the survey included 2,000 senior executives, split evenly between the United States and Germany. Körber states respondents worked for organizations with annual revenue above $250 million and at least 250 employees, and fieldwork ran from August 27 to September 8, 2026. That makes the findings a look at decision-making inside larger organizations in two markets, not a representative measure of every employer or of small businesses.

The accompanying release notes 74% of respondents reported that information volume can get in the way of decisions, while 75% mentioned decisions stall when critical information is not available at the right time. Having more data and having the right data when a decision is due are different problems.

Recent findings suggest that consider a manager who needs to decide whether to delay a shipment. Sales, warehouse operations and finance may have different records for the same order, all updated at different times. An AI assistant can summarize those records rapidly, but a faster summary does not establish which number is current or who can approve an exception. Unless the team can check the sources and resolve conflicting records, the tool can move the work of reconciliation from one person to another rather than eliminate it.

The survey also found that 73% of respondents were uncomfortable letting technology make the final decision. That helps explain why businesses may want AI in the workflow without giving it control of the outcome. Drafting an explanation, gathering supporting documents and making a decision that commits the company are separate jobs. The cost of an error differs for each.

Sources indicate that among respondents whose organizations described their transformation as enterprise-wide, 63.8% reported strong organizational performance, compared with 41.6% of those at the pilot stage. Future-readiness confidence was 66.4% and 46.5%, respectively. Prepares to invest significantly in AI decision support were almost identical: 59% for the enterprise-wide group, versus 59.4% for the pilot group.

Sources indicate that those are self-reported group differences, not measured returns from a controlled rollout. Organizations that can implement technology across departments may already have stronger data, more resources or better operating processes. The publication does not establish that expanding a pilot causes performance to improve. It is better read as a prompt to ask what mature implementations do differently and which of those practices a particular team can test.

The practical question for an operator is where a tool improves the work between a question and a decision. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework offers a way to formalize responsibilities and decide what to measure in context. Teams can pick a recurring decision and compare how long it takes to obtain the necessary evidence, how often a recommendation must be corrected and whether an approver can explain what they relied on.

Tracking these measures separately can reveal a trade-off that a single adoption number misses. A tool may produce reports faster while increasing the time spent checking them, or it may let people spend less time gathering facts and more time evaluating options. For the executives surveyed, the challenge is not simply making AI more available. It is defining the role it plays when information is incomplete and someone still has to own the decision.
