Recent findings suggest that many have classified the success of “Heated Rivalry” as an enigma, a phenomenon that burst out of Crave original containment and superseded even the most optimistic expectations. In early 2026, episodes averaged more than 10.6 million viewers in the U.S. alone. The show became the most-watched scripted title acquired in HBO Max history and Bell Media’s most successful original streaming series.

There is a great deal of “right place, right time” to the success of the show, but is it possible “Heated Rivalry” wasn’t just the stars aligning, but a well-timed adaptation with all the right components that met an audience ready to buy in? With all its success, you’d think there are plenty of lessons to be learned from the show, but are the studios even listening? Based on the second book in Rachel Reid’s series “Game Changers,” “Heated Rivalry” follows competitive ice hockey rivals, curly-haired Russian Ilya Rozanov (Connor Storrie) and freckled Canadian Shane Hollander (Hudson Williams). Over six episodes spanning nearly a decade, the show follows the pair’s complex relationship as they compete as league rivals while beginning an adrenaline-pumping affair that proves more compelling than Stanley Cup wins.

The show premiered in November 2025 to a modest audience, predominantly Reid’s readers, before word-of-mouth promotion boosted Jacob Tierney’s show onto a global stage.

First and foremost, “Heated Rivalry” clearly indicates an appetite for LGBTQ+ stories. The series has been celebrated for its unabashed portrayal of queer desire between two men in an environment of deeply ingrained homophobia and rigid expectations of masculinity.

While Storrie and Williams may beautifully perform “Heated Rivalry”’s depiction of queerness, the show’s heart is Tierney, a gay creator, writer, director, and executive producer whose brushstrokes eloquently articulate the fear, excitement, and intimacy of burgeoning queer infatuation. Tierney’s show is the product of a creator fulfilling their vision, uninterrupted by studio censorship or executive meddling.

Ultimately, the content of “Heated Rivalry” isn’t revolutionary, but its far-reaching viewership is: it reveals the profound longing for queer storytelling in the romance genre.

It’s telling then that, in the wake of “Heated Rivalry,” a wave of upcoming shows have latched onto hockey rather than LGBTQ+ characters. Since “Heated Rivalry” premiered one year ago, Prime Video’s adaptation of Elle Kennedy’s college hockey romance series “Off Campus” aired its first season and was renewed for a second season before the series premiere, while Netflix greenlit an adaptation of Hannah Grace’s “Icebreaker” novels. (The streamer is also releasing the YA hockey drama “The Sticks.”).

This doesn’t speak to the merit of each production, which arrive with their own fanbases and selling points, but to a wider complaint about how to capitalize on a moment. But there is no doubt that the ramped-up attention on these hockey shows is beholden to the success of “Heated Rivalry.” Yet, what has carried over is the game on the ice, not the potential of queer romance — especially eyebrow-raising when GLAAD reported LGBTQ+ characters on TV have dropped 25 percent over the past year.

Furthermore, it’s not just the TV industry that has failed to recognize this moment truly; even the National Hockey League has shown very little love for the hit show, despite “Heated Rivalry” undoubtedly contributing toward the 1.8 million viewers who tuned in to ice hockey for the first time last season, as ESPN reported of the National Hockey League’s research.

Another facet of the show’s success is casting unknowns, something the James Bond team continue to veto. Arriving on screen unburdened by the baggage of previous recognizable roles, Storrie and Williams were blank canvases on which to paint their characters and their burgeoning fling-turned-romance. There’s a sense of exciting discovery, with audiences investing in a character while uncovering a newcomer’s talent. For instance, many said they believed Storrie was a Russian man until the charming press tour revealed he grew up in Texas.

Sources indicate that it is apparent that bringing new faces into the fold is deemed unfavourable, with no established star power to lure in fans. Despite this concern, casting discussions also face pushback, as we repeatedly see the same batch of actors time and time again, to the point that their roles start to blend. With its casting decisions, “Heated Rivalry” launched the careers of its central duo, Storrie and Williams, swiftly becoming two of the most exciting rising stars of the moment.

It has been revealed that the pair are a prime example of the stardom potential that comes with investing in and trusting talent. The fine acting chops of Storrie and Williams are certainly part of the reason “Heated Rivalry” has been successful; while they discovered Ilya and Shane, viewers also discovered Connor and Hudson, a journey that wouldn’t be as impactful if they had notable pre-existing credits.

According to recent reports, “Heated Rivalry” also appears in a highly populated sea of book-to-screen romance projects, a competitive arena where adaptations can split viewers for disloyalty to the source material. “Heated Rivalry” hit a sweet spot.

Sources indicate that tierney is true to the essence of Reid’s book, not trying to outrun the words on the page. Additionally, Tierney doesn’t shy away from the romance genre, and you don’t need to understand the rules of hockey to latch onto the amorous bond of the two hockey captains. The show’s success in this regard could be viewed as a template (fans of the book love it for a reason, an overhaul is widely unnecessary). Yet a string of recent popular book adaptations has been criticised for turning their backs on the books, from watering down sex scenes in “People We Meet on Vacation” to major plot alterations in “Every Year After”.

Though “Heated Rivalry” has left its mark — celebrated for LGBTQ+ representation, casting unknowns and its elevated adaptation — has the show changed anything?

Taking a risk on bold strokes seems worth it when a show from a Canadian streamer goes on to make over $1.4 million from a limited theatrical release across 945 movie theaters in early November screenings. The show’s cultural relevance and staying power are a breakthrough, but a year after its premiere, it seems very little has been learned so far.
