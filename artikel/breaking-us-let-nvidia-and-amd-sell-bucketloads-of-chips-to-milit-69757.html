Breaking: US let Nvidia and AMD sell bucketloads of chips to military-linked Chinese companies

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Not many of the deals went through, but Chinese clouds no longer care about importing GPUs.

The US Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) allowed Nvidia and AMD to sell hundreds of thousands of advanced GPUs to entities that the Department of War classifies as “Chinese military companies” that help Beijing to build its military.

That news comes courtesy of Brian Mast (R-FL), chair of the House Foreign Affairs Committee, who asked BIS to provide information about NVIDIA’s and AMD’s applications for the export licenses required to sell advanced AI chips to China.

The congressman on Thursday revealed requests to sell 3.4 million AI chips to China, and that BIS approved the sale of 879,000 items.

BIS refused to authorize some giant deals for Nvidia, which applied to sell 610,000 chips to Alibaba and 508,000 to TikTok owner Bytedance.

But the agency allowed many other sales, including to Alibaba and Tencent.

Mast points out that both of those companies are on the Department of War’s List of Chinese Military Companies, the designation it uses for entities deemed to be a “military-civil fusion contributor to the Chinese defense industrial base.” That’s a polite way of saying that Washington thinks China relies on these companies to operate and improve its military, and therefore to challenge America’s national security.

Advanced chips are essential when designing nuclear weapons, stealthy submarines, or advanced aircraft.

Those applications are the core reason the US bans exports of advanced GPUs to China, so that the People’s Liberation Army can’t develop advanced weapons.

The congressman also claims that some of Nvidia’s applications were attempts to sell its wares to “chip-smuggling fronts to facilitate their sales of advanced AI chips to Chinese companies.” The Latest York Times reports that some of those front entities are linked to Megaspeed, the outfit fronted by suspected Chinese intelligence asset Alice Huang, whose intimate relationship with former Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang was a key factor in his resignation.

Megaspeed is accused of what the US calls “diversion” – claiming to buy chips for one reason while intending to either ship them to China or rent them exclusively to Chinese companies in ways that breach the spirit of export restrictions if not the letter of the law.

One of the documents Mast shared says “Every Nvidia and AMD application stated that the proposed transfer posed a ‘low’ risk of diversion.”.

Mast’s point therefore appears to be that the BIS has authorized sales that go against the clear intent of established policy, and the national interest, and that Nvidia and AMD may have been a little too optimistic in their assessment of diversion risk.

Earlier this week, Washington adopted a new policy that outlaws allowing remote access to advanced AI chips, in the hope it would stop diversion and dent China’s AI ambitions.

Two Chinese tech giants, Tencent and Alibaba, have signaled the policy had immediate impact.

Chinese web giant Tencent on Wednesday mentioned that fresh ban will reduce the quantity of advanced compute capacity available to the company and mean its efforts to train models will slow.

According to recent reports, alibaba offered similar sentiments on Thursday. Speaking on the tech giant’s earnings call, CEO Eddie Wu mentioned the newly introduced policy “will definitely have some impact on the long-term construction of Alibaba Cloud's data centers both domestically and globally.”.

“Based on our initial assessment, the long-term target of 20 gigawatts may be lowered, which will also lead to a reduction in our overall capital expenditure proposal,” he mentioned.

But both Tencent and Alibaba don’t see the new policy as creating long-term problems, because both companies believe Chinese chipmakers will be making advanced AI hardware at scale in 2027 or 2028.

Alibaba’s Wu predicted his firm “will go through a phase of the largest investment in AI infrastructure” once Chinese chipmakers get up to speed.

Once that happens, Nvidia and AMD may not even be able to sell chips to China through backdoors.

And for what it’s worth, even after the two sultans of silicon went through the front door by asking BIS for permission, many of the approved orders never resulted in shipments.

Sources indicate that that’s likely because Beijing needs to approve imports and chose not to do so while its own chipmakers develop improved products. ®.
