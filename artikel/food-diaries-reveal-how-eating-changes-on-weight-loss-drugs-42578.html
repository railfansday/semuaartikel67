Food diaries reveal how eating changes on weight-loss drugs

Patients taking weight-loss drugs may unknowingly eat too little, missing key nutrients and receiving limited dietary support.

Appetite is reduced by weight-loss drugs such as Wegovy and Mounjaro, so a drop in food intake is expected.

According to recent reports, what some patients may not know is how little they end up eating. Their intake of several nutrients may also be too low.

A small study at one English medical practice recorded intakes below 800 calories a day in three of 14 patients.

Too little fiber and vitamin D was obtained from food by most of the group. Protein intake, in contrast, was adequate in nearly every participant.

A possible gap in care is suggested by the findings. Little structured follow-up or dietary guidance was reported by many participants after the drugs were started.

The project was run by Dhanush Ammineni and Milou Ottolini, medical students at the University of Oxford. They worked with Dr. Ellen Fallows at Brackley Medical Centre, a family practice in Northamptonshire, England.

It has been revealed that only 14 volunteers took part, and no more than two days of meals were analyzed for each. The project therefore cannot show how common the problem is. Nor can it show that the drugs were the cause.

A text message was sent by the practice to 97 eligible patients on its register.

Recent findings suggest that all of them had been prescribed one of these weight-loss drugs within the past year. The project was completed by 14 people: 11 women and three men, aged 27 to 73.

According to recent reports, tirzepatide, sold as Mounjaro, was taken by twelve of them. Ten paid for their prescriptions privately.

The other four received them through the National Health Service (NHS), Britain’s public health system.

Since the start of treatment, the participants had lost between 6.6 and 75 pounds (3 and 34 kilograms).

Sources indicate that meals were recorded by each person in an online food diary called myfood24. Participants were asked to choose days they considered typical.

It has been revealed that up to two recorded days from each person were analyzed by the team. Intake of 28 nutrients was then compared with the UK’s recommended daily amounts.

An intake below two-thirds of a recommended amount was counted as a shortfall.

Each participant also took part in a 20-minute phone interview about life on the drug.

Fewer than 1,231 calories a day were eaten by half of the participants. Individual intakes ranged from 541 to 2,312 calories.

It has been revealed that weight loss on these drugs depends partly on a lower calorie intake. The concern is how low some intakes were. The food was also being chosen by the patients themselves.

UK health guidance covers meal-replacement diets of 800 to 1,200 calories a day.

That guidance claims such diets should be used only with specialist support and nutritionally complete products. No such oversight was available to the three participants with the lowest intakes.

According to recent reports, the lowest intake in the group was 541 calories a day, averaged by one woman. She fell short on all six nutrients the team focused on.

About a quarter of an ounce (7.8 grams) of fruit a day was recorded in her diary.

The six nutrients were fiber, iron, vitamin D, potassium, protein, and magnesium.

Less than two-thirds of the recommended fiber was obtained by twelve of the 14 participants. Eleven fell below that mark for vitamin D from food. Seven fell below it for iron.

It has been revealed that protein was the exception. Too little was eaten by only one participant. Half the group ate more than one and a half times the recommended amount.

It has been revealed that A possible reason was suggested by the interviews. Snacks had been cut out and portions had become smaller, participants said. Main meals that included protein were still being eaten.

Sources indicate that at least three of the six nutrients fell short in eight participants. Most of those eight spoke of cooking at home or of choosing food with their health in mind.

The researchers attributed the shortfalls mainly to how little food was eaten overall.

In a recent development, low intake was not the full explanation, however. Four nutrients were still short in one woman whose diary averaged 1,421 calories a day.

In a recent development, the quality of the food may matter as well. A second woman ate 677 calories a day and got 84% of them from ultra-processed foods. Five of the six nutrients fell short in her diet.

It has been revealed that the vitamin D figures have to be read with caution, since the body also produces the vitamin in sunlit skin. Production of that kind is not captured by a food diary.

Industry observers note that two participants remarked they were shocked at how little they were eating. This had not been realized until the diary was filled in.

Industry observers note that the researchers suggested that reduced appetite may lead people to under-eat without recognizing it. Under-eating of this kind would not be detected unless diet were monitored in some way.

Benefits were also described. Many participants mentioned they felt more in control of their eating. Food could be enjoyed without guilt.

Recent findings suggest that some of them also reported that pain had eased and that they could move about more freely. In many interviews the medication was spoken of as a turning point.

The interviews brought out drawbacks as well. Because of nausea or diarrhea, some participants had stopped eating in company.

It has been revealed that others were afraid of regaining weight once the drug was stopped. The long-term cost of the medication was a further worry.

A lack of structured follow-up was consistently described by participants.

Some participants stated they managed symptoms such as dizziness and low blood pressure on their own or sought advice from online peer groups.

Recent findings suggest that the researchers set this against the care given around weight-loss surgery. Screening for vitamin and mineral deficiencies is routine before such operations. Afterward, patients remain under the care of a dietitian.

“No drug-specific nutritional monitoring framework exists,” the researchers wrote.

UK dietary advice is based on the Eatwell Guide, a general healthy-eating guide. It was not designed for people taking these drugs.

An earlier analysis covered 461,382 adults newly prescribed GLP-1 drugs, the group that includes Wegovy.

In a recent development, A nutritional deficiency had been diagnosed in nearly 13% of them within six months. By 12 months, the share was more than 22%.

Sources indicate that the food diary was completed without help by eight of the 14 participants. In the researchers’ view, a diary of this kind might offer busy clinics a practical means of spotting nutritional risk.

It has yet to be tested whether patients’ health would improve as a result.

What participants ate before treatment began was never recorded.

It has been revealed that for that reason the diaries cannot tell the drugs apart from earlier eating habits as a cause. Something the project did not measure could also be responsible.

The project depended on meals reported by the participants themselves, and laboratory tests were not part of it. What people ate is described by the results, but a diagnosed deficiency cannot be inferred from them.

The volunteers also differed from typical patients. Each had chosen to take part, and women made up most of the group. Most prescriptions were private, and the practice is located in a relatively affluent area.

Fear that the medication would be withdrawn reportedly kept some patients from joining.

The volunteers were motivated and lived in a relatively affluent area. For that reason, the researchers suggested that the risk among patients more widely may be underestimated.

A larger study was called for by the team. It would cover several practices and include both NHS and private patients.

Three separate days of food records would be collected from each person. By their calculations, about 120 participants would be enough to estimate how common each shortfall is.

Larger studies would also need to determine whether the shortfalls affect patients’ health.

The full study was published in the journal BMJ Nutrition, Prevention & Health.
