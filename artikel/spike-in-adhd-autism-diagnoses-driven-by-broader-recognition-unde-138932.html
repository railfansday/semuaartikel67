Spike in ADHD, Autism Diagnoses Driven by Broader Recognition, Understanding

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Two upcoming studies suggest autism and ADHD are becoming better recognized, not actually more prevalent, and that executive function and social challenges seen in both conditions actually stem from different underlying brain mechanisms.

ADHD and autism spectrum disorder (ASD) share overlapping symptoms, and prior studies suggest that nearly half of autistic children also have ADHD.1 Now, two upcoming studies on ADHD and ASD profiles suggest that rising diagnosis rates are a product of improved symptom recognition and diagnostic practices, and that executive function and social impairments seen in both ADHD and ASD may be traced to different cognitive pathways.

The first study, published in JAMA Psychiatry,2 evaluated the health records of 71,000 children and adolescents diagnosed with ADHD or ASD and 713,000 controls; it found that the annual number of individuals in the nationwide Danish health registry with newly diagnosed ADHD and/or ASD increased from 4,944 in 2012 to 9,179 in 2022. The proportion of girls diagnosed with ADHD or ASD and the median age at diagnosis also grew during the 10-year study period from 2012 to 2022.

Recent findings suggest that however, traditional risk factors strongly linked to an ADHD or ASD diagnosis in 2012, such as low birth weight or lower household income, have become progressively less predictive with time. For example, children born with a low birth weight were 54% more likely to receive a subsequent diagnosis of ADHD or ASD in 2012; by 2022, that percentage dropped to 17%. Overall, the study found that children and adolescents diagnosed with ADHD or ASD today more closely resemble their neurotypical peers compared to those diagnosed 10 years ago.

These findings do not mean that ADHD or ASD has become less impairing; the spectrum of cases has shifted as clinicians get better at identifying less obvious cases. Greater awareness of ADHD and ASD, and broader access to diagnostic assessment tools, are also factors contributing to growing diagnoses among children.

“The study results do not show that these conditions are being overdiagnosed, or that they have become less severe,” claims Oleguer Plana-Ripoll, Ph.D., a senior author of the study. “Rather, they indicate that the population receiving these diagnoses has changed over time, and this needs to be taken into account when interpreting current trends.”.

The second, smaller study, published in Frontiers in Child and Adolescent Psychiatry,3 found that executive functioning (EF) and social cognition (SC) impairments in children diagnosed with ADHD or ASD may look similar, but the impairments stem from different underlying mechanisms.

The cross-sectional, four-year study analyzed performance-based measures of 123 children, aged 6 to 11 years, across three matched groups: mild ASD, combined-type ADHD, and neurotypical peers.

It has been revealed that findings indicated that both ADHD and ASD groups performed worse than their neurotypical peers on measures of EF and SC. However, children with ADHD demonstrated weak social cognition due to difficulties with response inhibition and attention. Social cognition weaknesses in children with ASD, on the other hand, appeared to result from difficulties in understanding and interpreting social information.

According to recent reports, how executive functions related to social cognition across disorders was the strongest indication of different cognitive processes between the two clinical groups. In the ADHD group, the theory-of-mind model explained 72% of the variance, compared to just 55% in the ASD group.

It has been revealed that the researchers surmise that a person with ADHD relies heavily on executive functions, such as inhibition, auditory attention, and design fluency, for social cognition. In essence, their difficulties in social situations are not because they don’t understand social concepts but because distractions and impulsivity interfere.

In the ASD group, social difficulties were more likely to stem from affect recognition (understanding facial cues) and response set (cognitive flexibility, shifting perspectives), rather than from attention deficit or impulse control.

According to recent reports, “ADHD and autism are two separate and distinct conditions that happen to be found together much more frequently than would be expected by chance alone,” claims ADHD expert William Dodson, M.D., LF-APA. “For people with co-existing ADHD and ASD, treating the ADHD is a means to an end. The world is a classroom for people with ASD, and they have to be ready to observe and practice what they’ve learned.”.

Results from the study suggest individuals would benefit more from targeted ADHD and ASD interventions matched to each disorder-specific profile rather than a single shared approach.
