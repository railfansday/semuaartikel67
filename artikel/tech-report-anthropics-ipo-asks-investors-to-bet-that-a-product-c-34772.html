Tech Report: Anthropic’s IPO asks investors to bet that a product can build a firm, not the other way around

🌐 Source: Fastcompany • 📅 18 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 7 min read (1477 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

When Anthropic releases its initial public offering this Thursday, it will be another test of how much further the AI industry’s seemingly unchecked growth can go. The company is seeking a valuation of $1.4 trillion, asking investors to buy into the idea that artificial intelligence will become a major contributor to the economy. But the IPO also asks investors to make a more unusual bet: on a company whose own product could eventually do much of the work of building the business itself. In its amended S-1 filing with the Securities and Exchange Commission, released last week, Anthropic claims that “each fresh version of a Claude model directly accelerates development of the next.” Claude already helps run experiments, generate training data, and test future models, the company says. As evidence, Anthropic notes that Claude wrote more than 90% of contributions to its code base in September 2026. In the three-month period through September, the daily volume of code contributed per engineer was more than 45 times its 2024 average. Traditionally, companies go public asking investors to bankroll employees who will build and sell better products. Anthropic is pitching something different: a business whose products increasingly help develop their own successors—and, in the process, help build the company itself. There is some precedent for a company using its own technology to improve it. When the software company GitLab went public in 2021, its prospectus described how employees used GitLab’s development platform to identify problems and determine what to build next, a practice known as “dogfooding.” But that was software helping human developers do their jobs, rather than AI increasingly performing the work itself. “It may be the first IPO in which this type of thing is happening,” says Sinan Aral, a professor at MIT Sloan School of Management. Still, Aral cautions against confusing AI’s growing role in developing products with the ability to decide which products should exist in the first place. Claude may be writing code, but humans remain responsible for the more consequential research decisions. “What to do, what goals to have and what to optimize, is generally being decided by human beings,” he says. Even so, experts say Anthropic’s pitch represents a striking departure from the conventional IPO. Until now, discussions of recursive self-improvement—the idea that AI systems could improve their own capabilities—have largely centered on the risks of systems becoming misaligned with human goals, says Jay Ritter, director of the IPO Initiative at the University of Florida’s Warrington College of Business. But Ritter sees a potentially transformative business opportunity, too. Unlike conventional processes of innovation, which often yield diminishing returns, AI-driven self-improvement could theoretically produce exponential gains. That would fundamentally change not only how products are developed, but how the companies behind them operate. “Anthropic and its competitors are companies that are increasingly capable of building themselves,” Ritter says. “Investors are essentially being asked to pay for the electricity to do this.” The prospect also raises questions about how much autonomy Anthropic is willing to grant Claude—and whether a system capable of directing its own development would make decisions aligned with human interests. For now, MIT’s Aral says, the company appears reluctant to surrender that control. Its S-1 contains “a risk section like we’ve never read before,” he says, reflecting the possibility that its technology could eventually begin making consequential decisions on its own. Not everyone is convinced that such a future is imminent, or even plausible. Kirill Skrygan, CEO of JetBrains, a company known for its software development tools, is skeptical that Anthropic has created an AI system capable of improving itself without human intervention. He also disputes the notion that Anthropic represents an entirely new kind of business, one whose development could eventually escape human control. “I think it’s more about hype before the IPO,” he says. Indeed, a closer reading of Anthropic’s filing reveals a more qualified vision than its headline figures might suggest. The company acknowledges that errors in existing models could carry over into future ones, and says it does not expect to let Claude develop successive frontier models without meaningful human supervision—at least not yet. Whether Anthropic can deliver on its vision of AI increasingly building the business behind it remains an open question. Investors who buy the stock this week will be betting that it can, even as the company itself acknowledges the risks and limitations of that ambition.
