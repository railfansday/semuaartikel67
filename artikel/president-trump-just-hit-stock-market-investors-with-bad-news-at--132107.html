Recent findings suggest that the U.S. stock market has come under considerable pressure in recent weeks. The benchmark S&P 500 (^GSPC -0.25%) and the technology-heavy Nasdaq Composite (^IXIC -0.62%) have dropped into correction territory, falling approximately 10% and 17%, respectively, from their October highs.

Corporate earnings growth has been exceptional this year, but investors are increasingly worried that technology companies are spending too much money on artificial intelligence infrastructure. Persistent inflation and the expectation of higher interest rates have also contributed to the sell-off.

Recent findings suggest that unfortunately, President Donald Trump just gave investors another reason to worry, and the news comes at a particularly bad time. On Nov. 25, his administration proposed fresh tariffs targeting 14 major trading partners, including China and the European Union. Those import taxes threaten to complicate the Federal Reserve's fight against inflation, which could hurt the stock market.

In May, President Trump said, "My favorite word in the dictionary is tariff." He believes tariffs could be a substantial source of revenue for the federal government, while also bolstering national security by incentivizing companies to move manufacturing operations to the United States.

However, several studies suggest tariffs imposed by the Trump administration have already increased costs for American businesses, and those businesses often pass at least a portion of the additional expense along to consumers in the form of higher prices.

Additionally, economists at the Newly introduced York Fed identified another troubling phenomenon in a recent research paper: Tariffs have actually increased the price of domestically produced goods, partly because American manufacturers use imported materials and partly because they face less competition from foreign businesses.

Those indirect price increases took as long as 12 months to work their way through supply chains, meaning inflationary consequences of higher tariffs are not necessarily apparent immediately. Regardless, history makes it clear that the latest round of import taxes could drive inflation higher.

The Federal Reserve raised its benchmark interest rate by a quarter percentage point in September due to persistent inflation, bringing the target range on the federal funds rate to 3.75% to 4%. Fed officials held rates steady at the October meeting, but CME Group's FedWatch tool highlights another rate hike is widely anticipated in December.

President Trump's latest tariff proposals could keep inflation elevated for longer, forcing the Federal Reserve to maintain tighter monetary policy or raise interest rates even further. Indeed, EY chief economist Greg Daco warned that newly introduced tariffs increase the risk of a "more hawkish Fed response." And that could spell trouble for the stock market.

Recent findings suggest that higher interest rates tend to suppress consumer and business spending, creating a headwind to corporate earnings growth. They also make bonds more attractive relative to stocks, putting downward pressure on equity valuations.

Sources indicate that nevertheless, investors have reason for optimism. Despite Trump's latest tariffs, Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanely believe the S&P 500 will reach 8,700 next year; that implies 21% upside from its current level of 7,182. UBS is even more optimistic, with a year-end target of 8,900, implying 24% upside. As always, investors should remain calm through market turbulence, and treat significant dips as buying opportunities.
