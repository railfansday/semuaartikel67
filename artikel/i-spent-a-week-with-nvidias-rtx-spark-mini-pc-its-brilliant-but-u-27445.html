I spent a week with Nvidia's RTX Spark mini PC — it's brilliant, but unless you're Tony Stark, the AI tax is optional

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Testing Nvidia’s RTX Spark inside laptops for the past month felt like the birth of a different species of computer. Confined to a portable chassis, that local AI prowess and raw horsepower make good sense to turn it into a real everything machine.

But when you rip that N1X out of a laptop, drop it into a desk-bound mini PC like the Asus ProArt GR1X, and slap a price tag of at least $4,499 on it ($6,999 for the 128GB version I’m testing), the entire narrative changes.

In a recent development, yes, when tethered to a wall, it is faster than its laptop counterpart. But this chip is no longer fighting the MacBook Pro. It is fighting the desktop market — a more ruthlessly powerful space where (in some ways) Nvidia is fighting with itself.

Sources indicate that I currently have two 128GB heavyweights on the test bench. In the green corner is the Asus ProArt GR1X, powered by RTX Spark and 128GB of memory. In the red corner is the Geekom A9 Mega, armed with AMD’s Ryzen AI Max 395 and the exact same 128GB memory pool, but priced $3,000 cheaper.

Industry observers note that and remember when I said Nvidia is fighting with itself in the desktop space? Well, to prove that point, I also threw my custom-built RTX 5070 Ti desktop into the ring for good measure (a quick foray on PC Part Picker shows that would be around $4,000 cheaper).

I’m going to do two things here:.

In a recent development, you are buying a 128GB mini PC to act as an always-on, hyper-secure household brain that you don’t pay subscriptions for. You want it to run a local model like Qwen 3.8 Flash via Hermes to autonomously organize your morning emails, scrape the web for news, or bridge with Home Assistant to tell your robot vacuum to clean the kitchen.

Get instant access to breaking news, the hottest reviews, great deals and helpful tips.

Measuring this comes down to two things: Tokens Per Second (TPS), which is how fast the AI types, and Time to First Token (TTFT), which is its reaction time before answering.

I pushed both machines through LM Studio using the Qwen 3.8 27B model on two standard generative tasks and one agentic task.

Sources indicate that nvidia’s chip has the reflexes of a driver hitting the apex at Silverstone. That sub-second reaction time makes conversational agents feel incredibly lifelike, and when I turned it into a self-hosted agent that I could talk to from my Telegram app, the end result was something that did the boring morning admin for me while I spoke to my wife on the phone instead.

Industry observers note that plus if you tap into Nvidia PAIR, you can use all the supported devices in your home to share AI compute. For example, a prompt on my MacBook Pro fired up the Spark machine for a longer task, while whirring my RTX 5070 Ti desktop into life for a short burst.

According to recent reports, meanwhile, the AMD machine is perfectly capable of this too, but it pauses just long enough for you to notice it clearing its throat. That being mentioned, 24 tokens per second on Geekom is still faster than any human can read.

And as for the desktop PC, this is technically possible but with some caveats (because it treats all your system memory as different pools rather than one giant unified ocean):.

According to recent reports, that gaming rig is a Ferrari driving on a 100-foot track — unbelievably fast, but the track (its VRAM) is so short that you can only go so fast before crashing into the wall. The 128GB mini-PCs are freight trains with a lower top speed, but enough space to carry massive cargo (huge models) without ever derailing.

My custom RTX 5070 Ti desktop will absolutely smoke these mini PCs in standard graphical tasks. It’s a monster card that consumes more than double the total wattage of the RTX Spark (paying my respects to your electric bill and fan noise compared to the Spark).

But as I mentioned above, standard GPUs are fundamentally bottlenecked by their memory. It may be fast, but it only has the surface area of an airplane tray table. The moment you try to render high-res AI video or compile massive 3D Gaussian splats, the PC runs out of room.

The massive unified memory pool of RTX Spark gives you the surface area of a massive dining room table, swallowing heavy generative workloads whole.

I booted up ComfyUI for local video generation and ran the synthetic Procyon benchmarking suite to measure raw compute overhead.

In a recent development, this is an absolute bloodbath. Nvidia’s proprietary Tensor cores and hyper-efficient NVFP4 precision formats completely lap AMD’s Strix Halo. As you can see, Spark generated the exact same ComfyUI video nearly three times faster.

According to recent reports, if you are a 3D artist, indie game developer or an ad agency director generating local video assets on a tight deadline, rendering time is literally money. In those professional environments, the Asus ProArt GR1X pays for itself.

Though, quick side note Asus. This is a creator machine — would it have killed you to put an SD card reader on here? For moving photos, I've gotta head back into dongle hell.

But for everyday people like you and me? It comes down to how you feel about all this stuff. Personally, there’s a major difference in my mind between human creativity and getting a typed prompt right to churn out AI stuff.

And while there are improvements on that human side — Adobe Creative Suite is now optimized for RTX Spark, for example — I didn’t really use it for anything beyond my YouTube video intro and some fun experiments with my wife’s doodles.

Recent findings suggest that you’re paying a massive premium for AI, so it might as well play video games well, too, at the end of the day. I mean, that’s a lot of money — you want this to be a true everything machine.

I ran all the usual 3DMark benchmarks to measure raw, unassisted graphics muscle, and checked the frame rates on key games before and after DLSS.

Recent findings suggest that in use, it’s nice to have a mini PC that’s capable of gaming in the way that it is. But in the desktop space, this is RTX Spark’s harshest reality check. If gaming is something you care about chiefly over getting stuff done, you know, I know (and Nvidia knows) you should get a gaming PC.

It has been revealed that not to say it doesn’t work brilliantly, because it does. This is a mighty integrated GPU that does smoke AMD, keeping the machine nicely viable for high-end 1440p gaming with all the DLSS support.

But a standard RTX 5070 Ti desktop, which you could easily build (or buy) for half the price of the Asus, doubles the Spark’s native performance.

According to recent reports, RTX Spark mini PCs, such as the Asus ProArt GR1X, are localized token generators disguised as PCs. It’s not going to be for everyone, so let me help you out here.

RTX Spark is a cheat code. Its power impresses, and the speed gains mean you won’t be spending time twiddling your thumbs waiting for it. And the RTX side of it for gaming makes this a machine you can work on by day and play by night.

According to recent reports, AMD power does 80% of what the Nvidia box can do in daily tasks, and it leaves you with a lot more money in your pocket. Of course there’s a speed advantage with Spark, but is it $3,000 of an advantage? That’s up to you.

Industry observers note that if you’ve got the space for an actual desktop, this is going to be the move. Component prices are steep, but even with that in mind, you’ll get better bang for your buck in gaming and short burst AI/creativity work.
