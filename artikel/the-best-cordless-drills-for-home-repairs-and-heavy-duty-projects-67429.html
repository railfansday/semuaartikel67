Understanding how to use a drill also helps users get the most out of whatever drill you choose.

Battery voltage helps indicate a cordless drill’s capabilities, but it doesn’t measure power on its own. Motor design, gearing, and battery performance also matter. When choosing a battery-powered drill, consider the work you initiative to tackle rather than voltage alone.

The distinction between 18V and 20V MAX can be confusing. These labels describe the same voltage class: 20V is the maximum initial battery voltage measured without a workload, while 18V is the nominal voltage. The difference isn’t a startup surge, nor does a 20V MAX label guarantee more power than an 18V model. Similarly, many drills marketed as 12V use batteries rated at 10.8V nominal.

In a recent development, battery capacity, measured in amp-hours (Ah), also matters. At the same voltage, a higher Ah rating generally means longer runtime, though actual results depend on the drill and workload. Smaller packs keep the tool lighter, while larger packs support longer work sessions but usually add weight and cost. A higher Ah rating alone doesn’t guarantee more drilling power. For occasional projects, a compact battery may be sufficient; for extended use, consider a larger pack or a spare.

Torque is the twisting force a drill delivers, while speed, measured in revolutions per minute (RPM), describes how rapidly the bit turns. A lower gear generally provides more torque for driving fasteners and drilling with larger bits. Higher speeds suit many smaller-bit drilling tasks, though the appropriate speed depends on the material and bit.

Most drills have an adjustable clutch that limits the torque transmitted to the chuck. When resistance reaches the selected setting, the clutch disengages to help prevent overdriving screws or stripping their heads. It responds to resistance rather than a preset screw depth, so choosing the right setting helps maintain control. Knowing how to change a drill bit also helps ensure the bit is properly seated and secured.

According to recent reports, look for at least 10 clutch settings to accommodate different fasteners and materials. A drilling machine with two gear settings offers more versatility, while a variable-speed trigger lets you fine-tune speed within the selected range.

It has been revealed that A drill’s size and weight affect comfort, especially during extended use. Lightweight models can weigh about a pound, while heavy-duty cordless hammer drills may exceed 8 pounds. Extra power often adds weight, so choose a tool that balances capability with comfortable handling. Check whether the listed weight includes the battery, which can make a noticeable difference.

A compact drill is easier to maneuver in confined spaces, and a smaller body doesn’t necessarily mean less power. Consider both weight and overall dimensions when comparing models.

According to recent reports, most cordless drills have keyless chucks for quick bit changes. Chuck capacity determines the largest bit-shank diameter the tool accepts, rather than the size of hole it can drill. Many general-purpose models have ⅜- or ½-inch chucks, while some compact drill/drivers use ¼-inch hex holders that accept only compatible hex-shank bits.

The best drill bits for a project must suit both the material and the drill’s chuck. Check compatibility before buying a bit set.

Sources indicate that corded drills sometimes come with a brushed motor. They are cheap and reliable, but the brushes eventually wear down and require replacement. Another challenge with brushed motors is that they create friction, which drains the battery faster in cordless models.

According to recent reports, the best cordless power drills often use a brushless motor. These virtually maintenance-free motors use the battery much more efficiently. However, brushless motors cost more. As a result, many budget cordless drills still use brushed motors.

For a typical DIYer, the difference is probably negligible. Those who want to maximize performance and runtime, particularly professional users, often choose a brushless cordless drill.

Like most modern battery-operated drills, the models in this guide use rechargeable lithium-ion batteries. Runtime depends on battery capacity and workload, while charging time varies with the battery and charger. For extensive drilling, a kit with a spare battery lets you keep working while the depleted pack charges.

Industry observers note that note that manufacturer runtime claims often factor backup batteries into their runtime estimates. For instance, the specs on a drill that comes with two batteries, each lasting 1 hour, may list a 2-hour runtime.

Additionally, many cordless drills include a smart charger that replenishes the battery faster, usually within 15 to 60 minutes. A charge indicator allows you to monitor battery capacity.
