Breaking: Denis Villeneuve on Finishing ‘Dune,’ Casting James Bond, and Why ‘Reality Is Unbeatable’

The last days of the making of the Dune trilogy take place on an unassuming Hollywood side street in September, in a dark, mostly silent room. “We were about to have a quick look at reel one because they did some adjustments, and it’s without sound,” Denis Villeneuve states, apologetically. “So it’s a way to discover the film that is a bit brutal for you.”.

Villeneuve, the director and cowriter of Dune, Dune: Part Two, and Dune: Part Three, is tall, slender, and bearded, with deep-set eyes. He has a disarming effect. Many directors of Villeneuve’s stature—Christopher Nolan or James Cameron—lead their productions with a kind of remote but dazzling competency: They can do everything better than you and are used to the deference that comes with that fact. Villeneuve’s charisma is closer and more mischievous: He speaks quietly and playfully, in Quebecois-accented English, and commands loyalty via the protective instinct he inspires in nearly everyone he meets. His method is emotional; his instructions are conveyed with sincerity and praise. When presented with the results he is seeking, he will let you know with an almost childlike enthusiasm: I deeply love it. Or: Wow.

Today, Villeneuve and his cinematographer, Linus Sandgren, are at the postproduction facility Company 3, where they have been the past weeks and months, color-timing—the process of adjusting and balancing a film’s many colors and contrasts—the 150 minutes of Dune: Part Three. For my sake, and because there is still work to be done on it, they have started, once again, at the beginning of the film. “So here we could have fucked up the Warner Brothers logo if we wanted,” Villeneuve states, gesturing at the screen at the studio’s iconic WB, “but we are nice guys.”.

Then he leans forward: “No, but seriously, without joking, are they too rich, the colors? A little bit? Or is it the original to your eyes?” Sandgren—a calm Swede in Birkenstocks—and Matt Wallach, the film’s colorist, chime in to agree and to tone down the blue. “Their logo is a little cartoonish,” Villeneuve says, still staring at it. “It’s not their best work, I will say. But that feels better. Thank you, Matt.”.

Villeneuve and his wife, Tanya Lapointe, who is a producer and second-unit director on Dune: Part Three, disagree on how long they have been working on Dune: Villeneuve tends to say nine years, and Lapointe says 10. (It’s 10.) Frank Herbert’s Dune books, which are notoriously complex and hard to adapt, have stymied directors as talented as David Lynch and Alejandro Jodorowsky. But Villeneuve seems to have found a way. The first two Dune films—Part One came out in 2021—have grossed over a billion dollars, been nominated for 15 Academy Awards, and helped make stars of Timothée Chalamet (who plays Atreides, the film’s complicated messiah figure and main character) and Zendaya (who plays Chani, a native Fremen warrior and the lover of Atreides) while still capturing the uniquely strange and surreal texture of Herbert’s novels. (Multiple people in the Villeneuve films imbibe what one character describes as “worm piss.”).

Right now Villeneuve is feeling the last decade acutely. “I’m at that stage of the process where all my body hurts,” he claims. On Dune: Part Three alone, the production shot for nearly a hundred days, in three countries: Hungary, where the film’s main stages were; Iceland; and the Liwa Desert, in the United Arab Emirates, which doubled for the sand planet of Arrakis. They wrapped last November, and Villeneuve went directly into the film’s edit and postproduction process. “It’s a feeling of some kind of deep melancholia to finally come to the conclusion of this long journey,” he says. “Because it has been nine years of my life dreaming about Arrakis, about sandworms, about the adventures of Paul Atreides. I’m pretty emotional about that.”.

Industry observers note that in the history of Hollywood, there have been only a handful of directors who have had the ability to work, again and again, at the highest level of the art form while also connecting with audiences at the widest possible scale. Villeneuve is one of them. His signature films—2015’s cartel thriller Sicario, or 2016’s science fiction film Arrival, which was nominated for eight Academy Awards—are knotty and severe and not always easy to follow; they are also beautiful and seem to play well anywhere, whether on an airplane, in an HBO rerun, or at an IMAX theater. Villeneuve’s reward for all his success is his next project: the 26th James Bond film. “It’s a blessing,” Villeneuve says. “Because to leave Arrakis, I need some kind of strong rocket, something that will grab me and pull me away from the orbit of that planet. I’m leaving a childhood dream. I need something so powerful to pull me away from it. And Bond is the one that will do it.”.

Recent findings suggest that in the dark screening room, Wallach lets the first minutes of the final Dune film play. We watch soldiers on a dimly lit warship prepare themselves for battle. Sandgren and Villeneuve discuss trying to bring out one of the actor’s features more concretely. “I guess what we’re fighting is we could have lit him more,” Sandgren notes, “but also what Denis really wants here is the realism and the sort of incidental light—like he just happens to sit in a shitty light here, and gets what he gets, which adds to the realism, which is nice. But you still want to see him. So it’s a balance.”.

“What Linus claims, in a very polite way, is that it’s my fault,” Villeneuve says, smiling, while Wallach finds a little more detail in the actor’s face.

“I deeply love it,” Villeneuve pronounces, when he is done. “Thank you. The scene is at the beginning, and becomes claustrophobic. In French, we say they are chair à canon.”.

“Cannon fodder,” someone supplies.

“Cannon food, we call it in Sweden,” Sandgren says.

Villeneuve watches his own film as it plays on. “Wow,” he states. “Fantastic. Fantastic. That’s beautiful. Nice work on the highlights here. Thank you, guys. Wow. Great work. It looks real now. Wow.” His voice is full of emotion as he turns to Sandgren: “It’s beautiful, my dear friend.”.

In the Dune movies, you can feel Villeneuve’s desire to go deeper into the desert, to shed all the substantial, loud machinery of his own films, and to get to the places out near the horizon. This is one of the reasons his trilogy works where others have failed: Despite Villeneuve’s talent for spectacle—his Dune is full of menacing, chromed-out oblong shapes and eerily familiar war machines—his true gift is finding the uncannily quiet spots in the worlds he creates, the lonely places where his characters are tested against themselves.

In a recent development, “That was the main condition when I agreed to make these movies,” Villeneuve indicates, about the way all three Dune films were shot in the desert, on location. “I mentioned that it was deeply key for me to shoot it in the real environment.” The Liwa Desert was so mind-bendingly hot while shooting Dune: Part Three that the director had to incorporate a midday break into the crew’s daily schedule. Robert Pattinson, who joined the cast for the third film, has talked about the set being so intensely warm that he basically disassociated for most of shooting. But being there is one of the main things Villeneuve is after. “I love reality,” he says. “I love to be in the environment. It’s like a pleasure that I give to myself to be in the crew with a camera in the desert. I feel that there’s all these shots that we did, the realism, that I think you cannot achieve otherwise. And you cannot feel the power of the desert if you’re not there. There are some shots you can cheat sometimes. But in general, reality is unbeatable.”.

Villeneuve, 59, grew up in Gentilly, Quebec, a small town right next to a river. From that place he emerged with “a relationship with horizon and silence,” he notes. “I was in contact since my birth with incredible gigantic skies, very dramatic, operatic skies. And colors as well. I grew up in a world that has a black-and-white winter, which has a tremendous, beautiful, precise palette of black and white and blue and grays.”.

In a recent development, villeneuve likes to point out that the two main structures in his town were a church and a power plant. “I grew up melancholic, a child that was dreaming significant alone in his corner and fascinated by the engineers that were working in that nuclear power plant,” he indicates. “I was raised as a Catholic, and I was raised going to the church, and those chants and the passion of religion [are] all that mixed together with incredibly powerful grandmothers. I think that’s the background of Dune right there.”.

According to recent reports, villeneuve was a reader, a dreamer. It was only as an adult, when Villeneuve became a documentary filmmaker, that he began to discover a larger world outside his own mind. “I think that there’s something about Paul Atreides in the way he’s introduced at the beginning, the way he is like a kid that has been protected in a small bubble all his life and suddenly finds himself confronted to a fresh reality where he has to consolidate his identity in another culture,” Villeneuve says. “I think it’s something that I, at the time when I was a kid, I really connected with. And early on when I became a young adult, I traveled a lot, a lot. I went a lot to Asia and was spending a lot of time there doing small documentaries and being in contact with so many perspectives, so many points of view on reality.”.

Villeneuve made his first feature, August 32nd on Earth, in Canada in 1998. He would go on to make three more films in French, before making his first English-language film, Enemy, in 2013. “I consolidated my identity as a filmmaker,” Villeneuve indicates about the time before he came to Hollywood “through documentaries and movies with less commercial pressure where I was free to express myself and find my own voice.” Partially, he learned by failing. “I will say that I don’t see my first feature films as...they had some clumsiness in them. I was a beginner. I learned my craft. I made mistakes and I learned from those mistakes, a lot more than from my successes.”.

It has been revealed that villeneuve, as a young filmmaker, was wary of Hollywood, even as he was pointed at it like an arrow. “Hollywood has always had that kind of dichotomy, to be a paradise for a filmmaker and hell at the same time,” he says. “Since the birth of Hollywood there has been a struggle between the art of cinema, the idea that you want to express yourself—and for that you need a lot of money, and somebody will write the check, and you have a responsibility. Since the birth of cinema, it has been like that.”.

In 2012, Villeneuve found himself facing a rare opportunity: He had two films greenlit at more or less the same time. One was Prisoners—an in-demand Hollywood script about the abduction of two children and the vigilante efforts of one father to get them back. The other was a coproduction between Spain and Canada called Enemy, about a college professor who becomes obsessed with another man with whom he appears to be physically identical. Both were English-language films, which Villeneuve had not yet done. Both films starred Jake Gyllenhaal. But to Villeneuve, they were very different propositions: Enemy was a deeply personal film about identity and the unknown nature of the self. And Prisoners, which also starred Hugh Jackman and Viola Davis and which would be largely distributed by Warner Bros. Pictures, represented Villeneuve’s first true chance to make a real Hollywood film.

Villeneuve decided to make both. But he made Enemy first. “I did convince the studio, Alcon”—the producer of Prisoners—“to wait a few months for Prisoners because first I wanted to make a first film in English in a smaller environment. And the real reason is that I wanted to make sure that I will shoot something that will be so close to me that if ever I was smashed and destroyed by Hollywood, there will be something. If people say, ‘What’s that? Oh, Denis’s there. He’s flying over there.’ Because I did feel that I was afraid.”.

Industry observers note that I remember watching Enemy in a small screening room the year it premiered, in 2013 (the movie was distributed in the United States by a then upstart studio called A24), and being increasingly unsettled and haunted by the film. Sometimes, in quiet moments, the final shot of Enemy still comes to me, unbidden. Who is this guy? I wondered then.

It has been revealed that enemy and Prisoners were both stylish, morally ambiguous films that showed the extremes of human behavior without ever venturing into sadism or pure spectacle, and this style of filmmaking—brutal, unresolved, lush, and heartbreaking—would become Villeneuve’s signature as he made progressively bigger, and bigger-budget, films in Hollywood: 2015’s Sicario, 2016’s Arrival, 2017’s Blade Runner 2049. “I always did projects I [felt] ready to do,” Villeneuve says. “I was ready to do Dune after Blade Runner. I would have not been ready to do Dune after Prisoners. I slowly learned my craft one step at a time—my skills with actors and then to work with more and more background action and increase the technical difficulty slowly. I just felt secure. And when I started Dune, I knew I could not go in front of people and ask them to invest $200 million [until I was ready]. I need to feel deep inside me that I can do that.”.

Villeneuve states he only recently stopped feeling like a tourist in Hollywood. “I think that feeling of having the right to be here in some ways came from other filmmakers, colleagues that wanted to reach out and share thoughts about cinema with me or some friendships that began and that I accepted,” he says. He’s coy about who exactly he’s talking about. But part of it, surely, has also been the acceptance of the films, which despite their increasing size and scale and sometimes exotic source material are consistently deeply personal.

“My work is all about me,” Villeneuve indicates, helplessly. “If you don’t like Dune, the movies, I’m 100 percent responsible because I made every single decision. I brought them to the screen, and they are very personal films. Even for me, it’s like—scope is not something that is contradictory with the ownership of a movie. These movies have been brought to life by me, and I say that without arrogance. I mean, I’m the one who brought them to life. And yeah: It’s my Dune.”.

In a recent development, A few weeks later, Villeneuve and Lapointe, who is shorter but equally warm and charismatic, are at the North Hollywood warehouse of Legendary, the production firm behind the Dune films. The warehouse is full of costumes from past productions: Pacific Rim, Crimson Peak, Godzilla. In the back, transported from another continent entirely, is one of the ornithopters—a helicopter-like vehicle—from Dune: Part Three. Soon this prop, which is the size of an actual helicopter, will join a bunch of others in an upcoming exhibition of work from the Dune films. But right now it is here, in a parking lot under the glaring Los Angeles sun. Its paint—an homage to the specific color of a 1981 Porsche—glows in the light. “The last time I saw this vehicle, it was in a deep desert,” Villeneuve states, looking on. “We had brought it to Abu Dhabi, and it was, I remember vividly, at the top of a cliff of sand. This thing I’ve seen a lot. It’s like an old friend.”.

Sources indicate that later in the day, Villeneuve tells me he is set to approve a final handful of effects shots. And then Dune: Part Three, and the director’s work on the trilogy, he states, will cease. “I do feel that I have to stop,” he reflects. “I don’t think I should spend another minute on it. Because I’m still curious, I’m still alive, and I do it for artistic reasons, not because I see that I’m comfortable or I see that it’s going to be good business-wise or for my career. I think it’s good for me to stop artistically.”.

As Villeneuve’s star has risen, he has increasingly had to think through the question of how to remain an artist at the very high tier of the business that he’s in. “In the past 25 years, there’s a tendency to try to find the perfect formula to make a film, and to try to reproduce, reproduce, and make sequels,” Villeneuve indicates. “I’m responsible. I just made three Dune movies. I’m about to make James Bond 26. So I’m a sinner. But the idea is that as an artist, I feel that there’s an urgency, and you feel it in the young filmmakers, which I deeply love—the idea to bring new stories, original stories to the screen. It’s very important. And that we have to try to, at the end of the day, we need to take risks.… I want to say that we, as humans, as artists, if we want to be better than AI, we have to be unpredictable.” Only humans, he says, “can step into the unknown and go deeper into the dream. That is the strength of a human being. And I think that no computers can do that.”.

Recent findings suggest that it’s the end of September, and the Paramount–Warner Bros. Discovery merger is about to close. “I think that consolidation, I mean, less players in the business is never a good idea,” Villeneuve claims. “Those studios are historical studios. And I hope, I truly hope, that the people in power will have the wisdom to protect the legacy of both studios. There’s a limit to profit. I mean, if the only goal is to make money, it’s really sad. Those things are historical gems of American culture and they have a duty to—I hope and I pray that they understand the responsibility that they have and that they will take care of both of these entities.”.

Villeneuve, for his part, is already turning his attention toward Bond—another character, and another world, he grew up fascinated with. “I adore James Bond,” he states. “I adore Sean Connery, the Sean Connery years, the verve of James Bond, his panache, his drive, his charisma, his unapologetic way of living the strength and what it says, good and bad, about masculinity. There’s something about James Bond that deeply inspires me.” He says the previous producers of the franchise approached him with the idea of directing the film a few years ago, right as he was beginning Dune: Part Three. Villeneuve says he hung up the phone and actually screamed out loud when they offered the franchise to him. Then he told them they’d have to wait. “I stated to them, ‘Yeah, I’m up for it, but I have something before called Dune: Part Three. I’m shooting in two weeks.’ And the only way I can do things in life is to fully commit to something. So I said to them, ‘If you want to work with me, you will have to wait many months because I will be entirely focused on Part Three.’ And so that’s why I’m sorry for everybody, but it took more time for the casting because I’m busy.”.

In a recent development, now we know: Robert Pattinson is James Bond. Villeneuve’s decision, albeit in consultation with his producers and the studio. He is incredulous that I am even asking. “I am the director!” he says. And then softens a bit: “I’m doing cinema because I love to share the creative process,” he says, about the conversations that led to Pattinson. “Otherwise, I will do ice sculpture in my basement. I love the idea to share creativity. But when it comes to casting, it’s something that—I’m doing the casting. But, of course, it will need to be accepted and approved by a ton of people, including you, the pope, my mother, my brother, who has a strong opinion about who should be James Bond.… I think Donald Trump wants to be James Bond. Everybody talks about James Bond. So yes, I will choose James Bond with my friends, producers, and hopefully everybody will.…” He pauses. “You know what? At the end of the day, the truth is, no matter who I choose, some people will be excited and others will be disappointed.”.

Sources indicate that now Villeneuve has a gleam in his eye, talking about what he was looking for in the part. “The first quality is that I need to believe that this man is a killer, because more than a spy, Bond is an assassin,” he claims. “I need that kind of anger deep in the eye. It’s not everybody who has that. I need someone who has that kind of unapologetic panache, someone that when he comes into the room, everything starts to gravitate around him, that kind of strong charisma and someone that has a lot of sensuality as well and that people are attracted to and that someone who expressed some kind of pleasure, someone who has width, sometime who has dark humor, sometimes that has pleasure into being in deep shit, someone who is not afraid to put his hand in the mud. I need someone that is strong, muscular, someone that is a force. And someone, of course, who has a deep intelligence in the eyes, someone that is always ahead of us.”.

He is grinning. You can feel Lapointe, in the nicest possible way, willing him to say less. But Villeneuve, as is typical, is swept away. “Every time I’m thinking about James Bond,” he indicates, “there’s an electricity that goes into my vein that is the electricity of pleasure. It’s like: For me to do that is just a pure cinematic pleasure. Incredible pressure, stress, yes. But the pleasure is bigger.” ✦.
