Rainbow trout faced a familiar virus, but tiny plastic fibers changed their chances of survival

In a lab study, young rainbow trout exposed to nylon microfibers were less likely to survive a common fish virus.

Microplastics come in many shapes, and a new study on rainbow trout suggests the shape matters.

In an eight-week experiment, young trout exposed to both nylon microplastic fibers and a common fish virus had roughly double the odds of dying compared with trout exposed to the virus alone.

Trout that lived with round nylon particles of similar width fared no worse than fish with no plastic at all.

Sources indicate that the fibers didn’t kill fish on their own. Without the virus, plastic of either shape made no difference to survival.

The study came from the Virginia Institute of Marine Science at William & Mary, where Meredith Evans Seeley, Robert Hale and Andrew Wargo work. Anna Mehlhorn, an undergraduate at the time, is now at Cornell.

“Not all plastics are created equal, and so that creates an opportunity to identify the worst forms of pollution and come up with solutions,” Wargo remarked.

It has been revealed that three years ago, the same group reported that microplastics made a trout virus deadlier. Nylon microfibers stood out: in that earlier work, fish exposed to them faced a hazard of death 6.4 times that of fish given the virus alone.

“We saw the effect with the nylon fibers and had to ask, ‘Is it the fact it’s a fiber or is it the fact that it was nylon?'” Hale said.

To answer that, the team kept the material the same and changed the shape. The fibers were about 0.02 inches (500 micrometers) long and 10 micrometers wide, the kind that shed from synthetic fabric.

The powder was made of rounded nylon grains averaging 15 to 20 micrometers across, about as wide as the fibers but nowhere near as long.

In a recent development, the team raised 800 juvenile rainbow trout in 40 tanks of 20 fish each. Every other day, the plastic groups got a dose of fibers or powder in their water, and the tank sat still for a day before it was flushed.

Most groups got plastic for the full eight weeks; two fiber groups got it only in the first or last four.

Halfway through, half the tanks were dosed with infectious hematopoietic necrosis virus, a major cause of losses on trout farms.

Fish started dying a few days later, and the dying largely ended within about two weeks.

By the end, 39% of the trout exposed to both the virus and eight weeks of fibers were alive. In tanks that got the virus with no plastic, 56% survived, and in tanks with neither, 86% did.

In the statistical model, eight weeks of fibers raised the odds of death 2.03 times compared with no particles.

None of the other plastic treatments had a significant effect, including the round powder for eight weeks and the fibers given only before or only after the virus.

Sources indicate that the team also sampled tank water every few days to measure how much virus the fish were shedding. Shedding peaked three days after the virus went in and fell to undetectable levels by day 47 in every treatment.

The plastic groups did not differ, though the eight-week fiber tanks highlighted a trend toward the highest peaks.

The authors wrote that measuring shedding from groups of fish in only four tanks per treatment may have limited the study’s power. A few super-shedders could mask the rest.

Sources indicate that so the fibers did not seem to make the virus more abundant. They seemed to make the fish easier to infect.

The researchers suspect the long, stiff fibers damage protective tissue such as the gills, which they described in the university’s account as the fibers’ pokiness.

“It’s likely creating an easier path for that virus to infect that host,” Seeley remarked.

If microplastic fibers open a door for the virus, the damage has to be there before the virus arrives, and it probably has to keep up afterward. That is what the tanks demonstrated.

Fibers given only after infection did about as well as no plastic. Fibers given only before the virus pushed mortality up toward the chronic group’s level, but the difference was not significant.

The authors pointed to tissue damage from plastic in other animals. In seabirds, scarring from swallowed plastic has been given its own name, plasticosis. Inflammation from microplastics is also under study in fish and in human tissue.

“One of the things that was becoming clear to us is that microplastics have really significant impacts, but they’re not always lethal,” Seeley mentioned.

The team used one high concentration of plastic, 10 milligrams per liter, to make the effect easy to see. The authors wrote that future work should test a range of doses to find the levels that matter in real water.

The fibers and the powder were also not chemically identical, since the fibers were a stiffer grade of nylon.

The work was funded by the NOAA Marine Debris Program. The group is now working with a commercial trout farm to find out which microplastics turn up in working tanks and where they come from.

Trout farms use nylon line and other plastic gear that can break down into microplastics, and the particles are also common in the water where wild fish live.

Earlier work has shown that microplastics can carry microbes and change how pathogens behave.

Recent findings suggest that people are also exposed to microplastic fibers through indoor dust. The authors wrote that the results call for research into whether microplastics change the course of infectious disease in wildlife and people.

They also want to know which features of a particle, such as rigidity and length, do the damage.

The full study was published in the journal Environmental Toxicology and Chemistry.
