Scientists can now uncover a person’s age, sex, diet, and DNA all from just one single tooth

Forensic scientists sliced a single tooth from each of 15 unidentified bodies and read birth year, age, sex, DNA, diet, and drug use from it.

By the time a body is discovered months or years after death, the face, fingerprints, and clothing are usually long gone. The remaining bones can give investigators a rough idea of sex and age, but that age estimate can be wrong by up to 30 years. Turns out, a single tooth holds far more answers.

Industry observers note that A team in Japan took a single tooth from each of 15 such bodies. From that tooth alone, they worked out each person’s birth year, age at death, sex, and DNA profile.

The same tooth also showed what kind of diet the person grew up on and which drugs they had been taking.

Hisako Saitoh, an associate professor of forensic dentistry at the Institute of Science Tokyo, led the work with her doctoral student, Sayaka Yamada.

It has been revealed that fuyuki Tokanai of the Center for Accelerator Mass Spectrometry at Yamagata University was also on the team. Colleagues at the University of Tokyo and Chiba University took part as well.

Sources indicate that all 15 people were unidentified when they reached the autopsy table. Five were skeletal remains, ten were badly decomposed. Fourteen of them were later identified.

Industry observers note that “Different tissues within a tooth preserve different forensic information. By combining multiple analytical methods, we sought to obtain as many clues as possible from a single tooth,” Saitoh remarked.

Bone is full of tiny channels for blood vessels and marrow. This means that soil, moisture, and microbes can get into it after death. Enamel is the hardest material in the human body, and it seals the crown of a tooth.

A thin layer called cementum wraps around the root, and this keeps outside substances away from the soft pulp inside.

A tooth also stops changing once it has formed. Bone rebuilds itself throughout life, but enamel laid down in early childhood stays chemically the same until death.

Industry observers note that that makes a tooth a sealed record of the years when it grew. This explains why researchers have used teeth to track where a mammoth or an elephant once lived.

Earlier forensic teams had combined several of these tests, but they used several teeth per person, or a tooth plus a toenail, and the bodies hadn’t been dead for long.

Saitoh’s team wanted to know how much information one tooth could provide after a body had been lying outdoors for months or years.

Industry observers note that the team chose the lower first premolar, the tooth just behind the canine. Its crown finishes forming at about age five, so its enamel records the first years of life. It has a single root, it’s easy to pull, and the lips cover the gap it leaves.

Each tooth was washed, dried overnight, and cut lengthwise into slices about 1/25 of an inch (1 millimeter) thick, which is thinner than a dime.

According to recent reports, enamel from one slice went to a radiocarbon lab, and a root slice rich in cementum went to DNA testing. Dentin from the other side went to an age test, and the central slice holding the pulp went to drug screening.

In a recent development, the age test relies on a slow chemical change. Amino acids in living tissue come in a left-handed form, and over a lifetime a small share flips to the right-handed mirror image.

The team measured that share in the dentin and read the person’s age off a curve built from six other premolars of known age.

The birth year came from radiocarbon, but not the kind used on fossils. Nuclear weapons tests in the 1950s and early 1960s sharply raised the amount of radioactive carbon in the air. The level peaked in 1963 and has been falling ever since.

In a recent development, enamel forming in a child’s mouth took up whatever level was in the air at the time, so its carbon indicates when it formed. Scientists have used the same spike to age a whale from its teeth.

Industry observers note that the catch is that the curve rises and then falls, so one reading usually matches two possible years. For one man, the enamel gave a birth year of either 1955 or 1979.

Recent findings suggest that adding the age-at-death estimate settled it. A birth in 1979 plus his estimated 58 years would put his death in 2036, so he was born in 1955. His real birth year was 1957.

Across the 15 people, the radiocarbon ranges included the true birth year in 14 cases. The age-at-death estimates on their own were off by about eight years on average.

Once the two tests were combined, the birth-year estimates landed within about five years of the truth, on average.

Sources indicate that DNA from the cementum slice gave the person’s sex in all 15 cases. In 12 of them it also produced a profile with enough genetic markers to match against a relative or a database.

The same kind of DNA work has recently corrected the story of a Roman-era skeleton in England and been read from Neanderthal teeth.

In a recent development, the pulp slice went through a drug screen covering 251 substances, and every one of the 15 teeth contained something, even if only caffeine. In nine cases the drugs in the tooth matched at least some of what the original autopsy had found in blood or muscle.

In five cases they matched prescriptions the person was known to have been taking, including antidepressants and antipsychotics.

The tooth didn’t catch everything, however. Drugs that appeared to have been taken shortly before death highlighted up in stomach contents and muscle at autopsy but not in the tooth.

Saitoh’s team suggested that drugs move into a tooth slowly, so a tooth reflects long-term use rather than a last dose.

The fifteenth body belonged to a man who has never been definitively identified. His tooth put his birth year at either 1956 or 1976, and his dentin put his age at about 38.

The 1956 option would mean he died in 1995, long before his body was found in 2013, so the team’s answer is a man born in 1976.

It has been revealed that investigators right now believe he was born around 1972 and was about 41 when he died. The two readings disagree by four years, which is inside the method’s average error of about five years.

The tooth gives investigators a second, independent estimate of who he might be.

Industry observers note that stable carbon in the enamel added a rough picture of childhood diet. All 15 readings fit a diet built on rice and wheat rather than corn, which is consistent with a traditional Japanese diet.

The authors wrote that carbon alone can’t say where someone grew up.

It has been revealed that the errors here were larger than in earlier studies that ran each test on its own. The birth-year estimates were least accurate for people born before the fallout spike began to climb and for those born in more recent years, when the curve had flattened.

It has been revealed that the age test usually works best on dentin from the center of the tooth. Here the center was needed for the drug screen. Instead, the team used dentin from the outer part, and that may have cost some accuracy.

One tooth also produced radiocarbon dates between the 600s and 800s AD, centuries before the woman could have been born.

That sample had the highest carbon content of the set, and the authors suspect carbon from outside the body had got into the enamel.

The team’s next step is more cases, and a closer look at exactly where in a tooth each slice should come from.

“Moving forward, we aim to further improve the accuracy and reproducibility of each biochemical analytical method demonstrated in this study and establish standardized methods that can be widely applied in routine forensic medical and dental practice,” Saitoh mentioned.

The full study was published in the journal Scientific Reports.
