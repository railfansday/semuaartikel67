Pay Off Your Home Before Retirement, Even If The Math Indicates Otherwise

  • Stocks fall 10% to 20% far more often than most people expect.
  • A 20% drop on a $500,000 portfolio is a painful $100,000 loss.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that every few years, the stock market reminds us why it's worth paying off your mortgage before retirement. Even if keeping a mortgage and investing the difference earns you more money on paper, a paid off home is one of the best moves you can make once you no longer want to work.

Stocks fall 10% to 20% far more often than most people expect. And the bigger your portfolio gets, the harder those swings are to stomach, even if you've kept your lifestyle inflation in check.

A 20% drop on a $500,000 portfolio is a painful $100,000 loss. But at that stage, you're probably still working and earning enough to make it back in a few years.

In a recent development, A 20% drop on a $5 million portfolio is a $1 million loss. If you're older and no longer working, there's no grinding your way out of it. You can only wait and hope.

According to recent reports, and if you follow a 4% withdrawal rate, that $1 million loss equals five years of retirement income gone. That's a devastating blow when you have no ability or desire to return to work.

I lost a little over three years of living expenses in the most recent downturn. It hurt. But having a paid off house made it much easier to sit tight and not catastrophize.

Now imagine adding a mortgage to the mix.

Recent findings suggest that let's say you retire with a $1 million mortgage at a 6.5% 30-year fixed rate. Your monthly payment is about $6,300. That's roughly $76,000 a year, every year, whether stocks are up or down.

With no day job, that money has to come from your investments or side hustles. At a 4% withdrawal rate, you'd need a $1.9 million portfolio just to cover the mortgage. That's before food, healthcare, property taxes, or your kids' activities.

Recent findings suggest that now imagine that portfolio falls 20%. Your $1.9 million is now $1.5 million, but the bank still wants its $6,300 every month. To keep paying, you'd either have to withdraw 5% a year or sell stocks at depressed prices. Neither is a great way to spend a bear market.

It has been revealed that triple everything and it gets uglier. A $3 million mortgage, which isn't unusual for a family home in San Francisco, New York City, or Los Angeles, costs about $19,000 a month, or $228,000 a year. You'd need $5.7 million invested just to cover it. After a 20% drop, that's $4.6 million left, and the bank still wants its $19,000 every month.

In a recent development, whatever the size of your mortgage, the math works the same way. Debt that feels manageable with a paycheck feels much heavier without one.

According to recent reports, the ratio holds no matter the size. For every $1 million of mortgage, you need about $1.9 million invested just to keep the bank happy. Carry a $5 million mortgage into retirement and you'll need almost $10 million invested before you've bought a single bag of groceries.

Recent findings suggest that this is one reason I paid cash for our house in 2023 instead of financing it and investing the difference. An all-cash offer helped me win the deal at a better price. Mortgage rates were high at around 6.7%, and I was happy to give up some upside for more peace of mind in retirement.

During every sell-off since, I've been glad I did.

The obvious fix is to buy a cheaper house with a smaller mortgage, or no mortgage at all.

But in San Francisco, Los Angeles, San Diego, Honolulu, Seattle, Miami, New York City, and Boston, a decent family home in a good school district can easily cost $2.5 million or more. And if you want something truly nice in SF and NYC, luxury generally starts around $5 million.

In a recent development, in other words, the $3 million to $5 million mortgages in the table above aren't fantasy numbers for people living in these cities. These are the everyday dual-income couples grinding 50-60-hours a week making $500,000 to $1 million a year on a perpetual treadmill, while paying the highest marginal tax rates possible.

It has been revealed that you could geoarbitrage to Boise to shrink your housing costs. But your job, friends, family, and kids' schools tend to have opinions about where you live.

So if you're going to own an expensive home in an expensive city, the next best move is to pay the mortgage down aggressively before you stop earning a paycheck.

I'm a major believer in owning at least your primary residence to get neutral real estate. Renting forever is like shorting the housing market. And shorting a long-term appreciating asset, like the S&P 500, is a bad idea.

According to recent reports, I bought my first property in 2003. In 2005, I bought a single-family home and rented out my first place. Today, I own my primary residence plus four rentals: three in San Francisco and one in Lake Tahoe. That semi-passive rental income is a substantial reason I was able to break free in 2012.

But there's one thing I'd do differently. I'd have entered early retirement with a paid off home.

Recent findings suggest that when I FIREd in 2012, I still had about a $1 million mortgage. It wasn't the end of the world, but it hung over me. My wife left her 13-year career in 2015, and once our son was born in 2017, the pressure of being the family's provider without a paycheck got much heavier.

In 2014, we bought a home on the west side of San Francisco for about 40% less than our previous house. A CD had just come due, and we were looking reinvest and lower our housing cost. We could have paid cash, but 5/1 ARM rates were only 2.5%, so I took out a smaller mortgage instead. In 2019, I refinanced into a 7/1 ARM at 2.625%.

Recent findings suggest that when I finally sold the larger house and wiped out its mortgage, I felt an enormous sigh of relief. The house was producing decent rental income, but the debt still weighed on me. It felt like taking off a weighted vest I'd forgotten I was wearing.

It was a level of freedom I didn't know existed, even after five years of early retirement.

In a recent development, plenty of people believe you should never pay off your mortgage and should invest the difference instead. I get it, especially if you locked in a sub-4% rate. Mathematically, it can make sense.

Industry observers note that but I have never once regretted paying off a mortgage. Here's why.

According to recent reports, if you already have enough to retire, you don't need to squeeze out more returns by investing the difference. What you start optimizing for is simplicity. One less account, one less bill, one less automatic payment to worry about.

It feels wonderful not to have a bank hold a lien on your home. You don't owe anybody anything. That's what freedom is supposed to feel like.

It has been revealed that the value of your home is the market rent you don't have to pay. With a mortgage, you don't fully capture that value. Once the mortgage is gone, your free cash flow jumps by the entire monthly payment.

In a recent development, for years, I plowed that freed-up cash flow into stocks and private investments – dollar-cost averaging. These days, I'm spending more of it on living. That's the whole point of saving for so long, isn't it?

When stocks melt down and you have a big mortgage, stress goes up. When stress goes up, you're more likely to panic sell at the worst possible time just to make sure you can pay your bills.

With a paid off home, that pressure declines tremendously. You can cover maintenance through cash flow. You'll still need to set aside money for property taxes and insurance, but those are small compared to a mortgage payment.

In a recent development, housing is usually the biggest expense for most households, followed by healthcare. Eliminate housing and life gets a lot easier to afford. You might even be able to take more investment risk because your downside is protected.

It has been revealed that let's say we enter an extended bear market and your stocks keep getting pummeled. Even if you lose a massive chunk of your funny money, you'll still have your paid off house. As long as you keep paying property taxes, nobody can take it away from you.

Psychologically, it's comforting to own a real asset that provides shelter and utility no matter what happens. Sure, your house can decline in value too. But at least you can enjoy it while it does. Your stocks just sit there on a screen looking sad. If you are the provider of the house, providing shelter, along with food, clothing, and love are the priorities.

Worst case, if everything else goes to zero, I still own a house on a big lot with views. I could sell it to some newly minted AI overlord, move the family to Hawaii, and live a simple life with the proceeds.

Recent findings suggest that when stocks are ripping higher, you may feel some regret having so much money tied up in your house. I certainly did after paying cash for our house in 2023.

But then came Liberation Day in April 2025, the Iran War sell-off in April 2026, and now the AI and semiconductor meltdown this November. Each time, I was glad I didn't have millions more invested in the market with a massive mortgage still to pay.

In a recent development, paying off your home is a forced de-risking that looks suboptimal in a bull market and wise in a bear market. Over time, your wealth should grow. And if your house is at my advised 30% of your net worth or less, then you still have plenty of net worth to risk in the stock market if you wish.

According to recent reports, to be fair, paying off your mortgage has real costs. You give up liquidity, because you can't buy groceries with your home equity. You also lose a cheap source of leverage if you locked in a 2% to 3% rate during the pandemic.

According to recent reports, if you have a 3% mortgage and can earn 4.8% risk-free in Treasury bills, keeping the mortgage is perfectly reasonable, and even encouraged. Just make sure you have a substantial enough cash cushion to cover at least a year of payments so a bear market doesn't force your hand.

It has been revealed that but if your mortgage rate is 5.5% or higher, paying it down is a guaranteed, after-tax 6% return. That's hard to beat in a market that just dropped 20%.

We should be thankful that banks are willing to lend us gobs of money to buy homes we usually can't pay for with cash. A mortgage is a great tool for getting into the game.

But once you're in, make it a goal to pay off the mortgage before you retire. The goal alone keeps you disciplined with your spending and saving. Pay down extra principal whenever you have extra money leftover. It adds up over the years.

According to recent reports, if you do pay it off, you'll have a solid asset to anchor your net worth even if everything else goes down. And if you're like most people with a paid off home, you probably have stocks, bonds, and other investments too. You're just making sure that if all else fails, your family still has a roof over its head.

Sources indicate that even if my stock portfolio went to zero tomorrow, about 55% of my net worth would still be giving my family shelter and generating rental income so we stay free. In a market like this, that feels a lot better than whatever extra return I might have earned by investing the difference.

And for those who already paid off their homes, have you ever regretted it?
