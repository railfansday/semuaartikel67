📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Calvin and Hobbes creator Bill Watterson is a genius. Over the course of ten years spanning more than 3,000 comic strips, he developed two truly irresistible characters that are still beloved by millions. There was a lot behind the success of Calvin and Hobbes, but I think part of the reason why Watterson is still so widely respected is that he was an incredibly versatile artist who managed to do a lot of different things in an artform that can sometimes be limiting.

While this selection only scratches the surface of what made Calvin and Hobbes such a brilliant creation, here are nine comic strips from the 1990s that illustrate Watterson’s incredible range as an artist.

It has been revealed that one of Watterson’s recurring themes in Calvin and Hobbes was his unabashed love and admiration for the natural world, a respect he channeled right into his two leading characters. Watterson returned to this subject many times and the points he made were always thoughtful. This one, from April 1992, is especially touching, as Calvin laments that humans have lost their “awe of nature.”.

Sometimes, Watterson seemed to use Calvin and Hobbes as an outlet for whatever big, philosophical questions were on his mind at the time. In this comic strip, Calvin and Hobbes have an in-depth conversation​​​​​​​ on the nature of luck. The best part about it is that they have it in a little wagon, careening down a giant hill. Calvin lands in some pricker bushes, which he seems to think is pretty unlucky.

According to recent reports, calvin often spoke well above the intelligence of a 6-year-old, but these comics were balanced out by adventures where Calvin is just being an imaginative little kid, like in this Sunday strip from 1995 where Calvin imagines meeting an alien who camouflages himself as Calvin. The real Calvin then goes to school pretending to be the alien, which lands him in the principal’s office.

Recent findings suggest that in addition to mastering the joke-a-day format demanded of comic strips, sometimes Watterson would tell longer stories which spanned several days or even a couple of weeks. For example, when his arch-nemesis, Rosalyn the babysitter, came over, their encounters often became epic sagas full of twists and turns​​​​​​​, like the story from 1990 in which Calvin drove Rosalyn nuts​​​​​​​ pretending to be Stupendous Man.

Recent findings suggest that there were many Calvin and Hobbes strips which were incredibly heartfelt, like this tender comic from 1993 that merely shows a day in the life of Calvin and Hobbes, with both longing to be reunited​​​​​​​. The best part about it is its complete lack of dialogue.

While Watterson was capable of being thoughtful and heartfelt, some of his comic strips featured a surprisingly dark sense of humor. This side of him was best captured by the strips depicting Calvin’s snowman sculptures, which always highlighted snowmen going through horrific things.

It has been revealed that this strip, from Jan. 21, 1991, contains​​​​​​​ three snowman sculptures in one. The first highlights a snowman being shot with a cannon, the second a snowman being buried alive, and the third a snowman being hanged by a noose.​​​​​​​ That last one is especially hard to picture alongside more child-friendly newspaper comics like Peanuts and The Family Circus.

Like most other comic strips, Calvin and Hobbes had a simple drawing style, but occasionally Watterson would find an excuse to do something in much more detail, like when Calvin would pretend he was a Tyrannosaurus rex, like he does in this Halloween-themed strip from 1993.

For the most part, Watterson worked within the traditional bounds of newspaper comic strips, but he never let those traditional rectangles hold him back from a really creative idea. His dinosaur comics were a great example of this, as he liked to make his dinosaurs as big as possible. Another example of Watterson breaking the traditional format is this August 1995 strip, in which Calvin had​​​​​​​ an extended internal monologue about his parents being aliens.

Sources indicate that of course, a collection of perfect Calvin and Hobbes comics from the 1990s would not be complete without Watterson’s final comic, which ran on Dec. 31, 1995. This open-ended sendoff was beautifully understated and deeply poignant. It could not have been better.
