Tech stocks are having a brutal November. The Nasdaq Composite (^IXIC -5.28%) is now down nearly 17% from its Oct. 6 peak. A correction means an index closes at least 10% below its record, while a bear market means a decline of 20% or more.

Several factors are weighing on the tech-heavy Nasdaq, including disappointing earnings from hyperscalers and a Blue Tsunami in the midterm elections that raised concerns about further regulations and restrictions on data centers.

Add in the Jensen Huang scandal and a Federal Reserve leaning toward more interest rate hikes, and investors have plenty to worry about. This is the Nasdaq's sixth correction in four years, which have overall been quite prosperous for investors.

History suggests these are the odds that the correction will turn into a full-blown bear market.

As I mentioned, while the stock market has had a terrific four years, it has been volatile and also had its fair share of ups and downs, including five corrections prior to the current one.

As you can see, corrections are more common than one might suspect. In fact, a 2024 Reuters study of LSEG data found that the Nasdaq has experienced 24 corrections over the past 44 years. According to Dow Jones Market Data, the Nasdaq has extended a correction into a bear market about 37% of the time since 1971.

According to recent reports, the good news is that the majority of corrections tend to revert back higher. The bad news is that there is still a decent chance that a correction could morph into a full-blown bear market, and the Nasdaq is not far from one as of this writing.

Sources indicate that this is the reality for Nasdaq investors. Investing more heavily in tech can yield fantastic gains, but typically entails greater turbulence. History also tells us the Nasdaq has generated fantastic gains. Since the turn of the century, the Nasdaq has generated a total return of 467%, besting the broader benchmark S&P 500's total return of nearly 379%.

This includes the Dot-Com Bubble, in which the Nasdaq lost roughly 77% of its value between 2000 and 2002. So, long-term investors can stay invested in the Nasdaq, but it really depends on your risk tolerance. If you can't handle big swings multiple times a year, then the Nasdaq might not be the right index for you.
