Tech Report: Tropical land-use intensification reshapes biodiversity in expected and unexpected ways

Industry observers note that this is a summary of: Guerrero-Ramírez, N. R. et al. Tropical land-use intensification restructures biodiversity. Nature https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11298-6 (2026).

Tropical rainforests contain extraordinary biodiversity, but large areas have been converted into agricultural plantations. This transformation is often accompanied by farming intensification, in which diverse vegetation is replaced by monocultures to increase production1. Losses of local species because of such intensified practices are well documented, but less is known about how agriculture alters the composition of biological communities across landscapes. One possible outcome is biotic homogenization, in which intensive land use makes these communities in agricultural sites increasingly similar to each other, compared with those of forests. Conversely, they might become more dissimilar in species composition in agricultural lands — called biotic differentiation. However, distinct groups of organisms might respond in contrasting ways that are not captured by measuring changes in the total number of species, or species richness. Understanding how biodiversity changes within and between sites is crucial for designing agricultural landscapes that reconcile farming with biodiversity conservation2.

We surveyed 24 groups of organisms, including plants, birds, bats, arthropods, soil animals and microorganisms, and recorded roughly 40,000 species across a gradient of land-use intensification in Sumatra, Indonesia. The sites ranged from tropical lowland forests and traditional rubber agroforests (in which rubber trees grow alongside other vegetation) to intensively managed smallholder cash-crop monocultures of rubber and oil palm. We could therefore test similarities and differences in biodiversity responses to various types of land use in this hyper-biodiverse region. We distinguished between changes within multi-taxon communities that arise from species replacement, and variation because of species-poor communities that contain subsets of the species found at richer sites.

We found marked biodiversity losses with agricultural intensification. Compared with forests, monocultures had around 50% lower local multidiversity (average species richness across the 24 groups) and 78% lower landscape-level multidiversity. Notably, traditional rubber agroforests retained many forest-associated species that were absent from monocultures (Fig. 1).

It has been revealed that unexpectedly, communities did not consistently become more similar at sites with the same land-use type than at forest sites. Some groups, such as understorey plants, soil bacteria and fungi, became homogenized, whereas others — arthropods in particular — became more distinct between agricultural sites. Aboveground communities in monoculture sites differed strongly because species-poor sites contained subsets of the species present at richer ones. This indicates that apparent differences between communities can conceal a progressive simplification of biodiversity.

Our results reveal how tropical land-use intensification restructures biodiversity across several organism groups and spatial scales through concurrent changes in species richness and community composition. Tropical forests and traditional agroforestry systems are irreplaceable biodiversity reservoirs, but their biodiversity is imperilled and requires urgent conservation and restoration. In addition to prioritizing forest preservation, conservation strategies should recognize the value of traditional agroforestry systems, which can buffer the loss of ‘generalist’ species found in both forest and agricultural land. These systems are increasingly converted to more intensive plantations, and are often overlooked in environmental and landscape planning. Implementing sustainable practices for monocultures could help to mitigate biodiversity losses and promote landscape differentiation3,4. Conservation strategies should account for the distinct responses of taxonomic groups and incorporate landscape-scale measures to maintain forest-associated biodiversity.

Our study focused on smallholder-dominated landscapes in Sumatra. The findings therefore might differ in other tropical regions or in large, more intensely managed industrial plantations5. Moreover, our ‘snapshot’ data cannot reveal whether observed differences among communities will persist or eventually give way to increasing similarity. Long-term studies should assess whether greater differences in species composition between sites reflect persistently distinct, species-rich ecosystems or uneven species loss.

It has been revealed that future research should track biodiversity changes over time and across large landscapes. Understanding whether community differences persist or represent transitional stages of biodiversity loss will help to inform management practices intended to maintain biodiverse communities while supporting agricultural production. — Holger Kreft and Nathaly R. Guerrero-Ramírez are at the University of Göttingen, Germany.

“What makes this study unusual is the sheer amount of data collected. Across a considerable geographical scale, the authors analyse several taxa from microbes to birds and trees, and a wide range of land uses, from low-intensity agroforestry to high-intensity agricultural monocultures. Using this impressive data set, the authors find some expected results, such as loss of diversity owing to agricultural intensity, and some unexpected ones.” — Thilo Gross is at the HIFMB Oldenburg, Oldenburg, Germany.

This study emerged from a long-term collaboration between researchers in Indonesia and Germany investigating the ecological consequences of tropical land-use change. Bringing together expertise spanning plants, animals and microorganisms enabled us to examine biodiversity on an unusually comprehensive scale. Besides the many logistical difficulties of organizing and conducting such demanding field surveys, one of the greatest challenges was integrating data collected by numerous specialists, using distinct sampling methods and taxonomic approaches, into a common analytical framework. The resulting data set, encompassing roughly 40,000 species across taxa, provided a rare opportunity for us to assess a representative fraction of tropical biodiversity and look beyond species richness. Particularly striking was the realization that substantial biodiversity losses were not accompanied by consistent homogenization across communities. This unexpected finding prompted us to examine more closely the processes that underlie differences in community composition, revealing patterns that would have remained hidden in analyses focused on species counts alone. — H.K. and N.R.G.-R.

This study stood out for the breadth of data brought to bear on the topic of what the impacts of land-use change are on biodiversity. It also highlights the lack of evidence for consistent homogenization across communities as land use intensifies. — Editorial team, Nature.
