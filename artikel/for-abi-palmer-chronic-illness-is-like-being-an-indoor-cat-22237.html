For Abi Palmer, Chronic Illness Is Like Being an Indoor Cat

“I have been trying so hard to capture the smell of rain for you,” an unseen narrator with a British accent explains in Abi Palmer Invents the Weather (2023), as cats slink across the screen. “But being as I am no god or scientist,” the voice continues, as human hands wring out a cloth soaking in a jar of dirty water, “I am forced to work backward.” Soon, dripping white fabric is seen hanging from twine inside a cardboard box, clumped like a cloud.

This non-god, the artist Abi Palmer, is recreating the weather for her two indoor cats, mixing rainwater with moss and earth. All the while, she muses on how chronic illness keeps her inside, catlike, yet also binds her intricately to meteorological variation. Palmer alludes to the ways joint pain can flare when pressure drops or smoky skies are felt by some in the lungs or in the back of the throat. As it happens, Palmer got her start making art in the form of wet white wads. As an “unsupervised child,” as she told me from her London apartment, she’d combine water and tissue into globby sculptures—“blob creatures”—then let them dry. Recently, making massive slug sculptures inspired by the way the species survives by ugliness, she realized how consistent her materials and methods remained. At the University of Cambridge, she studied literature, as is evident in her voice-overs and her two books: Sanitorium (2020) and Slugs: A Manifesto (2024). But soon, she found her hands hurt too much to type—though her muscles were “really bendy” and thus “good at squeezing things into quite unusual shapes,” as she had intuited at a very young age.

Sources indicate that it’s not so common, Palmer told me, for cats to live indoors in the UK: Some pet people even consider keeping them inside cruel. But her housing and health situations didn’t allow for her animals to roam freely, never mind that domestic cats kill billions of birds each year. So her video was born of a bit of guilt, an attempt at “navigating the imperfection of the world, but also the magic and beauty of it.” She admitted: “It’s absurd.”.

According to recent reports, for Palmer, retaining a childlike sense of play is essential. She came to art with little technical training. As she started to receive institutional recognition and awards—she’s been in group reveals at the National Museum of Contemporary Art (EMST) in Athens, Goldsmiths in London, EMAP in Seoul, and elsewhere, in addition to receiving the 2025 Heny Moore Foundation Award—she was struck by the haunting sense that she ought to be doing things more “properly.” But she realized: “The moment I start taking things too seriously, it’s a trap.” The external rewards come and go, often without much in the way of remuneration—so it helps to have fun with art. “I take play very seriously,” Palmer said.

Her work attests: In Abi Palmer Invents the Weather she not only elaborately simulates meteorological conditions but highlights the video within playful cardboard constructions that she reconfigures for each exhibition: a feline paradise. In a world that tends to address disability and illness in the gravest of tones, Palmer infuses humor. For disabled people, humor can be a survival tactic, a way of putting others at ease with difference—often, not without some resentment. But it is also simply true that life is never one-note.
