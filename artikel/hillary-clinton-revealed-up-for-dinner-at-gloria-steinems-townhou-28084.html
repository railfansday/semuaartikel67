Hillary Clinton Revealed Up For Dinner at Gloria Steinem’s Townhouse

It’s a dark and stormy November night at Gloria Steinem’s townhouse, and Hillary Clinton has something to say about the 2026 election. “One of my most anticipated moments will be watching Donald Trump walk into the State of the Union through the now more than one hundred women members of the House of Representatives. I just want them all to stare at him and say, ‘You fired us from jobs we were qualified for. You insulted us. You demeaned our ambition. You undermined our place in government and society. And we’re here.’”.

Huma Abedin, Busy Philips, and Paloma Elsesser.

Recent findings suggest that the former Secretary of State and 2016 presidential candidate is a last-minute but highly anticipated arrival to this dinner honoring Steinem, the late activist. She passed away in September, but the feminist pathfinder was known for throwing these salon-type events (she called them “circles”) at her Upper East Side townhouse, which she bought for just $30,000 in the late ’60s. It’s overflowing with books (I spy Marilyn by Norman Mailer, Jane Fonda’s War by Mary Hershberger, and several Eleanor Roosevelt biographies on the shelves), pictures (there’s one framed of Steinem alongside Mariska Hargitay) and Indian art. (Steinem spent two formative years in the country.) There are magnets in her kitchen of Wonder Woman, Frida Kahlo, and a cloth finger puppet of Hillary Clinton. A political button is pinned to it that claims “Another Bitch for Hillary.” The whole place smells like a Cire Trudon candle, and everyone’s drinking negronis. (Which actually isn’t technically very Steinem—her favorite cocktail was a margarita—but the negronis go with the night’s dinner, prepared by Lilia chef Missy Robbins.) Yet this is one of the first events at the townhouse that Steinem herself isn’t here to lead.

Jihae, Lux Pascal, Naomi Watts, and Bethann Hardison.

Amy Richards, a feminist activist whom many consider to be the daughter Steinem never had—a title Richards rejects because her mentor “didn’t want kids”—stood up at one point to tell the crowd that this is what Steinem would have wanted. Growing up with a transient father and a mentally volatile mother, the late activist took near-obsessive care in maintaining a welcoming home she could share with others. “As long as I knew Gloria, she always said, ‘I just want my home to be the way it’s always been.’ She wanted it to be filled with people,” Richards indicates. “She didn’t grow up with a home. She was traveling the country for the first 10 years of her life and then lived with her mother, who was not very stable.”.

According to recent reports, at least Steinem’s still here on the wall: Months before her death, she posed for a series of portraits by Paola Kudacki for Weldun, a platform that offers physically adaptive and functional fashion. It was a bit of a small miracle that Steinem said yes. “I will say, Gloria did not always love to be photographed,” Richards says. “Especially in her later years—not because of wrinkles or aging, but she was just like, ‘Aren’t there enough pictures of me?’” Weldun and the Gloria Steinem Foundation are presenting tonight’s dinner, and mementos of Steinem are everywhere. On the fireplace mantle sits a pair of her signature Ray-Ban aviators. But Gloria’s stuff isn’t the same as Gloria herself.

Former secretary of state Hillary Clinton hugging Melissa McCarthy.

Industry observers note that which is probably why, in a crowd of women that include Huma Abedin, Mariska Hargitay, Padma Lakshmi, Melissa McCarthy, and Bethann Hardison—who, at the ripe age of 84, shows up wearing Gucci—Clinton’s unexpected arrival feels seismic, like Gloria sent someone in her place. (Hardison tells me she thinks Steinem used whatever divine power she’s got up there to launch the Blue Wave: “She’s probably saying she helped it happen!”) Whatever your views on Clinton’s politics, she was the first woman to receive a presidential nomination from a major party. Paloma Elsesser and I sit crouched in a corner of the room, glued to the window. “There’s mother,” the supermodel whispers to me as Clinton exits her car into the pouring rain and walks up to the townhouse door.

Guests at dinner honoring the late Gloria Steinem.

According to recent reports, “Hi, everybody,” Clinton says as she walks in. The entire dinner comes to a silent halt. “Hi,” the room responds in unison. Madame Secretary doesn’t seem to like the fuss, probably because 30 or so people intensely staring at you when you arrive at a party is objectively unsettling. “Go back to eating!” she says, motioning to the Missy Robbins meal on the table. Clinton sits down next to Naomi Watts and warmly greets Busy Philips, who stumped for Clinton back in 2016. Hargitay blows her a kiss across the table. All anyone wants to do is talk to Clinton about the 2026 election, in which Democrats won at least 55 seats in the Senate and 256 in the House to take both majorities in the US Congress. At the end of the meal—and after a proper catch-up with Shonda Rhimes—Clinton tries to leave, but she can’t. Guests block the door, clamoring to chat her up.

Recent findings suggest that amid the Clinton traffic jam, I ask Hargitay about the photo. She can’t remember exactly what it is from. “Was it Bravo? Was it with Andy…what’s his name?” But she can guess what she thinks Steinem might say about the blue wave: “There is a God!”.

I watch Clinton finally make her exit. It’s late, so I wait several minutes before I descend the stairs to leave too. (I wait for Elsesser, Watts, Hardinn, and Lux Pascal to finish taking a selfie on the steps.) But I spoke too soon: Clinton’s stuck waiting for the coat check, another group of women crowded around her hoping to say something—or, more likely, hoping she states something. With the way this night is going, Clinton might be the last one at the party.
