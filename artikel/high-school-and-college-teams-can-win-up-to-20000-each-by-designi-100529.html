High school and college teams can win up to $20,000 each by designing technology for NASA’s Moon Base, and sign-ups close January 25

Industry observers note that NASA's Moon Base Challenge invites U.S. high school and college teams to design technology for a lunar base.

The Moon Base Challenge was declared on November 8 at a MAX POWER event at the Kennedy Space Center Visitor Complex in Florida. It is open to student teams at U.S. high schools and post-secondary schools.

Teams do not need prior specialized engineering or technical experience. In the final round, they can explain an idea with a mock-up made of cardboard. Students have helped with real research before, as when students helped astronomers track a rare alien comet.

NASA is building a Moon Base where astronauts will live, work and explore in the lunar South Pole region. The agency indicates a permanent human presence on the Moon paves the way for future crewed missions to Mars.

Industry observers note that the region may contain resources that could one day help support explorers. NASA looks to send a nuclear-powered rover called PROMISE to search for evidence of water ice and other resources there.

Industry observers note that “We’re looking for students ready to think creatively, work as a team, and take on real problems connected to NASA’s future on the Moon,” said NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman. “You do not have to wait until graduation to contribute.”.

“Students bring exactly the bold ideas that we’re looking for,” remarked Carlos García-Galán, manager of the Moon Base Program.

Each team picks one of six categories: power generation and storage, habitat construction using lunar materials, life-support systems, surface mobility, communication networks, or astronaut safety.

Habitat ideas can go beyond engineering, since researchers have argued that Moon bases could be designed to ease anxiety and homesickness.

It has been revealed that the designs have to work in a harsh place. According to the official rules, they must handle vacuum, radiation, micrometeorites, abrasive dust and temperature swings from 248 degrees Fahrenheit (120 degrees Celsius) to minus 274 degrees Fahrenheit (minus 170 degrees Celsius).

They also have to cope with day and night cycles that last two weeks each, and rely as little as possible on supplies from Earth.

Water is one of the hard parts: one analysis found that the Moon can generate enough power for a permanent city, but not enough water.

A team has two to five students and an adult coach, at least 18, who holds a formal role at the team’s school or organization, such as a teacher, faculty member or club advisor.

There are three divisions, judged separately. High school teams are for students in grades 9 to 12 who were between 13 and 18 years old when registration opened, including home-schooled students.

The other two divisions are for undergraduates at two-year schools, such as community and technical colleges, and at four-year colleges and universities.

Industry observers note that those students must be at least 16. A mixed team competes in the division of its most advanced or oldest member. Middle school and graduate students cannot take part.

Students must be U.S. citizens, lawful permanent residents or foreign nationals who are full-time students at an eligible U.S. school and physically live in the United States for the whole challenge.

Every student has to create an account on NASA’s STEM Gateway, and only the student team lead submits the application. Coaches should not register there; they are linked to the team later on the organizer’s platform.

In the first phase, teams write a Concept Overview Paper of 5 to 7 pages. It must explain how the idea works and how it survives lunar conditions, and include at least one page of diagrams or other visuals. Papers are due February 22, 2027.

Up to 100 teams will be named finalists on March 11. They will then record a pitch video of up to 10 minutes, with a deck of up to 15 slides, due April 19.

Recent findings suggest that finalists can explain their design with hand-drawn diagrams, posterboard schematics or low-cost mock-ups made of cardboard, foam or household materials. CAD software and physical prototypes are allowed but optional. Winners will be confirmed on May 6, 2027.

NASA’s announcement calls it a $1 million challenge, and the challenge website says up to $1 million in monetary awards are available.

The official rules for the 2026-27 challenge, however, list a total maximum prize payout of $500,000, and neither source explains the difference.

Sources indicate that each of up to 100 finalist teams gets $1,400. In the final phase, up to 12 grand prize winners get $20,000 each, and up to 12 runners-up get $10,000 each.

The rules also say payments depend on federal funding and NASA’s final approval, and NASA does not have to award the full amount if too few entries meet its quality bar.

The money goes to a U.S. bank account designated by the team’s coach, who decides how it is shared. Teams keep the rights to what they invent, although NASA may negotiate a license to use it.

A virtual opening symposium introducing the categories is set for December 3. Registered teams can also join live question-and-answer webinars on January 14 and February 11, 2027. The organizers say all dates may change.

Industry observers note that the challenge is run for NASA by the Institute of Competition Sciences. Full rules and registration steps are on the official challenge website.

The information was obtained from NASA’s announcement, its Moon Base page and the official rules.
