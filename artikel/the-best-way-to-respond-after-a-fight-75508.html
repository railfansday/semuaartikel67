The Best Way to Respond After a Fight

It has been revealed that comedian Brian Gaar has said, “Relationships are mostly you apologizing for saying something hilarious.” He is right that relationships involve differences about what is hilarious, and that partners need to apologize. People are human, with the weaknesses and differences that are part of life. Every couple gets off course at times, which can lead to each person becoming more attached to their position, resulting in a heated back-and-forth over who is correct. Proving a point can become more vital than stopping the harm that is piling up.

Marriage researcher John Gottman has mentioned that some fighting couples get trapped in the roach motel, where, as the old advertisement put it, “they get in, but they can’t get out.” These relationship “disasters,” as he calls them, contrast with relationship “masters” who have figured out how to either stop, calm down, or focus on solutions. One therapy approach calls this ability to exit damaging situations “workability.” This is a focus on what helps, instead of worrying about who did what. It may be true that the other acted like a toddler, slamming the door and stomping feet, but it might be more workable to focus on what would improve the current mood. It may be accurate to say they were wrong in the facts about what your mother actually said, but is that helpful to argue about? Is it moving the relationship forward? In throwing out opinions about who is right and wrong, it is good to consider the old advice: Is it true? Is it kind? Is it necessary? And, is it workable? Workability does not ask, “Who is right?” but “What is the right thing to do?”.

One of the most workable actions in a relationship is a repair attempt. Time will heal many wounds, but injuries heal faster with treatment. This can happen in a kind conversation that contains conciliatory efforts. One study looked directly at the power of healing gestures. People filled out questionnaires and described incidents in which they had recently been hurt. The researchers followed participants for several weeks to find out what the offender did to make things right. Some did nothing, but others apologized, made recompense, and had a peacemaking attitude. The more steps the offender took, the more the other forgave. Positive attempts changed the way that the hurt person viewed the relationship and the other. Trying to make things right is almost always a step in the right direction.

Couple therapist and researcher Sue Johnson offers guidelines for addressing injuries that are not healing on their own. First, partners should speak about the hurt honestly and simply. This should focus on the feelings, not the fault of the one who caused it. The listening partner acknowledges the pain without getting defensive or excusing themselves. Second, the injurer needs to take ownership of what they did. This needs to be done without downplaying what happened. A real apology is needed, not a "Sorrrrreeee! Sheesh!” Even if the offender doesn’t agree on every particular—and they probably won’t—it is notable to not get stuck rehashing details. Instead, partners should converse about what needs to be done next. This can be done by focusing on underlying needs, such as safety, responsiveness, and connection. This will provide a sense of moving forward and is reassuring. If couples can talk through the events without getting upset, it helps rework the memory into a less bitter version.

Industry observers note that it is important to nurture and treat wounds, but that doesn’t fix things immediately. Someone can apologize profusely for breaking another’s arm, but the pain will still last for a while. The person who caused the injury has to allow this and not get frustrated: “I noted I was sorry, just go use your arm already!” They also must feel regret for the pain, not just the problems it has created. Sincere apologies help couples recalibrate and commit to do better. The next time you say something hilarious but unhelpful, resist the urge to escalate. Try a sincere repair instead.
