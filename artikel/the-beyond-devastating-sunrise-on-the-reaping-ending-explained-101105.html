📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that crying too hard at the end of The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping to fully grasp those final scenes? It can happen to the best of us. Let’s break down the ending of Sunrise on the Reaping to make sure you didn’t miss anything, including some of the things that Suzanne Collins’ book explains in a tiiiiiny bit more detail.

Industry observers note that it should come as no surprise that Haymitch wins the Hunger Games. You should know that going in, even if you haven’t read Sunrise on the Reaping. When we first met the character in The Hunger Games, he was Katniss and Peeta’s mentor because he was District 12’s only living victor.

Recent findings suggest that what we didn’t totally know before the release of this book, and subsequent film, was that Haymitch teamed up with other recent victors like Beetee, Mags, and Wiress (as well as future gamemaker Plutarch Heavensbee) to try and take down the games from within. As we first learned that in Catching Fire, the flaws that Haymitch found in the arena contributed to his victory.

Industry observers note that president Snow does not like to be beaten and finds creative ways to get revenge against those who double-cross him. We see that early on with Beetee’s son Ampert, who gets reaped to punish Beetee for how he won his games. We see it later with Peeta and Katniss. And Haymitch is allowed to return home at the expense of his mother, brother, and girlfriend. All three die horrifically as soon as he gets back. As many have said: Haymitch didn’t drink enough.

Industry observers note that at the end of the film, we see how President Snow’s revenge against Haymitch’s community causes him to spiral fast. With his family and girlfriend dead, in addition to the PTSD from surviving the Hunger Games, he becomes inconsolable and addicted to sleeping syrup. It does not help that he is the only person living in Victor’s Village; because District 12’s only other victor, Lucy Gray Baird, went missing 40 years prior.

According to recent reports, one day, Plutarch comes to visit Haymitch ahead of his victory tour to film an upgrade for the Capitol News, and they have a chat. (In the book, this conversation happens during the tour.) He wants Haymitch to keep working with them to overthrow the Capitol. He refuses. “I am living proof the Capitol always wins,” Haymitch says. “You need someone different from me.”.

Plutarch insists that they need someone exactly like him, actually. Even if it takes generations and they don’t get to see the results, they have to keep imagining a different future so that one day it comes true. It doesn’t make them heroes. It doesn’t even make them good people. But they’re trying.

Haymitch drifts away from two of his only friends, Burdock Everdeen and Asterid March. If you couldn’t tell from their names, or didn’t hear what they call their infant daughter, yes! Those are Katniss’ parents. (Also, yes! Burdock Everdeen is a member of the Covey. That’s why he sings one of Lucy Gray’s songs, "The Old Therebefore," at the funeral for District 12’s tributes. One day, he’ll change the course of history by teaching her “The Hanging Tree.”.

The main reason that Haymitch cuts himself off is grief and guilt. He wants to be alone with his pain. Losing the people he was closest too makes him feel like he doesn’t deserve to be close to anyone, and maybe shouldn’t for their own protection. Movie protagonists are always thinking that, aren’t they? Like, okay Spider-Man! Whatever you say!

But the book reveals another, even more devastating reason: when baby Katniss grows up and starts wearing her hair in pigtail braids, she looks just like Haymitch’s “sweetheart” and first ally, Louella.

Sources indicate that as we know from reading and watching The Hunger Games, the rebellion in Panem did eventually find the right victors to raise an army and end the games for good in District 12’s own Peeta Mellark and Katniss Everdeen. They were able to finally implement the plans that Beetee, Wiress, and the other victors had been testing since the second Quarter Quell, if not before.

Without even knowing it, Peeta and Katniss helped Haymitch keep his promise to Lenore Dove. So, of course, the story has to end with them. We see Haymitch, again played by Woody Harrelson, finally celebrating his birthday with no reapings. In Collin’s novel, Haymitch tells Katniss and Peeta about Lenore Dove in order to add her to a District 12 book of memories they’re putting together. Katniss gifts him goose eggs to hatch and raise. The film, however, takes a simpler approach, and just shows old man Haymitch greeting his former mentees before going to spend some alone time with the geese. He’s finally earned some peace.
