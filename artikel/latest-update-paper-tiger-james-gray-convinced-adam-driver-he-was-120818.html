Latest Update: ‘Paper Tiger’: James Gray Convinced Adam Driver He Was Playing the Hero, Then Crafted His Mythic End

Editor’s Note: The following story contains spoilers for “Paper Tiger.”.

One night, after his second child was born, James Gray found himself up late watching C-SPAN coverage of the Global Economic Forum in Davos, and saw something he held onto for years.

“This corporate CEO noted, ‘95% of people don’t know there’s a game, and they lose,'” said Gray on IndieWire’s Filmmaker Toolkit Podcast. “Of the remaining 5%, 4% know there’s a game and don’t know how to play it, and 1% knows there’s a game and wins. And he said it with evident pride. I thought it revolting.”.

In a recent development, the CEO’s words hit home for Gray, showing a “cruel economic system that people can’t control,” where luck, timing, and “having skills that are popular at the right time” decide who wins. Sources indicate that “I would love a system where everyone got rewarded, not one that picks winners and losers,” said Gray. “The idea of losers breaks my heart. The film was about my heartbreak over the world’s losers.”.

Recent findings suggest that the script for “Paper Tiger” centers on three lead characters, each representing a different side of Gray’s cautionary tale about the dangers of chasing the American Dream. Of his three main characters, Gary (Adam Driver) is best positioned to be one of the “winners.”.

Industry observers note that “[Gary] is the one person who knows the game, knows how to play it, and just [can’t] live with himself if he makes certain choices that might allow him to, quote-unquote, ‘win,’” mentioned Gray of Driver’s character. “We don’t see that much in movies. We see people who are ‘bad guys’ who make the wrong choice, or ‘good guys’ who make the right choice and then wind up winning for it.”.

Gary, the ex-cop turned savvy businessman, presents himself as comfortable crossing that ethical line, but as the story slowly reveals, a strong moral code lies beneath Gary’s slick, tailored suit and wheeler-and-dealer image. It’s a distinction Gray noted Driver didn’t fully grasp at first.

“I would go up to [Adam’s] apartment, and we would talk about it, and I remember he was struggling and struggling and struggling, ‘Oh, what am I doing?’” said Gray. “One day in rehearsals, I said, ‘He has only the best intentions all the time. We have to love him. He always means well. He’s not a sleazeball.’ And Adam went, ‘Oh, I got it.’”.

Part of Driver’s problem was that Gary’s latest scheme places his brother Irwin (Miles Teller) and his family in the crosshairs of ruthless Russian mobsters, but Gray warned it was crucial, for both Driver and the audience, to understand that Gary’s actions weren’t particularly reckless at the time.

“We have the wisdom of 2026 about where organized crime would go in New York, but in 1986, the Russian organization was a totally unknown quantity,” said Gray. “He would not, as a retired police officer, have any knowledge of that.”.

Recent findings suggest that in 1986, the Italian mob’s stranglehold on the Gowanus Canal — where Gary is trying to help Irwin score a lucrative contract as an environmental consultant — had been decimated by RICO prosecutions. There was an assumption of a upcoming day dawning, as different immigrant groups began moving into the vacuum left behind by the aggressive prosecution of the Genovese crime family. No one imagined, according to Gray, that one mob would ultimately be replaced by a far more violent and ruthless mob altogether.

Sources indicate that “The sadness to me was that the story of immigration in Fresh York at that time was, ‘Oh, these are dissidents, these are engineers, they’re scientists, they’re doctors.’ The expectation wasn’t they were going to set up gasoline, oil, and racketeering. None of that was on the plate,” stated Gray, before connecting his point back to Driver. “Adam had the key now that everything was not done for nefarious reasons, that he was trying to bring his brother along. As soon as he could approach it that way, it really opened things up for him.”.

Much like the rest of the world, the ruthlessness of the Russian mob would catch Gary by surprise, but it would be how Gary responded to the threat that got Gray’s storytelling juices flowing.

“I was anxious to show a character who you might think would be the one who does the wrong thing, but in fact, does something beautiful in death, which is he sacrifices himself,” remarked Gray.

Sources indicate that this last part is notable to Gray, who discussed how his three young adult children constantly complain that “there’s nothing on TV,” despite having more channels and streaming services than ever before. “They always say that to me,” explained Gray, “and what they’re really saying is there’s very little with a streak of grandeur or nobility in it, because there’s a kind of smallness to the storytelling when you don’t have that quality.”.

Sources indicate that the question then became how to give Driver’s Gary that nobility in death, and build to a third act that gave Gray’s smaller film a sense of grandeur. As with most of his films, Gray drew on his life experiences growing up in Queens, along with great films from the past.

In Howard Beach, Queens, down by JFK Airport, a long stretch of marshes surrounded by tall reeds (phragmites grass) stands in sharp contrast to the urban landscape on the horizon.

“ My brother and I got lost in these very tall weeds,” said Gray. “I was very young. And it’s a recurring nightmare of mine, getting lost in there. And you have no sense of direction. Immediately, it’s extremely disorienting.”.

Industry observers note that gray had tried to use the location in his 2007 film “We Own the Night,” but he wasn’t satisfied with the results. In “Paper Tiger,” he cracked how to stage and shoot in the tall grasses, drawing direct inspiration from Masaki Kobayashi’s 1967 “Samurai Rebellion,” resulting in Gary’s last stand being one of the most memorable sequences in recent American movie memory.

In a recent development, A key was how Gray drew meaning from the setting: “I was trying to give Adam Driver‘s character a nobility, but the other part of it is how do you give his character a mythic dimension? And through death, a lot of the times, you can imply transcendence, and I was trying to say that his character was essentially going back to the primordial ooze, that he was returning to something that had nothing to do with the urban landscape.”.

Industry observers note that on the podcast, Gray talked about how the final scene is stripped of period signifiers, with no glimpse of the urban landscape beyond the weeds, allowing the sequence to “devolve or evolve so that [Gary] would be at peace as he died.” The moment was further brought to life through an effective use of sound, rather than a more traditional score for such an operatic ending. “I just decided this is about him transcending, and so you hear — the track is very weird — birds and insects. There’s the intrusion of aircraft, but it’s also the calm of the wind and the undulation of the phragmites grass. And I just thought that was a way to separate his character and say that he’s reached a kind of mythic status.”.

One lesson Gray took from his previous (and in Gray’s mind less successful) attempt to film in the Howard Beach marsh was that he would need more time for the hot and hellish shoot, in which the filmmakers experienced disorientation not unlike Gray did as a kid.

Sources indicate that “There’s one shot which is up high where you see these guys converging on Adam’s character. And I remember how difficult the shot was because the minute you put the actor in there and the camera went up here, we couldn’t find the actors in the weeds,” said Gray. “It’s very upsetting and disorienting even to just shoot in that setting, to say nothing of the ticks. But that’s a whole other story.”.
