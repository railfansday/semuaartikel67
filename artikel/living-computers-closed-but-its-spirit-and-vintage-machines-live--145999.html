When Seattle’s Living Computers: Museum + Labs closed for good in 2024, it seemed like the end of the line for anyone who wanted to do more than just look at old computers.

But in a Tukwila business park 20 minutes south of downtown, a sizable collection of vintage computers is still alive — kept running by a few of the same people who once did the same for Paul Allen.

It has been revealed that the Interim Computer Museum is a nonprofit that opened to the public in 2024 and has quietly grown into a hands-on showcase of dozens of machines, from early Apples and IBM PCs to hulking DEC systems that once filled university labs. At 4,400 square feet of public space, it’s nearly as significant as Living Computers was before that museum expanded in 2016.

ICM is led by Stephen Jones, who calls himself the museum’s “caretaker.” Jones spent two decades helping track down and restore machines for Allen, first at PDP Planet, Allen’s private collection of vintage systems, then at Living Computers, where he was senior engineering manager.

According to recent reports, when the late Microsoft co-founder’s estate wound down Living Computers, the majority of the collection went to the Mimms Museum of Technology and Art in Georgia, where Jones is also a curator. The machines in Tukwila are privately owned, and Jones has helped bankroll ICM with personal funds, with plans to eventually donate the collection to the nonprofit.

The Mimms museum now holds the Living Computers trademark and has granted ICM “legacy use” of the name, but Jones noted he didn’t want to revive it as ICM’s own because “it has a finite past.” Instead, he wants to carry on “the spirit of the engineering focus.”.

“Between Mimms and here, this is the legacy,” Jones noted. “We show the name and the connection to the past, but we’re doing newly introduced things.”.

ICM occupies space in Seattle Exchange @ Southcenter, a sprawl of low-slung flex buildings off Strander Boulevard near Westfield Southcenter mall. Its neighbors include a car detailer, a pest control company, a welding shop, an embroiderer and an upholsterer.

Industry observers note that more than four decades ago, Seattle Computer Products operated just down Industry Drive. That’s where Tim Paterson wrote 86-DOS, the operating system Microsoft bought and turned into MS-DOS for IBM’s first PC.

Recent findings suggest that jones calls that proximity part of the “magic” of the location. The museum has a Gazelle computer that SCP built nearby, using boards that Jones said the firm hired high school students to assemble and test. Volunteers recently pulled data off its hard drive, which boots into MS-DOS 2.11 and still holds SCP’s own source code.

In a recent development, allen’s fingerprints are everywhere. One display holds a 1971 letter from Hellmut Golde, director of the University of Washington’s Computer Science Laboratory, kicking Allen and his “friends from the Lakeside School” out of the lab. Among the offenses: tying up the teletypes to print “endless listings,” and taking an acoustic coupler from a professor’s office “without leaving at least a note.” Golde asked them to turn in their keys.

The letter sits beside an Imlac PDS-1, the same type of graphics computer Golde scolded the teens for using without permission. More than five decades later, the letter is headed back to campus: ICM plans to display it, along with the Imlac, at UW’s 60th anniversary celebration for its computer science program in March.

Sources indicate that jones recalled that Allen insisted visitors to Living Computers get “the 10-minute experience” of loading an Altair 8800 from paper tape, just as he remembered it. The kit computer was the machine for which Allen and Bill Gates wrote Microsoft’s first product, and Allen wanted to reinforce “how far we’ve come and how difficult old computers were.” ICM has an Altair 8800 on display.

Sources indicate that around the corner is Miss Piggy, a DEC datasystem 570 that Microsoft used in the early 1980s to support the group that built Multiplan, Word and later Excel. It was also the email host for much of the company, including Gates, who once had to print out an important message on its console printer when the system went down before a speaking engagement.

Industry observers note that recovered email shows Allen pushing to get Piggy networked. “Paul Allen is very keen on doing this,” Henry Burgess wrote in 1982. Retired in 1986, the machine now runs again in Tukwila. It’s one of ICM’s most popular remote systems, and some fans come in just to write programs while sitting next to it.

Other local ties include a PDP-7 that Boeing used to process flight data in the 1960s, and a Toad-1, a modern take on the PDP-10 built in the 1990s by Redmond’s XKL. Jones first got involved with Allen’s world through a 1997 Usenet post inviting PDP-10 enthusiasts onto a Toad. That machine became the cornerstone of PDP Planet.

In a 2024 video for Christie’s, Jones said what inspired Allen was “being able to recover old programs, mythical code, and get them running on hardware so that they can be studied.”.

Asked what Allen would make of ICM, Jones didn’t hesitate. “I think he’d be happy,” he said.

On a monitor beside ICM’s PDP-6, a 1968 artificial intelligence program is having trouble.

“SORRY, I DON’T KNOW THE WORD ‘STEEPLE,'” it replies.

Recent findings suggest that the program is SHRDLU, created by Terry Winograd at the MIT AI Lab. It let a user type plain-English commands to move blocks around a virtual world. Only the PDP-6’s front console and display survived, so ICM is rebuilding the machine as a “hybrid,” with the original hardware wired to an emulator.

In a recent development, jones sees a lesson in that old error message. “You look at this and you think of how we use ChatGPT now,” he said. “The cool thing about SHRDLU is that it can tell you ‘I don’t know.’ It doesn’t try to fabricate anything.”.

Industry observers note that ICM has its own ties to the modern AI world. An Anthropic employee is a member and strong supporter, and in October the museum brought six vintage computing stations to an Anthropic event, where the tech giant’s Claude chatbot wrote programs for machines like the Commodore 64.

Sources indicate that back in Tukwila, Claude has become one of ICM’s most useful restoration tools. The team feeds it huge stacks of scanned documentation, such as the full set of schematics for a PDP-10. When a machine has a logic fault, they ask where to look, and Claude points them to a page in the maintenance manual or a likely bad module.

“That’s turned things that would take a couple of weeks to solve into a couple of days,” Jones stated.

AI can point the way, but the repairs remain hands-on work. When Miss Piggy wouldn’t run properly this year, former Living Computers engineer Josh Dersch stepped through the machine’s microcode one instruction at a time using a recreated DEC test board, until he found a single bad chip. A spare board brought it back.

Asked if it still takes a human to keep these machines running, Jones agreed — at least for now. If a humanoid robot with AI built in ever shows up to do the repairs, he joked, “then I sit at the front desk all day.”.

In a recent development, nearly everything at ICM is meant to be touched, switched on and played with, and that can be intimidating. On machines with nothing but a text prompt, “you kind of have to know the commands in your head already,” Jones stated.

Recent findings suggest that that’s by design. From the start, the idea was to challenge people “either by confronting them with their past or demonstrating how difficult it was,” Jones said. His example: two friends, one with an Apple IIe and one with an IBM PC, whose computers “could never share” software. ICM continues that legacy, he said, “challenging the visitor to go beyond what they might feel comfortable with.”.

It has been revealed that volunteers have written cheat sheets to help newcomers, and Jones is encouraged when families come in and kids start typing BASIC programs. “That to me was the spirit of what PDP Planet and Living Computers was meant to be,” he stated.

Recent findings suggest that very little is behind glass. Jones jokes that a Timex Sinclair 1000, which would wipe your painstakingly typed program if you bumped it, is “the only computer that deserves to be behind glass.” The goal, he said, is to avoid “a boneyard that people walk through.”.

Other highlights include Galaxy Game, the first coin-operated video game, installed at Stanford in 1971 and now being restored, and a Thinking Machines CM-2 supercomputer, whose 40th anniversary ICM just celebrated with members of its original design team.

According to recent reports, for younger engineers raised on modern tools, Jones has a go-to move. He asks if they’ve used Linux, then walks them over to the PDP-7, the type of machine Unix was first written on more than 50 years ago. Many of the basic commands they type every day started there.

In a recent development, that remote access helps explain why ICM has about 500 members and sponsors, even though only 81 members have visited in person this year. Memberships start at $36 a year. Along with a King County grant through 4Culture, they help cover rent, with personal funds from Jones filling the gap. The museum hopes to become self-sustaining within five years, and maybe someday buy its own building.

In a recent development, the museum’s name reflects the urgency. “We’re in this magic window right now, where the hardware can still run, we can still maintain it, you can replace parts,” Jones remarked. “But slowly we’re getting further and further away.”.

Recent findings suggest that ICM is open Saturdays through Mondays from 10 a.m. to 4 p.m. Admission is $9 for adults and free for those under 18. Last May, the museum revived Vintage Computer Festival Pacific Northwest, which had been canceled in March 2020. The event drew 981 people, and it returns May 1–2 at the Tukwila Community Center.

Among the machines on the floor is MIT-AI, a DECsystem-2020 that once served as the ARPANET host for MIT’s Artificial Intelligence Lab. As Living Computers shut down in 2024, Christopher Zach, who sold the machine to Allen, told GeekWire he was ready to drive a U-Haul to Seattle to rescue it.

When a friend of Zach’s stopped by ICM’s booth at WorldCon and asked about “Chris’s computer,” Jones had a simple message to pass along: “Please just tell him it’s safe.”.
