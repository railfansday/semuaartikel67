It has been revealed that the holidays always bring about a flurry of travel, but even if you find yourself hosting in your own home this year, you can always explore new frontiers by way of a good cookbook. At Traveler, the titles we swear by take us through H Mart with an expert guide at hand to break down the aisles, to Sri Lanka, where the palette of London hot spot Hoppers was formed, and to the sugar-dusted counters of Broma Bakery, where Sarah Fennel has the key to 100 desserts sure to satisfy any sweet tooth. The best cookbooks immerse you in the corner of the world they come from, and through their pages, narrative transforms into a multi-sensory experience. Just as we're placing finishing touches on our holiday menus, our editors gathered their favorite cookbooks of all time that take readers all across the map, from recent releases that have become instant classics in our households to vintage volumes that continue to stand the test of time.

We'd love to hear about the cookbooks that take a place of pride in your kitchen on Instagram or Facebook.

I was lucky enough to taste chef Ifrah Ahmed's food at a beautiful dinner last year, so I couldn't wait to add her cookbook Soomaaliya to my shelf when it came out this spring. In Somalia, she writes, "knowledge is passed down orally," but she records the flavors and traditions of the culture in this stunning homage to Somalia's rich culinary legacy. —Sarah Khan, contributing editor.

Sources indicate that when I’m working my way through a recipe or just slapping something together for the sake of a pantry clean-out, odds are that the secret ingredient that ties it all together is an item I picked up at H Mart. Perhaps it’s the splash of Shaoxing cooking wine that makes all the notes in a fried rice sing that much more, or the half-packet of Buldak sauce that brings on the much-needed heat to some peanut noodles. As a card-carrying fan of the brand, I was ecstatic when I learned H Mart co-president Stacey Kwon had committed the splendor of the Korean grocery store’s aisles to pages in a cookbook. Beyond just housing recipes (the Galbi Jjim is a dinner party show-stopper), this omnibus also lets readers in on the store’s history, the tips and tricks of navigating its inventory, and handy guides that will have you cooking like it's second nature. This volume is a boon for H Mart enthusiasts and a fantastic tool for those just beginning their journey in the kitchen. —Kat Chen, editorial assistant, destinations.

It has been revealed that I love Hoppers, the dish. I love Hoppers, the homey London restaurant chain dishing out delicious Sri Lankan and southern Indian fare. So it’s a no-brainer that I love cooking from Hoppers, the cookbook. With the recipes in this book, author and restaurant owner Karan Gokani attempts to make traditional dishes like lamb kari and buriani feel approachable, all with the trademark come-join-me affability of his Instagram cooking videos. Some of these recipes are Proustian madeleines, taking me back to early-morning visits to fish markets in southern India or the many holidays spent along Sri Lanka’s southern coast, as well as to my grandmother’s dining table in Kerala. There’s a striking overlap in ingredients and flavors when it comes to Kerala and Sri Lanka, and it’s always a pleasure to get to sample that shared heritage. Now, if only I could summon a supply of the Arrack Attack cocktails from Hoppers as I cooked… —Arati Menon, global digital director.

Recent findings suggest that since I’ve moved to Latest York, I’ve known that whenever I’m looking for a taste of home, I can always head to the East Village or Williamsburg to Taiwanese-American restaurant Win Son (and its bakery off-shoots). And now with the restaurant’s cookbook, I can recreate their lu rou fan (braised pork rice) or tsang ying tou (fly’s head, a fragrant mix of pork and chives that goes over rice) for myself. I’m fortunate to have grown up with a mom who was a fantastic cook, but the magic she worked in the kitchen has always been obscure to me. She’s true to the trope of never writing any recipes down, instead relying on muscle memory and years of road-tested intuition. But with the wisdom of Ku, Brown, and Erway’s book by my side, I’ve finally been able to master my childhood dishes and resist the urge to order takeout. —K.C.

Industry observers note that I became interested in plant-based dining once I started dating a vegan, and one of the first restaurants that opened my eyes to all the possibilities of letting go of meat was Amanda Cohen’s Dirt Candy. It’s one of my favorite restaurants in the whole city, with a seasonal tasting menu that doesn’t break the bank but still stretches your imagination by showing you the full flavor spectrum of a leek, or proving by example that yes, eggplant really does belong in a dessert. Their cookbook is an absolute joy to read through, especially since it’s part comic book! It’s as fun as it is revelatory, and it’s a showcase of how cooking is an art as well as a form of play. —K.C.

A friend gifted me Table for Two when my partner and I moved in together a few years ago. It was sweet, and the cover added some much-needed color and beauty to my kitchen counter. But its not just a pretty face—this cookbook has challenged me. From slow-cooked lamb and cinnamon stew to fluffy, peach crepes, these recipes are made to impress. You’ll find a mix of one-pan meals, specialty dinners, delicious lunches, and rich desserts. If you’re trying to win someone over, this cookbook will help you every step of the way. —M.K.

It has been revealed that when it comes to educational heroes, Alton Brown is on the pediment alongside Bill Nye and LeVar Burton for me. Watching his show Good Eats on Food Network was a formative ritual; he just has this infectious geekiness about him. As a sort of food mad scientist, Brown can be technical bordering on obsessive, but it’s that attention to detail that makes his methods so approachable: cooking is something you can break down to its basics and understand. This first volume is all about the fundamentals, and with it you’ll never make a bad batch of brownies or dry turkey again. —K.C.

It has been revealed that I stumbled upon this vintage cookbook at a secondhand bookstore, and I initially purchased it as a novelty item. I’d never seen a cookbook dedicated to an appliance before, let alone one I had never associated with “cooking” to begin with. But much like how I’d imagine Methven’s initial readership reacted in 1978, I was pleasantly shocked and excited by how versatile the microwave really is. There are recipes ranging from Scallops Au Gratin to Barbecue Ribs to Seven-Minute Brownies in this book, and I’ve gleaned many staples from its pages. It’s only right to acknowledge you’re not going to get restaurant-quality versions or true-to-form authenticity out of every dish, but if you’re wiling to adventure, this is one of the most accessible ways to do it from your own home. Sometimes when I’m at a loss for what to make and feeling sluggish, I’ll perform a little bibliomancy on this book to figure out lunch. Even if you’re just interested in this title for your coffee table, the photography is beautiful and occasionally experimental in its efforts to display the science behind the latest-fangled microwave. —K.C.
