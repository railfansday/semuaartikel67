Tech Report: How cranberries went from a Thanksgiving favorite to a symbol of stronger farming for the future

Breeding cultivated cranberries with wild relatives may help protect future crops from extreme heat, frost and climate change.

Cranberries grow in some of the coldest parts of North America, but frost can still damage them.

In a recent development, in spring, frost can damage newly growing buds before they produce berries. In early fall, it can harm fruit that is still ripening.

Scientists crossed cultivated cranberries with a small wild relative from the far north called Vaccinium oxycoccos.

When exposed to cold, these newly introduced plants kept more genes involved in growth active than cultivated cranberries did.

The wild plant’s DNA could therefore be of use in breeding cranberries that handle cold weather better.

Audrey Dickinson, a researcher in agricultural genomics at the San Diego Botanic Garden and the Salk Institute, led the study.

Scientists from the USDA’s Agricultural Research Service in Chatsworth, Latest Jersey, also took part.

Only leaves were sampled, over a span of minutes. Berries and harvests were not included.

According to recent reports, the United States is the world’s largest cranberry producer. Strawberries are the only berry Americans eat more of. Most cranberries reach shoppers as juice or as sweetened dried fruit.

In a recent development, berries can scald once the air passes about 90°F (32°C), and scalded fruit rots more easily. Frost, on the other hand, can leave the fruit soft.

When growers were surveyed, New Jersey producers named heat tolerance first. Wisconsin and British Columbia growers named frost tolerance.

In a recent development, one round of cranberry breeding takes seven to eight years. Most varieties are only a few generations away from wild vines. Wheat breeders, for one, have borrowed heat tolerance from wild relatives.

The small cranberry grows across the cold far north. As its name suggests, its berries are smaller than those of the farmed species.

The two plants look different, but they cross easily. Gene by gene, their published genomes line up closely.

According to recent reports, wild plants for this work were collected in southeastern Alaska in 1996. In 1998, four of them were crossed with the farmed varieties Stevens, Ben Lear and Pilgrim.

For three days, the team kept Stevens and the four hybrid lines at 70°F (21°C). Then the plants went into incubators.

The heated plants reached 117°F (47°C) in 30 minutes, while the chilled plants cooled to 37°F (3°C) over 95 minutes.

Recent findings suggest that the team clipped leaves three times and froze them in liquid nitrogen. Their RNA was read with Oxford Nanopore sequencing, which kept the wild and farmed gene copies apart.

Recent findings suggest that plants need proteins to grow. Ribosomes assemble those proteins, and the genes that build ribosomes are tied to growth.

Photosynthesis genes, used by leaves to turn sunlight into energy-rich sugars, are tied to growth as well.

Ribosome gene activity declined during the cold test in both Stevens and the hybrids, but the decline was greater in Stevens.

Photosynthesis gene activity increased in the hybrids but decreased in Stevens.

Basic cell work, the researchers suggested, kept going in the hybrids under stress. In Stevens, that work was reduced. Total photosynthesis gene activity, however, stayed higher in Stevens under every condition.

The two groups differed much more in cold than in heat. In the cold test, 18 broad sets of related genes behaved differently between hybrids and Stevens. In the heat test, only one set did.

The team linked this to where the parents come from. The farmed cranberry is native farther south. It may photosynthesize best at warmer temperatures.

A chill that stresses Stevens may be closer to normal growing weather for its wild Alaskan cousin.

The four hybrid lines did not respond in the same way. Gene activity differed more among the lines than between the hybrids as a group and Stevens.

Two lines with the same Stevens parent also differed from each other. The wild parents were therefore likely a major source of this variation.

In the cold test, two lines revealed an unusual pattern. At 60 minutes their gene activity shot up. By 95 minutes it had fallen, although the air was still getting colder. In Stevens, large changes continued to the end.

In a recent development, the team sees that rise and fall as a possible sign of efficient adjustment to cold. Results in the heat test were different.

After 30 minutes of heat, Stevens showed the smallest overall change of all the plants.

Some hybrids may cope with cold better than Stevens, then, yet cope with heat worse.

Industry observers note that heat and cold tolerance may need separate tests in breeding programs. Coping with one is no guarantee for the other.

At room temperature, the hybrids mostly took after Stevens. Some 57% of compared genes tracked the farmed parent, against 14% for the Alaskan one.

About 22% of genes were less active in the hybrids than in either parent, many of them tied to photosynthesis. A cold acclimation gene called WCOR413 was among them.

That could hint at hybrid weakness, a known risk when two separately evolved genomes are merged.

Recent findings suggest that chloroplasts may be part of the explanation. These green structures are where photosynthesis takes place. Many photosynthesis proteins are coded in the cell’s main DNA but work inside chloroplasts.

Sources indicate that chloroplasts also carry a small amount of their own DNA. The two sets of DNA must work together. A mismatch between them could disrupt photosynthesis genes.

To keep the two in step, the authors suggested crossing hybrids back to a farmed cranberry mother several times.

It has been revealed that the experiments were short. Only the first 30 minutes of heat and the first 95 minutes of cold were studied. Longer or repeated stress may produce different results.

Gene activity is also only a partial guide to which proteins a plant makes.

The two temperature experiments were also quite different. The heat was intense enough to potentially kill the plants, while the cold temperature was still within the range that cranberries normally experience outdoors.

Sources indicate that the researchers also did not measure plant survival, growth or berry production. It is still unclear whether a calmer gene response means a hardier plant.

For Dickinson, the study also highlights why protecting wild plants matters.

“These results highlight the importance of conserving crop wild relatives in their natural habitats and in collections,” she wrote.

Future research could test whether these gene patterns match real frost and heat tolerance in the field.

The study is published in the journal Plants, People, Planet.
