Gratitude Is the Taproot of American Identity

Gratitude is like stretching, or deep breathing, or drinking water. It’s one of those miraculous things you can do for free, any time, with barely any effort, and it’s 100 percent certain to make you feel better — but somehow practicing it always feels like an exasperating chore. That’s how I experience it, anyway. There comes a moment at nearly all Thanksgiving tables when someone says, Now let’s all go around and say one thing we’re thankful for. And when this unimpeachably virtuous ritual is confirmed, my first thought is always, Oh God, when will it be over?

In a recent development, but then of course — like drinking water, or breathing deeply — giving thanks is also something you’re always glad you did. Lo and behold, there really are people and things you love in this world, and to make a point of looking on them with appreciation is to see them lit up again in the living color of your own happiness. You might start out by dragging your feet; you are guaranteed to end up flush with joy. Maybe that’s part of the point: It changes you inside.

Spencer A. Klavan is the associate editor of the Claremont Review of Books and a professor of classical literature at the University of Austin. His new book, A Way With Words: Why Language Makes Humans Irreplaceable, is available for preorder now.
