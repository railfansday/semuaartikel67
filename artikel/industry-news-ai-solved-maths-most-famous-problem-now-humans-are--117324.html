Industry News: AI Solved Math’s Most Famous Problem. Now Humans Are Struggling to Understand It

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Mathematicians are struggling to decode a 17-million-line solution to their field’s most famous problem, published by an Anthropic researcher on Friday.

According to recent reports, levent Alpöge released a proof in the programming language Lean of the 167-year-old Riemann hypothesis, which he had created with Claude. While the conjecture, which describes how prime numbers are distributed, is widely assumed to be true, many mathematicians have tried and failed to definitively show this, and the problem has become something of a holy grail of number theory.

According to recent reports, since Alpöge published his proof, more than 200 people have been collaborating to verify it. On Saturday, the nonprofit the Lean Focused Research Organization and volunteer-led organization Mathlib said on Saturday they have “full confidence” that the proof is sound. The problem is formulated correctly and the code compiles, they confirmed. Now, “we look forward to all the activity in the mathematical community that will help explain the new ideas in this proof,” they wrote.

In a recent development, lean proofs are written to be checked by machine, while mathematical papers are written to explain the results to the community so that they can be reviewed and advanced. But Alpöge hasn’t released the paper version, which clocks in at 250,000 pages and is, in his own estimation, a “travesty of slop.” (On Monday, he added in an X post that the Lean proof “is full of garbage obviously” but that it still “compiles.” He also mentioned that the 250,000 pages was a “joke … I find major number [sic] extremely hilarious.”) That’s left mathematicians to their own devices as they try to decipher what fresh findings they can obtain and build from the result.

“Knowing there is a proof is one thing,” Abhishek Saha, a professor of mathematics at Queen Mary University of London, tells WIRED, “but when we understand the proof, that would allow us to actually go beyond what has happened, to build latest branches of mathematics perhaps or formulate the next conjecture to push the horizon further.”.

In a recent development, saha reckons the community effort should be able to simplify the result into a human-readable form of under 10,000, if not 1,000, pages. But complicating this feat is the fact that the proof appears to draw on many advanced branches of number theory in a long and “incredibly counterintuitive” argument. “I don't think this kind of proof would ever come from a human,” notes Saha, “but AIs can do something completely different and almost alien, and that breaks our intuition about how very difficult mathematical results might come about.”.

According to recent reports, he was shocked to see even his own corner of number theory utilized by the proof, and has since been trying to understand how. Saha has been using OpenAI’s Astra model to convert sections of the Lean proof into more digestible formats, such as single-page posters like those presented at conferences and shorter documents. But there’s a limit to that approach.

“It may well be that no single person has the expertise to understand in detail every part of the proof,” he claims.

According to recent reports, more than 60 other individuals are using AI agents to try to translate the Lean lines into understandable ideas on Principia Math, a collaborative platform designed to facilitate the understanding of AI breakthroughs. So far, they have done this for more than 3,000 pieces of the Lean proof, though platform chief executive officer Anton Shakov expects it will take mathematicians months before they have a good conceptual understanding of the proof Alpöge produced with Claude in roughly six weeks. “I personally believe the picture we will get in the end, once the ideas of this proof have been properly understood, will be astoundingly beautiful," he tells WIRED.

Independent mathematicians have already understood and written up one smaller result to have come out of Alpöge’s proof.

AI labs have previously been criticized for rushing to publish breakthrough mathematical results, claiming glory while leaving the academic community to digest the results—and clean up mistakes.

According to recent reports, “No human being can reasonably claim to be responsible for, or even to understand, such a document,” the Association for Human Mathematics, a group of more than 1,000 mathematicians founded in September, wrote in a statement on Friday of the 17-million-line opus. It complained that Alpöge’s announcement “builds upon over a century of human work, but fails to advance mathematical understanding in the same way.”.

Alpöge, who before joining Anthropic was a researcher at Harvard University, acknowledged on Friday what an “extraordinary” situation it was to present “the world with a complete argument before having mastered a massive neighborhood around its mathematics,” and wrote that he would try his best to help digest the proof, including by writing it up properly. But having had to hold off from making the announcement ahead of Anthropic’s initial public offering on Thursday, “I just could not live if I waited even longer,” he wrote.

It has been revealed that anthropic rival OpenAI in October proved a weaker version of the problem, called the quasi-Riemann hypothesis. Alpöge had been scooped by OpenAI in September on another Millennium Prize Problem concerning the Navier-Stokes equations, which describe fluid motion. When the company said it had solved part of the problem, Alpöge’s collaborator, Upcoming York University professor Tristan Buckmaster, claimed OpenAI had rushed ahead to beat the pair to it after becoming aware of his work. The pair also confirmed on Friday that they had completed the remaining unsolved parts of the Navier-Stokes problem.
