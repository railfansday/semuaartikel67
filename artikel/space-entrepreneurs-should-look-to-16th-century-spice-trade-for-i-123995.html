Despite all the hype about a upcoming space economy, humanity has yet to reap much benefit offered by the prospect of offworld mining, manufacturing, or space tourism.

But historical precedent dictates that ventures into the unknown need to be driven by commerce to achieve profitability. Such was the case five centuries ago, when most of Western Europe was agog over the importation of precious spices from East Asia.

The idea was to circumvent the Silk Road from and drive the spice trade from overland camel caravans to large cargo-holding ships.

For centuries, camels had enabled trickles of spices such as cloves, nutmeg, and ginger to make their way into the kitchens of the high and mighty, be they landed gentry or royalty.

But as early as the late 15th century and the Age of Columbus, middlemen had run up the cost of these rare spices to such a degree that Portugal and Spain, in particular, were in competition for a sea route to the Moluccas. That is, the Moluccan archipelago in what is now Indonesia.

The idea was to travel point to point by sea and weed out a multitude of middlemen along the long Silk Road --- mostly stretching overland from the Black Sea and Mediterranean through northern India to China.

Thus, Portuguese explorer Ferdinand Magellan had a novel idea.

And then in a cost-efficient manner, reap the profits by bringing them back to Earth. Or at least low-Earth orbit.

Routine human sorties beyond low-earth orbit are key.

If commercial space transportation becomes routine, agencies such as the National Science Foundation (NSF) could support research that uses it, Worden argues.
