It has been revealed that I've been investing in stocks since 2004, when I left my first job in the public sector and moved to a corporate employer with a 401(k) blueprint. I had some money from cashing out my unvested public sector pension. And I had saved up a few thousand dollars to put into a Roth IRA.

I was in my mid-20s and I didn't know what I was doing. The stock market in those days didn't seem real to me. I wasn't a sophisticated investor. But I knew I wanted to save for the future, and that putting money into tax-advantaged accounts and investing in stocks was, in general, the right move for someone like me to make.

Let's look at what I learned from my first bear market -- and how that significant investing lesson still inspires my decisions today.

This wasn't my first rodeo. I had graduated from college in 2001 and lived through the dot-com bust and the Enron scandal. But the 2008 global financial crisis and Great Recession were much more severe and affected the entire economy. And on a personal level, 2008 was much scarier than 2001.

That's because by 2008, I had some skin in the game. I had a job at a major bank. I had become more established in my career and as an investor. I had built up a few years' worth of savings in my 401(k) and Roth IRA. My wife and I had a baby and a mortgage. We were just trying to have a happy little life with a home, family, friends, and some financial stability for the future. And all of a sudden, it seemed like the housing market, the stock market, and the entire financial system were on the brink of collapse.

In a recent development, all bad things seemed possible in 2008. Would I lose my job? Would we lose our house?

It's strange to think back on it now, because while 2008 was happening, I could barely focus on the stock market. Banks were failing and the market was crashing, but I was too worried about holding onto my job. I just wanted to keep paying the bills, putting food on the table, and taking care of our baby. I wasn't making clever moves like buying more stocks when prices were low. I was just trying to hold on to what we had.

Industry observers note that perhaps the biggest lesson from my first bear market is: I am incredibly lucky. I never lost my job in 2008. We never missed a mortgage payment. The bank that I worked for did not go out of business; it actually emerged from the financial crisis stronger than ever, unlike other major banks and subprime lenders that failed during the housing crash.

But being lucky is not an actionable lesson for investors. It's good to be lucky. But it's even better to have a strategy and stick with it. So the real big lesson from my first bear market is: Keep buying stocks. As long as you have a job, as long as you have steady income, try to keep putting some money into the stock market as often as you can. Every month, every payday.

Recent findings suggest that I never stopped buying stocks in 2008. I kept investing in my 401(k) plan every month, every payday, with dollar-cost averaging. I kept putting cash into my Roth IRA, even when money was tight. Those simple, steady, disciplined, "boring" little investments might end up being some of the most lucrative financial moves I've ever made in my life.

According to recent reports, since those crisis-era lows of 2008, the stock market has skyrocketed with a long, mostly uninterrupted bull market. A $1,000 investment in the S&P 500 at the end of 2008 would have grown to almost $8,400 today.

At this point in my life, I try to stay calm and unbothered during major swings in the stock market. Hopefully, 2008 will be the worst economic crisis of my lifetime. But even if there is another terrible short-term crisis like 2008, I know that in the long run, the S&P 500 is likely to bounce back.

Buying low-cost index funds like the Vanguard Morningstar Total Stock Market ETF (VTI +0.93%) tends to be a great move for people with a long-term time horizon, even if you end up buying stocks at "the wrong time" right before a crash.

Even if there is a tech stock crash or a big sell-off in artificial intelligence (AI) stocks soon, I believe that in the long run, index funds like the Vanguard Morningstar Total Stock Market ETF will eventually recover and rebalance. VTI lets me own the future "winners" of the economy, one way or another.

That's why I keep buying stocks. Even when the market is crashing, when investor fear is rampant, when the headlines are full of doom. I've lived through worse. And in the long run, the stock market tends to go up. If you keep buying during the lowest points of a bear market, when stocks are cheapest, your investments will slingshot upward as the market rises in the future.
