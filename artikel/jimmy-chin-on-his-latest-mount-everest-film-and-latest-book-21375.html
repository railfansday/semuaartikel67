Jimmy Chin on His Latest Mount Everest Film and Latest Book

In spring 2026, Jimmy Chin flew to Nepal. It was his 15th trip to the Greater Ranges over 25 years spent on expeditions to climb and photograph its snow-covered giants. The trip was a departure from the high-octane sufferfests he’s often done, and it also marked a different type of first: the first time he brought his kids, Marina (12) and James (10), along. Showing them the mountains that have inspired him for decades, and often pulled him away from them for months at a time, was memorable for Chin.

Together, they made their way to Everest Base Camp, stopping in the tiny villages that populate the roadless Khumbu Valley. When they crawled into their tents at 17,598 feet, they were proud of themselves.

Sources indicate that outside Phortse, a small village in Sagarmatha National Park, a helicopter dropped off a special guest who’d come to say hi: Jim Morrison. Morrison hadn’t wanted to miss the kids’ first trip to Nepal, and they were shocked to see him walking toward them on the trail. In town, they went to the Khumbu Climbing Center, where Nepali climbers and high-altitude workers train to operate safely in the substantial mountains, to hang with Sherpa friends and their families, and to pick up their guide, Pemba Sherpa (Sharwa).

Twenty years earlier, Chin had encountered a then 15-year-old Pemba for the first time in that exact spot. It was just a few years after the center had opened, and he and the legendary mountaineer Conrad Anker were on the way to climb Cholatse. “I really encouraged his photography and filmmaking,” said Chin.

It turned out to be kismet: Pemba worked with Chin as a cinematographer on his film, Everest: The Other Side. Pemba was there in October 2025 documenting Jim Morrison as he made his first turn off the 29,032-foot summit. Morrison made history that day after completing a mind-boggling ski descent: an unforgiving 9,000-foot line of the North Face of Everest via the Hornbein and Japanese Couloirs.

In a recent development, chin’s team of climbers, filmmakers, and support crew added 12 newly introduced names to the short list of people who had ever summited Everest via the North Face. Ascending the route alone would’ve been enough, but Chin was there as Morrison’s climbing partner while simultaneously directing a feature film. The 53-year-old has made a career of combining high-angle endeavors with filmmaking. In 2011, he climbed the Shark’s Fin, a 4,500-foot route, alongside Anker and Renan Ozturk to film what would become the 2015 movie Meru. When Alex Honnold climbed the 3,000-foot granite prow of El Capitan in Yosemite National Park without a rope, Chin was there to capture that too. Free Solo went on to win an Academy Award.

His newest film, which was co-directed with Elizabeth Chai Vasarhelyi and made its theatrical debut in IMAX on October 30, was built on every single one of the skills Chin has developed over his career climbing and documenting expeditions on some of the world’s most challenging peaks.

“This is kind of like the culmination of all my collective experiences as a climber and a photographer and a filmmaker and a producer and a director and all the things,” remarked Chin of making the film.

Recent findings suggest that when Chin took the last few steps to the summit of Mount Everest, his third time standing up there, he was surprised to find himself overcome with emotion. During the climb, he’d been so focused: He was thinking about breathing, consumed by the sheer physicality of climbing at altitude, busy worrying about Morrison and the team’s safety, and planning out shot lists and contingency prepares. He’d thought about every worst-case scenario, but he’d failed to consider what it would feel like to succeed. The years of hard work flashed in front of his eyes. He collapsed into Morrison’s arms, in tears.

“I looked over into Nepal and saw Makalu and Lhotse, and I mean the whole Himalaya spread out in front of me,” noted Chin. It had taken seven years to get the team up there. “Everything hit me at the same time and I totally fell apart.”.

While he felt a substantial weight lift from his shoulders after getting everyone up and down Everest safely, he still felt the enormous weight of having two of his closest friends, Morrison and Morrison’s late partner Hilaree Nelson, entrust him with their story. He’d met Nelson 20 years earlier when they both joined the athlete team at The North Face at the same time. He knew he had to get it right.

According to recent reports, after six years of working on the film, Chin and producer Shannon Dill traveled to Tahoe City last June to show Morrison a rough cut. When it was over, there was a lot of emotion in the room, and people were crying. Morrison got up and walked to the back of the theater to wrap Chin in a massive hug.

Industry observers note that during the final phases of editing the film, Chin was also working on a deadline to finish his latest book, Rules to Live By. “I had like three months of 16-hour days, seven days a week,” he said. “When people say it’s a marathon, not a sprint, I was like, ‘This is both.’”.

For the book, Chin interviewed ten people he looks up to, from Lynsey Addario to Simone Biles to Yo-Yo Ma, all of whom have helped shape the way he thinks about what really matters in life.

Both Bill King and Ridley Scott talked to him about honesty, and it’s stuck with him. “You have to tell the truth,” he noted. “It’s hard to do, but you have to do it, and you have to do it early because it only becomes harder.” Telling the truth is like training a muscle, he said, and exercising it becomes easier with practice and, with enough time, just becomes how you naturally operate. We’ve all had an experience that would’ve been easier if we’d just been honest up front, said Chin.

He draws inspiration from actress Annette Bening and how she leads: quietly, in a way that embodies the Daoist idea that the greatest leaders are the ones that no one knows are leading. “She makes people feel seen and heard, and when she walks on set, people will do anything for this woman because they want to,” he said. “I was really paying attention to how she moved through the world. It’s about listening to people and making people feel heard.”.

Years after Pemba met Chin, he started working on climbing expeditions, following in his father’s footsteps. His dad bought him a Lumix digital camera to take with him on his first expedition to Mount Everest. On the summit, he snapped a few photos.

In a recent development, when Meru came out, Pemba remembers feeling deeply inspired by Chin’s work. He could not have imagined that a decade later, after years spent working in the mountains and honing his craft, he would find himself filming for Chin’s production, with his camera aimed at Chin during the emotional moments that transpired on the summit before Morrison’s ski.

Chin has spent much of his life forging his own path in the mountains, making daring first ascents and ski descents, but has since shifted his focus toward giving opportunities to the next generation coming up behind him. When a significant objective is on the horizon, he makes sure to bring new faces along.

“Jimmy is a substantial influence on me, and a big mentor,” mentioned Pemba. “Experiencing a climb with the man himself and working for the man himself? Yeah, it was really an honor.”.
