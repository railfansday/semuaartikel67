Inside a Brooklyn Apartment That Feels Like an English Country Estate

Set in a wide Brooklyn Heights brownstone, photographer Ethan James Green’s apartment, though modest in scale, gives the impression of a breakfast room in some well-appointed Cornish estate. Hulking Victorian, Arts and Crafts, and Jacobean furniture dominates the space, Art Nouveau lamps aglow. The home draws an interesting contrast to the work for which he is best known—whether his black-and-white shoot with Kate Moss for Dazed or his recent Vanity Fair cover story with Carlos Alcaraz. Green’s apartment is, by comparison, more indicative of a bygone era, but it still reflects the irreverent, empathetic spirit at the heart of his practice.

The view from the bedroom into the living room.

Paintings by Raphael Soyer flank a portrait by Thomas Hart Benton in the living room; armchairs by George Mann Niedecken.

Sources indicate that just under 1,000 square feet, the one-bedroom unit serves dual purposes: to provide space to converge with his cherished friends and to house other faces that inspire him. Covering walls are the many 20th-century figurative paintings Green has gathered over the years, among them Social Realist portraits by Raphael Soyer, nudes both bold and tentative, and myriad depictions of introspective souls—images that captured Green in a spiritual grip.

He displays the varied mix in interiors conceived with his friend Dylan Bailey, the Upcoming York–based artist and set designer, whom he met years ago on a shoot. Their collaboration was informal. Green would come home and find paint swatches left by Bailey, who might pop by and hang up a work while Green was shooting. The furniture they found at auction. “I wanted to have matching couches so when a friend comes over we can both take one, sink in, and talk,” claims Green. The vintage sofas’ peach upholstery fits right in. “I didn’t realize until after getting them that the color showed up in several of the paintings,” Green says, referring to a portrait of an ironworker by Joseph Paul Vorst and a double nude by George Valentine Dureau.

A Victorian Renaissance Revival bed mingles with works by Maurice Grosser, Raphael Soyer, and Moses Soyer.

Green in the kitchen, surrounded by photographs.

Green grew up outside Grand Rapids, Michigan, surrounded by, as he recalls, “cornfields and dairy farms.” With missionary parents, unstructured days, and endless skies, he had the space and time to imagine a different life. “I knew I wanted to be a fashion photographer when I was 14 or 15, then found out I could model at 16,” he recalls. “I won the lottery. Modeling was a better way to learn than art school.” He moved to New York City at 17, the first student to graduate from his high school online. “I had to be here,” he says simply. “My first year in New York I shot with David Armstrong. He took me under his wing.”.

Now in his mid-30s, Green splits his time between the Big Apple and Los Angeles with his boyfriend. On the East Coast, between shoots, he runs Latest York Life Gallery, opened in 2022 and named after the Chinatown insurance firm across the street. His first show, dedicated to Baltimore-based photographer Steven Cuffie’s tender portraits of Black women, reflected Green’s devotion to his own medium and enduring interest in the variance of human faces. The 2023 exhibition “Sleeping Beauties” would blur the lines between Green’s life and work: Many of the paintings shown have come to reside in his apartment.

Green kneels before a bookshelf by Renzo Mongiardino in the bedroom, which is painted in a custom Benjamin Moore blue.

Sources indicate that portraits by Raphael Soyer and Leroy E. Mitchell Jr. flank The bedroom’s 19th-century French armoire, which reflects a view of another Soyer painting.

An Austrian Art Nouveau chandelier hangs in the living room, which displays paintings by (from left) Raphael Soyer, Joseph Paul Vorst, Raphael Soyer, and Hermann Grom-Rottmayer. The walls are painted in a custom Benjamin Moore color.

A 1949 self-portrait by Leroy E. Mitchell Jr., for instance, hangs in the bedroom. This was the first painting Green bought at auction, prior to which it had been found in a dumpster. Green’s research led him to Mitchell’s early life in Detroit, his community of peers, and his time as a teacher in Ghana. Meanwhile, one of Green’s most pedigreed finds, a painting by Thomas Hart Benton, whose 1931 mural of industrial workers hangs in The Met, stoked another uncovering. “I had gotten multiple paintings and then realized many of the artists knew each other or were friends,” he notes, noting that Benton and Vorst were studio mates. It is like Green is uncovering a parallel life of creatives finding true kinship.

Green Crowned the kitchen cabinet with art, including works by Raphael Soyer, Robert Philipp, and David Armstrong. To its right hang photographs by (from top) Nan Goldin, David Armstrong, and Richard Avedon.

Framed family snapshots fill the hallway shelves.

Industry observers note that it is these serendipitous journeys into the lives of other artists that Green cherishes as much as the art itself. “I love paintings,” he states. Perhaps a bit too much? Bailey would tease him that every time he arrived there was yet another portrait to place. “The reason there’s one hanging on the mirror over the mantel is because I had no more wall space,” Green says. As he explains, his collection, like his life and work, is ultimately about affection. “You cannot be a good photographer without being empathetic,” Green observes. “I love people. I love faces.” He’s made sure that love is hard to miss.
