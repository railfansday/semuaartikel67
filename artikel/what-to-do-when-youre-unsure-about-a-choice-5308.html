What to Do When You’re Unsure About a Choice

It has been revealed that many people struggle with making important decisions. These include which college to attend, which career to undertake, whether to look for a upcoming job, or how to find the right house.

Industry observers note that one way to settle on a decision is to make a list of pros and cons of a particular course of action. A cost/benefit analysis can also help: What would the cost be for a particular choice vs. the potential benefit?

For instance, here is a simple example: Suppose you are deciding between buying two houses that differ in price. The less expensive house will have a lower down payment that would allow you to have some money left in your savings account, but it is not in a great neighborhood. The more expensive house will drain your savings, but it is in a great neighborhood.

In a recent development, if the neighborhood is very important to you (great benefit), it might be worthwhile to drain your savings (cost). In other words, the benefit outweighs the cost. If the neighborhood is not that important to you, then the benefit of the more expensive house would not outweigh the cost, and therefore you should buy the cheaper house.

Sometimes, it’s hard to assess potential benefit vs. cost. For instance, if you are considering which college you want to attend, and different colleges provide different educational and extracurricular benefits that are hard to quantify, how do you make a decision?

When several colleges appear to be reasonable choices, I have a secret to share: It doesn’t matter which college you choose.

Industry observers note that among colleges that are a reasonable fit, what you do there matters more than which one you pick. As long as you work hard and take advantage of the educational and personal opportunities available to you at a college, you will gain a lot. If you don’t work hard and keep to yourself, you will gain little, no matter what college you attend.

Further, even if it were possible to make a “perfect” choice when you face a decision, within a few months the choice will no longer be perfect.

Because circumstances will have changed in a way that affects the benefits and potential costs of your decision.

For instance, if the decision you made related to choosing an undergraduate school, your preferences will likely change once you are exposed to new ideas, people, and situations that may alter your college experience in unforeseeable ways.

Bottom line: Even with vital decisions, pick one, and make the best of it. In most circumstances, you will do well. If that does not turn out to be the case, you often can change course, and will have become wiser because you will be able to figure out how to avoid making a similar wrong choice in the future.

It has been revealed that another secret to keep in mind is: There is no "one" correct path for your life. People don’t click along straight lines like trains on railroad tracks. We float and bob and turn like rafts on a river that’s always flowing and changing.

You may feel like you can’t afford to make any “wrong” decisions—but sometimes the less obvious choice is a great one that will create fresh possibilities for you.

Industry observers note that on my Center Point podcast, I have interviewed many successful people. I’ve been struck by many of the interviewees who changed course a few times until they identified their main career when they were in their late 20s.

It has been revealed that further, keep in mind that many people change jobs often, especially early in their working lives (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025), so your first choice is unlikely to lock you in permanently. With these secrets in mind, let’s look at thinking patterns that can help make good choices:.

Recent findings suggest that instead of asking, "What's the can't-fail choice?" consider asking, "What meets my needs?" Instead of "What could go wrong?" ask, "What are the possibilities? Instead of "What will everyone else think?" ask, "What is right for me?" When you ask these questions, you give your inner wisdom a chance to help guide you.

Sources indicate that some of the greatest blessings that come into our lives are surprises. When a decision doesn’t play out the way you want or expect, think about how you can embrace your latest direction or challenge instead of ways you can be unhappy about it. For instance, when you decide to audition for a part in the school play and don’t get chosen, think of what you can do with all the free time you’ll have.

Industry observers note that when you feel stuck about a decision, imagine how your future self will feel about your choice—maybe in a month, a year, or even 10 years. What advice would the future wiser, older version of you give? Let the answer help guide your choices today.

Sources indicate that 4. Be like Batman (or Sasha Fierce!).

Recent findings suggest that did you know that there’s a scientific principle called the Batman Effect? It’s just as cool as it sounds. Also known as self-distancing, this is the idea that you can create a little space, perspective, and extra strength to deal with any decision or situation by getting out of an “I” mindset. In one study, researchers asked people to give a small public speech. (Speaking in public is a common and severe fear.) One group was asked to think about the upcoming speech as if they were in the middle of it, making statements that started with “I am” and “I feel.” Another group was asked to consider the same things, but instead of using an “I feel” statement, a participant named Anne would say, “Anne feels” or “Anne thinks.”.

It seems like a small change, right? But the results weren’t small. Participants who used the third person were less afraid and anxious, and they gave better speeches (Streamer et al,. 2017).

Sources indicate that in a study with young children, researchers found they could take this idea one step further by having kids consider what Batman would do. Kids who were thinking like Batman were found to have thought more clearly than all the other participants (White & Carlson, 2015).

Sources indicate that this kind of thinking is not just for kids. Years ago, when Beyoncé was explaining how she gives commanding live performances, she explained that when she was ready to go on stage, she’d stop thinking of herself as a regular person and start thinking of herself as a strong, powerful performer called Sasha Fierce. Sasha Fierce was confident and bold, and she helped make Beyoncé one of the top-selling recording artists of all time.

Industry observers note that making key decisions can be stressful, especially when we worry that one wrong choice could change the course of our lives. But there is rarely just one correct path. By considering our options thoughtfully, asking ourselves helpful questions, and remaining flexible about what comes next, we can approach decisions with greater confidence.
