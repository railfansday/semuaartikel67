Voyager 1 is about to achieve a cosmic first: one ‘light day’ from Earth

A spacecraft is about to set a profound cosmic record. At 5.16 a.m. EST tomorrow, NASA’s Voyager 1 will reach a distance of one light day, or 25.9 billion kilometres, from Earth — farther than any object made by humans has gone before. That means it will be so far away that a signal travelling at light speed from Earth’s surface will take a full 24 hours to reach the trailblazing spacecraft.

Voyager 2 enters interstellar space after 41 years.

Sources indicate that the probe has been flying away from Earth for more than 49 years. “It has taken longer than half of a typical human lifespan to reach that distance — so on that level it’s amazing,” says Deana Weibel, an anthropologist at Grand Valley State University in Allendale, Michigan, who studies space exploration. At the same time, she notes, Voyager 1 is only a fraction of the distance towards the star closest to the Solar System: Proxima Centauri, which is 4.2 light years (almost 1.3 parsecs, or more than 1,500 light days) away.

It has been revealed that voyager 1 and its twin, Voyager 2, launched from Earth in 1977 to explore the outer Solar System. Both passed out of the heliosphere — the realm in which the Sun protects Earth and other bodies from the influence of deep space — years ago, and are now hurtling through interstellar space, where they are experiencing frigid temperatures nearing –80 ºC. Their plutonium power is dwindling, and mission controllers are having to turn off scientific instruments on the probes to conserve as much power as possible. Voyager 1 is expected to lose communications with Earth in 2029 and its twin will do so in 2031, when their power finally dies.

Until then, they continue to do science with the few instruments left — sending back the only direct observations of interstellar space that have ever been made. Every day, the Voyagers are measuring what happens in the space between stars, including how it is surprisingly affected by storms of particles streaming outwards from the Sun.

Between 1979 and 1989, the Voyagers reshaped scientific understanding of the Solar System as they flew past Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune. Their discoveries include volcanoes on Jupiter’s moon Io — the first volcanism known beyond Earth — and a crackly water-ice crust on Jupiter’s moon Europa — now thought to hide a potentially life-friendly ocean beneath it.

Voyager 1 travels faster than Voyager 2 and exited the heliosphere in 2012 (see ‘Leaving it all behind’). Voyager 2 followed six years later.

Industry observers note that even though they are now in interstellar space, the spacecraft continue to measure changes wrought by the Sun. They discovered how blasts of solar material sometimes make it all the way through the heliosphere to produce shockwaves in the otherwise quiet interstellar realm. The Voyagers have observed these disturbances as changes in magnetic fields, energetic particles and other environmental variables that they measure. Starting around 2020, for instance, an incoming pressure front caused magnetic fields around the Voyagers to increase drastically, then drop six years later. Researchers are working to find out why that is.

Industry observers note that “I think people didn’t quite anticipate how far out these influences could be felt,” claims Jamie Rankin, a space physicist at Princeton University in New Jersey and the Voyagers’ deputy project scientist. That suggests that stars beyond the Sun might also be influencing the interstellar medium more than was previously thought, Rankin says — “so that’s where it gets fun”.
