It’s late Friday afternoon in Manhattan and Alexis Piqueras has misplaced his phone. Not that he’s concerned. Bathed in the light of October’s early setting sun, the Spanish-born producer and founder of AP Studio, settles into a Charlotte Perriand dining chair at his Tribeca apartment. The vibe is calm: a mix of warm hues, rich textures, and timeworn woods. Candles perfume the air. And all around us the faces and limbs of figurative artworks seem to join our conversation. “I’ll find it eventually,” Piqueras notes, unperturbed by his absent device.

In a recent development, A break from our harried digital world was what he was seeking when, not long ago, he set out to buy an apartment downtown, having returned to Latest York after a three-year stint in Los Angeles. “Sometimes I need to hide amid the chaos,” claims Piqueras, who has built a busy career collaborating with brands on the order of Louis Vuitton, Celine, and Loewe. His real-estate ambitions transcended any simple notions of an urban oasis. Yes, he wanted peace and quiet. But he also wanted some untouched architectural grit that felt true to the city, in particular his visions of its post-industrial 1970s waterfront.

In a recent development, he found that in Tribeca. Its warehouses and sleepy streets also reminded him of his time in Berlin, where he fell in love with club culture. His fresh loft, located in what had once been a 19th-century caviar factory, retains its original brick walls, timber columns, and wood windows, through which he can view slices of the Hudson River. A previous renovation had made smart use of the 2,500 square feet, carving out a primary suite, guest bedroom, office, powder room and pantry alongside the open cooking/living/dining space. It all just needed to be pared back. “The moment you take away everything, you have all you need,” Piqueras says.

Recent findings suggest that working with the Brazilian-born furniture and interior designer Pali Xisto Cornelsen, he stripped back what time-stamped interventions had been made over the years. In the kitchen, they updated the existing high-gloss kitchen cabinetry with white-painted fronts and chunky solid-cherry pulls, also installing stainless-steel countertops and a fresh island, trimmed in wood. The walls were coated in Benjamin Moore’s Simply White, the floorboards refinished, and the windows dressed in linen roman shades. “I was conscious of creating a neutral canvas,” claims Xisto Cornelsen, who removed all the recessed lighting. “You’re gonna feel a certain calm.”.

It has been revealed that art introduces faces to that expanse. Working with advisor Joe Sheftel to expand his collection, Piqueras has assembled a sophisticated trove with a focus on works by Queer talents of recent or current memory. Those include photographs by Wolfgang Tillmans, Peter Hujar, and Alvin Baltrop, who documented the pre-AIDS gay revelry of the nearby Hudson River piers. Elsewhere are portraits by Larry Stanton and David Hockney. Some bare bodies, Piqueras admits, have left houseguests blushing. But the intention was never skin for the sake of shock. “It’s a lot about community,” claims Sheftel. “The apartment also functions that way, as a fun space for friends and play.” A romantic spirit comes across in still lives (a burning candle by Phoebe Helander) and landscapes ( a Doron Langberg painting of Fire Island Pines). Evident too is Piqueras’s deep love of New York City. “There’s also a narrative of activism—of power and creativity through crisis,” says Sheftel, pointing out works by David Wojnarowicz.

Industry observers note that elsewhere throughout the interiors, they added custom millwork, including the office’s daybed and wall-mounted desk, the living area’s bookshelves, and the dining area’s DJ station, a corner Piqueras calls his little Berlin. Tapered silhouettes and bevels soften edges. “There are all these happy surprises,” says Cornelsen, who referenced design heroes like Le Corbusier, Joaquim Tenreiro, and Jean Prouvé. Vintage furniture adds to that neutral rhapsody, with choice finds by Pierre Chapo, Isamu Noguchi, and Alvar Aalto, whose coffee table prototype anchors the living area. It is joined by a cast of other Scandinavian characters, among them light fixtures by Paavo Tynell, the upholstered Märta Blomstedt lounge chair where Piqueras enjoys his morning tea, and pieces by Axel Einar Hjorth.

Collecting and decorating, Piqueras has found, are not unlike his daily profession. “Renovating an apartment is a kind of production,” he says. “My skill is to orchestrate.” But it’s a break from the grind that he now gets to relish at home. “Work is insane. The streets are insane. But then I can come here,” he says. “I put down my phone—and then I don’t think about it.”.
