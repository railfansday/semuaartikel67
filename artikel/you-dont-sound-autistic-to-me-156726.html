"You Don't Sound Autistic to Me"

Autism Spectrum Disorder has historically been diagnosed with a full psychological assessment. This assessment includes observational tests and a full Intelligence Quotient (IQ) test. Autism is often easy to see on an IQ assessment because IQ isn’t just one number. It is an assessment of a person’s strengths and weaknesses across multiple domains. The IQ number we are used to seeing is a representation of the average of many different types of aptitudes and weaknesses. Verbal comprehension, fluid reasoning, processing speed, and visual-spatial abilities are all measured with an index score that is put together to form an overall IQ score. IQ itself is a measure of averages. 100 is the average IQ. 15 is the standard deviation of the mean, so if you have an IQ above 115, you are more intelligent than 84% of the general population, and if you have an IQ of 130, you are smarter than 98% of the population.

Industry observers note that for most neurotypicals, the IQ score is a good estimate of their ability to perform across multiple domains. It is rare for someone to have a verbal comprehension score in the 99th percentile and a working memory score in the 5th percentile. This is normal and is part of why, if you speak well, most neurotypicals assume you are intelligent across multiple domains. However, one of the hallmarks of autism that isn’t frequently discussed is what is known as a “spiky profile”. A spiky profile means that I frequently see clients with an overall IQ that is virtually meaningless because they will have verbal reasoning in the 5th percentile and be nonverbal, but have a fluid reasoning and visual spatial score in the 99th percentile, or vice versa.

Industry observers note that this spiky profile deeply impacts how the world and the public relate to autism and autistic people. Humans in general strongly associate verbal comprehension with intelligence and aptitude, so if an autistic person’s spiky profile has profound deficits in this area, they are often labeled as level three or requiring substantial support, and if their verbal comprehension is high, no matter how profound the other deficits, they are often told they “don’t seem autistic” or denied services they may need.

Recent findings suggest that I had a client a few years ago whose profile showed profound deficits in working memory and processing speed. He was unable to do basic living tasks like cleaning his room or brushing his teeth without support. Yet he was continuously told he didn’t need support and was denied support because his verbal comprehension was in the 99th percentile. Fluid reasoning is another part of IQ that people usually associate with high-functioning intelligence, and many autistic people are exceptional at spotting patterns, drawing inferences, or finding solutions to new problems and thus are assumed to be “high-functioning” or, in some cases, are told they “don’t seem” autistic.

This is the hardest part of being autistic. The spiky profile of skills and deficits is often profound in multiple areas, and people’s biases associated with aptitude and intelligence profoundly shape how much support and empathy an autistic person will get in their community at large. People think of the archetypal autistic person as being verbally awkward or less verbal, with stimming behaviors and no eye contact. But since this spiky profile is so variable in autistic people, they could have more profound deficits and be articulate and quiet and very able to mimic neurotypical social behaviors, but have a working memory that is so profoundly low that they can’t complete basic work-related or living tasks.

Recent findings suggest that I found my IQ test recently. My profile is typical for an autistic person. My verbal comprehension and fluid reasoning skills are in the 99.9th percentile, but my visual-spatial reasoning skills show profound deficits, and my working memory is also slightly impaired. This, combined with an inability to read most social cues, has made my life one of hostility from most neurotypicals. People perceive me as profoundly gifted. My IQ is high, and I am very articulate. People interpret this as pretentious and often accuse me of trying to “talk down to them”, but words are my natural habitat and my vocabulary is expansive. At the same time, I am an excellent problem solver, and I can easily find solutions to problems that confound others. Yet, mental rotations are almost impossible for me, and if I am asked to think in a room with more than one conversation or to multitask, I can shut down entirely. This, combined with the fact that I honestly misread most socially complex situations and offend people while trying to be nice or behave in ways I think are helpful but people find hurtful, makes people perceive me as a jerk instead of disabled.

This can go the other way. I have clients who are perceived as having an intellectual disability when, in many domains, they are gifted. They are never given the opportunity to show their strengths because people can’t see them.

My largest handicap is interpersonal interaction, and this is very evident in my personal life. This is largely due to my inability to see anything but the best in people and my spiky intellectual profile. After seeing me in several human interactions, my son was confounded and said, “You aren’t this stupid!” He listed all my accomplishments and said I couldn’t possibly be this dumb. He wasn’t being cruel. He was only coming to terms with the fact that the mother he had seen as brilliant and unbreakable had more disabilities than he thought were possible. This is a hard thing for a child to learn. Many people struggle with this discrepancy in me, and it erodes many of my relationships. Many people see my profound deficits as purposeful negligence.

In a world where autism acceptance and awareness are needed, I believe this is the part people need to understand the most about autistic people. Our “spiky profile” creates confusion in those around us. In the early 1990s, the film Rain Man created the archetypal image of a barely verbal autistic man with limited verbal skills who was inconceivably brilliant at math. I think it is crucial to remember that many autistic people are like this. We can seem like the smartest person in the room and at the same time be the most incapable person in the room.
