Childhood health problems may share hidden environmental links

A study following children into adolescence links early environmental exposures to overlapping patterns in physical and mental health.

The environment children grow up in can shape their health in more ways than we might realize.

From the air they breathe to the food they eat, everyday exposures may be connected to several health problems that continue into adolescence.

In a study of 586 European children, some exposures were linked to several problems at once.

A mother’s smoking during pregnancy was tied to a higher body mass index in her child and to more aggressive, impulsive behavior. Both links were still there in the teen years.

The findings suggest that a single early exposure may be tied to several parts of a child’s health.

Studying several health outcomes together can help reveal these overlapping links.

Augusto Anguita-Ruiz of the Barcelona Institute for Global Health (ISGlobal) and the University of Barcelona led the work with colleagues at Inserm, France’s national institute for health and medical research.

From pregnancy into the teen years, the researchers observed families and changed nothing about how they lived.

Xavier Basagaña, an ISGlobal researcher, is a senior co-author of the study.

“The study does not establish that these exposures cause the observed health outcomes,” stated Basagaña.

Industry observers note that all of the children came from six long-running birth studies in the United Kingdom, France, Spain, Lithuania, Norway and Greece. The six studies belong to a European project called HELIX.

For each mother and child, the team had 69 measures from pregnancy and 129 from childhood. Scientists have a name for that full mix of what a person eats, breathes and lives around: the exposome.

Blood and urine samples provided measures of tobacco exposure, metals, pesticides and other chemical pollutants.

Other exposure measures came from home and school addresses, which the team used to estimate outdoor air pollution, traffic and nearby natural spaces.

Questionnaires covered diet, sleep, physical activity and pets.

Industry observers note that the team compared those measures with 11 health outcomes in four areas: weight and blood pressure, mental health, breathing, and allergies. Each child was examined twice, at an average age of about 8 and again at about 15.

Researchers behind a recent US atlas compared 619 exposures with 305 health traits, but their survey data captured each person at a single point in time.

Six pregnancy exposures and 42 childhood exposures were linked to more than one health outcome. The researchers call these four health areas domains.

Valérie Siroux is an Inserm researcher and senior co-author.

“Although no exposure was associated with all four domains, around one in three of the selected exposures was linked to more than one, suggesting that certain exposures can have broad and multisystemic effects on health,” said Siroux.

Smoking during pregnancy was one of those exposures. It was also tied to more ADHD symptoms in the teen years.

Among childhood exposures, fine particles in indoor air, known as PM2.5, were linked to worse results for weight and blood pressure and for mental health.

Higher copper levels in a child’s blood were linked to worse results in the same two areas.

Secondhand smoke and benzene in indoor air were each linked to higher BMI or blood pressure at both exams.

It has been revealed that why would one exposure be tied to such different problems? The authors suggested that the four health areas share biological pathways, most notably inflammation throughout the body. The team didn’t measure inflammation here.

Of those 42 childhood exposures, 16 were linked to outcomes across more than one health area, and 12 of those involved diet.

Diet also appeared when the team analyzed weight and blood pressure alone. There, following a diet closer to the Mediterranean pattern and eating more fruit were each linked to lower BMI and blood pressure.

In the team’s models, childhood exposures explained up to about 15% of the variation in mental health and in weight and blood pressure.

Pregnancy exposures explained up to about 7% of the variation in mental health and about 1.5% in weight and blood pressure.

Recent findings suggest that so the measured exposures left most of the differences among children unexplained. Allergy outcomes were scarcely related to the exposures.

Some results were the reverse of what the team expected.

Higher blood levels of certain long-lasting chemicals, including PCBs and the PFAS chemical PFOA, were linked to lower BMI and blood pressure in children.

The authors didn’t read that as protection. These chemicals are stored in body fat, so a child’s body fat and growth are expected to affect how much of each chemical appears in blood.

Body size may be shaping the blood reading rather than the chemical shaping body size, the authors wrote, and they urged caution with those results.

The team also repeated every analysis six times, each time leaving out the children from one of the six birth studies.

Most links appeared again, in the same direction, in more than four of the six runs.

Sources indicate that A few links didn’t meet that standard. One tied how much meat a mother ate during pregnancy to asthma and wheezing in her child. Another tied fast food in childhood to worse mental health.

Such links may reflect conditions in one region or one group of families rather than a general pattern, the authors wrote.

It has been revealed that among the 1,301 mother-child pairs in HELIX, the 586 in this study were those with data from every stage. About 87% of the children were white, and 53% of their mothers held a university degree.

The authors described the group as highly selective, so the links may not apply to other populations. The group was also too small to analyze boys and girls separately.

Some chemicals with highly variable levels were measured in only one or a few urine samples, which could make a link look stronger or weaker than it is.

Industry observers note that the bacteria, viruses and fungi a child encounters weren’t measured. The authors suggested that could be why breathing and allergy problems were less clearly tied to the exposures.

Every association is posted in a free interactive online atlas that anyone can search or download.

“Further studies in larger and more diverse populations will be needed to better understand these relationships and determine whether modifying some of these exposures can alter health trajectories during childhood and adolescence,” Basagaña stated.

Future research could also add outcomes this study lacked, such as thinking skills and metabolic health, and examine children at other ages.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.
