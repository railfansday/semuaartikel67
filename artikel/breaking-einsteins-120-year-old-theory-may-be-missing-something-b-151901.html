Breaking: Einstein’s 120-year-old theory may be missing something big about the Big Bang

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a recent development, in the earliest moments after the Significant Bang, the universe was filled with an extraordinarily hot mixture of particles known as quark-gluon plasma. Temperatures reached trillions of degrees, and quarks and gluons raced through this primordial soup at nearly the speed of light. This extreme state of matter existed for only a few microseconds before quarks began combining into the more stable protons and neutrons that eventually helped form the matter around us.

Scientists can briefly recreate these conditions using powerful particle accelerators. By colliding ions at speeds approaching that of light, they produce tiny droplets of quark-gluon plasma about the size of an atomic nucleus. Each droplet survives for only tens of septillionths of a second, giving researchers an incredibly brief opportunity to investigate the substance that filled the newborn universe.

One way to explore this mysterious fluid is to observe how heavy quarks behave as they travel through it. Although these particles are not part of the surrounding plasma being studied, their movements can reveal key details about its microscopic structure. This approach builds on a principle first described by Albert Einstein in 1905.

According to recent reports, however, Einstein's original framework has limitations under the extraordinary conditions found in quark-gluon plasma. When particles approach the speed of light, interactions occur that his theory does not fully account for.

MIT Physicists Extend Einstein's Century-Old Theory.

According to recent reports, researchers at MIT and the University of California at Santa Barbara (UCSB) have now developed a theoretical framework that goes beyond Einstein's original description. Their work predicts how a fluid affects particles traveling at relativistic speeds, including the forces that accelerate, slow, and redirect them.

Sources indicate that the researchers believe their calculations could help scientists better understand how heavy quarks move through quark-gluon plasma. By comparing their predictions with results from particle accelerator experiments, physicists may be able to uncover latest details about the internal structure of this ancient form of matter and the conditions that prevailed shortly after the Substantial Bang.

Industry observers note that "Our result reveals a latest source of information carried by heavy quarks in rare, unusually large changes in their motion," claims Jean Du Plessis, a graduate student in MIT's Center for Theoretical Physics, a Leinweber Institute. "How often those large changes occur depends on the microscopic nature of the plasma, raising the possibility that they could provide a new way to learn what the quark-gluon plasma is actually like inside.".

Du Plessis conducted the research with Bruno Scheihing-Hitschfeld of the Kavli Institute for Theoretical Physics at UCSB. Their findings were published in Physical Review Letters.

How Pollen in Water Inspired Einstein's Discovery.

The origins of this research stretch back nearly two centuries to an observation made by botanist Robert Brown. In 1827, Brown noticed that pollen grains suspended in apparently motionless water moved unpredictably, constantly shifting and drifting despite the absence of any visible disturbance. Scientists eventually named this phenomenon "Brownian motion.".

Einstein provided a scientific explanation in 1905, showing that these seemingly random movements were caused by tiny, invisible interactions between particles suspended in a fluid and the atoms and molecules surrounding them.

Recent findings suggest that he demonstrated that two competing effects govern the movement of a particle through a fluid. The first is drag, which resists the particle's movement and slows it down. The second consists of countless random pushes from the surrounding atoms and molecules. Although individual microscopic particles are extremely small, their combined activity can deliver enough energy to make larger particles move unpredictably.

Einstein's theory established a relationship between these effects, allowing scientists to learn about the microscopic properties of a fluid by studying the motion of particles inside it.

In a recent development, over the following decades, researchers applied this principle to many different fluids, including quark-gluon plasma. They generally expected particles traveling through such plasma to experience a balance of drag and random impacts resembling the behavior of pollen grains in water.

Recent findings suggest that in conventional Brownian motion, the likelihood of different forces follows a bell-shaped curve. Moderate pushes are the most common (the highest point of the bell), while unusually strong pushes in either direction are much less likely (the tail ends of the bell). Over time, the balance of drag and random motion allows a particle to reach the same temperature as its surroundings.

But this familiar description becomes incomplete when particles move at speeds approaching that of light.

"Einstein highlighted that the drag and the random kicks must balance in just the right way for a particle eventually to come to the same temperature as its surroundings," Du Plessis explains. "For the types of fluids he was thinking about, he was correct. But for particles that are moving at relativistic velocities, this relationship breaks down. And we've known for 20 years that something is missing. So we have to go beyond the work that Einstein did.".

Unexpected Forces Reveal Hidden Physics in Quark-Gluon Plasma.

To investigate what Einstein's traditional model was missing, Du Plessis and Scheihing-Hitschfeld calculated how a heavy quark would respond to the forces inside a fluid while moving close to the speed of light.

Their calculations focused on the strong nuclear force, one of nature's fundamental forces, which governs interactions between quarks and gluons and holds them together.

The team began with a relatively simple model consisting of a gas of quarks and gluons that interacted only weakly with one another. Starting with these weaker interactions made it possible to calculate the forces acting on a heavy quark passing through the gas.

In a recent development, they then determined how those forces would change the quark's momentum as it moved. A stronger push produces a larger change in momentum, so the calculations allowed the researchers to predict the likelihood of impacts of different strengths.

According to recent reports, at first, the resulting probability curve looked similar to the bell-shaped distribution associated with Einstein's theory. For small changes in momentum, the familiar pattern still held. But when the researchers examined larger changes, they discovered a striking difference.

Instead of the expected symmetrical curve, the distribution was tilted, with unequal probabilities for extreme changes in opposite directions. Powerful impacts occurred more frequently than the traditional Brownian model predicted, and unusually large slowdowns were more likely than similarly large increases in speed.

In a recent development, "The usual Brownian picture assumes that large kicks become exceedingly rare," Du Plessis says. "For heavy quarks, however, these rare kicks are more common than the bell curve predicts, and are not equally likely in opposite directions: A large kick that slows the quark down more than expected from drag is more likely than an equally large kick that leaves it moving faster than expected.".

A Universal Pattern Could Reveal the Universe's First Fluid.

The researchers next examined whether this unusual behavior depended on the particular particles making up the plasma.

They repeated their calculations using plasmas containing different types of particles and found that the same tilted distribution appeared regardless of the composition.

According to recent reports, even more intriguingly, they recognized the same underlying pattern in an earlier calculation involving strongly coupled plasma, where particles interact much more intensely with one another. Despite the substantial differences in microscopic behavior between the two types of plasma, the probability distributions shared the same distinctive structure.

This suggested that the researchers had identified a potentially universal feature of how fast-moving particles interact with fluids, rather than an effect confined to one particular model.

Industry observers note that "This is the first time that this seemingly universal structure was realized and really formalized," Du Plessis states. "This gives a concrete path forward for how to better understand the quark-gluon plasma, using heavy quarks.".

Industry observers note that the discovery could offer physicists a valuable new tool for investigating the extraordinarily hot matter that dominated the universe immediately after the Big Bang. By studying the rare, extreme changes in the movement of heavy quarks, scientists may be able to learn more about the interactions occurring deep inside quark-gluon plasma.

In a recent development, the results also point toward an crucial characteristic of this primordial substance. Rather than behaving like a relatively loose collection of particles, the universe's first fluid may have been governed by much stronger interactions than a conventional Brownian motion model would indicate.

According to recent reports, du Plessis suggests that primordial quark-gluon plasma was likely "more strongly interacting than this Brownian motion way of modeling it would suggest. It's more like a liquid and less like a gas.".

By extending a theory Einstein developed more than a century ago, the researchers have opened a promising latest approach to studying one of the most extreme states of matter in nature and potentially uncovering what the universe was like during its first fleeting moments.
