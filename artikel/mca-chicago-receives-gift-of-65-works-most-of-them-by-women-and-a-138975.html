MCA Chicago receives gift of 65 works, most of them by women and artists of colour

A recent gift of 65 works to the Museum of Contemporary Art (MCA) Chicago honours the institution's upcoming 60th anniversary and its outgoing director, Madeleine Grynsztejn.

Sources indicate that the philanthropists Jack and Sandy Guthman worked with the MCA’s curatorial team, poring through the couple’s extensive collection to choose the pieces. Grynsztejn got the selection down to 65, then hit a wall. Paring it down to 60, as originally intended, became an excruciating process. The Guthmans, champions of the MCA for decades, did not force the museum to choose just 60.

In a recent development, grynsztejn considers this an extraordinary model for what can happen when a museum and philanthropists are in alignment. To date, the Guthmans have donated 130 works to the MCA—the largest donor gift in the museum’s history.

For Grynsztejn, building a great museum collection takes not only visionary leadership but visionary philanthropy. “The Guthmans have invested in Chicago artists, making their gift one that also has a civic impact,” she tells The Art Newspaper. Included in the gift is work by Amanda Williams, Dawoud Bey and Rashid Johnson.

The Guthmans have spent decades supporting Chicago’s civic and cultural institutions. As a real estate lawyer, Jack started out by providing pro bono services. Meanwhile, Sandy directed her family’s foundation Polk Bros. Jack was elected as a trustee of the MCA in 1989 and currently serves on its board. For the MCA’s 50th anniversary, the couple donated 50 photographs by women artists.

Christopher Wool’s Untitled (1995–96) Photo: Nathan Keay, © MCA Chicago.

It has been revealed that the current gift includes 23 pieces by artists of colour and 19 by women. Thirteen artists are newly introduced to the MCA’s collection, including Mohamed Bourouissa, James Casebere, Julia Fish, Christian Marclay, Adrian Paci, Elle Perez and Shinique Smith. Other gifts, like photographs by Bey, deepen the MCA’s holdings.

“In some cases, we are adding an entirely fresh collection,” Grynsztejn claims, referring to Bey’s early photography in Harlem, “and in some cases, we are gaining an icon, as in Christopher Wool’s Untitled (1995-96).”.

The MCA began as a kunsthalle in 1967. It started receiving gifts, so in 1974 it formalised its collection. Despite the fact that the very first piece to enter the MCA’s collection was by Marisol, it eventually began to reflect the usual gender and racial biases.

Industry observers note that during her tenure, Grynsztejn has sought to change that by establishing the Women Artists Initiative, inspired by the experience of many colleagues who have found it more challenging to fundraise for exhibitions by women artists. The museum’s proof-of-concept came with the success of exhibitions by Faith Ringgold, followed by a traveling Yoko Ono retrospective. The MCA has added women artists to the collection at four times the national average. Recently, the initiative has been renamed the Madeleine Fund for Women Artists and now has a $5m endowment.

Sources indicate that since Grynsztejn’s arrival at the museum in 2008, the MCA’s collection has grown from approximately 1,300 to 3,800 works. At least half of the acquisitions under her leadership have been by women and artists of colour. “This is not woke or [diversity, equity and inclusion],” she notes. “It’s good business and good culture. Because if you are a public trust, you are in service to your public.”.
