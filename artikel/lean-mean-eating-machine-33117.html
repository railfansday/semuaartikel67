Lean, Mean, Eating Machine

By the middle of August, I’d been walking EABO for more than three weeks. I was 30 miles from the Ice Age Trail’s central region, which, from its north end to its south, spans 400 trail miles. It’s also so full of road-walks (aka Connecting Routes – CRs) that they spill over into the western region. Less trail means fewer camping opportunities, more variation in daily mileage, and sometimes more problems caused by longer days, e.g. blisters. I also had to start looking further ahead (in the FarOut app) when planning my campsites.

After a night at Mueller Lake, I continued south past a sign depicting a horse and buggy. Horseshoes had left their marks on the pristine, recently-laid asphalt. It was the smoothest, blackest blacktop I’d ever walked. At County Highway F, I ignored the recommended CR and made a beeline for the town of Antigo.

In a recent development, I’m sure hiker safety is the IAT Alliance’s top priority when choosing a CR: the quieter, the better. County highways are busy, state routes even more so, and CRs seem to avoid both where possible. My priorities are a little different. If it cuts my mileage, I’m willing to risk heavier traffic – up to a point. Here’s what I learned from almost 500 miles of CRs.

Traffic on County Highway F was light, and there were no crotchety canines in any of the houses along the way. I arrived safely in Antigo at 11 AM. The day was getting warm, so my first stop was for ice cream and a drink. Afterwards, I only managed about 500 feet of progress before getting distracted. Four blocks of Fifth Avenue were closed to traffic for the “Badgerland Classics and Customs” auto show. I felt a little conspicuous – plenty of people gave me a sideways glance – but I wasn’t about to miss out on an entirely latest thru-hiking experience. I spent an hour looking at cars, then ducked into a diner for lunch.

On my way out of town, I stopped at Jaycee Park to charge my phone and collect water. While the phone was charging, I watched a two-foot long turtle swim lazily by in the adjacent brook.

It has been revealed that I also stopped at Dollar General, Kwik Trip, and a tiny convenience store. Even though I was starting to experience the central region’s abundant towns and gas stations, I was still in backcountry mode.

According to recent reports, I’m going to blame backcountry mode for what I did the next morning. At the roadside, I found a Ziploc bag containing 12 individually-wrapped Wint-O-Green Lifesavers. The calories were tempting, but dubious. Then I remembered a helpful flowchart I’d seen online somewhere.

Also useful if you find something on trail.

I celebrated the official start of the IAT’s central region with another mint, and with an hour-long break at Dells of the Eau Claire County Park. I ended the day camped at a trailhead next to a busy road. There weren’t any better options, unfortunately.

The next day was a low-mileage day. By 9 AM, I was sitting on a bench outside the library in Hatley. An hour later, when the park opened, I went and sat in the pavilion to charge my phone and enjoy the shade. At noon, I ate lunch at the only bar in town. I visited Dollar General to resupply, and made the gas station my last call: old Mountain Dew habits die hard. I arrived at my campsite having walked just 13 miles.

The day after that was my only 30 miler on the IAT. The weather was overcast and cool, and the roads were quiet. I picked up the trail again after 25 miles, and spoke to the only two people I saw that day. The first was a local hiker, the second was a volunteer who was singlehandedly cutting up a tree that had fallen across the trail. That night, I stayed in a fancy shelter with lights and power outlets.

At six the next morning, my breakfast was interrupted by a couple of off-leash dogs. One was friendly, one wasn’t. I never saw the owner, but my guess is that he/she heard the barking and blew a whistle. Both dogs suddenly froze, turned their heads in the same direction, and rocketed back the way they came.

Recent findings suggest that the faucet outside Iola Winter Sports Club was just a mile away. I took enough water to last 25 miles, and hit the road. There wasn’t much trail that day, but there was a formidable highway crossing. Fortunately, I avoided getting pancaked on state route 10, and arrived at Hartman Creek State Park mid-afternoon. I paid for a campsite and headed directly for the concession stand.

It was closed: E. coli bacteria had recently been found in Hartman Lake, so the beach was closed, and so were the concessions. I shed a tiny tear, and went to set up my tent. I also showered, rinsed my clothes, and lit a fire. Someone had trimmed the thorny bushes surrounding my campsite and draped the cuttings on top of the remaining brush. All of the dead stuff was dry, burned brightly, and the site looked tidier when I was done.

According to recent reports, there was nobody about when I left in the morning. My first section of trail was closed because of logging, but I did encounter a Long-tailed weasel during the detour. At the next trailhead, I spoke to members of a trail-crew who were in the process of installing a boardwalk. It looked like a major project.

My blueprint was to camp at Bohn Lake. It had no official campsites, but FarOut commenters mentioned a couple of flat spots. I couldn’t find those spots, or anywhere suitable, so I continued to the trailhead on the other side of the lake.

It has been revealed that my strategy B was to wait until early evening, then pitch my tent in a corner of the gravel parking lot. Again, I was thwarted. After I’d waited for an hour, visitors were still coming and going at the same rate.

According to recent reports, I walked 1.5 miles to the next State Ice Age Trail Area, where, as before, camping wasn’t explicitly prohibited. That didn’t necessarily mean it was allowed though, and I was hesitant about stopping. In the end, I decided I’d gone far enough. I picked a nice flat spot and made it my home for the night.

It has been revealed that at 5 AM, light rain started to fall. It grew steadily heavier for 90 minutes, accompanied by the approach of thunder and lightning. In the meantime, I ate breakfast, reloaded my pack in the cramped vestibule, and lay down on my bare tent floor to wait for the downpour to depart. At 7:30 AM, I rolled up my soggy accomodation, and hit the trail.

According to recent reports, for the next few hours, the sky cleared, the air warmed, the vegetation dried, and the humidity increased. It was a nice hike, and I especially enjoyed the section along the Mecan River.

It has been revealed that in the town of Richford, I decided not to stop at Johann’s Bar because it might take too long. Instead, I ate a late lunch at an exclusive establishment beside Interstate 39. They had the finest selection of vending machines of any rest area I’ve ever visited. Oh, and the air-conditioning was literally to die for! My sweat-soaked clothes made hypothermia a real possibility, so I sat on the bench outside. Al fresco and à la carte, I spent $20 on 1400 calories of the highest quality junk food that money can buy.

Industry observers note that A few miles later, I arrived at the north shore of Pleasant Lake, where there’s a small pavilion and a pit toilet. I was keeping an eye on the storm approaching from the northwest, and my timing couldn’t have been better. As I started filtering some lukewarm lakewater, I heard a rumble of thunder in the distance, and suddenly felt very happy to be under cover. There were a handful of boats on the water with people fishing from them. I was really glad not to be out there.

Sources indicate that I was sure a pavilion with a metal roof and no walls didn’t qualify as “indoors”. I was also sure that the houses and trees in the vicinity were better lightning targets.

Industry observers note that the distant rumbles were definitely getting louder, but for a time it looked like everyone was ignoring the warnings. Then, one by one, the boats darted to safety. Big, heavy raindrops began hitting the roof above me, and as their tempo increased, I noticed the first faint flashes of lightning. The storm closed in, wind gusts picked up, each flash brightened, and the report that followed became menacingly loud. There were no more rumbles; only bangs, booms, claps, and cracks. I didn’t feel untouchable, but the rate of rainfall was now biblical, so I did feel surprisingly cozy.

The storm took an hour to pass, and although my view of the western sky was limited, there were hints that more trouble was on its way. I decided to substitute a mile of trail and three miles of official CR with a shorter road-walk. Instead of continuing west around the lake, I backtracked a short distance and headed south along its eastern side. With little more than two miles between me and my planned campsite, the hike was turning into a grudge match. The day’s weather had taken aim twice, and I was determined to be in my tent by the time it tried again.

Pleasant Lake, unpleasant weather.
