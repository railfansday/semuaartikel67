Scripts can’t run AI factories: Dell moves cluster management from the server to the rack

UPDATED 05:46 EST / NOVEMBER 18 2026.

As artificial intelligence clusters grow to supercomputing scale, the hard part of the AI factory is no longer buying hardware. It is keeping racks, switches, GPUs and cooling running once they are switched on.

According to recent reports, that operational burden is reshaping what infrastructure providers sell. Dell Technologies Inc. has spent the past year pushing its AI Factory beyond servers and storage, and customers are now asking for best practices and better tools to stand up and run what they buy, according to Andrew Vaz (pictured, left), vice president of product management for the Telecom Systems Business at Dell.

Industry observers note that “What we are seeing is a fundamental shift from procuring supply to actually standing up and running these very complicated AI clusters and data centers,” Vaz said. “One of the interesting things that we’ve seen is it’s actually now impossible to manage these things with just a few scripts, manually, in an old world manner. The toolsets are becoming more important.”.

Vaz and Saurabh Kapoor (right), director of product management and strategy for AI compute and networking solutions at Dell, spoke with theCUBE Research’s John Furrier and Gemma Allen at SC26, during an exclusive broadcast on theCUBE, SiliconANGLE Media’s livestreaming studio. They discussed supercomputing, rack-scale management, AI observability and why networking sits on the critical path for AI clusters. (* Disclosure below.).

In a recent development, dell used the conference to introduce Dell OpenManage Rack Scale and Dell Fabric Manager, two tools for running AI infrastructure from deployment through daily operations. OpenManage Rack Scale manages 100,000 devices in its first release, up from 8,000 servers for its predecessor, OpenManage Enterprise, Kapoor explained. Systems such as Nvidia Corp.’s NVL72 racks bundle compute, power shelves, switches and coolant distribution units into one unit.

“OpenManage Enterprise, the previous product we had, was perfect for servers. Server was the unit of compute,” Kapoor said. “But you have seen in the last 18 months, the world has moved very quickly to larger ecosystems and infrastructures. Rack is the upcoming unit of play.”.

Visibility is the other half of that equation. Dell’s iDRAC controller pulls more than 320 metrics from every server, and an AI-assist chatbot can query that telemetry through local large language models, according to Kapoor. A single cooling leak can damage millions of dollars of gear, so every layer has to report in, Vaz noted.

According to recent reports, “It could be something as simple as, ‘Hey, this transceiver’s about to die that you can’t diagnose with metrics you have today,'” Vaz mentioned. “You need to use AI to go look at it. You need telemetry off the system to go collect all of that, correlate it, run your algorithms on them, train it and understand what’s happening.”.

It has been revealed that the network is where those pressures concentrate. Dell Fabric Manager turns validated designs built with Nvidia and Advanced Micro Devices Inc. into deployable blueprints across different network operating systems and silicon. Agentic AI is also reshaping traffic, and enterprise bandwidth is outrunning a projected 3.5-fold increase, Vaz noted.

It has been revealed that “We’re getting blown away by it. It’s more like nine to 10x,” Vaz said. “It’s going everywhere: east, west, north, south.”.
