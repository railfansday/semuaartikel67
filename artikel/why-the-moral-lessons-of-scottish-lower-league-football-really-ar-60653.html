Why the moral lessons of Scottish lower league football really are important – Craig Levein

We often take pride in Scotland in our more authentic, less corrupted professional football. Sure, it lacks the glitz and glamour of the English game, awash with American and Middle Eastern money, but as a result it has remained more rooted in our communities, and more accessible to the working class fans who made the game great in the first place.

The recent Man City scandal has only served to sharpen this contrast. While Pep Guardiola’s teams played undeniably beautiful football, the subsequent revelations of financial impropriety have put the validity of their titles under question and damaged the sporting credibility of the Premier League.

It has shone a light on the trade-offs English football has had to make: creating an elite, globally renowned product, but at the cost of affording dubious characters free rein over long-standing, treasured community institutions.

In a recent development, scotland’s approach has been different. There is less money, to be sure, but that has insulated the Scottish game from some of this scandal. As a result, it feels more real, a purer form of the game.

Sources indicate that however, the truth is that Scottish football is a tough place to ply your trade. This is especially true in the lower divisions, where I started my career with Cowdenbeath in the early 1980s.

Industry observers note that football at this level couldn’t feel further removed from the clashes between Wall Street billionaires and petrostates so common south of the border. It was hard work and often brutal. Although we might take pride in it, the undeniable truth is that for many clubs further down the pyramid, life is tough. Razor thin margins, poor facilities, and low attendances can make it demanding work both on and off the pitch.

Recent findings suggest that it’s hardly surprising then, contrary to how we might see ourselves, that Scottish clubs are not immune to finding themselves reliant on financial benefactors whose interest in them, and in the areas they represent, is passing. When the money dries up, the very existence of these clubs comes under threat.

It has been revealed that take Gretna FC as an example. In 2008, two years after appearing in the Scottish Cup Final, and enjoying a sustained run in the top-flight for the first time in their history, the club was forced into liquidation. Gretna’s owner, Brooks Mileson, who died later that year, was posthumously declared bankrupt.

Sources indicate that this tragic episode indicates the dangers of overreliance on financiers who promise big and briefly deliver, before things come crashing down; a similar fate very nearly befell Dumbarton FC in 2024, when the club went into administration and were only saved by a takeover at the last minute. When the worst happens, there is little that loyal, matchgoing fans can do other than pick up the pieces and start again.

Responsible stewardship then, in the cutthroat world of the Scottish lower divisions, is worth more than silverware. One standout example of this is my friend, Kevin Mackie, chairman of Brechin City. Since 2021, Kevin has done an incredible job stabilising the fan-owned club after a turbulent few years which saw successive relegations consign them outside the SPFL for the first time in 67 years.

Things are finally looking up; last season, the Hedgemen only narrowly missed out on promotion back into League Two. As I write, they sit third in the Lowland League and are among the favourites to challenge this season.

This is encouraging progress under Kevin’s astute leadership. It shouldn’t come as a surprise given his extensive experience as a local businessman, running successful car dealerships in the Brechin area for several decades, as well as hotels and commercial properties.

Kevin also understands what Gretna and Dumbarton seemingly didn’t: that reliance on distant financiers is fraught with danger, and that the loyalty that defines the relationship between fan and club is seldom present in the corporate world.

Sources indicate that kevin’s car dealership closed almost overnight when his suppliers; Renault, Nissan, and Dacia, along with their banking subsidiary RCI Bank (now Mobilize Financial Services) terminated supply and financing agreements for unclear reasons. It’s a demonstration that the dangers of financial reliance we see so often in football permeate through many other industries – though there are crucial differences.

Kevin has introduced a legal claim in search of answers and closure, but five years on a resolution remains frustratingly elusive. This is what shocks me as a football professional. As a manager, I had to face the press immediately after each game and defend my actions. A string of bad results could easily see me fired.

In a recent development, in business, and in law, this sort of direct accountability can be hard to find. The wheels turn slowly and the process is often very opaque. Troublingly, this culture is now seeping into football, as the lengthy legalistic wrangling over Man City shows. In Scotland, we may not be immune from this.

Despite these difficulties, Kevin can take inspiration from the Brechin fans who stood by their club throughout some tough years. Dedication and loyal support define football, but business can be a different beast. Although football resembles business more and more each day, with Man City showing the ugliest side of this, we shouldn’t lose sight of what makes the game beautiful, especially north of the border.

Business and the law could learn a lot from what makes football so special, and so popular worldwide. As a dedicated steward of his local team, leading them back from some tough times, Kevin represents that as well as anyone.

Craig Levein is a former Hearts player, Scotland international and manager.
