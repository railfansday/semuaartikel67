Breaking: Fresh off a record $3 billion CMU pledge, Ken Griffin sends Pittsburgh 3 dinosaurs and $5 million

Nearly two months after his record-breaking $3 billion pledge to Carnegie Mellon University, billionaire Ken Griffin is sending Pittsburgh three dinosaurs.

The founder and CEO of Citadel is lending three rare long-necked dinosaur fossils to the Carnegie Museum of Natural History for 10 years, alongside a $5 million donation to support paleontological research and education, the museum unveiled Wednesday.

The deal adds to the tens of millions of dollars Griffin has already poured into dinosaur collecting, research, and exhibitions. It also keeps a rare find in scientists’ hands at a time when record auction prices are pushing more fossils into private collections, a trend many paleontologists warn could put specimens out of research’s reach.

The fossils, nicknamed Daisy, Lucky, and Lucy, belong to three plant-eating sauropods, one adult and two juveniles, discovered together in roughly 150-million-year-old rock in Wyoming and excavated between 2023 and 2025. Pittsburgh public radio station WESA reports that all three are Diplodocus carnegii, the same species as Dippy, the museum’s most famous dinosaur, which was named for founder Andrew Carnegie.

Griffin purchased the fossils last year through Sotheby’s for an undisclosed amount, according to the Financial Times.

Finding an adult and two young animals together gives researchers a rare chance to study how these dinosaurs grew and potentially interacted, according to a statement from Griffin Catalyst, Griffin’s philanthropic initiative. Scientists will also examine fossilized skin that the Pittsburgh Post-Gazette reports may be the largest sauropod skin sample found to date.

The $5 million will also give visitors a closer look at how paleontologists work. It will fund a fresh fossil-preparation laboratory open to public view, where visitors can watch scientists free fossils from rock and preserve them, a painstaking process that can take years and usually happens behind closed doors.

Sources indicate that the specimens are expected to arrive as soon as December, museum director Carey Miller told WESA. Preparation will begin in the museum’s existing lab before moving to the fresh facility once it is completed. Miller called the chance to prepare the fossils in public view and display them later “something that is unusual and really exciting.”.

WESA also reports the donation will fund two additional fossil preparers and educational programming, as well as expand the museum’s annual DinoFest event.

According to recent reports, “Daisy, Lucky, and Lucy offer an extraordinary window into our planet’s history,” Griffin stated in a statement. “I hope these experiences spark curiosity and inspire the next generation of scientists.”.

Griffin is part of a growing set of billionaires treating dinosaur fossils as a luxury asset, as Fortune previously reported.

Sources indicate that in July 2024, he paid $44.6 million at Sotheby’s for a remarkably complete stegosaurus skeleton nicknamed Apex, then a world-record price for a dinosaur fossil. That record fell this July, when a T. rex nicknamed Gus sold for $50.1 million to an unnamed bidder.

It has been revealed that griffin loaned Apex to New York’s American Museum of Natural History, where it is on display. Alongside the four-year loan, he provided undisclosed funding for educational and research initiatives, including a new three-year Griffin Postdoctoral Fellowship for Paleontology.

His other dinosaur projects include a $16.5 million gift to Chicago’s Field Museum in 2017 to revamp its dinosaur exhibitions, and funding of an undisclosed amount for an excavation and research project in South Africa aimed at better understanding the end of the dinosaur age.

His scientific philanthropy extends beyond paleontology. In 2019, he gave $125 million to Chicago’s Museum of Science and Industry, which has since been renamed in his honor.

The 58-year-old has an estimated net worth of $56.3 billion as of Thursday, according to Forbes, making him the world’s 33rd-richest person. He began investing from his Harvard dorm room in 1987 and founded Citadel in 1990, a year after graduating. He cofounded market-making giant Citadel Securities in 2002.

Griffin Catalyst claims his total giving exceeds $5.7 billion, and education has been a major focus.

In 2023, he gave his alma mater, Harvard University, $300 million, and its Graduate School of Arts and Sciences now bears his name. His biggest commitment yet came on Sept. 30, when he pledged $3 billion to Carnegie Mellon University.

Sources indicate that of that, $2 billion will build a fresh campus in Miami, where Griffin moved Citadel’s headquarters in 2022. The remaining $1 billion will support the university’s Pittsburgh campus, including $500 million for its computer science school, now the Kenneth C. Griffin School of Computer Science.
