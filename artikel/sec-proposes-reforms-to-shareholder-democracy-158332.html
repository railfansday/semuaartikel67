📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, every year, corporate shareholders have several hundred proposals to vote on at corporate annual meetings or, more often, via their proxy ballots. Those proposals have become more controversial and divisive over time. The last several years of battles over “woke capitalism” and its inevitable backlash were largely fought through the shareholder resolution process. Now the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) is about to rewrite the rules for what makes it onto those shareholder ballots, and that could mean a reset for how politics impacts corporate America — and vice versa.

Unlike many federal rule changes, which often include relatively minor tweaks to the existing bureaucracy, the SEC’s current proposal would repeal a major regulation known as Rule 14a-8 that’s been on the books for decades. Originally implemented in 1942 and amended multiple times since, the rule gave the SEC the power to referee which ideas corporate boards need to put up for a vote and which ones they can safely ignore. But the SEC is now proposing to repeal the rule entirely, leaving those decisions up to state governments and individual companies.
