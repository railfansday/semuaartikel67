Wealthy investors seek tax savings from municipal bonds as yields remain elevated

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that the business of tax-loss harvesting is booming with wealthy investors pouring billions of dollars into products like tax-aware long-short strategies. As 10-year Treasury yields have climbed to levels not seen since 2007, tax-savvy clients are also turning to municipal bonds to keep more of what they earn.

"High-net-worth investors, because of all the noise in direct indexing and tax-aware long-short strategies, have begun to expect tax management from all of their investments, including their fixed income," mentioned Gavin Romm, senior vice president and head of separately managed accounts at AllianceBernstein.

In a recent development, while tax-loss harvesting can be done with other types of fixed income, high-income investors typically gravitate to municipal bonds for their tax-exempt income. Separately managed accounts, known as SMAs, held about $1.6 trillion in municipal bonds at the end of 2025, a 44% jump since 2017, per JPMorgan's estimate.

For top-bracket investors, municipal bonds can offer a twofold benefit: tax-equivalent yields that approach long-run stock-market returns, and losses that may offset gains elsewhere in a portfolio, according to Ran Leshem, BlackRock's head of SMA solutions. BlackRock, which manages $42 billion in muni SMAs, estimates that assets across muni SMAs have grown by 10% industry-wide over the past three years.

In a recent development, "You can get a tax-equivalent yield of over 7% that is becoming quite compelling for a lot of clients," noted Leshem. "We are seeing yields that haven't been around for quite a few years, so it's a good opportunity for clients to lock in yields at these higher rates.".

According to recent reports, rising yields earlier this fall pushed bond prices down, leaving some investors with paper losses, Leshem remarked. However, tax-loss harvesting can be harder with bonds than with stock, particularly municipal bonds, he noted. The municipal bond market is highly fragmented with more than 50,000 issuers, and it costs more and takes longer to transact, he said.

"There might not always be liquidity or someone willing to buy it for a fair price," Leshem said.

That said, bonds can offer a distinct tax advantage from equities, according to Romm.

By selling a stock at a loss, an investor can reduce the current tax bill by offsetting gains elsewhere. In exchange, the investor faces a potentially larger taxable gain if they later sell the replacement stock.

However, if an investor sells a bond at a loss and buys a replacement bond for more than its par value (or face value) the replacement bond's tax basis is generally adjusted down over time as it approaches maturity, Romm remarked. If the investor holds it to maturity, the amount repaid may match that adjusted basis, leaving no capital gain or loss on the replacement bond, he added.

Industry observers note that "Tax-loss harvesting with equities, by definition, is tax deferral," he said. "With bonds, when done thoughtfully, it is actually tax avoidance.".

That noted, investors must be careful not to violate the IRS wash-sale rule by buying a "substantially identical asset" within 30 days before or after the sale.

That tax benefit gives fixed income another job beyond providing income and stability, Romm said.

According to recent reports, "These aren't really positioned as get-rich strategies. They're more of stay-rich strategies," he said.
