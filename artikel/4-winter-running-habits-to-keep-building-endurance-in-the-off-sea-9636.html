4 Winter Running Habits to Keep Building Endurance in the Off-Season—No Treadmill Required

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

When days get shorter, temperatures get colder, and you no longer have a race on the horizon, the motivation to run consistently can seriously start to wane.

But prioritizing your aerobic fitness all year means you don’t have to start from scratch when it comes time to train for a race again, says Brant Stachel, an Ontario-based USATF-certified endurance coach at Fast and Free Performance Coaching. “Anytime you’re taking big swaths of time off, you have to go back into rebuilding mode where you spend weeks getting back up to the fitness that you previously had,” he says.

Recent findings suggest that whether you’re a longtime treadmill hater in need of more exciting options or you simply don’t have access to one, we tapped experts for their best alternatives that allow you to maintain your endurance without hopping on the belt. Follow these habits to keep your aerobic engine revved in the off-season.

Recent findings suggest that the minimum amount of training you need to do depends on your fitness level and your current (or most recent) training load. “If you’re somebody who’s running five to seven days a week, you probably still want to do three to four days a week,” Stachel suggests.

Industry observers note that even one to two days a week can keep you from starting completely from scratch in the spring season, indicates Danielle Kroot, DPT, certified run coach and physical therapist based in Portland, Maine. “It’s also keeping your tissues and muscles exposed to that type of load so that there’s not this shock to your system when you start training for a race again, and can help decrease injury risk,” she adds.

Keep in mind it usually takes six weeks of consecutive low-mileage weeks to really lose fitness gains, states Andrea Dell, an RRCA-certified run coach, so taking a few weeks off (especially if you’re recovering from marathon season) won’t do any harm; it’ll actually help you recharge.

Sources indicate that unless you’re training for marathons year-round, you’re likely periodizing your training, Stachel says, which means cycling through off-seasons, shorter distances, and maintenance mode in between major race training cycles. He explains that periodizing your training is really important for staying well-rounded and building adequate speed, strength, and endurance—all factors that make you a stronger, faster runner.

In a recent development, winter is a great time to analyze any gaps or weaknesses in your fitness, especially following a fall marathon season, Stachel indicates. Look at your recent training cycles and factor in what didn’t work for you. You might address strength and injury prevention after dealing with nagging calf pain, or consider focusing on speed workouts you didn’t get to hit as much during a marathon season, like mile race pace, 3K, and 5K pace efforts, he adds.

According to recent reports, strength work builds stability, power, and durability for better performance, and can help you handle mileage and speed. Building more strength in the off-season will translate to more speed, power, and injury prevention when you decide to ramp mileage back up.

Off-season strength should generally target major muscle groups runners rely on most, like the calves, glutes, and quads. Both Dell and Kroot recommend focusing on deadlift variations, squat variations, lunges, calf raises, step-ups, and plyometric movements.

Generally, you want to choose weights that make you feel like you could do only two more repetitions; that signals that the weight is heavy enough, Kroot explains.

“Even if you’ve got those five-pound weights hanging out in your basement, you can see how many squats you can do until you feel close to exhaustion,” Stachel adds.

If you have limited equipment at home, each expert recommends combining movements, such as doing a 15-minute stair climb, followed by bodyweight burpees or planks.

It has been revealed that one particularly snowy winter, Stachel broke up his 90-minute run into five different sections, utilizing the equipment at his local gym and combining running and cross-training in one session. He ran 15 minutes to his local gym, went on the elliptical for 20 minutes, biked for 20 minutes, went on the treadmill for 20 minutes, then ran 15 minutes home. “The idea of simply going on a treadmill for 90 minutes was mentally numbing,” he indicates.

Stachel explains that while it’s best to complete a 90-minute run through running alone to stay specific to your sport, you have to find a workout you can stick with. “The best winter workout is the one you’ll actually repeat and build on next week,” he notes.

In the off-season, cross-training is your friend, Stachel says. Even if you don’t have any injuries, it’s a good idea to add some form of alternative aerobic-focused movement to your routine such as swimming, aqua jogging, elliptical, or cycling. “Then it doesn’t seem like a shock to the system when that first snowfall drops and you’ve got a week where you can’t go outside and run,” he adds. Stachel explains that gym memberships can be especially helpful for this reason.

Stachel’s go-to workout: a tempo session on the elliptical. “This has the closest carryover to running in terms of cardiovascular function,” he indicates. “It also loads the tissues because you’re more upright, whereas on the bike you don’t get that.”.

Recent findings suggest that you can do any run interval workout on the elliptical or another cross-training machine, Stachel notes, but the general rule is to take your typical outdoor recovery time and cut it in half. For example, if you give yourself two minutes to recover between tempo intervals outside, take one-minute recoveries on the elliptical. This might look like three 10-minute bouts at tempo pace with one minute of easy efforts in between.

If you don’t typically go to a gym, Dell and Kroot recommend stair workouts, at-home workout videos that provide follow-along Zumba or high-intensity interval workouts, and jumping rope.

Recent findings suggest that each expert recommends making an effort to still get outside as much as possible, even if it’s once or twice a week, or more during weeks when your local weather cooperates. Bundling up for the occasional outdoor run keeps training exciting, and helps your body stay adjusted to the roads.

Here are a few of their tips for making the most of those outdoor runs.

Industry observers note that oftentimes the best way to stay consistent with running in the off-season is to find safe, paved places to run when roads might be wet or covered in snow. Stachel recommends asking other runners in your community or talking to runners at your local run store to find the best routes in your area.

Recent findings suggest that “Every city I’ve been to or coached in during the winter has had some hidden gem that’s plowed for some reason,” Stachel claims. This might be a municipal building, a local mall, hospital, or other parking lots. “Those little tips and tricks can make it all the more fun,” he adds.

Recent findings suggest that good gear becomes your armor for bracing cold winter runs. “If you’re out there and you’re underdressed, you’re miserable the entire time. If you’re overdressed, then you’re sweating through your clothes and your heart rate’s high and then you’re frustrated,” Dell says.

It has been revealed that to dress according to winter weather, use the Runner’s World what to wear tool, which highlights you how to layer based on different weather patterns. Dell also recommends investing in a good pair of fleece-lined tights or joggers, as well as ear covers and gloves. Stachel also recommends microspikes that you can strap onto running shoes for better traction on wet or snowy surfaces.

In a recent development, view the off-season as an opportunity to rise to another challenge, Stachel notes. “There are so many times that as athletes, we feel hardcore for doing things. You could probably figure out a way to feel really hardcore for navigating a crazy winter or running,” he says, noting that a sense of empowerment comes from training through a season that many people don’t train through.

In a recent development, “Winter doesn’t change what builds endurance, it just changes how you collect it,” states Stachel. You might spend more time hacking your training and moving workouts indoors, or bundling up to run outside.

By spring, you can expect an “epic” racing season that feels rewarding and full of satisfaction because of all the hard work you put in during winter months, Stachel notes. Then, you will likely find the urge to continue training every winter because you know you’ll feel rewarded when the spring comes and you’re much closer to hitting your paces and PRs.
