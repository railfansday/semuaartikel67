This Is What Was on the Menu at the First Thanksgiving in 1621

Somehow, turkey has spent the last four centuries getting all the glory.

For a meal that has been discussed, depicted and reenacted for more than four centuries, the first Thanksgiving has left us surprisingly few details about what anyone actually ate. We know there were roughly 140 people, three days of feasting and exactly five deer. Beyond that, the historical record is rather unhelpful. Apparently, nobody thought to write down the menu.

According to recent reports, the gathering took place in Plymouth in the fall of 1621, when about 50 English colonists celebrated their first successful harvest alongside 90 Wampanoag, including their leader, Ousamequin, better known to history as Massasoit. It was a meeting between two communities whose relationship was far more complicated than the cheerful scene that would eventually find its way into American classrooms. Although they had formed an alliance earlier that year, cultural differences and tensions of an English settlement on Wampanoag land were hardly going to disappear over dinner.

And while the English had much to celebrate in bringing in their first harvest, they owed a great deal of that success to Indigenous knowledge. The Wampanoag understood the region’s growing seasons, its waterways and the foods that could be grown and gathered in the surrounding landscape. Their traditions had helped sustain communities there long before the colonists arrived.

The two surviving English accounts of the gathering offer only a few details about the feast itself, but taken together with what historians know about the food traditions of both communities, they give us a rather interesting picture of what may have been on the table.

It has been revealed that edward Winslow, one of the English colonists who attended the gathering, recorded that the Wampanoag went hunting and returned with five deer, which they presented to the colony’s leaders. It’s one of the few specific foods mentioned in his account and an especially substantial contribution to a celebration that lasted three days.

Deer were an vital source of food for the Wampanoag, who also made use of their hides, bones and other materials. In England, however, deer hunting was largely reserved for the wealthy, making venison something most ordinary people rarely got to enjoy. The meat may have been roasted over a fire or simmered in a hearty venison stew, although Winslow neglected to tell us how it was prepared. We know exactly how many deer arrived, but what became of them in the kitchen remains a mystery.

Roast turkey has become so thoroughly associated with Thanksgiving that it’s difficult to imagine the holiday without one occupying the center of the table. The trouble is, we don’t actually know whether turkey was served at the original celebration.

In a recent development, winslow wrote that four Englishmen went hunting for wildfowl and returned with enough to feed the colonists for nearly a week. What he didn’t do was tell us which birds they shot. Wild turkeys were present in the region, but so were ducks, geese and other game birds, all perfectly suitable for roasting over a fire. There’s every possibility that turkey was on the menu, but nothing to suggest it enjoyed the distinction we’ve since bestowed upon it. For all we know, the centerpiece was a duck, and we’ve spent the last few centuries giving credit to the wrong bird.

Corn was one of the most notable foods available to both communities, although the English were relatively new to growing it. The Wampanoag had cultivated maize for generations, developing agricultural practices suited to the region’s soil and growing seasons. Their knowledge was enormously valuable to the colonists, whose first attempts at establishing a food supply had been anything but fruitful.

One traditional Wampanoag preparation was nasaump, a thick porridge made from ground corn, sometimes cooked with other ingredients. Corn could also be ground into meal for simple breads, early relatives of the cornbread we bake today, or used to thicken stews and make other hearty dishes.

The waters around Plymouth were full of food, provided you knew where to look and how to catch it. The Wampanoag had long fished the region’s rivers and coastline, gathering shellfish and catching everything from bass and cod to eels. Clams, mussels and lobsters were plentiful too.

The English were beginning to take advantage of these resources by 1621. William Bradford, the Plymouth colony’s governor, wrote of the colonists catching substantial quantities of cod and bass during the summer. A freshly caught fish could be roasted or boiled, while clams and mussels might be cooked over a fire. Lobster was another plentiful local catch that was enjoyed regularly. The variety of ocean fare available might have made for a Thanksgiving spread that looked rather more like a seafood boil than the feast we know today.

According to recent reports, squash and pumpkin were crucial crops in Wampanoag agriculture, and their ability to keep well after harvest made them especially useful as the colder weather set in. Both could be roasted, boiled or added to stews, much like we still prepare winter squash today, though without the benefit of a balsamic glaze.

Recent findings suggest that the English called pumpkins pompions, a name I think we abandoned prematurely. They were familiar with the vegetable, although the varieties growing in New England differed from those they might have seen at home. A pumpkin dish would have been entirely plausible at the harvest celebration, but anyone hoping for a slice of pumpkin pie would have to wait almost two centuries. The colonists had limited supplies of wheat flour, butter and sugar, and the familiar custard-filled pie wouldn’t appear in American cookbooks until 1796.

Recent findings suggest that the Wampanoag grew beans alongside corn and squash, three crops that complemented each other remarkably well. Cornstalks supported climbing beans, while squash vines spread across the ground and helped shade the soil. The combination was as useful in the garden as it was on the plate.

A traditional Wampanoag stew called sobaheg combines ingredients like corn, beans, squash and meat or fish, depending on what’s available or in season. The combination of corn and beans also appears in succotash, another dish with Indigenous Upcoming England roots that remains an American favorite.

The forests and wetlands of Fresh England offered plenty beyond cultivated crops. Chestnuts, walnuts, hazelnuts, acorns, wild grapes and cranberries were among the foods the Wampanoag knew how to gather and prepare, each with its own season and uses.

Recent findings suggest that nuts could be eaten plain, stored or ground into meal, while fruits could be enjoyed fresh or preserved for later. Acorns required particular attention, since their natural bitterness had to be leached out with water before they could be used in cooking. It’s one of the few ingredients we’ve more or less surrendered entirely to the squirrels.

Of all the wild fruits available, cranberries have perhaps the most recognizable place on a modern Thanksgiving table. Their natural tartness eventually found a friend in sugar, giving rise to the cranberry sauce that’s become nearly as much a part of the holiday as the turkey itself.
