📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

From Judge Steven Merryday (M.D. Fla.) yesterday in Trump v. N.Y. Times Co.:.

It has been revealed that times v. Sullivan states that the First Amendment "prohibits a public official's recovering damages for a defamatory falsehood relating to his official conduct unless he proves that the statement was made with 'actual malice'—that is, with knowledge that it was false or with reckless disregard of whether it was false or not." … {[N]otwithstanding the ordinary meaning and usage of "actual malice," "allegations of ill-will, improper motive or personal animosity play no role in determining whether a defendant acted with actual malice," which "does not concern ill-will, improper motive, or personal animosity," a conclusion which appears correct, in law if not elsewhere in the English language or in the common understanding. In sum, "actual malice is not about whether the speaker had evil intent or a motive arising from ill-will."} "[F]ailure to investigate before publishing, even when a reasonably prudent person would have done so, is not sufficient to establish reckless disregard[.]" Harte-Hanks Communications v. Connaughton (1989).

[But] "reckless disregard may be found where there are obvious reasons to doubt the veracity of the informant or the accuracy of his report." "Although failure to investigate will not alone support a finding of actual malice, the purposeful avoidance of the truth is in a different category." ….

The court concluded that a large set of statements that Trump alleges to be libelous aren't actionable, "for one or more of the many and varied reasons amply and ably explained by the defendants in the motion to dismiss." But it concludes that several statements may be actionable, even given the "actual malice" requirement:.

[A]s alleged in the complaint, the book, Lucky Loser, includes these statements: (1) "Donald Trump had received the equivalent of more than $400 million from his father, much of it through fraudulent tax evasion schemes"; (2) "In the language of the tax code, Fred had given his son taxable gifts masquerading as loans, a likely tax fraud that went unnoticed"; (3) "A plan was soon devised to funnel some of Fred's cash to Donald and his three siblings. They would accomplish that goal by adding a sham layer to Fred's business, one designed to evade the gift and estate taxes"; (4) "So the Trumps began submitting the padded bills to request rent increases from state officials. Part of the cost of Donld Trump's off-the-books inheritance would be passed along to this father's middle-income tenants"; (5) "Between the trust funds, the invoice-padding operation, and other devices created to siphon off his father's wealth outside the gift and estate tax system, Donald was already collecting almost $2 million a year from his father companies. His own business failures meant he kept that money tax-free"; and (6) "Donald Trump had faced these moments before, periods when it seemed his failures could not be saved only by aggressive tax maneuvers or not paying his debts". As described in the complaint, the second article includes the statement (7) "Federal, state and local authorities looked into his ties with the Mafia, found violations of money laundering rules and penalized him for skirting stock trade rules.".

In these seven instances, the allegations of the complaint quite plausibly and reasonably create an inference that the authors and the publishers knew that these statements, accusing the plaintiff of planning and perpetrating complex crimes and knowingly receiving the proceeds and other benefits of these crimes, were "based on incomplete, minimal information" and that the author and the publisher "realized that a responsible assessment of the Trump family's compliance with tax laws would have required forensic auditing, expert tax and legal advice, and other expensive investigatory tools." Further, the complaint alleges that the authors and the publishers knew that "neither President Trump nor Fred Trump was investigated for, charged with, or convicted of tax fraud ….".

In other words, the facts alleged in the complaint create at least a plausible inference that the authors and the publishers knew that no reliable conclusion about violations of the tax laws, including especially the estate and gift tax laws, is possible without reliance on qualified opinion formulated after the necessary factual and legal research and analysis, which they knowingly failed to obtain.

The defendants respond in part to the plaintiff's claims based on allegations of crime as follows:.

Craig and Buettner "analyz[ed] about one hundred thousand pages of audited financial statements, tax returns, bank records, general records, and legal papers." Their conclusions regarding tax fraud were informed by numerous experts. The 2018 Article quotes Lee-Ford Tritt, an "expert in gift and estate tax law," who mentioned the Trumps' use of All County was "highly suspicious" and could constitute criminal tax fraud; Adam S. Kaufmann, "a former chief of investigations for the Manhattan district attorney's office," who said it "smells like a crime"; and Mitchell Gans, a tax law professor, who explained how gift tax returns can evade IRS scrutiny. Reliance on experts controverts actual malice—particularly when, as here, Plaintiff makes no effort to challenge such reliance. See, e.g., Levan v. Cap. Cities/ABC, Inc. (11th Cir. 1999) (reliance on "numerous objective experts" and "industry professionals" defeated actual malice); Silvester v. Am. Broad. Cos., Inc. (11th Cir. 1988) (no actual malice where challenged publication "was thoroughly researched through interviews with experts").

In response to a question about the basis for the accusation of tax crimes, the defendants identified page 451 in the "Acknowledgements" section of the book, which states, "In coming to terms with the highly technical areas of tax law, we would have been lost in the weeds without the generous help of several experts. We hold a special place in our hearts for Jim Ledley, one of the most prominent estate and trust lawyers in New York, who was endlessly patient and exceptionally generous with his time. Jim made the arcane work fun.".

Obviously, even a qualified expert's offering a superficial characterization such as a transaction is "suspicious" or "could" violate the law or attributing to a transaction the (unspecified and undefined) "smell" of crime or noting that a gift tax return can evade IRS scrutiny or generously giving his immensely valuable time to aid laypersons "lost in the weeds" is far from a formal legal opinion sufficient to remove serious doubt about the truthfulness and accuracy of a statement accusing a specific person of fraud or tax evasion or some related crime arising from a specific transaction, including a crime allegedly effected through "ties to the Mafia." The confident detection of tax and other crimes, including money laundering, of the kind that some of the challenged statements attribute to the plaintiff, that is, tax crimes including estate and gift tax crimes and the like, is a task that is intensely fact-dependent, and the law that governs is singularly complex and within the immediate competence of only a relatively small cohort of specialized practitioners.

It has been revealed that anyone who publishes the claim that someone else has committed a crime of the complex nature described in some of the challenged statements and who has not received an explicit opinion from a qualified source after a thorough examination of pertinent facts necessarily proceeds with particularized reason to doubt the truth of their statements and with knowledge of a high degree of probable falsity of the statements; in other words, they know that they don't know, but they are undeterred. Knowing of the necessity for that opinion but failing to procure that opinion is a fine example of the purposeful avoidance of the truth, a knowing failure that remains actionable, even after Times v. Sullivan….
