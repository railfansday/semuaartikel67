The Investigatory Powers Tribunal – essentially a British court that deals with complaints about state surveillance – has ruled that Apple is allowed to reveal a secret order it received from the UK government.

Apple had already found a clever way to alert British customers to the classified order without breaking the law, but it can now confirm what we all already knew, and talk openly about the issue ….

The story centers on Apple’s introduction of the Advanced Data Protection (ADP) privacy feature back in 2022. This extended end-to-end encryption to almost all your iCloud data, meaning Apple has no access, and therefore cannot grant access to any government agencies who come knocking on its door.

The feature is off by default because it means Apple cannot help you access any of your data if, for example, you forget your iCloud password. However, those who want to maximize the privacy of their iCloud account can enable it.

In 2025, the British government ordered Apple to create a backdoor to allow access by security services and law enforcement. Even worse, the order was issued under legislation that prohibits companies from revealing that they have received this demand.

Recent findings suggest that this would have meant that British customers who enabled ADP would think that their data was safe when in fact it wasn’t. As we’ve said many times before, any backdoor created for use by the good guys is vulnerable to discovery and exploitation by the bad guys.

It would have been illegal for Apple to reveal that it had been instructed to compromise the privacy of customer data, but the company found a clever way to effectively communicate this without breaking the law. Apple announced it was withdrawing ADP from the UK without explaining its reason.

Sources indicate that “Apple can no longer offer Advanced Data Protection (ADP) in the United Kingdom to fresh customers and current UK users will eventually need to disable this security feature. ADP protects iCloud data with end-to-end encryption, which means the data can only be decrypted by the user who owns it, and only on their trusted devices. We are gravely disappointed that the protections provided by ADP will not be available to our customers in the UK […] As we have remarked many times before, we have never built a backdoor or master key to any of our products or services and we never will.”.

Tell me you’ve been ordered to create a backdoor to ADP without telling me you’ve been ordered to create a backdoor to ADP ….

Industry observers note that at this point, it was an open secret. However, ridiculously, despite the fact that everyone in the security and privacy field knew about it and was talking about it, Apple was still not allowed to officially confirm it.

The iPhone maker asked the Investigatory Powers Tribunal (IPT) to allow it to openly discuss the order, with privacy campaigners backing this call. The Financial Times reports that the court has now ruled in Apple’s favor.

The UK government must admit that it issued a secret order to Apple to allow it access to encrypted customer data, a court has ruled […] The Home Office has “not established that it would be damaging to national security” to reveal that Apple had received a “technical capability notice” (TCN), the tribunal stated. The ruling means that […] “much” of Apple and the non-profits’ legal challenge can now take place in open court.

One of the great ironies is that most people had never heard of ADP until the government effectively made it headline news in the tech press three years after it released – a classic example of the Streisand Effect.

Industry observers note that this ruling is a win both for transparency and commonsense. To be clear, the actual requirement for a backdoor remains in place, but this can now be challenged in open court.
