GOP lawmakers are rushing through high-priority bills before newly elected Democrats are sworn in.

Climate groups fear lawmakers could pass a bill by the end of the year that would give fossil fuel companies immunity from lawsuits and legislation that aims to hold them accountable for the climate crisis.

After the blue wave election, Republican lawmakers are rushing to pass high-priority bills before newly elected Democrats are sworn in. Hundreds of elected leaders and climate groups sent a letter to Democratic leadership urging them to reject any proposal that would shield fossil fuel companies from liability for the climate crisis.

Iyla Shornstein, political director for the Center for Climate Integrity, said the election results highlighted voters don’t want this type of legislation. “Voters at the polls sent a clear message about how costs are impacting their everyday lives. Gas prices are skyrocketing, but why would we spend our time giving special treatment to massive oil, who are also simultaneously enjoying record profits at our expense? … The elections sent a pretty clear message that our elected leaders need to be spending their time helping working families, not major corporations like big oil.”.

In April, Republicans introduced the Stop Climate Shakedowns bill that would shield fossil fuel companies from various attempts to hold them accountable. Ted Cruz and Harriet Hageman, two Republican lawmakers, introduced companion Senate and House bills. “Radical environmental groups have waged a coordinated campaign to weaponize our judicial system against American energy producers, including many in Texas,” Cruz remarked in a statement announcing the Senate bill. “They’re using meritless lawsuits to bankrupt our energy industry, kill good paying jobs and drive up the cost of electricity and gasoline for hardworking families.”.

Eleven states, two tribal governments and dozens of municipalities have filed lawsuits against major oil companies to hold them accountable for the climate emergency. The lawsuits are grounded in science that connects climate disasters to emissions from fossil fuel companies and increasing evidence that the companies deceived the public about their own internal science warning them about the dangers of fossil fuels.

Industry observers note that another accountability effort has come from states such as Newly introduced York and Vermont that have passed climate superfund laws to recover costs by shifting the financial burden from climate disasters onto the fossil fuel companies that caused them. Both New York and Vermont’s superfund laws have faced opposition including challenges by the Department of Justice; a judge struck down the New York superfund law in September, the state’s governor, Kathy Hochul, is appealing.

The fossil fuel immunity bill has four key parts, said Shornstein: it would dismiss existing climate liability lawsuits against fossil fuel companies, prohibit upcoming lawsuits, void climate superfund laws and assert that the federal government is the sole authority over the regulation of greenhouse gas emissions and climate crisis, which pre-empts states from acting on it.

Stopping climate lawsuits and superfund laws was a top priority for the American Petroleum Institute this year, but the bill stalled in September when the House judiciary committee failed to advance it.

Shornstein stated Republican lawmakers could still rush through the bill or attach immunity language to other legislation before the end of the current Congress. “We’re at a critical moment, because Trump and the GOP and significant oil’s allies want to bail out the fossil fuel industry while they still have power,” she said. “Immunity for big oil has been high on the list of priorities, so this is their last ditch effort.”.

In a recent development, “We need to be extremely on our toes and vigilant and ready to mobilize to make sure that Congress rejects this sweeping giveaway to major oil,” mentioned Kathy Mulvey, program director for fossil fuel accountability at the Union of Concerned Scientists. “I expect that they would see the lame duck session as a real opportunity to push through the Cruz-Hageman bill.”.

The bill would block source attribution science, a growing field of science that connects climate disasters to the emissions of specific oil and gas companies. The bill says such scientific efforts “lack scientific credibility and are, therefore, arbitrary”.

“The fact that [they] are specifically going after this field of science and the scientists who are playing a leading role indicates really how much they’re threatened by being held accountable for the harms that they’re causing,” Mulvey said.

Recent findings suggest that the immunity bill follows a pattern of powerful industries asking Congress to shield them from liability – although not all have succeeded. In the 1990s, when tobacco companies faced dozens of lawsuits seeking to recover costs from smoking-related illnesses, they unsuccessfully lobbied Congress to introduce legislation to shield them. And in 2005, in response to a wave of lawsuits attempting to hold gunmakers liable for violence, the National Rifle Association (NRA) achieved a lobbying victory when Congress passed a liability shield law, the Protection of Lawful Commerce in Arms Act.

That gun law may have inspired the push for a massive oil immunity law. In a letter last yeat to Pam Bondi, the former US attorney general, Republican state attorneys general asked for a liability shield for big oil, which they directly compared with the shield law for gun manufacturers. “The fossil fuel industry explicitly invokes the liability waiver given to the gun industry in 2005 as its model for what it’s trying to achieve,” remarked Mulvey.

At the state level, fossil fuel company allies are engaged in a similar effort to shield them from liability. A ProPublica investigation reported that immunity bills introduced in a number of Republican-controlled state houses were part of a coordinated effort by a rightwing network that included activist Leonard Leo and the American Legislative Exchange Council.

In a recent development, in a statement to ProPublica, Leo did not dispute the findings. “For decades, the left had leveraged immense resources to capture the institutions that shape our society,” he wrote in a text. “That takeover resulted in a radically woke culture that does not reflect the will of the American people, or the pillars of limited constitutional government that made our country great. That is why our enterprise supports organizations that are committed to crushing liberal dominance and restoring balance in the institutions that shape society.”.

It has been revealed that “We’re also seeing this push at the state level too,” Shornstein said. “There were five states that enacted legislation this cycle that provides pretty expansive legal protections to the industry. So they’re taking a very holistic, if you will, approach to escaping accountability.”.
