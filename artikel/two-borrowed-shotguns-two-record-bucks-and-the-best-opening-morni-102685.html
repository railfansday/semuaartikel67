The 1965 deer season in the hill country of southeastern Ohio was 15 minutes old, but the woods were silent. I was hunkered down with my back against a tree in an open place on the side of a massive ridge. The place was too open, but I hadn’t realized that when I sat down there just before the opening hour of 9 o’clock. A deer could see me a mile away, I told myself.

But I didn’t want to risk moving, now that shooting was legal. Anyway, I didn’t know where there was a better stand. So I just sat still, hoping for the best.

Sources indicate that it was a clear November morning, frosty but promising to warm up later. Most of the timber on the side of the ridge was oak. That meant acorns, and acorns are likely to mean deer. Maybe I hadn’t picked such a bad spot after all. It was warm and pleasant there in the sun. I’d wait awhile and see what happened.

In a recent development, suddenly, the stillness was shattered by a shot. A few seconds later another one rapped out. Though I wasn’t sure which direction the shots came from, I decided it was some hunter over the ridge behind me. I didn’t dream what had happened. The woods got quiet again, and then bluejays started to holler on the ridge.

According to recent reports, I’d hunted deer only three years before this and was too inexperienced to know what those noisy bluejays meant. I’ll know next time.

Recent findings suggest that the next thing I heard was something moving in the dry oak leaves behind me. It didn’t sound big enough for a deer, but it startled me. I swiveled my head slowly for a look. It seemed to take me an eternity to turn my head and body without making any sudden motions or noise.

Industry observers note that whatever I was hearing kept coming, walking slowly and deliberately. I finally got twisted into position and eased one eye around the trunk of the tree I’d been sitting against.

What I saw then literally took my breath away.

According to recent reports, walking slowly down the slope, only 15 or 20 yards away and coming almost straight toward me, was a giant buck with the biggest rack I’d ever seen. The morning sun glinted on his polished, brown tines, and he looked as if he were wearing the bleached roots of an upturned stump on his head. I didn’t know it then, but I was looking at a whitetail deer that was better than any that had ever been killed in Ohio.

He stopped for a second and looked back up the ridge, the direction he’d come from, and I remember thinking that somebody over there must have spooked him. Then he turned my way and came on, walking calmly and unafraid. To this day, I don’t know why I didn’t have the worst case of buck fever a young deer hunter ever had.

In a recent development, my dad, Don Allison, and I had left home the previous evening and driven to Wellston, where we were to stay for the hunting season with dad’s friend and hunting partner, Jim Arthur. We live near South Solon, in the flat prairie country about 40 miles southwest of Columbus. Dad runs a 232-acre farm on some of the nation’s richest land. He handles a herd of 20 Black Angus cows and their calves and feeds out about 350 hogs a year.

I was 17 years old at the time and a senior at Madison South High School south of London.

I’ve hunted with dad ever since I could carry a gun. We get rabbits, pheasants, and fox squirrels in the farming areas around home, and the last few years we’ve made trips for grouse to the hill country of eastern Ohio. In 1962, dad took me deer hunting for the first time.

The deer season is the only real vacation dad takes. He started hunting deer in 1944, the second year Ohio had an open season, and has missed only one fall since, except for years when the season was closed. But he’s been plagued by hard luck. In almost 20 years of hunting, up to last fall, he’d never killed a deer.

The closest he ever came was getting a shot at a doe, but he confessed that buck fever — if a female deer can give you buck fever — got the best of him that time, and he scored a clean miss. He loves to hunt, though, and has never gotten discouraged.

“I’ll be lucky one of these years,” he always noted, not knowing just how lucky.

In a recent development, my record was no better. In three falls of hunting, I hadn’t even seen a deer. But, like dad, I was sure my time would come.

Sources indicate that ohio’s 1965 gun season on deer was a restricted one, varying from region to region according to herd conditions and the management aims of the state game men. In the northwestern counties, level farm country, there was a three-day either-sex season, but hunters had to have in advance a landowner’s written permission to hunt in order to get a deer permit.

Sources indicate that in the hill country of southeastern Ohio, where we’ve always hunted, there was a bucks-only season beginning on Monday, November 8, and lasting four days. Hunting hours were from 9 a.m. to 5 p.m., and only shotguns loaded with slugs or pumpkin balls were legal, as in the past.

As November drew near, dad and I both developed the usual deer-hunting itch. It’s hard to tell anybody how much our annual whitetail hunt means to us. But for a time, it didn’t look as if either of us could make it. Dad had almost 100 acres of corn to pick. Deer season was about a month earlier than usual, and the picking job would be no more than well started by November 8. If he took time off to go hunting, wintry weather could set in before he finished the corn picking.

“I’m going,” he finally told us, “even if I have to pick corn when the snow’s blowing.”.

Sources indicate that my problem was basketball — I had a good chance to make the school team. We’d practiced about a week when deer season rolled around, and the rule was that anybody who missed practice unexcused would be dropped from the squad.

It has been revealed that it was a fair rule, and I knew going deer hunting wasn’t a valid excuse. If I went, my basketball career would end before it started. But the hunt was too much to pass up.

“If you’re going,” I told dad, “so am I.”.

There was one other problem. We both had gun trouble. Dad usually shoots a Model 12 Winchester 12 gauge that he’s had for years. But it was out of order, so he borrowed a 12 gauge Remington autoloader. I was getting ready to buy a new shotgun, so I borrowed a Browning 16 gauge automatic on trial. We’d both be using guns we’d had little chance to get familiar with.

“Well, if we see deer and miss,” dad stated with a grin, “at least we’ve got a good alibi.”.

Dad hunted for many years in the Shawnee State Forest, along the Ohio River west of Portsmouth. It’s a 60,000-acre tract, rugged and steep, with oak-covered ridges and few roads. It’s easy to get lost there unless you’re familiar with the hills. Other parts of Ohio may have more deer, but many hunters prefer this wild country.

It has been revealed that dad had another reason for hunting there. It was the favorite spot of a good friend of his, Belvin Adams. Adams lived in Wheelersburg, only a short distance away, and he and a party of his friends had hunted the Shawnee for years.

It has been revealed that but three years ago, the rubber firm Adams works for moved him to Indonesia. Since then, Jim Arthur, who lives in Wellston and had worked with Adams, has invited dad and me to stay at his home and hunt with his party in the Raccoon and Zaleski state forests, in Vinton County. These forests have a combined area of about 30,000 acres, and the timberlands are mixed with some farming acreage, making good deer range. That was where dad and I would go for the 1965 season.

Sources indicate that jim Arthur has a son, Steve, who’s my age and, like me, was a senior in high school then. We have a lot in common, especially our enthusiasm for deer hunting.

In a recent development, we got to Wellston Sunday evening, too late to scout the area we intended to hunt. And Jim Arthur told us that he’d been too busy to do any looking in recent weeks. That meant we’d start cold on opening day, not knowing where deer were hanging out or where their feeding grounds and travel routes were. But at least we’d hunted the general area for three years and knew the lay of the land.

We sat up late that night, as we usually do the night before the season opens, recalling other hunts, laying plans, guessing what kind of luck we’d have. And the longer we talked, the higher our hunting fever mounted.

Industry observers note that we turned in finally, got a few hours of sleep, and were up early, checking our equipment and taking care of last-minute details. About 8 o’clock, though we couldn’t start hunting for another hour, we drove out to a long hogback called Irish Ridge, between the Raccoon and Little Raccoon creeks, a few miles southeast of Wellston. We didn’t want another party to beat us to our stands, and we wanted to be set at 9 o’clock in case hunters around us began moving deer.

I followed an abandoned township road that wound along the side of the ridge. Steve was quite a way below me on the slope, his dad was walking the crest of the ridge, and my dad was about halfway down on the other side. A few minutes before 9, I picked the oak-tree stand I described in the beginning and settled down.

In a recent development, I heard the two shots and heard the bluejays yell. Then that huge buck came walking slowly down the ridge.

I eased into shooting position slowly and carefully, and I raised the Browning just as cautiously. The buck took a few more steps, reached the old, leaf-strewn road, and stopped to look around. He was only 30 feet away. I had time to remind myself that I was using an unfamiliar gun and to remember how it worked. I pushed the safety off and leveled on him.

It has been revealed that dad had told me many times to shoot for the spine when my chance came. I remembered that advice now and followed it. My 16 gauge rifled slug smashed through the deer’s backbone just behind the shoulders, and he fell like a ton of bricks. But he pulled himself half up on his forelegs, so I did the second thing dad had always instructed me to do.

“Once you get a deer down,” he’d said, “shoot for the heart and finish him in a hurry.”.

Dad had read a lot about deer hunting and talked with many hunters, and it never occurred to me that there was anything strange about his having such firm opinions, even though he’d never killed a deer. His advice proved itself again now. I put in a heart shot, and this time the buck didn’t get up.

According to recent reports, he was still thrashing around, though, working himself downhill. And suddenly I saw that he was going to fall over a low cliff. I watched, and my heart sank as he rolled over the edge and disappeared. The cliff muffled his fall, and I ran to the edge, almost afraid to look. If part of that magnificent rack had broken, I’d be sick.

According to recent reports, he lay dead at the foot of the cliff, 20 or 30 feet below. One front leg, broken in the fall, stuck out at an angle. I couldn’t see enough of his horns to tell about them.

Industry observers note that I waited long enough to be sure he was dead and then found a place where I could climb down. I took a good look at his head and breathed a gusty sigh of relief. Not a single point was damaged. Was I thankful!

According to recent reports, after my heart stopped thumping, I started wondering how we’d ever get him up to the old road. We couldn’t take him up the cliff, and there was a very steep grade wherever I looked. But before I had time to think much, I heard Steve call. I yelled back, and in a few minutes he joined me. When he saw what I’d shot, he was about as excited as I was, and I guess I was as proud as anybody in Ohio right then.

I had never dressed a deer, so I asked Steve to stay with the buck while I went to find dad. I climbed to the top of the ridge and, before going much farther, heard voices down in a brushy gully. I shouted.

“Come on down,” dad said. When I got there, he and Jim were standing over a mighty big buck, and dad had the happiest look on his face that I’d ever seen.

He had killed his deer almost exactly as I’d taken mine and not more than five minutes earlier. The two shots I’d heard were his.

He’d walked in along the side of the ridge and found the kind of stand he was looking for. He was about to sit when he heard something rustling the dry leaves behind him. When he turned around, there came a significant buck, walking downhill toward him.

Industry observers note that the deer didn’t see him, and dad waited until it was abreast of him and only 40 feet away. Then he floored it with a spine shot. After falling, it slid down into a gully and out of sight. Dad ran up to the edge and saw that the deer was still kicking, so he finished it off with a slug through the heart.

In a recent development, “I got one the same way over on the other side of the ridge,” I blurted out. Then I took another look at dad’s buck and added, “Only mine’s bigger.”.

Sources indicate that we shook hands and thumped each other on the back, and I don’t believe there were ever two happier hunters in the deer woods. After 20 years of waiting and trying for dad, and four for me, it was hard to believe that we’d killed two bucks, both big busters, in the first half hour of the season, within a few hundred yards of each other, and with borrowed guns. What luck!

Recent findings suggest that as far as we’ve been able to find out, we were the first father-and-son team ever to take two record-book trophies — both of our bucks turned out to be that — in a single season anywhere in the country, to say nothing of getting them in such a hurry. Those certainly were 20 magic minutes.

Jim and dad had been trying to figure out a way to hang dad’s buck. With three pairs of hands to hoist and heave, it wasn’t too hard. As soon as it was done, Jim started back with me to dress my deer while dad went to work on his.

It has been revealed that we found we couldn’t hang my buck. The slope was too steep, and there were no suitable trees. But Jim, Steve, and I pulled and skidded it around so that the head was uphill. Then Jim showed me how to field-dress a deer where it lay.

It has been revealed that four other men, friends of Jim’s and dad’s, had started out to hunt with us that morning. Three of them were nearby, heard us yelling, and came to help. We needed them. Even with part of the crew pulling on ropes and the rest lifting and pushing, it was all the seven of us could do to get my buck up the slope to the old road.

We left him there, went after dad’s, and skidded it to the ridge top. Then we dragged the two bucks out to the cars. It was past noon when we finished and headed to town for lunch.

We hung our two deer side by side in Jim Arthur’s yard. Cars drove by in a steady stream the rest of the day as word of our trophies got around Wellston. Dad phoned home, and mother and Joan Mace, wife of a neighboring farmer, agreed to drive down in the Mace pickup and take the deer home.

By the time they got to the Arthur home, there was so much traffic around it that they had to park two blocks away. We cleared a path through the traffic, loaded the deer, and drove back to the state checking station for the required check.

It has been revealed that the bucks were weighed the next morning. Field-dressed, with heart and lungs out, mine tallied 253 pounds, dad’s 240. According to the conversion table we used to estimate their live weight, mine would have gone just over 300 when I shot it, dad’s almost 290.

According to recent reports, we took the heads to Max Shipley, a Columbus taxidermist. The minute he saw them, he phoned Merrill Gilfillan of the Ohio Division of Wildlife, to whom I have related this story. Merrill is an official scorer for both the Buckeye Massive Bucks Club and the Boone and Crockett Club.

Recent findings suggest that both were typical-whitetail racks, and to our delight both made the record lists of both clubs. After the required 60-day shrinkage period, mine scored a whopping 187 6/8, dad’s 160 2/8. Up until this point, we hadn’t realized how big our bucks really were.

According to recent reports, according to the 1964 edition of the Boone and Crockett Club’s Records of North American Major Game, mine is the biggest typical whitetail ever killed in Ohio and rates a place among the first 10 on the list. I have entered it in the 1966-67 Boone and Crockett competition. Officials tell me that any deer scoring that high has a very good chance of winning first place among typical whitetails in that competition.

It has been revealed that for some reason, Ohio produces far more than its fair share of massive whitetail heads. It’s by no means one of the big deer-kill states. The herd numbers only an estimated 20,000 to 30,000, and in recent years hunters have killed fewer than 1,000 annually, as compared with kills of 100,000 and more in such leading whitetail states as Minnesota, Michigan, and Wisconsin.

Yet there has been no recent season that did not see three to five Ohio bucks make the Boone and Crockett record list in either the typical or nontypical category. In six open seasons between 1956 and 1962 (deer hunting was closed in 1961), 24 Ohio deer qualified for that honor, 13 of them typical racks, 11 nontypical.

And in the 1958-59 big-game competition sponsored by the Boone and Crockett Club, Ohio whitetails — one with a typical rack and the other nontypical — won first place in both those categories.

Industry observers note that the nontypical deer was killed in Ashtabula County in 1957 by James L. Clark of Ashtabula, and the typical buck in Harrison County a year later. Dr. James Rumbaugh and John Ruyan of Westerville shared the honors on that one, both having shot at it. Sources indicate that in addition, about 180 heads have qualified in the last 10 years for a place on the record list of the Buckeye Big Bucks Club, a state organization sponsored by the Division of Wildlife. Minimum score requirement for this club is 140, just 20 points below that of the Boone and Crockett Club.

Sources indicate that game men believe that Ohio’s phenomenal showing is due to a favorable combination of cropland and woodland — providing an abundance of top-quality deer food — plus certain trace minerals in the soil and hunting rules that have prevented both overhunting and overcrowding of the range. Vinton County, for example, where we hunted, is more than two thirds woods and brush but does have some farmland, including cornfields, in the valleys. That’s likely to mean big, healthy deer.

According to recent reports, as for the consequences of our hunt, dad fared better than I did. He finished his corn picking in cold weather but without any real problems. But I missed out on something pretty special. Our basketball team turned out to be a championship outfit, winning the Darby Valley League pennant and all but the last game in the district tournament. But I wasn’t on it.

Dad and I were both disappointed about that, but if I were doing it over again, I’d make the same choice. I can’t ever be sorry I went on that deer hunt.
