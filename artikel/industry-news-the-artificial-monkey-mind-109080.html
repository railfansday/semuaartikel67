Industry observers note that I’ve been practising meditation for decades, but I’m barely a beginner. What I find most challenging is what the Buddhists call the monkey mind. Instead of being aware of the moment, my brain is elsewhere. It’s dwelling on the past or worrying about the future, rather than paying attention to what’s happening right now.

It is not possible to stop this kind of thinking. That’s just what brains do. The goal is to let my thoughts go and not get caught up in them. I practise regularly to learn how to remain detached from that chaotic stream, to just observe it and not follow or react. I want to be able to make conscious choices about where I spend my attention.

Maybe someday I’ll manage whole seconds of this ideal state.

Sources indicate that it occurred to me recently that we are now dealing with a kind of artificial monkey mind in large language models (LLMs) like ChatGPT or Claude. They mimic our own thinking so well that we anthropomorphise them. They appeal to our monkey minds in a way no previous technology has. I’m talking daily with people who seem to be verging on an unhealthy connection to these things, using them like a therapist or a GP, for example. They’re getting caught up in the chatbot’s responses, just as we get caught up in our thoughts.

It has been revealed that but despite the technical twist, this isn’t a fresh problem. People have been trying to tame the mental chatter and instinct-driven behaviour for thousands of years. From ancient Hindu and Buddhist traditions, through early Christian and Islamic contemplation, to modern mindfulness, humans have been struggling to rise above the clattering cacophony since at least the beginning of civilisation.

LLMs are a uniquely powerful distraction, but I wonder if these practices could teach us to interact with them in a healthier way.

The part of the mind that “thinks” is the “doing” part of our brain. It’s a what-if engine or analyser, taking often fragmentary and incomplete evidence from our senses and building a theory of reality in real time. By necessity, the mind fills in the gaps in its data. It confabulates. It’s not hallucinating, exactly – we’re not hearing voices – but we are unknowingly making up facts to fit our current picture of the world.

The brain also has basic assumptions, many of which are wrong. My impostor syndrome, which I’ve written about before, is one example of that kind of distortion. There’s also depression or other forms of mental illness.

Industry observers note that but the tendency to misread reality is more basic than that and affects everyone. Our instincts come into play. For example, getting along with others in the tribe, troop or herd has kept us alive for millions of years, so it’s a powerful urge.

With our eagerness to find patterns and our subconscious instincts, it’s no wonder that we have a hard time staying in the moment.

With chatbots, it’s even worse. We dealing with two monkey minds that play off each other. We assume we understand what the LLM – that statistically generated, stochastic parrot – is on about (pattern matching), agree with it and just become its yes man. Our instinct to gel, to go with the flow, is also triggered. An LLM doesn’t just sound like another person. It sounds like one who’s eager to agree with us. Flattery goes a long way!

In a recent development, the counterpart to the thinking mind’s “doing” mode is our “being” mode. This is our awareness, our consciousness, or what some call the soul. It’s where we experience reality. Meditation in all its forms aims to help us get to a state where we are fully aware of the present moment (the only moment we have, really) and where we are fully free to experience it, just as it is, with as few distortions as possible.

Detaching from our monkey mind, with its bad assumptions and distortions, is part of developing wisdom. At its most basic, wisdom is a sceptical attitude toward your own thinking. The wise one has learnt that they are often wrong, that first impressions are often misleading, that the best-laid intends go awry. The wise aren’t attached to their own attitudes or theories. They readily adjust when facts don’t fit any longer.

Recent findings suggest that the daily practice of meditation is like exercising the mental muscles we need to walk this path. We gently bring our attention back, without judging ourselves for drifting. In mindfulness, for example, we connect with reality in a simple, physical way: focusing our attention on the sensation of breathing. The key skill is to notice when we’ve been carried away from the present moment. It’s this, in particular, that I think will help us most with LLMs.

So, when our minds or an LLM deliver a verdict with confidence, we should say “That’s nice” or “Good boy” and treat it as another hypothesis to test. Instead, we fail to notice that we’re being carried away by this stream. We stop paying attention.

Sources indicate that of course, as my decades of flailing with meditation have taught me, it’s a lot easier said than done. As a general rule, we’re pretty helpless in the face of the egotistical monkey mind. It’s our mind, after all, and we’re pretty attached to it. We give it the benefit of the doubt, completely unconsciously. And LLMs do a pretty good job now, and are getting better, at ingratiating themselves with us.

Industry observers note that when using an LLM, it’s really tricky to remain engaged – to bring ourselves, fully and attentively, to what we’re doing. It’s very easy to get lulled into the flow with an LLM – just like it’s easy to daydream.

I mostly use LLMs for programming, so what works for me might not work for people who use them for other kinds of work. The aim is to stay present and grounded. One trick I use is to make my LLMs speak “caveman” – simple, no unnecessary words, no fluff. Just the facts. I’m less likely to treat them like people or to get stupefied by a wall of words.

Another method is to use the LLM to proposal, but do the work myself. I took a similar approach with Stack Overflow. I’d search for an answer, but I wouldn’t paste it into my code. I’d type it out, line by line. Just doing that engages your attention surprisingly well. I find myself making small changes, more fitting to my style, taste and the actual specifics of the problem at hand, even if I’m just copying someone else’s work or following another’s plan.

Finally, and most importantly, I limit my use of these things. Do I need to use an LLM for this? If I’m so lost in the code that I can’t work out where to make a small change, something’s wrong. I’m no longer in charge.

In a recent development, when I first started using LLMs for coding at work – mostly for auto-completion – they were quite clunky and stupid. But modern models are getting good. They almost feel miraculous. On code I’m especially familiar with, I can use LLMs to offload a lot of drudgery. When I know the architecture and the trade-offs that have been made, I can set the guardrails correctly and ask for specific things to be done – and the work gets done, like magic, much faster than I could do it by hand.

It has been revealed that but over time, if I continuously use LLMs to do everything, the code becomes less and less familiar. I start losing touch.

Now that I’m retiring, I’m wondering if it makes sense to use LLMs at all. At work, when I need to upgrade the runtimes of a dozen AWS Lambda functions by a particular deadline, I can justify using LLMs to churn through those kinds of tedious tasks much faster and more efficiently. But at home, I don’t have those kinds of time pressures. I’m doing the work because I’m having fun learning something fresh. I learn best by doing, by trial and error.

What am I using LLMs for, exactly?
