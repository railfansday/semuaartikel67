'Alien' Meets 'Vikings' in a 2008 Sci-Fi Spin on Beowulf That Deserves Redemption

Beowulf is one of the oldest surviving pieces of English literature, and its story of a hero defending the Danes from a monster has inspired numerous adaptations. John McTiernan told the story in his 1999 film The 13th Warrior, and Robert Zemeckis used motion capture for a 2007 version. But my favorite telling of the story eschews history and fantasy to turn the tale of Beowulf and Grendel into an Alien-inspired sci-fi action flick.

It has been revealed that howard McCain’s 2008 film Outlander has no relationship to the megahit time-travel romance series. It was an enormous flop, earning only $7 million on an estimated budget of $47 million, and was panned by critics for being a derivative genre mashup with wooden performances. Those complaints are certainly fair, but Outlander also brings a surprising amount of depth and pathos to its absurd premise.

Industry observers note that the film doesn’t waste too much time with exposition. In the year 709 AD, Kainan (Jim Caviezel) crashes on Earth, a seed colony abandoned by his militaristic spacefaring people. That explains why he looks human and can quickly learn the local Norse dialect. The accident was caused by a stowaway, a monstrous alien known as a Moorwen. Now it’s on the loose, slaughtering Vikings, and causing chaos in an already fraught political situation. Kainan has to explain the threat in terms the locals can understand, so he claims to be hunting a dragon.

According to recent reports, outlander borrows heavily from both the Alien franchise and The Lord of the Rings: The Two Towers. The legendary actor John Hurt brings some genuine gravitas to the aging king Rothgar of Heorot, who is trying to secure his people’s future by marrying his daughter Freya (Sophia Myles) to Wulfric (Jack Huston), a hotheaded warrior very clearly modeled after Karl Urban’s Éomer. Freya is a reasonably compelling blend of Eowyn and Ripley, getting revenge on the toothy monster that drools all over her by hacking it to death with a sword.

The Moorwen first strikes a neighboring village with a longstanding rivalry with Heorot, and Rothgar is more afraid of reprisals than the actual monster. The three-way conflict leads to a series of tense, chaotic fight scenes. A party involving a drinking game where Kainan and Wulfric balance on shields held by other warriors while chugging mead and doing sick flips is interrupted by raiders. After a few skirmishes, Ron Perlman’s King Gunnar bursts onto the scene like a video game boss swinging around two maces and nearly killing Rothgar. After Gunnar is pushed back, the Moorwen attacks.

Industry observers note that the CGI isn’t great, but McCain makes the most of the limitations by relying on smoke, shadows, and some clever creature design. The Moorwen looks a lot like a glowing displacer beast from Dungeons & Dragons, using its whiplike tail to grapple and decapitate Vikings in ludicrous gouts of blood. Bioluminescence ominously foreshadows the Moorwen's approach and makes it look decidedly alien rather than like a fantastical beast.

According to recent reports, the Moorwen also has a surprisingly sympathetic backstory. It’s not just a mindless killing machine but a survivor of a genocide Kainan helped carry out to create a new colony for his people. The scenes of its planet being firebombed and bulldozers sweeping piles of bones into mass graves speak to the same idea of humans as evil alien invaders that James Cameron would use in Avatar just a year later, albeit with much better special effects.

According to recent reports, outlander keeps its fights fresh with a strong mix of environments and tactics. The game of shields is introduced as a fun diversion but then plays a critical role in a trap laid for the Moorwen. The beast’s lair is an appropriately spooky volcanic cave, which both makes sense geographically and really adds to the alien dragon vibe. Dan Trachtenberg made Vikings fight a Predator as part of his 2025 film Predator: Killer of Killers, but Outlander did it first — and better — since its Vikings always feel at an enormous disadvantage, even with Kainan’s knowledge to help them.

Beowulf is a tragedy, but while Outlander is full of death, Kainan gets a happy ending in the form of a fresh start. Likewise, the film deserves a shot at redemption. It’s far from perfect, but it’s a highly entertaining and ambitious retelling of a classic offering fantasy and sci-fi fans something to love.
