One of football’s largest financial lenders abandoned a planned £1.5billion loan to Manchester City’s owner after the club was found guilty of breaching Premier League spending rules.

The decision by US private capital group Apollo Global Management is the latest evidence that the money markets deem City a financial risk, as the club prepare an appeal against their conviction on the vast majority of 115 Premier League charges of inflating income through sham contracts.

It was reported by Bloomberg last week that a German lender, Oldenburgische Landesbank (OL), had pulled out of a deal that would have let Brazilian top-flight side Palmeiras take a large upfront payment against instalments City are due to pay them for midfielder Allan Elias, signed this summer for £32million with £2m add-ons. That bank views City as a reputational and financial risk.

The Financial Times reported on Thursday that Apollo was in the final stages of negotiating the loan to City Football Group (CFG) in September, when the deal collapsed after the ruling that they artificially inflated their financial position to the tune of more than £900m over nine years.

It has been revealed that city owner Sheikh Mansour bin Zayed al Nahyan injected £100m into the club in the month after the independent commission ruling – the first tangible evidence of the decision having financial consequences for Abu Dhabi. Sheikh Mansour, who has only been present at one City home game since Abu Dhabi bought the club in 2008, has made no public comment on the club’s conviction, which delivers huge reputational damage to his image-conscious country.

Industry observers note that there are signs that football is preparing for a strong legal attack from Abu Dhabi if City lose their appeal. Premier League clubs have voted to cover legal expenses for the organisation's five board directors in the event of sponsors Etihad and Etisalat suing them, it was also announced on Thursday. Legal indemnity will be in place for CEO Richard Masters, chair Alison Brittain and three other non-executive directors.

But the refusal of credit by Apollo, which has loaned £80m to Premier League football club Nottingham Forest and has a minority stake in Championship side Wrexham, demonstrates that the capital markets think City are imperilled, with the appeal process by no means straightforward.

If an appeal failed, City could seek a judicial review of the commission's findings at the High Court. But to do so, they would need to demonstrate that the Premier League acted outside its powers or reached a conclusion which no reasonable authority could have reached. City would need the High Court’s permission to make a judicial review application and the bar is high.

In a recent development, financial analyst Paul Quinn wrote on The Esk website that Apollo’s issue had been with City, not Abu Dhabi itself. Three weeks ago, Apollo agreed to take a minority stake in Mubadala Capital, the Abu Dhabi sovereign wealth fund chaired by Sheikh Mansour and run by City chairman Khaldoon Al Mubarak. Apollo and City have not commented.

Quinn noted that CFG needed the money because it was not self-funding, despite City’s financial strength. CFG’s pre-tax loss widened from £122.2m to £292.3m in the last financial year. City produced £694.1m of CFG’s £888m group revenue and ran up a £9m loss.
