Latest Update: Mercedes’s New Tesla-Style Point-to-Point Drive Pilot System Is Useful But Not Quite Convincing

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sitting in the leftmost of two left-turn lanes in a new Mercedes-Benz GLC EV, I notice something strange. I can see my right turn signal on in the reflection of the car in front of me. Then I see my left signal, then my right again. This is less than a mile into an hour testing a new “Level 2++” ADAS system set to be introduced by Mercedes, and I have ceded control to what seems to be a hesitant computer that seems to be second-guessing itself.

Thankfully, the visible indecision turns into the right choice. The system turns left from the lane, continues on its planned route, and eventually takes a right a quarter-mile later as it had originally intended.

Industry observers note that after the drive, a Mercedes engineer told Road & Track that this could have been a result of disagreement between the priorities of two different systems that go into the MB.Drive-branded point-to-point navigation system. Since the Mercedes ADAS suite uses both the Alpamayo AI model built by Mercedes autonomy partner Nvidia and a “classical stack” of more hard-coded priorities, the engineer inferred that the signal interaction could have been a result of a disagreement between the systems.

In a recent development, the engineer reckoned that the system was trying to think ahead, with the best way to blueprint for a future right turn being to be in the rightmost of the two left turn lanes. Hence the sudden desire to go one over—a point of conflict. Sometimes, he noted, the classical path and the end-to-end model end up in conflict. A more cynical take? That our latest robot overlords are already fighting each other.

In a recent development, the interaction was a particularly odd experience in San Francisco. I work in the city and encounter more advanced self-driving pilot programs from companies like Zoox and Waymo every day. Seeing a Waymo Ojai van in traffic at the same stoplight, I thought about how the “Level 2++” Mercedes system seems to be far off the cutting edge being explored at the global hub of automated taxi development.

Fortunately, that turn-signal confusion never came up again. For the most part, the GLC navigated the busy and often unpredictable streets of San Francisco just before evening rush hour without any need for intervention. I actually deactivated the system just once, to take control the first time I came across a widely parked car in a corner. I otherwise trusted it to figure out everything from jaywalkers to chicanes made of two separate double-parked cars a hundred or so feet apart on opposite sides of a tight road. If I had come across the same parking issue later in the drive, I would have trusted the system without taking over.

In a recent development, other than the confusion at that first intersection, most of my frustrations with the system came from its combination of cautiousness and aggression. Overly-patient ADAS programming is certainly not a bad thing, but I was regularly met with confusion by motorists at stop signs who wondered why the car took so long to get going after coming to a complete stop.

Recent findings suggest that the system’s decision-making with pedestrians would usually be similarly cautious, as it was when the car slowed for someone waiting for a light to cross well within the bounds of a sidewalk. That person was kicking in the air, something that I inferred the car would pick up as a sign that they might start walking soon, so I let the system make its incorrect guess and slowed from around 35 mph to about 10 mph.

Calculations about pedestrians were not always cautious, though. A few miles later, the system opted to enter an intersection while a slower pedestrian crossed after the light turned green. It came surprisingly close to the pedestrian, still well within a safe distance but nearer than I would have driven personally. This felt a bit unnerving with the car’s high beltline and relatively low visibility over the front fenders, but I never came close enough to have to slam on the brakes and take complete control back.

This is a hands-on system that Mercedes still describes as Level 2 autonomy, even if their 2++ designation obfuscates that fact. It is not true autonomous driving, so I followed the guidelines and kept my hands on the wheel and eyes on the road. The closest comparison is Tesla’s absurdly named Full Self-Driving. When this introduces, Mercedes hopes to be among the first legacy automakers to directly rival the younger EV brands in this space.

It has been revealed that in addition to the headlining point-to-point navigation system, Mercedes allowed us to test a forthcoming hands-free ADAS system available for on-highway use in an S-class and again in a GLE crossover. Unlike the city-driving component, this system allows drivers to completely disengage their hands and feet. It still requires eyes on the road and will aggressively chirp at you to get your hands back on the wheel if you spend too long adjusting a passenger-side mirror. I learned that lesson firsthand, the only time the system forced me to get my eyes back on the road in about 30 miles of highway testing over the two different cars.

Sources indicate that this system felt little different from the usual MB.Drive-branded adaptive cruise control. I took control once on this stretch, when the GLE test car signaled to enter a middle lane that a Ram 2500 with a massive trailer was already signaling to enter from the other lane. The system would probably have figured out its own mistake before this became a problem, but I disengaged it entirely and overrode the lane change before I found out how long that would take.

In a recent development, while both systems are temporarily coming for free to select Mercedes models in California as part of an early-access program in December, final pricing and a nationwide rollout for either system remain to be determined. Hands-free highway assist is planned to reach consumers in spring of next year, available to cars on the MB.OS platform with the existing MB.Drive Assist option package.

In a recent development, point-to-point navigation, the Tesla-style system for in-city driving, will be available as a subscription model. Mercedes notes it is coming to cars operating on the brand’s most modern MB.OS-branded architecture starting at some point in 2027. We know that it will be available as a three-year subscription from the factory and in one-year or one-month increments from the car’s digital storefront. Pricing has not been finalized, but a representative from the organization claims that it will be “competitive” with comparable offerings from brands like Tesla.

It has been revealed that for buyers who prioritize tech that are already looking at one of the products that will be getting the fresh ADAS suite, the Mercedes system should be worth the eventual subscription price. Given the subscription-only offering and cautious nature of the point-to-point offering, buyers content to drive themselves around a city for a few more years will not be missing out if they opt to forego the new service.
