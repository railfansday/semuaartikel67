Microsoft Says Of The Next Xbox: ‘Helix Is A Console Capable Of Playing PC Games’

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Microsoft has put out an online magazine to celebrate 25 years of Xbox, and unquestionably to attempt even more damage control after a gruesome year of studio closures and mass layoffs. Within all the huff and puff lies a surprise: seven pages’ coverage of Project Helix, the codename for the next Xbox console. Within this is revealed the initiative for Helix to be able to play games and even progress from every previous Xbox generation, as well as removing “the arbitrary constraints between console and PC games.” So, asks the magazine and indeed us, “is Project Helix a console or a PC?” The answer is very definitively: a console. Sort of.

The current generation of consoles were notably described ahead of launch as being far more like PCs in a sealed box than the traditional console designs of yore, and this appears to be something Microsoft is looking to get away from this time out. The emphasis here is on the so-called instantaneous nature of a console. Jason Ronald is Xbox’s VP of “next generation,” and is quoted saying, “When I think about what makes a console magical, it’s that it just makes my games instantly accessible and ready to play in an instant. I don’t have to worry about updating my operating system, updating drivers, configuring different settings.” Which is…a bold claim, given anyone who owns an Xbox will know about having to install the latest updates, waiting for game patches to excruciatingly slowly download without taking advantage of your 1TB ethernet connection, or that unbearable pause each time you load it up for it to fill in the dashboard.

Industry observers note that but of Project Helix, Ronald notes, “it’s really designed to be the ultimate gaming machineâone that removes all the barriers of play that have historically existed. My entire library is accessible to me. I’m able to bring four generations of my Xbox library and progression with me and have it available instantly,” adding, “We’re very clear on the design goal: Helix is designed to be a console that is also capable of playing games from your PC. It is not a PC that can also play console games.”.

However, it’s hard to know just how clear this distinction is from Ronald’s very next words. “But if you’re a hardcore gamer or a power user who wants to have that deeper level of customizability across settings and performance options, you can absolutely go do it.” So it is a PC then? So confused.

Other comments from Ronald include that “We don’t think the next generation is defined by 8K or 240 hertz, or some of the traditional metricsâit’s really about gameplay innovation.” He adds, “It is about leveraging new technical advancements such as neural rendering, to deliver a new level of visual fidelity in more efficient and innovative ways.” Which all rather seems to be laying the ground for a machine that’s not focusing on the very latest and most expensive chipsets, but rather one that’ll take advantage of the more recent progress in upscaling and the like, perhaps to prevent the machine costing an absolute fortune?

Industry observers note that this topic isn’t ignored. Describing it as a factor “the team couldn’t have foreseen when Project Helix began,” the component crisis gets a mention. “Meeting this challenge has become a primary driver of Helix’s innovation,” claims the article. Ronald is quoted once more. “It’s definitely a challenging time, but as a system and a platform designer, it’s also one of the most exciting times because we have to invent entirely new technology to continue to advance the state-of-the-art while also continuing to make things accessible and affordable.” This, he says, is why there’s the focus on neural rendering, adding, “I think you’re going to see more innovation over the next three to five years than you’ve seen in the last 10 years.”.

It’s all a lot of words to avoid pointing out that Microsoft itself is one of the primary causes of the crisis in the first place, one arm taking all the available components from the other in order to power its AI hubris.

Beyond this, the piece delivers some very confusing messaging regarding Xbox’s current desperate rebranding. After a disastrous attempt to call everything from your console to your toaster “an Xbox,” the recent emphasis has been to return to the core idea of the Xbox itself, yet that message gets very lost in Microsoft’s own marketing. We’re told at once that this is all about “the Return of Xboxâthe return of a console,” and then that “Project Helix aims to take a wrecking ball to the dividers currently keeping players and their games apart,” quoting Ronald saying Helix is about “enabling [players] to experience those games wherever they choose to play. It’s about removing barriers to play, removing friction, and those arbitrary boundaries that have existed in the past.” So which is it? There are claims the new UI will adapt to whether the Xbox is being played on the living room TV, or streamed to a mobile device or a PC with mouse and keyboard. This really does sound like a confusion of previous messaging crashing into the current reverse-ferret.

According to recent reports, there’s no word one when we can expect to see Project Helix in more detail, and certainly nothing on an intended release date. It seems that Microsoft and Sony are currently playing chicken on revealing whether they’re aiming for 2027 or 2028, or perhaps even later given the tumult in the hardware industry. But at least we know it’s a console, not a PC, but also like a PC, and can be streamed to your mobile, or played on PC, and has all of the annoying complexity of PCs taken away, but also still there.
