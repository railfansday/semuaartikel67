Defending in the Premier League has changed – and it’s causing chaos

As Ronald Araujo prepared to play a long ball forward and Leny Yoro stood close to the halfway line, alarm bells were ringing in the heads of several former Premier League defenders.

Everything about Yoro’s positioning and response to Araujo sending the pass over the top went against what those former players would recognise as the principles of playing next to a centre-back partner, the rules of playing in a back four that have been passed down for generations.

According to recent reports, yoro did not begin to drop when it became clear that Araujo was going long. He did not anticipate the threat of danger by providing cover for Matthijs De Ligt, as he and Alexander Isak challenged for the ball. Instead, Yoro watched on, as if it was not his responsibility.

By the time he had reacted to De Ligt’s mistake, it was already far too late; in chasing Isak down and crashing into the striker outside of the box, he left Manchester United down to 10 men for the majority of their 4-0 defeat to Liverpool at Anfield in Mauricio Pochettino’s first match in charge (the Frenchman’s one-match ban was subsequently overturned after United won their appeal for wrongful dismissal).

Yoro, though, had only followed the instructions Pochettino and his coaching staff had issued him. The 21-year-old wasn’t even playing as a centre-back partner for De Ligt, as such; with United instead looking to defend Liverpool man-for-man, it was, in a sense, every man for himself. Yoro appeared more concerned with Florian Wirtz, the Liverpool creator who was positioned 15 yards away, than covering Isak with De Ligt. He was, effectively, tasked with thinking as a defensive midfielder as well as a centre-back.

Sources indicate that to measure just how the responsibilities of the Premier League centre-back have changed, take how Gary Neville spent the closing stages of United’s defeat during his commentary for Sky Sports describing the lessons he learned in his formative years. As a youth, Neville was taught how to play in a back four by holding onto a long rope with his fellow defenders. The exercise was designed to build cohesion and form the idea that the pieces of a defensive line were inter-connected, so that when a full-back rushes out wide to close down a winger, the rest of the defence moves with them, or that when one centre-back jumps forward to attack a long ball, the other drops in behind.

Those commandments are likely to still be repeated across parks and astroturfs as youngsters learn the game today, but it sounds horribly out of date when compared to the very elite, where tactical shifts within the Premier League and top leagues can make modern football appear as a different sport – where bravery and risks are embraced and caution is cut out.

Recent findings suggest that fulham’s approach at Manchester City on Saturday was another example. Fulham had lost 17 consecutive matches against City in the Premier League and were facing the league’s best striker in Erling Haaland. Did Alvaro Arbeloa’s side look to be safe? To keep it tight and compact to get through the opening stages of a difficult away game? It wasn’t even a consideration.

It has been revealed that within two minutes, Calvin Bassey could be seen leaving his inexperienced (in Premier League terms) centre-back partner David Affengruber one-on-one with Haaland on the halfway line, as he pushed forward to mark Phil Foden. For Arbeloa’s gameplan to work and Fulham to press City, Bassey had to trust that Affengruber, in his sixth Premier League appearance, could be left to deal with Haaland by himself.

In a recent development, bassey and Affengruber were individually outstanding as Fulham held City to a 1-1 draw, but then they had to be. There is an argument that playing centre-back for a team that looks to press high and go man-to-man across the pitch is now the hardest position in the sport. It requires defenders to not only win their individual duels against skilful dribblers but cover huge areas of space against fast forwards, often as the last one back. They have to be strong physically, have excellent recovery speed and be dominant aerially, as well as being technically sound and capable of playing line-splitting passes, or else they would be seen as a liability in possession. They have to be seen as leaders, to be commanding, brave, and an effective communicator.

Sources indicate that and there is nowhere to hide if one of those pieces falls through. The decisions and tactical set-up that led to Yoro’s sending off at Anfield was brilliantly covered by Jamie Carragher and former Tottenham centre-back Toby Alderweireld on Sky’s Monday Night Football, but the struggles of Levi Colwill and Wesley Fofana following Chelsea’s 4-1 defeat at home to Leeds was dissected as well. “You feel like you’re watching kids play in a men’s game,” was Carragher’s assessment of Colwill and Fofana. Yet Fofana was a £75m-rated centre-back when he was signed from Leicester at the age of 21 four years ago; Colwill may have gone for a similar fee following his breakthrough season on loan at Roberto De Zerbi’s Brighton three years ago.

The value of finding a player who can meet the demands of the modern-day centre-back was underlined when Arsenal decided to spend up to £55m on 28-year-old Ezri Konsa, rather than play the first three months of the season without a suitable replacement for the injured William Saliba. Liverpool similarly went big on Jeremy Jacquet, spending up to £60m after he had barely played a full season at Rennes. The early impact of the 21-year-old, next to Virgil van Dijk, suggests Liverpool knew what they were getting; the combination of technical quality and athleticism, which has become even more valuable in a high-pressing team under Andoni Iraola.

Recent findings suggest that yet one of the reasons Liverpool landed Jacquet was because they missed out on signing Yoro two years before when he was an 18-year-old at Lille. United swooped to pay a reported £52.1m for a player who had entered the final 12 months on his contract, with Real Madrid and Paris Saint-Germain also pursuing a deal. They all saw what United did too: a young centre-back who had all the attributes needed to play as part of a high defensive line and win his duels in one-on-one situations.

Recent findings suggest that yoro can still be that player. He is, in many ways, an ideal centre-back for Pochettino, who learned at Anfield that De Ligt, post back injury, is not. Yoro will be available for United against Brentford on Saturday, attempting to rebuild his confidence on the high wire that modern-day centre-backs have to tread.
