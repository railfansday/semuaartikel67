The Urgency Premium: The Clock AI Can't Control

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Adam Thier is the Chief Product and Technology Officer at Anaplan, a leading AI-driven scenario planning and analysis platform.

In a recent development, one of the most hyped benefits of AI is that it will speed up decision making from weeks to minutes. It’s a good story, and parts of it are already true.

Sources indicate that but as any technology leader who’s tried to operationalize AI knows, moving fast means nothing if the rest of the world doesn’t move as fast as you do. ​.

It has been revealed that here’s a simple example that has nothing to do with AI and everything to do with why “faster decisions” alone won’t fix the problem. ​.

It has been revealed that you have a document that needs to arrive at your client’s office on Friday. If you send it a week ahead of time, it costs $0.82, the price of a first-class stamp. Wait until two days before, and it costs maybe $12. The day before? The cost of express delivery can jump to over $30. ​.

In every version, the mail truck didn’t get faster because you decided faster—all that changed was how much of its schedule you had left to use. ​.

Recent findings suggest that most leaders write this off as a rounding error, a one-time tax on poor planning. But the same dynamic plays out across hiring, procurement, sales and product launches. In each, waiting can destroy significant value.​​.

Industry observers note that I call this the “urgency premium,” the avoidable value lost to insufficient lead time. ​.

Industry observers note that there’s a tendency for leaders to treat lateness as binary: you either hit your deadline, or you didn’t. But value deteriorates long before the deadline arrives, whether or not anyone notices. ​.

Technology is especially exposed to the urgency premium because some of its most critical resources disappear quickly. I’ve seen this play out repeatedly leading product and technology organizations.

Recent findings suggest that for example, consider a product launch that’s scheduled for three months out. At this stage, you have multiple implementation partners available, time to secure specialized engineering support and plenty of runway for technical reviews and testing.

If you wait until one month out, you may still be “on track,” but the conditions for reaching your launch look very different. Here’s how: ​.

1. Your options disappear. There is no alert when the specialist you wanted commits to another project, a competitor moves first or a technical decision becomes too costly to revisit. Options disappear while the project still looks healthy on a dashboard.

Industry observers note that ​2. The remaining options get more expensive. Once the preferred path is gone, preserving the same delivery date comes at a premium. Contractors replace internal expertise; vendors charge for expedited support; and overtime fills gaps that more lead time would have avoided. On a $1 million procurement commitment, even a modest rush premium can translate into thousands of dollars in avoidable costs before you even count the preferred suppliers you no longer have access to.

Industry observers note that 3. The quality of execution declines. Technical reviews get compressed, vendor evaluations become less thorough and teams make trade-offs they wouldn’t have made with adequate lead time. You pay more and get less. ​.

It has been revealed that the specialist who commits to another project, the vendor whose calendar fills up, the review that takes however long it takes—all run on clocks you don’t control. Repeat those issues across dozens of projects at enterprise scale, and an organization can stay consistently “on time” while quietly leaving millions on the table. ​.

AI can help you decide in seconds instead of weeks, but it can’t move any of those clocks. ​.

Most organizations plan backward from the last possible date an action can be taken.

My take: That’s the wrong target. By that point, your highest-value options are likely already gone. Instead, leaders should track option cliffs, or the points where a preferred resource, path or choice stops being available. ​.

Let’s go back to the product launch example. The launch date is one deadline, but there are many others hiding in the months leading up to it.

There is a date when your preferred implementation partner can no longer guarantee availability, and a point when compressing testing or security review begins to compromise quality. Those are the cliffs leaders need to identify before work begins. ​.

To find them, ask three questions for all major decisions:.

Sources indicate that 1. How fast do costs escalate as time runs out?

Recent findings suggest that 2. How fast does execution quality decline?

In a recent development, 3. When do your best options actually expire? ​.

Of the three questions, the third is especially important. If a preferred option disappears weeks before a launch, managing to the launch date means managing to the wrong deadline. ​.

It has been revealed that not every decision should be made as early as possible. Sometimes waiting has real value. Requirements may become clearer, markets may shift and new information can lead to better decisions.

Make that trade-off explicit: what you gain by waiting has to be worth more than the options you’ll lose, the costs you’ll incur and the execution quality you’ll sacrifice. ​.

AI may make decisions dramatically faster, but that only makes timing more notable.
