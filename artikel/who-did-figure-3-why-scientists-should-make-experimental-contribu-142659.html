Who did figure 3? Why scientists should make experimental contributions visible

I ask this question surprisingly often when reading the literature. I might be interested in a particular imaging approach, trying to understand an experiment in detail when reviewing a manuscript or simply want to contact a study author who has experience with a technique. I look at the author list, then the contribution statement, and reach the same conclusion: I have no idea who did the work.

Recent findings suggest that after years of working in experimental cell biology, collaborating with other laboratories, reviewing papers and handling manuscripts as an editor, I have come to recognize that this is more than an inconvenience. By obscuring who does what in a research project, we are losing an important piece of information about how science is done — and the people who do it.

This has become particularly obvious to me as my research has become more collaborative. My projects can involve advanced microscopy, molecular biology, animal experiments and computational analysis, each performed by a different group. This is one of the things I enjoy most about modern science: collaborations allow us to answer questions that none of us could address alone. But they also make individual expertise increasingly difficult to see.

When I started in research 20 years ago, papers generally had fewer authors and the experimental work was performed in one or two laboratories. Today, papers routinely have 10, 20 or more authors, with projects spread across several institutes. A 2024 study1 in Scientometrics found that the mean number of authors on papers in the PubMed database grew by more than 50% between 2000 and 2020 (from 3.99 to 6.25). Author-contribution statements help to delineate individual roles in a project, but only to a point. If five people are listed under investigation, three under methodology and four under analysis, I still cannot tell who performed any one experiment.

Anyone who has spent time at the bench knows that experiments require more than simply following the methods section of a paper.

I have seen young researchers spend months, sometimes years, developing expertise in a technique. When the resulting study is published through a large collaboration, that researcher might become one name among many, credited with investigation or methodology. But those words do not tell the person reading the paper that this is the scientist who knows how this experiment works.

For early-career researchers, that’s a missed opportunity. Publications are not only records of scientific findings, they also demonstrate technical proficiency. Being able to say, “I am an author on this paper”, is valuable. Being identifiable as the person who generated a particular piece of evidence provides a different and, in some circumstances, more useful form of recognition.

And there’s another benefit. As a reviewer and editor, I sometimes have technical questions. The corresponding author is, appropriately, my point of contact. But they might not be the person who performed or directly supervised the experiment I care about and, over time, they might not remember who did. PhD students graduate, postdocs change institutions and collaborations evolve. Five years after publication, identifying the person who remembers exactly how an experiment was performed can be difficult.

We rightly devote enormous attention to reproducibility. We record catalogue numbers for antibodies, identify instruments and software versions and deposit raw data sets. Yet we often fail to record something much simpler: who generated the data. I am not suggesting this as a way of assigning blame when something goes wrong — authorship implies collective responsibility for a published study. I see it instead as a strategy to provide traceability of expertise.
