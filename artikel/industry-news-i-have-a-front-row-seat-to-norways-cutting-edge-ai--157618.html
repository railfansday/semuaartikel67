Industry News: I have a front-row seat to Norway’s cutting-edge AI newsroom experiment. Even with AI on more than 200 stories a day, a human still checks every one

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Every day, Norway’s largest newspaper, VG, asks an AI model to suggest a headline, search terms and other details for each of its roughly 200 fresh articles. Before a journalist sees anything, a second model checks the first. In October, across all the newsrooms that use the system, it rejected about one in six of the first model’s attempts. Then the journalist checks the suggestion, and so does an editor, depending on how the newsroom is set up. The cheapest checker goes first, and the people who answer for the story still go last.

“Keep a human in the loop” has become the standard answer to AI’s mistakes. I wanted to know what that human costs, so I asked three Norwegian news organizations what it takes, and whether that has gone up or down as the tools improved. What I learned is that the cost of a check depends heavily on who does it, and on how close that person is to the original work.

Sources indicate that I direct a research center on media technology in Norway, where newsrooms began publishing AI-written summaries in 2023. The answers come from the AI Foundations unit at Schibsted Media, the group that owns VG and the regional daily Bergens Tidende; from an editor at Bergens Tidende; and from an editor at Fjell-Ljom, a small local paper in the mountain town of Røros. I am not a neutral observer here: Schibsted Media and Bergens Tidende are partners in my center and help fund it, and I am preparing a grant application with Fjell-Ljom.

Nobody had a measured figure, and the numbers that exist elsewhere come from surveys, not stopwatches. But in one newsroom I could set the check against the work it replaces.

In a recent development, at Bergens Tidende, known as BT, reporters publish 15 to 20 stories of their own a day. AI is not, for now, permitted to write them, but it does write a short version of the finished story, which the reporter is responsible for checking before an editor checks it too. Jan Stian Vold, BT’s development editor, estimates that a reporter who knows the story needs anything from a few seconds to a couple of minutes, and that the editor’s look adds a minute or two. Writing the short version by hand, he told me, takes a reporter three to five minutes. These are his estimates, not measurements. Still, for the person who wrote the story, checking the machine’s version takes less time than writing one.

Vold’s guess is that about one short version in ten needs a fix, and he notes the summaries have kept improving with the models. The usual problem is clumsy grammar, or a summary that misses what the reporter considers the most vital point. “Almost never hallucinations,” he wrote to me. As far as BT knows, it has never published a critical mistake this way.

It has been revealed that office workers describe something different. In a Workday survey of 3,200 employees who use AI at large companies, nearly 40% of the time AI saved was lost to rework. In a survey of 1,150 U.S. employees by researchers at BetterUp and Stanford, each piece of workslop, AI-generated work that looks finished and is not, took nearly two hours to deal with. These are self-reports about longer documents, not a like-for-like comparison with a newsroom summary. But one difference stands out. In the office, the check often falls to someone who did not do the work and first has to find out what a right answer would look like. At BT, it falls to the one person who already knows.

Sources indicate that A Georgia Tech researcher argued this month that catching AI’s mistakes is a mental burden that eats the gains. In the one newsroom where I could estimate it, the check took less time than the writing it replaced, and the reason is who does the checking.

In a recent development, johanna Johansson, who works in AI Foundations at Schibsted Media, told me that “hallucinations are surprisingly common.” Vold had written “almost never.” Both can be right. She was speaking of AI-generated content in general, he of one narrow task, the summary of a text the reporter has just finished. Two things may be at work here, and I cannot separate them: a narrow task may produce fewer errors, and a checker who knows the material may spot the ones that remain faster.

Industry observers note that on one point they agree. “Generally we always have a human in the loop,” Johansson remarked. That was always a firm principle, she said, and the huge improvements in the models have not made it any less important.

A judgment call, I would add, is the kind of error that only someone who knows the story is likely to notice.

In a recent development, so has the checking gone up or down? For BT’s newsroom as a whole, Vold could not say. His own work is administration and presentations, not journalism, and there his checking has gone down as he has learned to use AI as an assistant and not as a source of facts. At Fjell-Ljom it has stayed constant, because it was never a separate step. What nobody described is letting go of the check on what gets published.

I think they are right to hold that line, and the reason goes beyond the errors that remain. The check at BT is cheap because of everything the reporter did before the summary appeared. Hand the same summary to someone who had not reported the story, and the check would either take far longer or turn into a formality. A freelance journalist interviewed for a 2025 master’s thesis at Aalto University made a similar point: typing out an interview word for word “really gets you inside it in a way that reading over a transcription and just checking for errors doesn’t.”.

For companies outside the news business, I take three things from this. Count the check: how many items, how many people look at each one, and how many minutes each look takes. Then set that against what the task took before. Do not treat a falling error rate as permission to stop checking. Nobody I asked has. Above all, give the check to the person who knows the work, instead of adding a reviewer downstream who has to start from nothing. In these newsrooms, that is what keeps the check manageable.

As AI takes over more of what comes before the check, I would worry less about the minutes than about whether anyone is left who can tell in seconds that something is wrong.
