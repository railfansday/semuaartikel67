Is Yeti’s Upcoming Performance Outdoor Clothing Any Good? I Put It to the Test to Find Out.

IN A SURPRISE move not many saw, Yeti recently launched a performance outdoor clothing line. It’s a deviation, sure, but perhaps a sensible one given the brand’s commitment to outdoor gear and all things insulation. As frequent testers of new products in this ever-expanding category, we could not resist the chance to throw them on and put them through the paces in the elements.

We put three signature garments from the latest five-piece line to test: The Tensleep Hooded Hybrid Jacket, the Tilloo Hooded Rain Jacket, and the Sansaba Fleece 260 Full-Zip. For context, the other two pieces are the Caprock Merino Quarter-Zip and the Heuco 310 Full-Zip, essentially siblings of the fleece we tried out. To the brand’s credit, no PFAs are used in the line and all items come with a three-year warranty.

Before even testing, there’s plenty to be intrigued by. The pieces show some clear restraint from a brand that tends toward boldness and large logos (you know, like the beloved coolers). You’d barely even know these were Yeti tops, as you really have to hunt for the brand stamp. And there’s a less-is-more approach in the design here, clearly made with active lifestyles and packability in mind (even color-wise, there are only two options max per item, a somewhat refreshing factoid).

I tried all three pieces over a couple of weeks in the dead of fall in the Pacific Northwest. Translation? They were subjected to downpours, foggy night jogs, chilly mornings, and windy afternoons. I wore them on long walks with the dog, bike commutes in the rain, adventures on muddy trails, and to evening soccer games. I even brought them to the coast during a storm that stirred up high surf and sideways precipitation. Here’s what I think after all the initial testing.

According to recent reports, the Tensleep Hooded Hybrid Jacket looks good, there’s no denying as much. The sleek, trim fit works for even the most active functions and we found it never overheated in testing. Lightweight and packable, it’s somewhat nimble thanks to two-way stretch fabric but not as much so as the other two items tested.

Recent findings suggest that the water resistance is also impressive. I walked the dog through a downpour in this jacket for about 30 minutes and came back with just a few moisture spots on my layer beneath. Not bad for a jacket that certainly doesn’t claim to be waterproof (though does sport a water-repellant finish). In drizzle and light rain, you’re just fine in this puffer jacket. In fact, the water would bead up with the same intensity even after many wears, meaning that exterior was done right.

Recent findings suggest that while I liked the felt-lined pockets, I think they could be even warmer. The blend of Expedry Down and traceable European Lavalan Wool insulation is perhaps the jacket’s strongest feature. Going with a hybrid blend affords an ability to tightrope walk that fine-line between breathability and protection from the elements—and the proof was in the performance.

At $400 though, this is not a cheap item by any stretch of the imagination.

This was my favorite piece of the trio, and arguably the most straightforward. From the superior YKK Aquaguard zipper to the extremely packable nature of the jacket, the Tilloo is a genuine winner. Like a significant boss in your favorite video game, so many jackets in the category have a weak spot hidden somewhere. It usually translates to water seeping in from a cuff or finding its way in through an average zipper. Not here—this jacket is watertight yet breathable.

It has been revealed that one of the coolest features is just how quiet this jacket is. As somebody born and raised in the Pacific Northwest, I’ve worn a fair number of waterproof shells. Many of them swish loudly as you move (not unlike Jim Carrey in Dumb and Dumber as he’s pretending to run while driving). This less crinkly option is quite quiet, a bonus you perhaps never knew you desired (but once you have it, it’s hard to go back).

According to recent reports, if you’re like me, you like to get outdoors to get away from all the noise. A loud jacket can disrupt an adventure, whether you’re birding in a marsh or scanning for wildlife on a hike. This jacket is not silent but it’s noticeably more hushed than most others, even during animated motions and heavy exercise. The Velcro cuffs create nice clasps over base layers (or just above a smartwatch during a run) while the liner itself is fairly soft to the touch, unlike the less appealing, membrane-like feel you get from so many options. The hood is adjustable and offers a nice panorama without hampering much of your view. And the way it pulls rain off is impressive, meaning you never have to shake off pooling areas like you might with a lesser jacket or umbrella.

Industry observers note that it has become my go-to for offseason excursions. While $350 is an undeniable expense, the jacket is worth the price.

Straight on, the Sansaba Fleece 260 is incredibly soft (like, you’re gonna want to nuzzle it). The proprietary PrimaLoft Bio Fleece is a sensory joy, with a lovely feel and a density level that seems to hit the Goldilocks spot. Best, it’s a composite of 100% post-consumer recycled materials. These shorter fibers are densely distributed, creating what Yeti classifies as a midweight garment. We’d argue that it performs on the higher end of the spectrum, at least in terms of insulation and warmth. No, it wouldn’t quite cut it on its own for significant stretches in below-freezing conditions, but it does just find in the kind of cold we’re normally subjected to.

The fleece also breathes quite well. I found that it paired nicely with a brisk mountain hike or even a sweaty workout in damp conditions. However, playing under anything more than a light drizzle requires a proper shell to fend off precipitation. We liked the length of the collar, stylish yet still major enough to hide under during chilly gusts of wind.

Billed as a fleece made for movement and strenuous activities, the Sansaba fared pretty well. I really put it to the sword one day, zipping it up for a four-mile run in light rain. Normally, I’d wear a long-sleeve moisture-wicking shirt beneath, but I wanted to see how the fleece would fare atop a regular cotton T-shirt. While it did well at fending off light moisture and pulling some of the sweat from my arms, I was pretty soaked in sweat at the end. A pretty easy fix with a better undershirt, sure, but it indicates that perhaps the fleece can’t quite do the most strenuous activities without an unwanted side effect (props for drying so quickly after the fact, however).

Additional cons? The interior of the pockets could use some work. The plain mesh is boring and doesn’t do much to warm the paws. But the fleece is so warm and welcoming throughout the top that you can just rest your hands elsewhere for a quick warm-up. The collar also hugs the neck nicely. This is bittersweet, as it really cradles the area and keeps it warm; however, if you like to duck your chin and mouth (and a little nose) beneath this part of a top during a wind gust, it’s harder to do as it’s fairly snug. For $180, this is a pretty good deal, but not necessarily superior to several other fleeces at a similar price point.

While you don’t need the whole set, you might want a few of these pieces to bolster your outdoor apparel game. I recommend them all, but with some caveats. The rain jacket is the best item for the price and is very much on par with others in the category.

The puffer is nice but I don’t find it a whole lot better than, say, this one from Wren that I’ve been wearing for a couple of years now (the Columbia Terwilliger Treail Heat Seal Jacket is another fine option). The neat, textured, and non-glossy (per so, so many) look is hard to beat, but it’s a significant chunk of change even give its merits.

Recent findings suggest that the fleece is also an attractive piece of outerwear with some high-performance capabilities, but perhaps not at the same level as some of its counterparts at its decidedly high price point. I tends toward options like the Patagonia Better Jacket and the Trailmade from REI, a bargain-and-a-half. But the Sansaba is an undoubtedly good-looking option with a lot going for it.

Yeti is clearly on to something here and has turned heads with a combination of clean fits, useful tech, and designs with mobility in mind (not to mention some environmentally conscious moves too). But this writer would suggest splurging on perhaps just one of the items depending on your adventuring angles and lifestyle.
