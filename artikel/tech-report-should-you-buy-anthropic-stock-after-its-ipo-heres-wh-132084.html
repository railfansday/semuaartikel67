Anthropic (ANT +1.08%) just set a record for the largest amount of capital raised in an IPO. The AI lab's offering raised $81 billion, topping the $75 billion raised by Space Exploration Technologies (SPCX +1.30%) earlier this year. Anthropic also took in about $26 billion in cash from a separate private placement with several big tech companies.

Like many IPO stocks, Anthropic enjoyed a first-day pop, sending the shares to $97.17 at the close. That could leave many investors with FOMO, as the AI lab looks to inject upcoming life into AI stocks that have been sluggish over the last few weeks.

But investing in mega-IPOs like Anthropic after their debut isn't always a great decision. Here's what history says about buying into substantial IPOs.

Recent findings suggest that IPO stocks don't have a great track record. Investors often end up losing money on their investments after three years. Indeed, 60% of all IPOs between 1975 and 2021 ended with prices below their first-day close after three years of trading publicly.

Sources indicate that the odds are much better for bigger companies. Companies with at least $1 billion in sales (adjusted for inflation) saw an average three-year buy-and-hold return of 32.4% from their first-day closing price, well above the 17.8% average for IPOs with less than $1 billion in trailing-12-month sales. That mentioned, the average mega-IPO still produces an average return below the rest of the market in its first three years of trading.

Sources indicate that recent history hasn't been too kind either. SpaceX and SK Hynix (SKHY -0.75%) had two of the largest IPOs in history this year. As mentioned, SpaceX raised $75 billion when it went public in June. While SK Hynix traded on the Korean stock exchange, its Nasdaq debut raised an additional $26.5 billion by making it easier for American investors to buy the stock. Today, both stocks trade below their first-day closing price.

As you can see, the track record isn't great for the biggest U.S. IPOs, which tend to generate significant media attention and investor buzz. As such, the stocks are priced rather than valued, and with so much excitement about the companies, that excitement can push prices well above fair value.

Sources indicate that while history isn't kind to the biggest IPOs, investors need to evaluate stocks on a case-by-case basis. Anthropic's valuation was previously floated at around $2 trillion before AI stocks began facing selling pressure amid financing concerns and potential regulatory hurdles following the midterm elections. At a fully diluted market cap of around $1.8 trillion after its first day of trading, Anthropic hasn't been immune to those pressures.

Sources indicate that but Anthropic is arguably well positioned to handle financing challenges. It now holds $232 billion in cash and equivalents on its balance sheet. What's more, it has financing in place using convertible note purchase agreements with multiple strategic partners totaling $77 billion.

That remarked, Anthropic will have to continue growing revenue at an extremely rapid pace over the next five to 10 years while demonstrating considerable operating leverage. That's definitely possible based on its recent results, but there are significant risks in forecasting the future of a rapidly evolving industry. That means Anthropic shares are likely to be highly volatile in the near term, which may present a better buying opportunity than right after its IPO.
