The push to cut AI inference’s energy use could pay off in unexpected ways.

According to recent reports, data centers already use light in networking. Startup Optalysis aims to use specially engineered waveguides to add computational capacity to the networking path, doing math while data is in transit.

The idea of computing with light has been around for over 70 years, but until recently it’s remained largely in the realm of research. Over the past few years, though, optical computing has emerged as a fast-growing industry: Market valuation of companies in this sector now totals US $2.22 billion, up from $0.4 billion in 2022. One projection suggests that the market will continue to grow exponentially in the coming years.

Sources indicate that the reason for this sudden surge will surprise no one: AI. In particular, AI inference—the process of using AI models to respond to user queries—has driven much of the construction of huge, power-hungry data centers, and relatively energy-efficient optical systems are seen as a potential way forward.

“Most of the interest in [photonics] is driven by the fact that AI inference has literally become limited by power, and in a rather striking manner,” indicates Peter McMahon, associate professor of applied and engineering physics at Cornell University, in Ithaca, N.Y., who has researched optical computing for over a decade.

Industry observers note that optics, at least on paper, carries the promise of doing the same math as today’s GPUs at a drastically lower level of power. Switching from electronics to optics would be a pretty radical solution. Nevertheless, like mushrooms after a rainstorm, optical-computing startups are popping up in the United States, China, Germany, and Israel. And while competing against such GPU makers as Nvidia is an ambitious goal, the investment in optical computing has spurred development of complementary approaches, such as designing alternative AI algorithms, taking on scientific computing, and enhancing in-network computing.

Sources indicate that optalysys, based in England, is inserting optical-computing elements into the networking path, where data centers already use light. The manufacturer’s basic on-chip optical element, shown in the inset, is a Fourier transform calculator.Optalysys.

“I think it would be good for the technology if many of these companies made a good run at it,” says Logan Wright, assistant professor of applied physics at Yale University, in New Haven, Conn., and a former postdoc in McMahon’s lab. “I think that the technology that will be developed through this would be really, really useful, and enable photonic systems that maybe don’t get on this wave of AI but would be capable of doing really quite special things.”.

Inference makes up the bulk of AI energy use. The industry is already spending billions of dollars designing advanced GPUs and other chips to run inference as efficiently as possible. At the heart of the inference computation is one operation, done countless times a second: matrix-vector multiplication.

Industry observers note that back in 1978, optical-computing pioneer Joseph W. Goodman and his coworkers proposed a method for doing matrix-vector multiplication with light. The setup was relatively simple. Let’s say a vector of length N needs to be multiplied by a matrix of size N by N. N light-emitting diodes, all in a row, encode the elements of the vector into the intensity of light—the brighter the light from an individual diode, the greater the value that diode encodes. Then, a cylindrical lens spreads out the light vertically, essentially copying the vector N times. The light then passes through an attenuator of N by N pixels. At each pixel, the light is attenuated according to the corresponding value at that location in the matrix. Passing the light through the attenuator performs the multiplication. Finally, a cylindrical lens oriented perpendicular to the first lens re-collects the light from each row, summing it up. The final vector is measured by a row of detectors.

VECTOR-MATRIX MULTIPLICATION The most computationally expensive part of common AI today is vector-matrix (or matrix-matrix) multiplication. For a vector of length N (here, N = 3), it takes N2 (here, 9) multiplication steps and three accumulation steps—multiple additions at once. The argument for why optics might be advantageous over electronics goes something like this: Multiplying a matrix of size N by N by a vector of length N takes N2 multiplication steps. In electronics, each multiplication takes some energy, so the total energy used scales as N2. In optics, however, the multiplication step is passive: As long as the matrix stays the same, passing light through the attenuator costs almost no energy. The only cost is encoding the vector into light and reading out the answer, but the vector is of length N, not N2. So the energy used will scale as N, not N2, as bigger vectors and matrices are multiplied.Since Goodman’s 1978 proposal, the quality of the available optical elements has improved dramatically. Indeed, in 2022, a team of researchers at Cornell led by McMahon demonstrated an optical neural network that consumed less than one photon of energy—approximately 2.5 x 10-19 joules—per multiplication operation. In a standard computer, a single multiply operation can take between 10-14 and 10-12 joules, which suggests that optics could possibly reduce energy use by a factor of 100,000.But McMahon cautions against such extrapolation. First, the cost of encoding data into optics was not included in the calculation. Even if the scaling is favorable with light, in practice it’s not just the scaling that matters, it’s also the coefficients. Let’s say the energy cost of doing one multiplication with electronics is 1 picojoule, and the cost to encode one vector element with optics is 1,000 picojoules. The electronic approach still wins if the length N of the vector is fewer than 1,000 elements.McMahon states this example is not far off from reality: “One of the key questions is, How big do you have to make the system before you start winning? Lots of people have done this calculation for various kinds of neural networks. The very rough answer is somewhere between N equals 1,000 and N equals 10,000.”It’s also crucial to keep in mind a computing principle known as Amdahl’s law.“Amdahl’s law shouldn’t even be called a law. It’s a simple piece of common sense,” McMahon says. “If you have something that’s going to accelerate a computation, and it can accelerate 90 percent of the code, but there’s 10 percent of the code that it can’t accelerate, then the maximum possible speedup you can get is 10x.”With AI inferencing, the point is to save energy, not time. But Amdahl’s observation still applies. What fraction of today’s AI inferencing code can be better handled with optics is a complicated question that varies by approach. But it’s not 100 percent. Semiconductor-manufacturable integrated photonic circuits are at this point way more promising to reach volume production.”.

In Q.ANT’s approach, the input data is encoded into the phase and amplitude of light pulses via on-chip modulators. A vector in this scheme is represented as a sequence of such pulses, each representing one vector element. The light is guided around the chip with waveguides. The basic computational element is an interferometer: A waveguide splits into two paths, and the light beam traveling down one path is subjected to a phase shift, controlled by a set voltage. When the two paths recombine, the resulting interference performs a computation. Then, photodetectors and analog-to-digital converters turn the result back into digital data. The serialized vector-matrix multiplication approach is slower than Goodman’s computer, where the whole calculation happens at once. However, the slower speed can be mitigated by using multiple wavelengths at once, effectively encoding multiple vectors into light simultaneously.

Q.ANT’s Native Processing Server is already being used in research institutions like the Leibniz Supercomputing Centre in Munich and the Jülich Supercomputing Centre in Jülich, both in Germany. Earlier this year, the firm announced a commercial sale to the cloud provider Ionos. Similarly, Lightelligence’s PACE2 processor is capable of multiplying 128-by-128-element matrices. In a paper released in 2025, the company described vector-matrix multiplication at a 1-gigahertz frequency and 3-nanosecond latency, claiming two orders of magnitude lower latency than with traditional GPUs.

The energy advantage is yet to be conclusively demonstrated, however, and Q.ANT’s Förtsch admits there is still a ways to go. The main problem is the energy cost of analog-to-digital conversion and back. As long as a fast, low-energy converter does not exist, Förtsch indicates, “we don’t see realistic chances that…a general vector-matrix multiplication machine in optics makes sense.”.

According to recent reports, not everyone in the field is focused on matrix-vector multiplication, despite it being all the rage in the AI inference world. In fact, while the optical vector-matrix multiply idea goes back to the 1970s, other computations with optics have been investigated as far back as the 1950s. For example, a Fourier transform—a common operation that decomposes a complex wave or signal into its constituent frequencies—can be done in optics with a single lens.

“A digital chip has to approximate [Fourier transforms] in many small steps, using hundreds to thousands of transistors and more compute cycles,” states Q.ANT’s Förtsch. “Models that use such nonlinear functions can reach the same or better accuracy with fewer parameters and fewer operations.”.

Even though Q.ANT’s optical processor has been used for traditional vector-matrix multiplication, the organization believes its greatest advantages lie in alternative AI architectures that leverage more complex functions. “Tasks such as image classification and segmentation rely heavily on operations like Fourier series, transformations, and other nonlinear functions, which photonics handles natively. We see similar potential in generative AI, especially image and video generation,” Förtsch notes.

According to recent reports, the Israel-based startup LightSolver is also targeting algorithms that are a more natural fit for its device. “If you look at propagating electromagnetic waves, essentially, they are running differential equations in their nature,” says Ruti Ben-Shlomi, CEO and cofounder of LightSolver. She says differential equations can be solved much faster and with higher energy efficiency on the company’s laser processing unit (LPU) than on a traditional computer.

In a recent development, lightSolver’s LPU is an interesting twist on the Goodman optical-computer design. It has input light, a programmable membrane in the form of a spatial light modulator, and output light, but the whole thing is placed in a cavity, where light bounces back and forth many times before reaching a steady state. In addition, the team places a gain medium inside the cavity. This gain medium functions much like those found inside a laser, causing the bouncing light to build up in intensity.

In a recent development, lightSolver’s team is right now working on AI applications for its LPU as well, using the tech giant’s own algorithm to do image generation from text. “We’re not doing vector-matrix multiplication very fast. We are doing something different. We need to assume that the way you train the model, and you then do inference, is completely different. But if you do it differently, you’ll be able to do image generation with dramatic energy-consumption decrease and speedup,” Ben-Shlomi notes. LightSolver has a working prototype of its LPU, and the team is working on a larger, rack-mountable box.

Sources indicate that yet another way to leverage light’s particular strengths is being pursued by Optalysys, a startup based in Leeds, England. Nick Upcoming, founder and chief product officer, has a background in free-space optics, and so he understood that taking a Fourier transform is very easy to do with light.

Sources indicate that the key idea behind Optalysys’s technology is taking that Fourier capability of optics in free space and making it work on-chip. “Rather than using a lens these days, we have our own component, which is a passive component in the plane of the silicon that does that same job,” Upcoming says. And by cleverly designing that component with multiple outputs, the team figured out how to perform different operations as well. “Because of how the Fourier transform response is generated, you can actually pull out different mathematical operations from it,” New says. “We can produce very large, high-precision digital functions at superhigh efficiencies, using our analog photonics approach.”.

“We’re effectively adding processing to where there is none right now.” —Nick Fresh, Optalysis.

Sources indicate that instead of redesigning an accelerator from scratch, Optalysys is targeting a piece of the AI workload that’s often already in the optical domain: networking. In the data center, racks of computers are connected to each other with fiber optics, and the optical connections are increasingly getting closer and closer to the GPUs and CPUs themselves.

Some computations already happen while the data is en route. These computations, which include combining results from multiple GPUs, are executed inside network switch cards. But the cards naturally use power to do that math. With passive optical components, these computations could be done essentially for free.

Latest says that in addition to communication between GPUs, Optalysys’s technology could be used to facilitate a GPU’s communication with memory, doing things like compression and format conversion on the fly. Going back to AI inference, this approach could improve the decode part of the workload. Unlike prefill, decode is memory bound: The most costly part is shuffling model data in and out of memory. “We’re more focused on the memory-bound applications, which fits very well with this principle of adding processing into the communication path,” New says. “We’re effectively adding processing to where there is none right now.”.

Industry observers note that how likely are optical computers to replace GPUs in the data center? Yale’s Wright is not too optimistic. “The direction of algorithm development is not going to be a direction that is automatically favorable for optics,” he says. “In fact, it’s most likely to go in the opposite direction, because [AI model developers] want it to work better on GPUs.”.

However, even if our AI queries aren’t going to be computed in light in the next few years, that investment in the technology won’t be in vain. Notes Wright, “The reason that I and many other people in the field are at least somewhat optimistic is that there’s real investment from companies like Nvidia and TSMC in developing co-packaged silicon photonics—not for computing, but for circuit switching and interconnects, and that just kind of opens the door to do a little bit more in the future.”.

Sources indicate that whether that technology ends up crunching vector-matrix multiplication, doing bespoke simulations, or performing in-network computations, the computing ecosystem seems ripe for a variety of approaches. “We see the data center of the future as heterogeneous: CPUs, GPUs and photonic NPUs [native processing units] each take on the tasks they are best suited for,” says Q.ANT’s Förtsch. “Running each task on the most efficient hardware can significantly reduce energy use and operating costs.”.
