Most Gift Cards Can’t Expire for Five Years – Spend Them Within Months Anyway

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Federal rules keep most gift cards from expiring for five years and bar inactivity fees until a card sits unused for a year. Use your gift cards within a few months, and spend any from a chain that’s closing stores first. None of those rules helps if the store goes bankrupt and stops taking its cards.

At that point, the balance is a claim in the bankruptcy case, which may pay part of it or nothing. A $50 card for a chain that files in the spring may end up worth nothing, while one spent in January bought $50 of goods.

The federal gift card rules, adopted under the 2009 Credit CARD Act, cover expiration and fees instead. They apply to store gift cards, good at one merchant, and network gift cards from Visa, Mastercard, or American Express, good wherever that network is accepted. The money on either can’t expire until five years after the card was bought or last reloaded. An inactivity fee can start only after a year unused, at most once a month, and only if the card states it. The card itself can carry an earlier date, but while the money is still good, the issuer has to replace it for free.

Recent findings suggest that promotional and reward cards, like the $10 card a restaurant adds to a $50 gift card purchase, aren’t covered and can expire on any date printed on the front. Some states go further. California’s gift card law bars expiration dates on most store cards, and Upcoming York’s law gives most cards at least nine years and bans inactivity fees.

A card for a store you never visit is worth more sold or traded than left unused.

Move the store cards to your wallet and use each one on your next routine purchase at that store, like groceries or a meal out.
