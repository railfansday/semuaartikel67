Tech Report: Delta CEO’s Cope: Ed Bastian States Doesn’t Need Starlink, Current Wi-Fi Good Enough

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that delta Air Lines CEO Ed Bastian claims “customers are not choosing airlines based on the Wi-Fi.” That’s a remarkable claim from an airline that’s spent years promoting free internet as a reason to choose its premium experience. Speaking at the Fortune 500 Innovation Forum in Detroit on Monday, Bastian defended passing on Starlink and waiting for Amazon Leo which is still unproven and whose timetable is now in question. He downplayed competitors’ progress, argued Delta’s current Viasat service comes close enough, and expressed confidence that Amazon would match or beat Starlink. It was pure cope. Delta doesn’t blueprint to begin installing Amazon until 2028 and that’s if the service works and delivers on schedule. United and Southwest both plan to finish their Starlink installations in 2027, and American is adding the service as well (so are Alaska and even Frontier). Delta’s Wifi Was Fine When Fewer People Used It Delta’s Viasat internet was fine when a few people on each flight were paying to use it. Make it free, get a much larger share of the plane online, and the experience can become unusable. I wrote about this last December after flying Delta to Las Vegas. The system repeatedly bogged down until nothing would load. I’d rather pay for internet that works than get free internet that doesn’t. Bandwidth has to be shared. Removing the price doesn’t create more capacity, but it gives a lot more people a reason to connect. A connection that works well with light use just isn’t the same thing – and Starlink’s low earth orbit satellites simply give the signal less distance to travel, delivering almost no latency. Bastian points to two latest Viasat satellites. ViaSat-3 F3 entered service in August, and F2 entered service in September. But Delta’s own current wifi explanation acknowledges that performance varies with customer demand and satellite capacity, with less consistent service in high-demand or capacity-constrained areas. The airline knows this problem exists, which is why they’re touting Amazon’s service as a future improvement. United And Southwest Are Already Flying Starlink Bastian suggests most airlines promoting Starlink haven’t started yet. He doesn’t identify which airlines he’s counting. Here’s what the competitors actually say: United Airlines: More than half its daily flights already have Starlink. It expects nearly 64% by year-end and completion of installations by the end of 2027. Southwest Airlines: Its latest quarterly filing confirms equipped aircraft are already in service, with the full fleet expected to be finished in fall 2027. Last week it publicized gaming and livestreaming aboard a flight from Austin to San Diego. Alaska and Hawaiian: The group’s October 26 installation update highlights all 93 Embraer 175s, Hawaiian’s 18 Airbus A321neos and 24 Airbus A330s equipped, along with 16 Boeing 737 MAX aircraft. Even Southwest expects to have Starlink throughout its fleet before Delta starts putting Amazon on its first 500 aircraft. That’s an awkward comparison for the airline charging a premium when it’ll be universal on the only U.S. airline without first class even. Wifi Doesn’t Have To Decide Every Ticket To Cost Delta Money I explained last month that I’ve avoided United and Southwest when I couldn’t count on functional internet. Starlink changes that calculation. I’ll pay more to work onboard because losing several productive hours costs more than the airfare savings. Recon Analytics finds wifi ranks among the top five business-trip airline selection factors for 32.6% of flyers, and first for 6.5%. Even among leisure travelers, 3.8% rank it first. Delta doesn’t need to lose everyone. It can lose a few expensive trips from customers who fly often, buy premium seats and carry airline credit cards. That’s precisely the business it wants. United is already using wifi to recruit those customers. It says its wifi-focused status match campaign produced a tenfold increase in daily applications. Amazon Still Has To Deliver. Bastian says that when Delta gets Amazon Leo, “we’ll be at least as good as Starlink if not better.” He hasn’t demonstrated that. Amazon needs enough satellites in the right orbits, working aircraft equipment, certification, and an airline installation program. It then has to deliver consistently with actual passengers using it. Delta’s own materials make the 2028 start subject to technical, certification and deployment milestones. And 2028 is the start of installations on an initial 500 aircraft. As I covered yesterday, Sunday’s failed Vulcan launch lost 40 Amazon satellites. We don’t yet know whether that pushes back Delta’s rollout. But the risk extends beyond replacing those satellites: Amazon contracted for 38 Vulcan missions to launch more than 1,500 of them. Amazon’s prelaunch photo shows the satellites stacked for that mission. Bastian’s account of testing Starlink is consistent with reporting from 2022. Delta had an early look at the product. It chose different commercial terms. I’ve covered the dispute over Delta’s branded portal. And last month Bastian said the branding disagreement out loud: “(Elon) thought it should be called Starlink on the planes. I said ‘No, it’s gotta be Delta.’” Amazon could offer a broader deal involving cloud computing and entertainment. Delta also gets to keep directing passengers through its own platform, where it promotes partners and sells them things. But a cheaper contract has to save enough to cover the business lost while passengers wait. If someone moves expensive flights to United, earns status there and starts spending on a United credit card, Delta may have to buy that loyalty back long after Amazon finally arrives.
