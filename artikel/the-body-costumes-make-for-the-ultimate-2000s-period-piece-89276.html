‘The Body’ Costumes Make for the Ultimate 2000s Period Piece

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Costume designer Mirren Gordon-Crozier, who counts the mid-century Lessons in Chemistry and the hyper-contemporary Margo’s Got Money Troubles among her credits, has no trouble flitting between eras. But Netflix’s latest hit, The Body, a coming-of-age psychological drama set at a Catholic high school in 2004, posed a new challenge. “I hadn’t done that period before, even though I lived in it,” Gordon-Crozier tells Vogue. “I was about these girls’s age during that time. I sort of know all these girls, in a way. I think we all do, maybe.”.

Industry observers note that gordon-Crozier grew up in London and moved to New York to attend Parsons School of Design, where she adapted to American culture by discerning what signaled status to her classmates. “Having a Balenciaga bag was really important, and that was my main takeaway from college,” she jokes. The City bag does indeed make an appearance, as do a host of other It bags—the Dior Saddle and Louis Vuitton x Takashi Murakami Pochette among them.

“Everything about that era was very important to me because it was also when I was 18 and understanding how you could bond with someone over fashion,” Gordon-Crozier says. Indeed, on-screen interloper Lisbeth (Geena Meszaros) uses fashion to mark her shift from Pentecostal outsider to dance team member. But Gordon-Crozier notes that, although Lisbeth’s sartorial transformation follows her character arc, she ends the season still an outcast. “I think she doesn’t really know about pop culture or any sort of contemporary references at all. I think she was just emulating whatever Anya (Kristina Bogic) was doing,” she says.

In a recent development, A stint at Catholic school also informed Gordon-Crozier’s approach to the costumes. “The way to show your personal style is very minute, since everyone’s skirt has to be the same,” she notes. Both on The Body and at her school, girls would roll or alter their skirts to raise the hemline and accessorize their uniforms with designer bags and jewelry. But Gordon-Crozier and creator Quinn Shephard gave themselves an extra challenge. “We set constraints for ourselves: no makeup, no jewelry. Only religious jewelry,” she explains.

Recent findings suggest that one area where there was no room for even subtle individuality? The cutout leotards festooned with teal sequins that the Lions wear as their dance competition costumes. “The girls were not happy about those costumes, let’s just say that,” Gordon-Crozier laughs. “I’m like, You’re not supposed to be happy with them, okay?”.

The inspiration came from a surprisingly fashionable source. “I saw this image of Chloë Sevigny in a cutout black bodysuit, probably around the same year,” she says. “It was just with a low-waisted strappy thing—very early 2000s.” Et voilà: with matching dance shoes, thick nude tights in just the wrong shade (Gordon-Crozier notes they are the same brand that Hooters uses for its uniforms), and hair rhinestones, they had the perfect ugly dance costume. “We wanted it to be as tacky as possible, like dance competitions are,” she says.

Offstage, though, Gordon-Crozier assigned each of the girls on the dance team a specific celebrity or pop star to emulate. “Anya always felt like Misha Barton and Sienna Miller, Elise (Sara Boustany) had a lot of Devon Aoki, and then Maddie (Shirley Chen) is Hillary Duff,” she claims. “We tried to define every one of them. It just makes it easier when you’re shopping and putting looks together. Everyone has their own color palette, so they’re not really clashing.”.

It has been revealed that gordon-Crozier had a particular fondness for Elise’s edgier wardrobe. “Sara personally also has very similar tastes in a way: She likes dark and edgy designers like Comme des Garçons and Plein Sud and Diesel,” she claims. It was Boustany’s personal tastes that helped bolster the character of Elise to feel less like a Y2K totem and more like a real person. “I had references for her, but I feel like what we made was more unique. It came alive in the fittings rather than completely copying references.”.
