Gemini Notebook's New Interactive Site Builder Is Much More Useful Than I Expected

Sources indicate that gemini Notebook, Google's AI tool for study and research, already lets you do a lot with your notes: You can turn them into flashcard quizzes, interactive podcasts, video explainers, mind maps, and infographics, as well as interrogating the sources you've collected through AI-powered prompts and responses. Now there's something latest. You can also build interactive websites from your notebooks.

Sources indicate that that might not immediately seem like the most obvious fit, turning your research notes into a mass of HTML code. But it gives you another way to look at your material and to get insights from it. Here's how it works, what you could potentially do with it, and how impressively it worked when I put it to the test on some early 20th-century Irish literature.

Website building is rolling out now for Gemini Notebook's web app for all users, and is "coming soon" to the mobile app. You won't see a website option in the Studio panel alongside Audio Overview, Reports, Quiz, and the rest; instead, you type something along the lines of "make me a website" in the prompt box.

Recent findings suggest that to make a website, you need sources, so I set Gemini Notebook to collect information about James Joyce's 1914 collection of short stories, Dubliners. As I studied Dubliners many years ago (I highly recommend it), I was hoping I'd spot any AI errors and hallucinations, and be able to properly assess the quality of Gemini Notebook's analysis.

It has been revealed that with a suitable amount of information absorbed into the AI brain, I got Gemini Notebook to make me a website. Google doesn't give too much official guidance here, but claims you should add "a description of what you want to build for work or study," so if you have a particular style or angle in mind, include it.

Here was my prompt: "Build a website using all the sources and information collected so far. Make it an interactive guide to the individual stories in Dubliners, as well as the overarching themes and context. I want to use it as a study aid to explore different aspects of the story collection.".

The finished website pops up in the Studio panel, and in my case it took around five minutes to put something together from 26 different sources. Click on the website, and it opens in a new window—you can use it from there, open it in a separate tab, or download the code to your computer.

Recent findings suggest that I was honestly impressed by how much Gemini Notebook managed to pack into a single website: I got plot summaries, analysis, critical context, and some of the main themes and motifs outlined. There was an interactive quiz at the bottom of the page to test my knowledge, and even a graphic I could click through to reveal different elements of how the stories fit together.

It has been revealed that everything was well laid out and functional, albeit with the standard "sheen" you get from anything made by AI (which tends to always pick the same colors and fonts). I wouldn't have necessarily thought that I needed a website if I was still studying Dubliners today, but I can definitely see how it would have helped.

In a recent development, for something like my project, a website really works well as a study aid, since it offers a different way of interrogating the information. You can already do that in the main chat, and with tools such as the audio and video overviews, but this is different. It's a bit like having a mini-Wikipedia focused just on whatever you're researching.

The interactive elements that are included—so in my example, I could click from story to story, and theme to theme, within the same page—make a site more engaging than a standard report document and a static wall of text, and should help the information stick if you're trying to study it.

According to recent reports, I can also see how this might be useful for sharing data with others. Maybe you need to analyze monthly report figures, or you want to chart how your organization has developed a product over time—perhaps an interactive website will relay that information in a more compelling way than a slide deck or PDF.

Maybe you have a notebook stuffed with details of a massive family vacation. Putting together a website through Gemini Notebook would give your family a hub that they could use to easily check up on the details of the trip—where you're going and when, what you'll see there, what they need to pack, and so on. Another idea might be a family tree, if you have ancestry data and sources packed together in a notebook. Putting together a website would be a great way to visualize it, and, again, it's something you could share with relatives. If there were updates to the data you've collected, you could generate a new website.

In a recent development, bear in mind that you can do a lot with your prompt. My website turned out the way it did because that's what I asked for, and if you need more interactivity or specific types of sections, then you can ask. Based on my follow-up prompts, you can also specify colors and certain layout choices, though it's not quite a full-scale website builder.

According to recent reports, it looks like these sites are single-page creations, though they do have interactive elements and sections that change as you click around. It might be better to think of them as mini apps rather than sites. As of yet, there's no built-in option for publishing a site online or sharing it with others, so you need to download the HTML and do that manually.
