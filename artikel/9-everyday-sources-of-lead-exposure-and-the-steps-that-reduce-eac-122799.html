9 everyday sources of lead exposure and the steps that reduce each one

Lead reaches children through dust, soil, water, toys and work clothes. See 9 sources and the steps EPA advises to lower the dose.

In a recent development, household dust and peeling paint are one hazard at two stages. Lead paint that chips, peels or gets rubbed down around doors and windows becomes fine dust, and in that form it ends up in a child's lungs or mouth. The U.S. EPA advises families to damp-clean once a week and keep paint in good shape, because dust and deteriorating paint may hold lead. The two chores share a target: families clear the dust before a child takes it in, and they maintain the paint before it breaks down into more dust. A weekly schedule assumes latest dust will build up and need clearing again as long as worn paint remains. A clean floor this week claims nothing about how much dust will gather by next week.

Lead paint hazards are widespread in American homes, with approximately 29 million U.S. housing units affected. About 2.6 million of those homes have young children living in them, and those families are the ones a weekly cleaning routine is meant to protect. Lead-based paint was banned for use in homes in 1978, so a house built before then is more likely to have it on its walls. A construction date is only a clue, though, and it can't show whether the paint in any one older house is a hazard.

Parents can't always control what a toddler puts in their mouth, but they can control what goes on the plate, and a child who eats well absorbs less of the lead they do swallow. The U.S. EPA reports that nutrient-rich meals reduce how much lead the body absorbs. Food can limit the lead a child takes in, but it is not a treatment for lead already in the blood. Iron, calcium and vitamin C are the nutrients the agency singles out. A parent can turn general advice about healthy eating into a simple check of whether those three show up regularly in a child's meals. Families already cook these meals, so better nutrition is one of the cheapest steps a household can take.

A well-fed child is better protected, but no diet makes up for a dusty windowsill or a lead pipe. "Good nutrition is one way to protect children from lead," Michigan's WIC program , as reported by USDA WIC Works. Better meals lower the amount of lead a child's body keeps, while paint, dust and water go on supplying more of it. Diet belongs beside the steps that remove lead at its source, and a box of fortified cereal does not replace any of them.

A backyard or a city park looks like the healthiest place a child can spend an afternoon, and nothing about the dirt warns a parent away. The risk depends on what has stood nearby. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency advises families to have children play on grass or on bare soil that isn't contaminated, because soil near roads, junkyards and old buildings may contain lead. The warning signs are therefore in the neighborhood rather than in the ground: traffic, scrap yards and older structures close to where a child digs. Steering children toward grass is a sensible precaution based on where lead is likely to be. It can't tell a family whether its own bare patch of ground is contaminated or clean.

Children carry yard soil indoors on their shoes. A child coming in from play should clean the soles and take the shoes off at the entrance, which keeps the play area's dirt off the floors where a toddler crawls. Like the grass rule, this habit lowers how much lead reaches a child without answering whether the soil needed avoiding at all.

Recent findings suggest that parents who have checked the paint and the pipes in their home can still bring lead into it themselves. The U.S. EPA reports that adults can carry lead home from worksites and hobbies on their skin, hair, clothes and shoes. A house with no lead paint and no lead pipes can therefore end up with lead dust inside, brought in by the adults who live there. The lead starts in places the family doesn't control, such as a job site or a workbench. The adult who was there, though, decides where that lead gets left. Families can cut off this route at the door, through choices the adults make every day when they come home.

The EPA recommends a shower after any activity with possible lead exposure, because showering removes lead dust from skin and hair. A child touches an adult's skin and hair most directly during a hug or a ride on a hip. A shower does nothing for the work clothes and boots that came home from the same job, however. Lead left on them can spread to floors, furniture and laundry after the adult is clean. A child who crawls across the floor or climbs onto a lap can then pick it up there. For this reason, the routine has to treat clothes and shoes as carefully as the body, keeping them away from the spaces where children play.

It has been revealed that lead in tap water comes through the plumbing, out of sight behind walls and under the street, so a family can't judge the risk by looking around the house. Tap water can contain lead from old lead pipes, the US EPA says, and flushing the line, using cold water and filtering all reduce how much of it a child drinks. CDC names faucets and plumbing fixtures as lead sources alongside pipes, so the lead can be in the hardware at the sink as well as in the lines that feed it. "Lead service lines can be a major source of lead contamination in water," the EPA said. That makes the pipe connecting a home to the water main the first suspect, but a home without a lead service line can still have lead at the tap.

Many parents think of lead poisoning as a problem from an earlier era, one that regulators dealt with decades ago. Children's exposure has in fact fallen a great deal. "Blood lead concentrations have decreased dramatically in U.S. children over the past 4 decades," the American Academy of Pediatrics noted in 2016, as reported by ATSDR. The problem has not ended, though. Approximately 500,000 American children have enough lead in their blood to reach or pass the benchmark the agency sets, according to CDC, and behind that number are a large number of families still dealing with the problem. Children are exposed through the ordinary parts of home and neighborhood life rather than through one unusual source, and CDC sorts the common sources into six categories. No family can protect a child by fixing a single thing.

Recent findings suggest that children can take in lead in so many small ways that the useful question for a parent is how much of it a child takes in, not whether the child ever encounters it. Any amount matters. Health officials have yet to find any amount of lead in a child's blood that they consider safe, according to ATSDR, and CDC ties even small amounts to slower development, trouble learning and problems with behavior. Daily habits are therefore worth the effort, since each one lowers the amount of lead a child swallows or breathes. Cleaning and washing leave the paint, pipes and soil that produce the lead in place, however. Protecting a child takes two kinds of work. The first is the routines a family can do at home. The second is finding out what is hidden in the walls, the water lines and the yard.

The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency laid out simple steps families can take to prevent children's lead exposure, covering everything from a toddler's pacifier to backyard soil and a parent's work boots. Families can use the steps as a household to-do list for prevention, not as a scientific measure of risk. Some are chores that cut how much lead a child takes in. Others are checks that show where the lead in a home comes from, such as a soil screening, a call to the water utility or a blood test. The chores are cheap, and a family can start them this week. The checks take more effort and are easier to put off. They matter just as much, because only a check tells a family whether its home holds a source that no amount of cleaning will remove.

Recent findings suggest that cleaning manages the dust, but the paint the dust comes from stays in place. Whether that paint is a hazard, and what to do about it, is a question no amount of mopping can answer. A risk assessment identifies lead hazards and the actions to take. Until a family has one, it keeps up the paint without knowing what the walls contain.

The weekly damp mop is a family's first protection, and a risk assessment is how a family in an older home learns whether that protection is enough.

Industry observers note that parents can also add lead to a child's diet in the kitchen, often during the most routine tasks. Water for cooking and for mixing baby formula should come only from the cold tap. For a baby on formula, the rule covers the water in every bottle a parent prepares. Heating water to a boil leaves any lead in it untouched, so a parent who boils tap water for a bottle has not made it safer on that count. The EPA also advises families to know where herbs and "natural" ingredients come from before using them. A "natural" label does not tell a parent whether an ingredient contains lead.

Meals with enough iron, calcium and vitamin C, cold water at the stove and spices from known sources all help hold down the lead in what a child eats.

For a toddler, the lead in a house usually reaches the body on the child's own fingers. CDC's National Center for Environmental Health reports that lead dust is the most common source of exposure in young children, who put their hands and objects in their mouths. A toddler with dusty hands is swallowing lead a little at a time. The US EPA advises parents to wash children's hands frequently, especially after they play outdoors or with animals. A parent doesn't need to trace where the dirt on a child's hands came from to follow that routine. That makes handwashing especially valuable for the youngest children, who put their hands in their mouths the most.

Handwashing costs almost nothing, because plain soap and water is enough. What it requires is repetition. A crawling child picks up dust again on every floor and table, and a single wash before dinner doesn't cover the rest of the day. Each wash lowers the amount of lead that reaches the child, but it does nothing about the paint or soil that is the source of the dust.

In a recent development, adults need to wash too, because their own bodies can carry lead into the home. CDC names clothes and skin as the ways lead gets brought home, and the EPA advises adults to wash after activities that might have put them in contact with lead. A parent who washes up protects the child they pick up next, not only themselves. A child who scrubs at the sink while an adult skips it is still exposed. Even a household where everyone washes can't rely on washing alone. Clean hands pick up lead again from dust left on floors and furniture, so handwashing works only alongside the cleaning that removes that dust.

Washing gets a child's hands clean, and only a clean house keeps them that way.

A family can stop guessing by having its soil screened. ATSDR, the federal agency that runs the soilSHOP screening events, reports that the screenings are free. The grass rule works from where contamination is likely, while a screening settles the question for one specific yard. Because a federal public-health agency does the screening, a family gets a measured result rather than an estimate. Results come back the same day, so a parent learns right away whether to keep letting a child dig in that spot or to move play to the grass.

It has been revealed that taking shoes off at the door is the chore, and the soil screening is the check. A family that does only the first is still guessing about the ground its child plays on.

In an older house, lead becomes a danger to children when someone cuts, sands or scrapes its painted surfaces. For any home built before 1978, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency advises families to bring in certified professionals for that kind of work. The construction date marks where the advice applies, and it does not prove that a given house contains lead. Most houses from those years do have it, though, with lead-based paint in 83% to 86% of them. A family planning a project in such a house should assume the paint contains lead and test it or hire carefully before work starts, without waiting for chipped paint to raise the alarm.

The older the house, the greater the risk, because the earliest coats of paint held the most lead. ATSDR reports that white house paint made before 1955 could be 50% lead, and that the U.S. had about 23.2 million homes dating from before 1950 in 2006. A worker who sands or scrapes those early layers creates dust that is heavy with lead. The EPA's renovation advice therefore covers millions of ordinary homes along with the rare historic property. An owner of a house that old should treat even a small repair as lead work.

A fresh coat of paint does not make old lead paint safe, because the old layers underneath can still wear into dust wherever painted surfaces rub together. Routine repairs disturb that paint, so the choice of contractor matters as much as the project itself. The EPA advises families to hire a contractor who is Lead-Safe Certified, a credential a family can ask for by name. That certification comes from the EPA's Renovation, Repair and Painting Rule, so a family that asks for it is requiring a federal standard. Careful work still raises dust, and the EPA says residents should stay out until the work is done and the area is cleaned. For a family with young children, that means planning to stay somewhere else during the job.

In an older home, the safest repair is one planned around the lead before anyone picks up a scraper.

The habit protects a child only when it covers everything that came back from the worksite or the hobby: the person, the clothes and the shoes.

According to recent reports, A pacifier or a chewed toy is the shortest path between household dust and a child's body. The U.S. EPA advises families to keep these items clean because children handle and mouth them every day, and the dust they gather may contain lead. Dust on a rattle is a dose rather than a housekeeping problem. Chewing on toys and other objects is one of the ways lead dust reaches many young children, according to CDC. For that reason, the EPA tells parents to wash pacifiers, bottles and toys daily, on the same schedule as the child's chewing. The habit costs a family a sink and a few minutes, and it lowers the lead that reaches a young child who puts everything in the mouth.

A parent who washes a toy removes what sits on its surface but leaves what the toy is made of. The daily wash deals with the dust that settles on items a child uses regularly. Some toys carry lead in the object itself. The EPA advises keeping painted toys out of children's mouths, and CDC lists some imported toys as a source of lead. A child can take in lead from a painted or imported toy even after a parent has scrubbed it clean. Painted toys belong out of a child's mouth altogether, and parents should not assume an imported toy is safe because it has been washed.

In one small object, a family meets both kinds of work: a chore that lowers the dose and a source that no amount of washing can reach.

A family can flush and filter every day without ever learning whether its own line contains lead. Those habits lower the dose without revealing where the lead comes from. The answer belongs to the water utility, which families should contact both to check the service line and to test the water. That answer can also change while life indoors stays the same. Crews working near a service line may release more lead, so a family has good reason to call the utility again when the street outside is dug up.

Until the utility answers, the daily habits are the protection a family has, and they cost almost nothing to keep. A parent who runs the tap before filling a glass, drinks and cooks with cold water, and uses a filter has made the routine part of the day. That routine is worth keeping whatever the utility reports.

The habits lower the dose, but only the call tells a family whether it is guarding against a lead pipe it actually has.

A family that has mopped the floors, scrubbed hands, run the cold tap and hired a certified contractor still can't tell by looking at a child whether lead has reached the child's blood. "A child with lead in their blood may not have visible signs or symptoms and in fact many children who have lead poisoning look and act healthy," the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency said. A toddler who eats, sleeps and plays normally gives a parent no assurance at all. The household chores lower how much lead reaches a child, but none of them shows whether any lead is already in the child's body. A blood test answers that question for one specific child.

In a recent development, whether a child gets a blood lead test depends on the family's health coverage. Children on Medicaid are required to have one at 12 and 24 months, the EPA states, while for every other child a test at those ages is a recommendation from the CDC. The schedule itself comes from the CDC, and the EPA passes it along to parents. A parent outside Medicaid can ask the doctor for the test directly. The EPA offers a postcard that parents can bring to the child's appointment to make that request.

At the 1- and 2-year checkups, a single blood draw tells a parent what a healthy-looking toddler cannot.
