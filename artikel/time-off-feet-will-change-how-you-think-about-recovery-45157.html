Time Off Feet Will Change How You Think About Recovery

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Back when Kerri (Gallagher) Inman was training for professional track races, she told her coach that between runs she would be working as a waitress. Absolutely not, was his answer. “It’s not as if he would have assigned me a specific time off feet metric,” Inman says, “but my feet needed to rest, and being a waitress was too much time on feet for someone racing the way I did.”.

According to recent reports, now, as a New York City-based TeamRunRun coach, Inman carefully considers her runners’ jobs when creating their training plans. For example, a nurse who is “always on her feet,” can’t have runs that overload her so much that she couldn’t keep training or working after a race, Inman says.

Industry observers note that while time on feet is a metric that many coaches use to quantify a runner’s training volume, few of them use time off feet in a measurable way. “We do have an old saying, ‘Never stand when you can sit and never sit when you can lie down,’” indicates Will Baldwin, USATF and VDOT-certified running coach. Like any generalization, though, that rule isn’t very helpful.

Additionally, despite the many ways runners dig into their metrics and researchers look at small running form and running shoe discrepancies, there is little hard science regarding how much time off your feet you need and how you should best spend that time.

“Instead, you want to use comfort as your paradigm or framework on whether your recovery method is working for you,” claims Jim Jastifer, MD, a fellowship-trained orthopedic surgeon specializing in foot and ankle reconstruction with OSMC Orthopedic and Sports Medicine Center in Indiana.

🏃🏻‍♀️The Runner’s World Guide to Recovery is your home for a smarter—from managing soreness and fatigue to easing back into running after a race, offseason, or injury.

Sources indicate that in fact, “be comfortable” and “do what makes you happy” may be the best metrics for recovery, according to Jastifer. That’s because it’s not just your feet that need a break—so does your brain.

Are joy and time off feet really related? Turns out, they are. Kara Goucher, distance runner extraordinaire and cohost with Des Linden of the running podcast Nobody Asked Us, has her own way of making sure she gives herself enough time off feet.

Industry observers note that “I love playing Sudoku!” Goucher noted on the pod, and she considers her game playing part of her rest and recovery. “It’s time off feet that really does calm my mind and it’s so much better for my brain and mental health than doom scrolling on my phone! It's time to reset my body and my mind.”.

In fact, according to the experts, your goal around recovery and time off feet should be to become self-aware of what your body needs, what your feet need (which may be different than what your larger muscles and soft tissues need), and what your brain needs.

Your feet may actually be the best way to determine how to spend your recovery time; your wearable likely doesn’t correlate its opinion of your recovery to the stress that your feet feel, Jastifer says. “If your Garmin says you’re ready to run, but your feet hurt, you should listen to your feet,” he adds.

In a recent development, “Someone who is running 70/80/100 miles a week isn’t going to recover more by going for a walk,” indicates Baldwin. This is precisely why Inman’s coach didn’t want her, a professional runner at the time, to add waitressing on top of her training schedule.

“Instead, I try to get my athletes to do low-level movement, such as time on the bike.” For his own time off feet, Baldwin saves his favorite shows to watch during these sessions so he has less mental taxation.

In a recent development, “Swelling and pain are really the only two reasons you absolutely need to stay off your feet,” says Baldwin. However, you’ll need to be aware of the difference between swelling due to injury and swelling that occurs simply because fluid has gone to your feet because of extensive motion.

In a recent development, “Keeping your feet in motion can actually help swelling,” says Jastifer. Walking can stimulate the movement of blood and lymph (a fluid that carries nutrients to your blood and tissues), which can help recovery, but only if your feet aren’t fighting injury. Signs of injury include pain and discoloration.

Of course, you don’t always have to walk—or do anything with impact—to move your feet. Making circles, rolling a ball under them, or stretching them can reduce the swelling. Again, though, if you feel pain, stop and go to a doctor before you run again.

Percell Dugger, Runna run coach, suggests that you always consider rest and recovery within the context of your training proposal, and, if your feet hurt, balance that notable metric against the intensity and effort of a planned workout, even if it’s active recovery.

According to recent reports, “In practice and by observation everyone recovers differently,” Inman claims. “So, you want to be responsive to whether your feet are telling you they need to rest. If so, maybe get in a pool or get on a bike.”.

Even though there is no clear time or method for recovery, the most key thing to do between runs is sleep and eat, advises Kristine Sweezey, PT, DPT, CSCS, a physical therapist in Rock Hill, South Carolina, and Charlotte, North Carolina.

It has been revealed that jastifer agrees, adding that rest, cross-training, and time off feet are not at all the same as sleeping. “They serve different functions,” he adds. Without sleep and proper nutrition, athletes cannot actually begin to recover. Moreover, any tricks or tools you use outside of sleep and proper fueling won’t add anything to the recovery equation without a commitment to the basics.

Between sleep, time on feet, and time off feet, the experts recommend that you add foot strengthening to your routine. Because improving the abilities of your intrinsic foot muscles will help your feet cope with long and hard miles on the road. So, what are your intrinsic foot muscles? The small muscles that connect within your foot, but not to areas outside of it, such as your Achilles. The muscles in your toes and arch are intrinsic.

“By analogy, we have all the same muscles in our feet that we have in our hands, and while they aren’t significant from a force standpoint, they help regulate the force proprioception in ankle stability and are part of a neuromuscular feedback loop so you don’t have to think about how your feet land which will provide you with stability,” Jastifer says. “If you have foot pain, it might be from these muscles.”.

According to recent reports, sweezey agrees and says you should do all the toe scrunches and toe lifts that you can. “I keep a lacrosse ball under my desk to roll around with my feet,” she says. “It helps decrease the soreness and flushes things out.”.

Finally, when you are ready to get back on the road with your revived feet—whether it’s been a day or a week—make sure you don’t pair two long runs or speed workouts back-to-back, says Dugger. “Progressive loading is better than high spikes and a dive.” If you and your feet (and your brain) need a break, don’t push too much on your next run.
