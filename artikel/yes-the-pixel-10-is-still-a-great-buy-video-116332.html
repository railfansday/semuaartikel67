If you’re looking for a decent replacement for an older phone or a backup, and the Pixel 10 looks eerily similar to the Pixel 11, is it still worth it? Well, of course it is, but let’s get into why in this deep dive.

One of the best things about the Pixel 10 is that it looks almost identical to the Pixel 11; in some cases, it’ll work with the accessories for the newest phone – but check that before you try. So that means you’re not getting a majorly out-of-date design.

In a recent development, that doesn’t matter to most people, but it could be important to you. Pixelsnap magnetic accessories still work, and while the Pixel 11 trimmed down the camera bar to an all-glass visor, the overall footprint is virtually identical.

Using the same formula might be stale to some people, but it works, so why change it for the sake of it?

I’d argue that the best news is that this finally highlights Google is confident, given its success with the Pixel 9 and 10 design. The tech giant may make changes in the future, but it’s a fairly solid design that feels great after they settled on a physical form factor that works after years of fiddling, and it’s no secret that the goal is to make the switch from iOS to Android as “easy and painless” as possible. A familiar design goes a long way to calming the nerves of a potential switcher. The Pixel 10 is a perfect example of this philosophy. The Pixel 11 essentially proves that too.

According to recent reports, over a year on from release, there don’t seem to be any major or widespread reports of issues on the Pixel 10. I’d say that is a good omen, as people will often know within that timeframe if there are quality control issues and problematic hardware faults.

Sources indicate that that remarked, the only problem I think you have to deal with is the base 128GB storage configuration. It’s not insignificant, but still not going to be tons of room for everything. If you take lots of photos and download loads of things locally to your device, then maybe go for the 256GB model.

One of the weirdest things about the Pixel 10 was that after the Pixel 11 released along with all other supported handsets, it had a newer version of Android than the latest phone.

Yes, you read that correctly: the Pixel 10 had QPR1 before the Pixel 11.

The entire experience is just more of the same goodness that started with the addition of Material 3 Expressive last year. Android 17 keeps improving; the quality-of-life changes make for a really compelling experience that, while taking some cues from iOS, hasn’t gone full Liquid Glass. Google hasn’t quite kept the day-one update promise like the old days of Pixel, but you still have the cutting edge of the software. Even the Galaxy S26 series isn’t running a release this fresh and probably won’t for a while.

Sources indicate that rambler has even been added recently, so there is a function bleeding down from the Pixel 11, and there are loads of other quality-of-life changes and features that came with Android 17 QPR1 thrown in. Any bugs that might have been there, however limited, have been addressed in the year the phone has been around.

With the Pixel 10, you may miss out on some of the extra Gemini Intelligence perks or a couple of visual gimmicks like the Pro’s HiLight glow, but none that is yet groundbreaking enough to be drastically upset about.

Sure, the competition might offer a faster processor or newer silicon, but can they offer the fluidity of a system that is fully integrated into all parts of your digital life? That’s what the Pixel 10 delivers.

Sources indicate that as I noted, the Pixel 10 has already seen multiple updates, smoothing out early thermal quirks and optimizing battery efficiency. These monthly security patches and Pixel Drops are a testament to the fact that your phone is a constantly evolving product.

In a recent development, with more updates due very soon, the prospect of an even more optimized experience, and many of the best bits of Google all rolled into one handset, it’s a damn good buy, especially as other OEMs are still struggling to get their own Android 17 updates out in a timely fashion. We’re several months on from the full launch, and barely anyone outside of Samsung has offered a wide rollout. Of course, all Pixel 10 devices get day-one updates, with six years of support still left on the clock.

There is one sore point on the base Pixel 10 that I think might make it hard to recommend over the Pixel 10 Pro. The main camera is the same as the Pixel 9a and the 10a on the base 10. It makes it harder to suggest if you care about camera quality.

Industry observers note that it’s still super reliable and does take great photos, but it might not be the best in class like previous generations may have been. In fact, I’d go so far as to say that while it’s great you get a telephoto zoom lens, the lower-quality main sensor diminishes it somewhat.

Most people will be better off with the Pro because it has a lot more to work with, but the cameras are still very good on the Pixel 10 and are backed by Google’s excellent updates.

According to recent reports, even video quality stacks up, though I will say Google’s reliance on Video Boost as a workaround for lower-quality footage is a bit of a cop-out. However, it’s a good workaround, and the resulting footage is still excellent, even if I’m hoping for better on-device processing in future Pixel generations.

Recent findings suggest that ultimately, the camera is a prime example of the Pixel value proposition. You don’t need the absolute latest, greatest, multi-lens Pro Max Ultra setup to take amazing photos. You need a Pixel. The camera on the 10 series is still right up there with the best from Samsung, OnePlus, and others. It’s doing just as much with technically much less.

The biggest reason that many people who are looking for a new phone might want to consider at least the Pixel 10 lineup is that, with discounts, you’re getting a great deal — especially since Google bumped the entry price on the Pixel 11. Sure, bigger, better, faster, and shinier phones exist. They don’t give you the same experience, though. You’re paying for something that others can’t replicate, even at higher price tags.

With more updates due very soon, the prospect of an even more optimized experience, and many of the best bits of Google all rolled into one handset, it’s a damn good buy.

Is the Pixel 10 the best option for a phone right now? Suppose you want arguably the best software and are willing to forgo a few of the bells and whistles. In that case, it is an excellent alternative for anyone looking to save a little money without sacrificing too much. It’s a better buy than the Pixel 10a for most people, in my opinion, and you’ll be hard-pressed to find a more compelling mix of software intelligence and hardware value.
