Recent findings suggest that in an increasingly fire-prone western North America, Jennifer Grenz, an ecologist and member of the Lytton First Nation, looks at fire with a holistic eye. She considers her work with landscapes recovering from catastrophic wildfire to be “deliberately unfocused,” spanning soil microbial ecology, restoration ecology, invasion biology and historical biology.

It has been revealed that grenz analyzes historical ecological records, like how Indigenous people have shaped and cared for ecosystems over time, to paint a comprehensive picture. “I’m interested in how those histories leave ecological legacies and hints of the stewardship of the past,” claims Grenz, who leads the Indigenous Ecology Lab at the University of British Columbia in Canada. “What changed on the land that made it more vulnerable?”.

Grenz spends time with many kinds of specialists, such as archaeologists, anthropologists and Indigenous elders who provide oral histories.

Moving forward, her lab will examine ecology below ground, including soil, microbial communities and root networks. The goal is to understand the role of underground ecology in critical topics like carbon sequestration, climate change, biodiversity and sustainable management.

“My motivation is in our interior Salish Territories in the southern interior of British Columbia, where my ancestors rest and where my people are,” Grenz claims. “The responsibility I feel to them and to our Tmícw, which means our lands, [is] to not take us back to the past, but to show that we have the deep knowledge to help us move forward.”.
