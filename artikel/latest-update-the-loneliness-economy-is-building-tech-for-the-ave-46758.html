Latest Update: The loneliness economy is building tech for the average customer, but that customer doesn’t exist

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Investors have poured money into startups promising to solve our loneliness epidemic. Earlier this year, Fabio Bin, CMO and cofounder of the adventure travel company WeRoad, claimed his $150 million startup could “solve the $406 billion loneliness problem,” and he isn’t alone. In 2022, Adam Neumann, of WeWork fame (or shame), secured $350 million from Andreessen Horowitz to launch his residential real estate firm that claimed to “solve loneliness.”  But Noreena Hertz, a University College London professor who coined the term “loneliness economy,” is skeptical. She recently told Vox that of the 50 to 60 startups targeting this economy that have approached her, not one convinced her they have a good business model. But what if they have the “market” wrong? Despite millions flowing into startups, entrepreneurs are focusing on the wrong things to address the crisis and capture the market opportunity. My new research explains why. In brief, the loneliness economy treats loneliness as one market, but in reality, it’s at least five. And surprisingly, some of the biggest market opportunities don’t focus on connection, at least not at first. For many struggling individuals, solving loneliness starts with reclaiming their identities. Of all the lonely people, my research focused on parents, for two reasons. First, they’re among those hit hardest by loneliness. In 2024, two-thirds of parents reported feeling lonely. Second, their loneliness has long-tail ripple effects. It impacts their health and their children’s, and a parent’s loneliness can predict their child’s years later. Solving parental loneliness is one of the best levers we can pull for societal benefit, and to recapture our current $406 billion opportunity cost. Serving the market starts with categorizing it correctly “Loneliness” is an umbrella term that lumps all experiences of “I’m lonely” into one category, predominantly with one solution: make more social connections. It would be akin to having one solution for pain, regardless of whether that pain exists because of a headache, the beginning of childbirth, or a broken limb. But we don’t. We have Tylenol, epidurals (and doulas), and casts instead. Just as a one-size-fits-all approach to pain management doesn’t work, neither will one for loneliness. Yet, innovators targeting the “loneliness economy” are building for an average customer who doesn’t exist. And that’s why we haven’t moved the needle on the loneliness epidemic in the years since we’ve identified it. People aren’t averages. They’re individuals. And, while most innovators design for who customers are, this still doesn’t tell the whole story. At the Christensen Institute, we leverage Jobs to Be Done theory, developed by the late Clayton Christensen, innovation thinker and professor at the Harvard Business School, and Bobby Moesta, an entrepreneur and lecturer at Northwestern University’s graduate business school. It’s built on the idea that demographics like income or age don’t tell you why people buy what they do. They only tell you who people are. But customers buy products and services because of why they’re struggling and what they’re trying to achieve in a given situation. Using this approach, my colleagues and I asked parents who had recently recovered from chronic loneliness what drove them to change and what helped them overcome it. Their answers revealed five different combinations of struggles, motivations, and desired outcomes (aka “Jobs”). That means parents experience loneliness in five different ways, not just one. Loneliness isn’t always about a lack of social connections Two of the five Jobs we discovered weren’t rooted in a lack of social connection, but instead in a loss of identity. For example, one of the Jobs was about feeling depleted and needing to reclaim time for oneself. One parent we interviewed remarked, “Everyone else comes first, and then me at the end.” She was suffering from caregiving for her children as well as her own aging parent, and she needed validation and a pathway to invest in herself. Parents in similar situations turned to time alone, volunteering, faith, and visits with old friends. Importantly, those who turned to friends weren’t driven by a need to connect, but because time with friends meant time for self. The second Job rooted in identity loss was about being stuck in a rut. One dad shared, “My life contains pretty much either work, kids’ after-school activities, or my house.” These parents wanted to fix the “I’m bored, therefore, I’m boring” ache and pursued activities that made them feel more capable and engaged in their lives. They joined sports leagues and workout groups, and started gig work and new hobbies. One parent even quit an all-consuming job to make a life she was excited about. Parents with either type of loneliness won’t be interested in a group travel solution or a friendship app, at least not at first. They feel lonely, but not for other people. They need to reconnect with their identities more than connect with others. When it comes to social connection, depth, breadth, and timing matter  The other three Jobs were driven by a longing for more human connection, but each called for a different kind, on a different timeline. Some parents needed a nurturing confidant right now. They were overwhelmed by life, wanted someone who could relate to their situation, and didn’t want to wait. Group therapy that involves a waiting list fails to meet their need for timeliness, but a weekly gathering of parents in similar life stages meets them where they are. Others felt alone after losing an vital relationship or career. They longed to reconnect with friends, but first they needed reassurance that they weren’t alone. As a result, solutions that push them straight into friendship-building will fail. As one parent put it, “I’m not calling someone at 11 o’clock at night, at 41 years old, to talk about my feelings.” Instead, she turned to artificial intelligence when she needed reassurance. And while AI may fill that short-term need, no parent we spoke to credited it with solving their loneliness. The last group wanted long-term belonging and a village of people to do life with. They wanted to be known and understood, and have a community to rely upon. This is the customer most loneliness products are built for. It’s also where AI falls short, because AI can’t pick up your kid from school or bring a meal when someone is sick. Capturing the market everyone else is missing  Entrepreneurs in the loneliness market fail to impress loneliness experts like Hertz because their value propositions target an average “lonely” consumer. But averages describe populations, not people. To succeed in the loneliness economy, innovators need to focus their business models on one Job, or two closely related ones, and build around it. This focus will drive higher engagement and better market traction. For example, parents who are struggling with depletion need permission that it’s okay to take time for themselves, and it’s encouraged. Without encouragement, many won’t change behaviors because of the guilt they feel. But parents who are looking for community don’t need encouragement; they need people like them. Conversely, a product for parents stuck in a rut should offer something to look forward to, along with a pathway to demonstrate capability. Volo Sports, a rapidly growing adult sports league across the U.S., does this well. They lead with the individual benefit: “sports . . . unveil our determination, perseverance, and heart,” and the social connection is secondary. And an offering focused on building community must tap into the specific struggles that keep parents from forming community in the first place. One example is Our Circle, a social connection app that serves parents of children with support needs. It taps into why they’re struggling to form a community and helps them overcome those specific barriers. Our Circle is specific about the struggles it serves, and isn’t seeking to serve every parent searching for community. Based on the types of loneliness we uncovered, social entrepreneurs, city planners, health innovators, and investors need to redirect their efforts and dollars to capture the true market opportunity, not the average one that ignores important individual context. Capturing the opportunity will also ensure that solutions help those suffering from high-stakes loneliness (i.e., parents) before they pass it on to the next generation. A one-size-fits-all approach to loneliness, finding more social connections as the first step, becomes a business with few real customers. The loneliness economy doesn’t have a demand problem. It has a diagnosis problem. With better categories, innovators can now course-correct.
