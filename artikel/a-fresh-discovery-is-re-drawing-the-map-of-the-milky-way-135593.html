Sources indicate that star formation is a galaxy's foundational process. Astronomers study how star-forming gas moves around in a galaxy, where it's compressed, and in which parts of a galaxy stars form. In a barred spiral galaxy like the Milky Way, star formation is mostly concentrated in the spiral arms. In the central bar, star formation is more sluggish.

According to recent reports, recent research demonstrates that though the central bar contains many stars, star formation there is mostly concentrated in the bar ends. Shear forces, streaming motions, and overpowering shocks that make the gas in the bars turbulent all combine to to inhibit star formation.

In a recent development, the Milky Way is a typical barred spiral galaxy, and astronomers have studied it intently to understand where star formation is concentrated. There's ongoing scientific debate about what happens in a barred spiral galaxy's central bar. Observations of other spiral barred galaxies show that star formation suppression there isn't necessarily universal. Part of the difficulty in understanding our own galaxy is observing the region. Since we're embedded in the galaxy, it's difficult to observe the bar. In many ways, astronomers have an easier time observing other galaxies.

It has been revealed that researchers from Australia and Sweden have found an unusual structure in the Milky Way's center that appears to set it apart from many other barred spiral galaxies. It's a vast collection of rotating stars in the central bar that's generating powerful and large shock fronts in the gas. These shock fronts are triggering star formation in the structure, which goes against the idea that stars can't form there.
