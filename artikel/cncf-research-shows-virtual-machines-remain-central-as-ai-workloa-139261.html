InfoQ Homepage News CNCF Research Shows Virtual Machines Remain Central as AI Workloads Evolve.

Nov 24, 2026 3 min read.

Sources indicate that virtual machines (VMs) continue to account for a substantial share of cloud-native production infrastructure, despite the widespread adoption of containers and Kubernetes. Fresh research from the Cloud Native Computing Foundation (CNCF) and Linux Foundation Research finds that VMs host an average of 53% of production applications, while emerging AI and agentic workloads are creating additional demand for virtualisation, particularly where stronger execution isolation is required.

It has been revealed that the report surveyed 492 cloud-native practitioners and technology leaders in September 2026. Among organisations expanding their VM footprint, 34% identified AI and agentic workloads as a contributing factor. The findings suggest that rather than replacing virtual machines with containers, organisations are increasingly combining the two to accommodate different application and security requirements.

The research challenges the assumption that cloud-native adoption necessarily means moving away from virtual machines. While containers provide portability and efficient application packaging, VMs continue to offer isolation and support for workloads that depend on dedicated operating systems or established virtualisation environments.

The survey found that 51% of respondents run at least half of their production applications on VMs. This rises to an average of 64% in organisations with more than 10,000 employees, compared with 45% among organisations with fewer than 500 employees.

Furthermore, 71% of respondents who provided both current and expected workload figures anticipate their VM share remaining within the same range over the next year. This points towards continued coexistence rather than a wholesale migration to one deployment model.

One of the more significant findings concerns the relationship between AI adoption and infrastructure security.

It has been revealed that AI agents increasingly generate and execute code, interact with tools, and access external systems. This creates latest risks when generated code is executed in environments containing sensitive data or shared infrastructure.

In a recent development, virtual machines offer a stronger isolation boundary than conventional containers, which share the host operating system kernel. The report identifies hardware-level VM isolation, containers running inside VMs, and MicroVM sandboxing as approaches organisations are using to contain AI workloads.

Among organisations expanding their VM footprint, 47% cited security and isolation as a contributing factor, while 34% specifically identified AI and agentic workloads.

The survey also points to growing interest in stronger isolation technologies. MicroVM sandboxing adoption is expected to increase from 20% current use to 30% planned use, while confidential computing is expected to grow from 12% to 26%. These figures represent reported intentions rather than confirmed deployments.

Some 61% of respondents described their VM and container administration teams as collaborative or fully integrated, suggesting that managing different workload types is increasingly becoming a shared platform engineering responsibility.

Kubernetes is also beginning to play a role in virtual machine management. Among respondents using VMs, 8% reported using KubeVirt, the CNCF project that allows virtual machines to be managed through Kubernetes resources and APIs.

This brings VM lifecycle management into existing Kubernetes operational practices without requiring applications to abandon their virtualised environments.

In a recent development, the findings reinforce a shift in how cloud-native infrastructure is evolving. Kubernetes and containers remain important, but they are not necessarily the right execution model for every workload.

Technologies such as KubeVirt and Kata Containers show how virtualisation can integrate with container orchestration, while MicroVMs provide lightweight environments to isolate short-lived or potentially untrusted workloads.

For platform engineering teams, the research highlights the need to accommodate different execution environments without fragmenting operational practices. AI adds another dimension, making isolation, resource utilisation and workload security increasingly important infrastructure considerations.

The survey was sponsored by CNCF and Broadcom, and its results should be read as reported industry perspectives rather than a complete census of cloud-native deployments. Nevertheless, its findings provide a useful indication of where infrastructure priorities are moving.
