Now that we have officially entered the holiday season, many of us will attend social gatherings to enjoy special foods, rituals, and festivities with family and friends. Although these events bring cheer, they can also initiate stress related to judgments about physical appearance.

For young girls, these gatherings can be a jarring exposure to comments about their bodies and society’s emphasis on the importance of physical attractiveness. For girls approaching adolescence, this is a particularly vulnerable time for body image development (e.g., Rodgers et al., 2014). And no one is immune to body comments. Even celebrities such as Selena Gomez and Drew Barrymore, who have been praised for their attractiveness, report psychological damage from negative comments about their weight earlier in life.

Research highlights that family members are a major source of weight-based comments and that these comments predict body dissatisfaction and disordered eating. Ironically, such comments are also associated with long-term weight gain (Hunger & Tomiyama, 2018). In focus groups for our book, Beyond Body Positive: A Mother’s Science-Based Guide for Helping Girls Build a Healthy Body Image (American Psychological Association Books, 2024), women reported long-lasting shame, low self-confidence, and body dissatisfaction associated with comments made by family members decades earlier.

Industry observers note that exposure to extended family members and friends typically increases over the holidays, and relatives may notice changes in girls’ bodies, particularly if they haven’t seen them in a while. Because women are raised in diet culture, we may be inclined to make negative comments about our own bodies, what we and others are eating, and how we and others look, setting a social script for expectations at these events. We may not even be aware of these tendencies, such as “fat talk,” because they are normative (Britton et al., 2006).

Unfortunately, you are not in direct control of other people’s behavior. It is key to catch and respond to or redirect people’s comments about your daughter’s body, particularly when they are made in front of her. Comments do not have to be actual insults to be harmful; more subtle innuendos like the ones below can fly below the radar but need attention.

In a recent development, it is entirely appropriate to shut down these kinds of comments to protect your daughter, but how? You can take the straightforward approach (“Oh, our family doesn’t talk about people’s bodies”). You can opt to re-frame comments (“Isn’t it great that our bodies are unique to us and that there’s no reason to compare them to one another?”) or redirect to focus on your daughter’s non-appearance related qualities (“More importantly, Savannah started soccer this year and she mastered fractions!”). You can also simply ignore the comment and redirect with another conversational point at the ready (“So, what else are you doing over the holidays?”).

It has been revealed that it may feel uncomfortable to respond to comments in this way, but is it worth sacrificing your daughter’s emotional and physical well-being just to avoid awkwardness? It is great practice to “debrief” your daughter afterward to explain that the comments were inappropriate (“Aunt Edna talks about bodies a lot and that’s her problem. We know that other things about people are much more important.”). For girls in late childhood who can assert themselves, reassure your daughter that it is not rude to tell her people that she doesn’t want her body to be a topic of conversation.
