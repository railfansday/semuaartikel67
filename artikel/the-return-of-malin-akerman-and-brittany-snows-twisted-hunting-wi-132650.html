Industry observers note that in 2021, at the recommendation of a film producer, the writer and showrunner Rebecca Cutter read a pulpy murder-mystery novel on a flight from her native Massachusetts to her current base in Los Angeles. By the time she landed, Cutter had finished the book and was already mapping out the TV adaptation in her head.

Recent findings suggest that the plot heavily involved a Texas abortion clinic, and the state had just passed SB 8, one of the country’s most restrictive abortion bills until Roe v. Wade was overturned the following year. As she contemplated bringing The Hunting Wives to life, Cutter saw the political stakes come into focus as well.

“I started thinking about the ‘red state, blue state’ of it,” she tells Vanity Fair ahead of the show’s season two premiere on Thursday. “What if…we really play this culture war?”.

“I don’t think I do gratuitous sex scenes,” claims showrunner Rebecca Cutter. “I think you always are seeing a power dynamic play out or a plot point play out.”.

In a recent development, the result was one of Netflix’s biggest breakout hits in recent years, sent up in a Saturday Night Live sketch as the “straight but lesbian, horny Republican murder drama.” As a show, Hunting Wives amps up the noirish aspects of the suspense thriller written by May Cobb, a native of the East Texas Piney Woods, and adds a heaping dose of MAGA-adjacent camp. Cutter finishes the mix with plenty of sex scenes, particularly between Malin Akerman’s Margo, a femme fatale partly inspired by Melania Trump who is married to a Republican gubernatorial hopeful, and Brittany Snow’s Sophie, a liberal do-gooder who, like Cutter, arrives in town from a blue corner of Massachusetts. (Cutter was raised in Cambridge and graduated from Wesleyan University, the Connecticut liberal arts college known for its progressive politics and pipeline to Hollywood.).

Sources indicate that hunting Wives executive producer Erwin Stoff told Cutter he’d pay for her to go to Texas to meet Cobb and breathe the Piney Woods air herself. She met Cobb’s father and her best friend from high school. She shot skeet with the women—she even got it in three, just as Sophie does in the first season. At a high-end Dallas shopping area where the likes of Gucci can be found, Cutter got a better sense of the hair, clothes, and bodies of her eventual characters. “I understand the notion that Sophie could be amused and entertained and intrigued by these women,” Cutter claims, “and eventually seduced.”.

It has been revealed that cutter thinks of the show primarily in terms of its tonal mash-up: “I think we managed to wrangle it into one piece of art,” she says, “but there’s comedy, there’s violence, there’s noir, there’s soap, there’s earnest emotion.” To the extent that she assigned any political beliefs to the rich white women of Cobb’s novel, it was in the spirit of grounding her characters in the specifics of their world. Still, as we speak, Cutter decides “to get real with it.”.

According to recent reports, cutter on set. She describes Sophie, a fellow native of blue Massachusetts, as a surrogate for her own awakening to MAGA Texas.

“The whole thing was a little bit of a parable, in my mind, of how you could fall for someone like [Donald] Trump,” she admits. “Sophie has her principles, but they only go so far. They end right where something starts to make her feel good.” Cutter was as curious as anyone else from her educated milieu about the quirkier aspects of Trump, like the Diet Coke button he had installed on the Resolute Desk. How liberating must it feel to embrace a movement so openly untethered from the pieties of politics and taste?

“Not that my show is a deep dive on Trump,” Cutter says, “but maybe there is an element that’s sort of looking under the hood.”.

Sources indicate that the twisted love story between Margo and Sophie entangles all of the wealthy, hard-drinking, gun-toting women in their orbit, and its central killing—one of several propelling the show forward, with a third season slated to begin filming this year—stems from an effort to cover up Margo’s abortion as her NRA-loving husband plots his political ascent. (In this proudly oversexed town, he has his own share of affairs.) Hunting Wives is the rare Netflix soap to receive steady coverage from mainstream national outlets; the fascination undoubtedly has something to do with the impression that, like Yellowstone or Morgan Wallen before it, the show unlocks something about MAGA’s cultural staying power. In a Latest York Times guest essay this month that evaluated the post-2024 Hollywood landscape, Sharon Waxman, the editor in chief of The Wrap, wrote, “From Melania on Amazon’s streaming service to The Hunting Wives on Netflix to the ever-expanding Taylor Sheridan universe on Paramount+ to Bari Weiss’s being put in charge of CBS News, the vibe shift was visible on all our screens.”.

Margo and her politically ambitious husband are partners in subterfuge and debauchery.

Sources indicate that cutter indicates she had no particular agenda nor any “ultra-evil villain initiative of how it was going to be received or how it would be culturally relevant.” But Hunting Wives is sprinkled with enough proper nouns—a Peter Thiel mention here, a Walmart reference there—and distinctly modern aesthetics that she can acknowledge its appeal as something of a Rosetta stone for the moment in MAGA femininity. Even in the ostensibly waning days of the movement, the plastic surgery and soaring heels continue to captivate and horrify coastal elites. Cutter says that Callie (Jaime Ray Newman), another of Margo’s lovers, has a bit of Kristi Noem and Marjorie Taylor Greene in her: that “sort of slightly butch, real gun, real ‘back the blue’ [element]…the toughness of those women.”.

Industry observers note that the second season of Hunting Wives maintains the slipperiness that keeps the show from veering into moralism; it stands apart from other Trump-era art in its refusal to take sides. “I think I remember, in the pitch, someone saying, ‘But will red-state people like it too?’” Cutter recalls. “I was like, ‘I think so.’” Both Margo and Sophie are awash in their respective red and blue stereotypes, but neither is more admirable than the other. Their sordid union is a testament to the flexibility of both their principles and their preferences. From one vantage, it is, in the end, all about sex.

Industry observers note that “Margo is the symbol of freedom and sexuality and feminine power and empoweredness and selfishness that [Sophie] feels she’s lost,” Cutter says, adding that Akerman’s comfort in such a role helped drive her liberal use of physical intimacy. “The audience may disagree with me,” she says, but “I don’t think I do gratuitous sex scenes. I think you always are seeing a power dynamic play out or a plot point play out.” Last year, though, when Netflix acquired Hunting Wives, which was initially ordered by Starz in 2023, Cutter had a more practical concern.

“I literally googled,” she recalls, “‘Does Netflix show boobs?’”.
