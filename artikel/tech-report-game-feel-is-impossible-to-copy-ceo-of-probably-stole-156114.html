According to recent reports, genAI game clones are apparently becoming a real enough threat to the industry that Take-Two CEO Strauss Zelnick pre-warned "this is likely to invade our business to a greater or lesser extent," while threatening legal action against anyone who naughtily tinkered with GTA VI. More recently, one or more apparent crypto grifters claim to have naughtily tinkered with GTA VI, sparking attempts to vibe-recomp an unoffical PC version. However, contrasting Zelnick's position is tinyBuild CEO Alex Nichiporchik, who quite confidently stated in an interview with Gamesindustry.biz that "devs shouldn't be afraid of it.".

It has been revealed that nichiporchik discusses tinyBuild's approach to the gap between announcement and release being key in the age of slop copies (I am trying not to write 'sloppies' into my article, but it is tempting), explaining that "The traditional wisdom previously was to announce early, build up as many wishlists as possible over time, and then you'll have a good basis to launch your game eventually. And that seems to be changing fundamentally." He adds that maintaining a short gap also helps as, to put it simply, "people have short memories, right?" And, unfortunately, he's right; there are so many games coming out that adding something to your wishlist often feels like resigning it to remembering it exists six months later.

In a recent development, he recalls an example of someone making an Android port of Graveyard Keeper, which "barely fit into memory", and was surprised by how "easy" it seemed. But then, he clarifies that the reason it seemed so easy was a lack of care. "There's a reason that every kind of AI slop game feels the same; it's because when you try to make something from zero, AI will multiply your skills, and zero times a thousand is still zero." The people claiming to 'develop' recomps do not care for making a game that feels right, or even one that works; they care only for the short-lived dopamine of being popular on Twitter for fifteen minutes before another slop copy rears its head (and is inevitably revealed to be jankware). Nichiporchik specifies that "game feel is impossible to copy; as long as you have a great game feel and a great game loop, I think you're going to be okay." 'Game feel' is one of the vaguest terms that exists in this field, but I do feel that it refers to something tangible and immediately noticeable when it's gone.

Sources indicate that for example, I've recently been replaying the wonderful Celeste, and the 'game feel' of its wavedashing is core to making the practice of blazing through levels so engaging. The wavedash works the same every time, due to precise programming from Extremely OK Games spending time and effort painstakingly ensuring it did in the interest of consistency and unity in movement mechanics. You can't make good art without caring about the quality, even if there are now faster ways to knock together a 'working' game. To borrow an overused but still salient phrase: AI could never.
