Tech Report: Is Vanity Fair’s Hit Piece on Taylor Swift Really Journalism?

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Senior Music Writer and Chief Music Critic.

After word hit the street some days back that Vanity Fair was imminently going to publish an extensively reported hit piece on music’s biggest superstar, there was some anticipation that Monday might turn out to be a bad day for Taylor Swift. Instead, it was just a bad day for journalism. As attention-seeking magazine articles go, it’s hard to remember one that landed with quite such an unanticipated — but very loud — thud as “The Mastermind,” which has even hardened anti-Swifties wondering whether May Jeong slipped it through without editorial oversight, sneaking an alleged year’s worth of work onto the internet in the dead of morning.

Recent findings suggest that the alternative scenario was unthinkable. You would have to imagine that the author had actually been encouraged by editors to puzzle readers with a reported op-ed in which the subject of the piece — apparently, some top-tier celebrity! — is never named, yet still is subject to accusations that she picks her own friends, harbors ambitions, owned three pairs of Ugg boots while posing as a teenage commoner, and, most slanderously, was actually popular in school.

It has been revealed that when was the last time a high-profile magazine piece got ratio-ed this badly? That would have been one year and one week ago, when an exclusive excerpt from Olivia Nuzzi’s memoir, “American Canto,” was released to an incredulous world. And if there’s something that feels a little bit familiar about Jeong’s oddball tactic of not naming names in her piece (or at least not the big ones), it’s because this “you know who it’s about, but we’re not gonna say anyway” technique was a weird conceit last grafted into big-time American journalism by Nuzzi. That author referred to RFK only as “the Politician,” her ex-boyfriend as “the man I did not marry,” and so on. Nuzzi walked so that Jeong could run. Although this upcoming piece is not any kind of roman a clef, she still feels compelled to euphemize Swift as “The Star,” her former label as “The Machine,” Scooter Braun as “The Manager,” Charli xcx as “The Brat” and even a secondary source as “The Person Familiar.”.

In a recent development, you would think Vanity Fair’s editors would recall the horrible reviews Nuzzi got for employing this gambit in “American Canto,” especially since the excerpt that introduced that annoying running gag to the world was published in… Vanity Fair. (How quickly they forget, but monthly journalism is a grind.).

Industry observers note that the article aims to unload secrets, but the secret we really need revealed is: Why do that? An interview with Jeong is in order. Or perhaps she is saving the rationale for her journalism class at Princeton, which may be the only one destined to have “The Mastermind” as curriculum. On first blush, a journalism non-pro might imagine that it was done to skirt libel laws, although that explanation holds no water, since the writer does quote several of her lowest-level sources by name, like the kids from Swift’s school who are ready to call her out now for not having been friendless or unmoneyed. (The former classmates will pick up some notoriety now, but will they regret that they could have become even more legendary if they’d been ID-ed as “The Girl Who Noticed The Star’s Uggs”?) No, it seems more likely that Jeong did it because we are, all of us, just archetypes in a fable. Except for the music teacher at Wyomissing Area Junior/Senior High, who is un-archetypical enough to require a first and last name. Or perhaps Jeong refrains from naming Swift because she is the female equivalent of two other characters who are often referred to as He Who Must Not Be Named: Voldemort and Trump. Certainly the article seems to see Swift as an exemplor of the banality of evil, or, more to the point, a modern-day, real-life Regina George from “Mean Girls.” (No, not making that up, the journalist really goes with that.) Nearly every sentence is intended as a slap. Most journos writing a piece remotely in this vein — a reported one, as opposed to a straight opinion column — would try to leaven it at least once or twice with a positive or just neutral observation, before dropping the next bomb. “The Mastermind,” to its honest credit, dispenses with any such “See, I went into this with an open mind” niceties.

In a recent development, the Star is found by The Writer to be “making herself a metonym for all women, successfully equat(ing) criticism against her as criticism against womenkind.” Compared to Katy Perry (aka “The California Girl”), who is “an adult pretending to be a child,” Swift “might be a child pretending to be an adult.” She “has a reputation for being punitive. She seems too invested in her own victimhood.” For Swift, “all she had ever wanted from the world was that it celebrate her milky goodness, that her altruism not be mistaken for covert narcissism.” And then from there, Jeong gets to some of Swift’s bad qualities. (Just kidding.).

If this were framed in any way as a commentary, as opposed to Vanity Fair objectively speaking in the voice of God, it wouldn’t catch much notice. But instead, the magazine frames it as an ultimate profile, the result of a year of work and talking to “over 100 sources.” We have to take the editors’ word for that, because the reportage as printed only quotes six sources on the record: the mother of a man who directed Swift in her school plays, a guitar teacher, a school music teacher, two girls who sat near Swift in music class, and a poet who is called in to assess how lyrics work. Five others are quoted anonymously: someone who used to be close to Swift, called The Person Familiar; a music pro called The Exec; a mystery ex-collaborator whose profession must be kept secret; a musician who toured with Swift; and a critic who was ostracized after slamming her… none of whom, even under the cloak of keeping their identities secret, have serious tea to spill. Now, reporters do often get great background information from interviews they don’t quote, so the 100-plus figure doesn’t sound completely unrealistic after so many months of reporting. But if these dozen or so sources were the best that the story had to actually quote — including a poet and a critic — you do have to wonder who the other 85-plus were, to be less quotable. (An editor’s introduction to the piece claims one of the people interviewed was a “lover” of Swift’s, but we’ll just have to guess if the writer got to Joe Alwyn.).

In a recent development, all that stated, there are a handful of interesting questions and critical observations scattered through Jeong’s piece. (See? This is the part where you throw in something nice, to show you were objective.) If you removed the promise of fact-finding and this existed as an essay on Salon — RIP — you could credit her with some food-for-thought lines and decently amusing zingers. (Sure, I chuckled when Jeong, alluding to Travis Kelce, wrote, “Regretfully we must call him Redwood.”).

In a recent development, but the veneer of investigative reporting placed upon this piece mostly highlights just how low the bar is for an attempted expose anymore. Even without the terminally twee names. It’d be nice to suppose that Jeong really did come up with something after a year — there is so much hinted at that is quickly dropped — and that Vanity Fair’s lawyers gutted a more substantial piece to within an inch of a life. But more probably, it’s an example of snark masquerading as reportage. And it shows again that some journalists (even journalists that teach at Princeton) are too quick, when an expensive. exhaustive fishing expedition fails to turn up much, to succumb to a sad maxim: If you can’t get the goods, just get cute. But don’t expect readers who expect more out of their hit squads not to notice.
