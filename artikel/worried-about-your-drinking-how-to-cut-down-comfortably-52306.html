Worried About Your Drinking? How to Cut Down, Comfortably

How is your relationship with alcohol? Do you feel like you drink too much, perhaps vowing to cut down but slipping back into uncomfortable but familiar patterns? Many of us do. And that's hard, because alcohol is a socially acceptable drug, especially in Western societies. In the UK, you can buy it in supermarkets, grocery shops on every corner, in pubs, restaurants, and cafés. If you go to a party, alcohol is served. Or at dinner, people keep topping up your glass.

Alcohol is ubiquitous and hard to consume healthily, even with the best of intentions. Even if you're not alcohol-dependent, you may be uncomfortable about the amount you consume, but feel powerless to change that. Unfortunately, numerous studies show that regular drinking can have a major impact on your health, contributing to diseases including cancer, cardiovascular disease, dementia, and gastrointestinal diseases. It's clear that, in an ideal world, sobriety is the healthiest choice we can make. And if you do enjoy a glass of wine or beer, drinking in moderation is the next best option.

It has been revealed that if you would like to cut down, the first step is to understand why you drink too much. Let's start with the fact that alcohol tastes and feels good. If you've had a long day, a glass or two of wine helps you relax, gives you a warm, fuzzy feeling, and helps you transition from work to downtime. These transitions can be tricky for us, and we may use substances to help—for example, the morning coffee that provides the energy boost we need to transition from sleepiness to an alert, ready-to-work mode.

In a recent development, so, like other oft-consumed substances, alcohol feels good and helps us transition from one state to another. Another factor in our alcohol consumption is peer pressure. In my country, this starts as a teenager, when friends encourage you to drink excessively to be "cool" or "fun." This pressure continues at university, where heavy drinking is often socially sanctioned and encouraged.

Even in middle age and later years, having a beer in pubs or half a bottle of wine with dinner is seen as normal and desirable. Opting not to drink, although more and more common, can be tricky if your friends are on the boozier side. Although sobriety is increasingly embraced by the younger generation, people of my age (approaching 60) have grown up with alcohol, and opting for sobriety, or occasional drinking, is an effortful, often socially frowned-upon choice.

Industry observers note that viewed through an internal family systems (IFS) lens, there is another pressure to drink, coming from inside your head. In IFS, drinking to excess, or in any way compulsively, is due to the actions of a protective part known as a "firefighter." This part's sole purpose is to extinguish the flames of pain that another—young, vulnerable—part might be struggling with. Firefighters are reactive and impulsive and have no sense of consequences—if you drink a whole bottle of wine, you may feel awful in the morning, but if it helps you feel less stressed, anxious, or lonely in the moment, the part's work is done.

Recent findings suggest that although the strategy of these firefighting parts can be terrible (they are also the parts that get people addicted to opioids, use pornography, gamble, and do all kinds of risky, self-destructive behaviour), they mean well. This cycle—a hurt, young part getting triggered and a protector urgently getting rid of the pain—is called an "addictive process" in IFS. And the same basic process applies, whether you are using substances or activities to numb, avoid, or distract yourself from painful feelings.

Industry observers note that this can be hard to understand, so let's use Grace* as an example. Grace was a middle-aged white woman who came to see me for help with her drinking. She was a successful marketing executive with a young family, so she could ill afford the regular hangovers that blighted her life. "Every Monday morning I wake up with a pounding headache and a cold sweat," she told me in our first session. "I always overdo it on Sunday nights, because I'm so stressed out about going back to work. Then it's a bottle or more of wine, which helps me de-stress but of course makes the next day a nightmare.".

According to recent reports, one of our early tasks was to translate Grace's suffering into parts language. I explained that she had a young part, Little Grace, who found her high-pressure job extremely stressful. We later learned that it also triggered memories of being under intense pressure from her high-flying, academic parents, making school and exams hellish for her. When this stressed little girl got triggered by the mammoth to-do list waiting for her on Monday morning, a firefighter stepped in to wash away all that stress with way too much Chardonnay.

Sources indicate that one of the many ways in which IFS requires a paradigm shift from other therapies is that, in order to help Grace drink in moderation, we had to work with the wine-drinking firefighter, not try to get rid of it (the well-meaning approach of 12-step programmes). When I spoke to this part, I learned that it had developed when Grace was a child, getting her to eat sweets and zone out in front of the TV after a day of grinding through her studies, while Grace's parents criticised her harshly for any mistakes. This showed us that, like all protective parts, this one meant well, even if it was causing chaos in her life.

According to recent reports, we got permission from the firefighter to help Little Grace, which we did using IFS's "healing steps"—a roadmap for connecting with, comforting, and ultimately healing hurt young parts like this. We transported her from the past to live with adult Grace in the present, joining her loving, rambunctious family, which made her very happy. Only then could we work with the firefighter on changing their approach, which they gladly did. After much debate about becoming entirely sober or cutting down, Grace chose the latter, so we came up with a plan to only drink on Friday and Saturday with dinner, and nothing on Sunday or during the week.

In a recent development, because all her parts had bought into this strategy, Grace could stick to it with relative ease, unlike the get sober/relapse/get sober again cycle common to many models for treating addiction. She ended therapy feeling optimistic about her future and with all the skills she needed to keep checking in on both Little Grace and the drinking part, to make sure they were OK, especially when she had a wobble and felt like slipping back into her old patterns.

It's always possible to heal early wounds and change these painkilling habits, so don't suffer in silence—life is too short to waste feeling bad about yourself and your choices.
