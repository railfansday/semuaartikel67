The Scaling Problem No One Talks About in Education: Who Checks the Teacher?

I remember when we started discussing a major venture round to open up 30 upcoming children’s clubs a year, one investor’s question stuck with me: “How will you keep the quality of teaching the same in location number 30 as in location number one?”.

According to recent reports, frankly, he asked me the right question. In education, scale is rarely limited by capital, real estate, or demand. It’s instead limited by the one thing that is hardest to copy: what actually happens between a teacher and a group of children when no one is checking?

Industry observers note that most education networks control quality the way they did 30 years ago. A supervisor visits the site, sits in on a class, and takes notes. Then, someone else watches a recording later. It works at three locations, but this could be broken at the last branch.

Recent findings suggest that let me explain this using GMNSM as an example. Take 10 locations, with four groups a day at each, six days a week. At 30 minutes of review per session, that adds up to 480 review hours over four weeks. Using our own staffing costs for manual reviews, I estimate that would cost about US$6,100. The estimated workload grows with every club you add. Sampling keeps that workload down, but it leaves most lessons unseen.

The lesson for education is straightforward: if you can only inspect a sliver of what happens, you are not managing quality, but guessing at it.

Speech AI has become good enough, and cheap enough, to turn a lesson recording into something a manager can act on. The research is also catching up. In a pre-registered randomized controlled trial with 224 Utah mathematics and science teachers, automated feedback on classroom audio increased teachers’ use of focusing questions by 20% compared with a control group.

Sources indicate that commercial tools are following now as well. Coverage in Education Next describes apps that separate teacher and student speech, and reports talk time and wait time, “like a Fitbit for instruction.” However, the same piece also raises the hard question: what happens when this data moves from private reflection into a formal evaluation?

At GMNSM, we built Allim AI, an internal system to automate this quality loop, and the process was more instructive than the technology.

It has been revealed that the idea is simple. You give the model your methodology – values, annual programme, lesson structure, and expected outcomes. The system reviews the coach’s speech in the discussion after practice, not the training session itself. It checks which questions the coach asked against the lesson plan and flags moments that may warrant human review. If the lesson is about building confidence, we ask: “did the coach discuss the assigned video, ask the required questions, summarize the takeaways, and help children apply them to real life?”.

The output is a score and practical coaching notes: fewer filler words, less judgemental language, and more room for different perspectives instead of one “correct” answer.

Three design decisions mattered more to us than the model itself.

Industry observers note that not everyone welcomed this in GMNSM. Some colleagues left because mandatory microphones felt like we didn’t trust them. I understand their reaction, and I would even be suspicious of any founder who dismisses it.

The wider data notes this is not an edge case. Gallup research found that only 20% of employees who work on a computer or digital device have a great deal of trust in their leaders to use employee data responsibly. A 2025 ExpressVPN survey found that one in six employees had considered quitting over monitoring they considered invasive. As one expert put it in a recent HR Daily Advisor-style analysis of AI that detects toxic managers, the same tool can look like support in a high-trust organization and “Substantial Brother” in a low-trust one.

So, my practical advice to other operators is this: treat it as a development tool first. If staff only ever see it as a scoreboard, they’ll either game it or leave. If it gives them specific, private, useful feedback, many will start using it the way it’s intended to be used. Tell people what gets recorded, why, and who sees it before it goes out. Also, accept that some people will opt out. When we did this, we explained the purpose to some colleagues and parted ways with others, which we respect.

Recording children’s classes raises obvious data-protection questions, and in Europe, the rulebook is tightening. Under the EU AI Act, certain uses of AI in education and employment can be classified as high-risk, depending on the system’s intended purpose. Not every use falls into that category, but the direction of travel is clear: the more an AI system influences decisions about people, the more scrutiny it should expect.

Sources indicate that whatever the legal classification, the practical principles are the same. Disclose what is recorded and who can access it. Minimize the data you collect. Keep people, not algorithms, responsible for decisions. These practices help earn the trust that makes feedback at scale possible.

Finally, AI will not fix a weak methodology, and it cannot replace a coach who genuinely cares about children. What it can do is make quality visible, consistently and affordably, so the lesson in location number 30 is no more of an accident than the lesson in location number one.

Andrej Kuprejev is the founder and CEO of GMNSM, a Dubai-based martial arts and holistic education network, and the founder of Allim.AI, the AI platform that supports coaching quality across its academies. A Lithuania-born former competitive judo and jiu-jitsu athlete, he founded GMNSM in 2022 to combine physical training with soft-skills development for children. It now runs 11 academies across the UAE, Qatar and Cyprus.
