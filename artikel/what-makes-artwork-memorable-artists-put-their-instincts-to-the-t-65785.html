What makes artwork memorable? Artists put their instincts to the test

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Artists found that making forgettable artwork was harder than making memorable pieces, while AI predicted viewers' memories better.

Could you make a painting that someone would look at and forget minutes later?

In an art contest, researchers gave artists a choice: Create something viewers would remember or something they would rapidly forget.

In a recognition test, entries meant to be memorable were recognized more often than entries meant to be forgettable.

The memorable entries also came closer to the researchers’ target for their category.

A computer model called ResMem, trained on photographs, was better than artists and a separate group of raters at sorting the most- and least-remembered artworks.

Trent Davis and Yifei Chen carried out the study together with Wilma Bainbridge of the University of Chicago.

Bainbridge leads the Brain Bridge Lab there and is an associate professor in the Department of Psychology.

Memory was tested within minutes of the art having been seen in every part of the study.

For three months the art contest remained open to adult artists who lived in the continental United States.

Each of them was permitted to enter a single piece and had to choose a category for it. Memorable and forgettable were the two categories on offer and an artist could not enter both.

Industry observers note that the organizers accepted only flat physical works, of which paintings, drawings, and collages are examples. Digital art and photography had been excluded under the rules that the team had drawn up.

Recent findings suggest that in all, 144 submissions were received from 20 states and 86 of them met the rules. Of those 86 artworks, 62 had been entered as memorable and the other 24 as forgettable.

The first memory test was completed online by a group of 119 adults who were living in the United States. All 86 of the artworks were presented to these viewers one after another in a single long stream.

Some of the artworks were shown for a second time once at least 30 seconds had passed. The viewers had been instructed to press a key whenever they believed that an artwork had appeared before.

A score for every artwork in the contest was then worked out by the team from those key presses.

Mean scores of 0.86 and 0.79 out of 1 were obtained for memorable and forgettable entries respectively. Although modest in size, this difference was unlikely to be attributable to chance.

A high target score had been set by the team for memorable entries and a low one for forgettable entries.

It was found that the memorable entries had come closer to their target than the forgettable entries had.

The team then selected the works that had been remembered best and worst by the online viewers.

Thirty-nine of those works were shown for a month at Connect Gallery in the Hyde Park neighborhood of Chicago.

In a recent development, sixty-two visitors walked through the show and were free to look at the works in whatever order they wished. They had been informed that memory was the theme of the show but not that a test would follow.

Recognition was assessed afterward in a separate room where artworks were presented to the visitors on their own phones.

A mean of 89% of the tested visitors recognized any given piece from the best-remembered group. The corresponding proportion for pieces from the least-remembered group was 70% of the visitors.

Industry observers note that bainbridge called the show a risky experiment for the lab. Visitors to a small exhibition about memory had been given a reason to try to remember each of the pieces.

Works from the two groups had for the most part been hung on opposite walls of the gallery.

The test was therefore repeated with 141 people at a vision science conference where paper prints were mixed together.

The works remembered best online were again recognized more often than the works that had been remembered least.

According to recent reports, the artworks were also rated on 21 qualities by a separate group of 892 people. One of those qualities was the degree to which each artwork seemed memorable to the person rating it.

The works described as memorable by these raters were not reliably the ones remembered by online viewers. It was the goals chosen by the artists that the ratings were instead found to match.

Higher memorability ratings were given to art that people found complex, surprising, interesting, or likely to become famous.

The works that viewers tended to remember were those that raters felt able to rebuild easily in the mind. Works in which a person was shown were also remembered better whereas landscapes were remembered worse.

Works that looked digitally made were remembered better too. Every entry in the contest had nevertheless been made by hand.

“What people think is memorable is different from what actually is,” Chen said.

ResMem is a neural network, which is a kind of AI program that learns from examples. It was built in Bainbridge’s lab and was trained on nearly 70,000 photographs.

The model takes in an image and returns a score that highlights how likely people are to remember it.

In an earlier study, memory for more than 4,000 paintings from the Art Institute of Chicago was predicted by ResMem.

Recent findings suggest that the researchers compared three ways of predicting which artworks people would remember. They looked at the 20 most remembered and the 20 least remembered pieces.

The AI model, ResMem, correctly sorted 80% of the 40 artworks. The artists’ own choices were correct 67.5% of the time.

The separate group’s ratings correctly classified 62.5% of the artworks.

According to recent reports, resMem also predicted which artworks gallery visitors would remember. People’s ratings could not do this reliably.

The researchers think the AI model may perform better because it does not judge art the way people do. For example, people may assume that interesting or surprising art is easier to remember, even when that is not true.

The study did not explain why making memorable art was easier than making forgettable art.

It has been revealed that researchers think memorable images may share certain features that catch people’s attention. Forgettable images may not have anything in common, making them harder to create on purpose.

Artists chose whether to enter the contest and which category to compete in. Some had taught themselves to make art, while others had master’s degrees in fine arts.

Neither education nor professional experience clearly affected their success. However, the researchers had only basic information about their training.

Gallery visitors may also have been more interested in art than the people who took the online test.

The researchers want to study more highly trained artists. They also suggest tracking where people look in a gallery to better understand which artworks stay in their memory.

The full study was published in the journal Psychological Science.
