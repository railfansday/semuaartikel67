Tech Report: Why Do I Have to Do Everything?

  • Sometimes these differences create a power imbalance.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Posted November 20, 2026 | Reviewed by Davia Sills.

Every relationship has its own way of giving and receiving. Partners contribute their money, time, emotional and logistical support, and domestic labor, among other activities and resources. Both give, and both take. But it’s rare that both people contribute equally in all these domains. In other words, in most categories, one person is doing more than the other. Often, one partner ends up feeling angry that they are “doing it all” in the sphere of their focus.

Sometimes these differences create a power imbalance. For example, if one person earns significantly more than the other, this can subtly influence how the couple makes a large financial decision. The person who earns less may feel less entitled to their opinions, needs, or wants. They may feel less autonomy. They may also feel an obligation to reciprocate in other ways, even if it’s unspoken. As for the person who contributes more, their role may create a feeling of burden or over-responsibility. This can lead to feelings of resentment or burnout.

It has been revealed that people in intimate relationships may try to rectify or avoid these pitfalls by attempting to make every contribution equal. But given that equal contribution in all spheres is virtually impossible, perhaps avoiding pitfalls needs to entail something other than that. Perhaps the focus needs to be on seeing the system as a whole and evaluating the contribution each makes within it. In other words, one person may earn more, but the other may provide more logistical support or domestic labor. In this way, there may be differences in the spheres of contribution, but as a whole, each partner may feel the arrangement is fair.

Furthermore, it’s crucial that regardless of how much each person contributes, both partners’ decision-making authority, financial independence, and freedom from indebtedness be protected. In other words, just because one partner spends more time on child-rearing, it doesn’t mean the other shouldn’t have an equal say in where that child attends school. And just because one partner earns more money doesn’t mean they should have more authority over how the couple spends it. Of course, the person who carries more responsibility in a particular area may have more expertise or experience, and that deserves consideration. But having more expertise isn’t the same as having more power.

It has been revealed that equality in romantic relationships doesn’t necessarily mean contributing equal amounts of money, time, or labor. It means ensuring that differences in contribution don’t become differences in autonomy, dignity, or power. The goal isn’t to contribute equally to a relationship, but to be aware of all the contributions in all aspects of a shared life. And to recognize that fairness doesn’t necessarily mean equality of contribution. What matters is making sure neither partner’s contribution gives them more ownership of the other person’s life or shared life decisions.
