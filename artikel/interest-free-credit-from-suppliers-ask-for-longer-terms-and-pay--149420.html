Interest-Free Credit From Suppliers – Ask for Longer Terms and Pay on the Due Date

🌐 Source: Moneycrashers • 📅 26 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 2 min read (497 words, 📖 Standard)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Supplier payment terms are credit that charges no interest, and every extra day you get keeps cash in your business. Once you’ve paid a supplier on time for a few months, ask for 30 to 60 days to pay. Then pay each bill on its due date, not early, unless the supplier offers a discount for paying sooner.

Paying suppliers on delivery, or paying each invoice the day it arrives, hands over cash that terms would let you keep for weeks. That cash could be covering payroll, rent, or the next order.

It has been revealed that terms like net 30 give you 30 days after the invoice date to pay the full bill, with no interest. Take a gift shop that buys about $12,000 a month from its two biggest suppliers and pays on delivery. Moving both to net 45 means it pays for each order a month and a half later, so about $18,000 more stays in its account going into the holiday season.

Ask for longer terms after a few months of paying a supplier on time, since that record is your best argument. Go from paying on delivery to net 30, or from net 30 to net 45 or net 60. Better still, ask for terms that match how long your own customers take to pay you, so their payments come in by the time your bills come due.

In a recent development, then pay on the due date, not before and never after, with each payment scheduled in your bank’s bill pay to arrive that day. Many suppliers report how you pay to Dun & Bradstreet, whose PAYDEX business credit score tracks how promptly you pay them. A late payment can pull that score down, and it gives a supplier reason to shorten your terms, not lengthen them.

Recent findings suggest that the exception is a discount for paying early. Terms of 2/10 net 30 take 2% off a bill paid within 10 days and otherwise give you 30 days to pay it in full. Saving 2% for paying 20 days sooner is like earning about 37% a year on that money, far more than a savings account pays, so take the discount whenever you have the cash to spare. Offering your own customers an early-payment discount turns that 37% into a cost you pay.

Start with the supplier you pay the most, since every extra 30 days of terms keeps about one month’s worth of its bills in your account.
