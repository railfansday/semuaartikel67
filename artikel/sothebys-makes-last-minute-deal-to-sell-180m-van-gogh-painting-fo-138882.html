Sotheby’s makes last-minute deal to sell $180m Van Gogh painting following dispute

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that sotheby’s today revealed that a settlement has been reached over the sale of Vincent van Gogh’s Blossoming Chestnut Trees (Châtaigniers en fleurs) (May 1890). The work has been poised to achieve a record price for the artist but became the subject of a claim by the heirs of its Jewish former owners, who called for the work to be withdrawn from the 18 November auction in Newly introduced York.

The Sotheby’s statement records that the auction house is “pleased to confirm that an agreement has been reached between the Blaquier-Arrieta family and all of the legal descendants of Hedwig and Samuel Fischer regarding the sale of Vincent van Gogh’s Châtaigniers en fleurs”. The sale will now proceed as planned.

The work is estimated at around $180m ($207m with fees) and has been guaranteed by Sotheby's to sell. This is far in excess of the previous record of $117m, paid at Christie’s in 2022 for Van Gogh’s Orchard with Cypresses (April 1888).

In a recent development, the Blaquier-Arrieta family, from Argentina, bought Blossoming Chestnut Trees in 1975. As the Sotheby’s statement explains: “In recognition of the painting’s complex Nazi-era history and the suffering endured by the Fischer family during the Holocaust, the two families have reached a mutually satisfactory resolution and the work will be sold pursuant to their agreement.”.

The provenance in the Sotheby’s catalogue entry notes, as it has done since last month, that the painting was acquired by the Berlin publisher Samuel Fischer and his wife Hedwig in 1909. Their son-in-law, Gottfried Bermann Fischer, deposited the Van Gogh with the Amsterdam gallery Paul Cassirer in 1932 or 1933—and it was then bought by Paris collectors Henriette and Marcel Kapferer in 1936.

In a recent development, last Thursday (12 November), in a surprise move, the Berlin-based restitution researcher Gunnar Schnabel sent a letter to Sotheby’s, on behalf of the Fischer heirs. He questioned the 1936 sale—which he states took place three months after the family had left Berlin for Vienna—asking Sotheby’s to prove that the picture had been sold at its market value and that its sale was not forced. In the meantime, Schnabel asked for this week’s sale to be delayed.

In a statement, the Fischer heirs commented: “We are grateful for the trusting and fair negotiation and collaboration with Sotheby’s and the Blaquier-Arrieta family, which make it possible to acknowledge the suffering of the Fischer family during the Nazi era and honour Gisela Berman Fischer's [granddaughter of Samuel and Hedwig] history of seeking justice for her family.”.

According to recent reports, the Blaquier-Arrieta family commented: “We are pleased to have reached an amicable resolution with the Fischer heirs. We appreciate the constructive dialogue that made this resolution possible and look forward to an extraordinary result and to passing along this masterpiece to a latest generation of collectors.”.

No details have been released on the nature of the agreement and whether the Fischer family will receive financial compensation.

What impact, if any, the short-lived dispute will have on the sale will only become apparent with the bidding.
