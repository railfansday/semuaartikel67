Industry News: Plug-in Solar UL Certification for US Safety Regs — What UL 3700 Does & Doesn’t Say

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Millions of renters, apartment residents, and condo owners can’t put solar on a roof they don’t control. Plug-in solar, also called balcony solar or plug-in PV, offers a workaround: one or more panels connected through a microinverter to an ordinary wall outlet, with no roof work and, in a growing number of states, no utility interconnection application — though, this is only applicable in select states (users should refer to their state laws before installation). A new safety standard, UL 3700, is helping this market mature, and it comes with a lesson every buyer should learn: a compliant component is not the same thing as a certified system.

Rooftop solar assumes you own a roof, can afford a large upfront cost, and can wait through permits and installation. Plug-in solar flips that. States are responding. As of mid-October, ten states had enacted plug-in solar laws: Utah, Maine, Virginia, Colorado, Maryland, Upcoming Hampshire, Connecticut, Vermont, New Jersey, and California. Effective dates vary, with several laws not taking effect until 2027, and New York’s SUNNY Act is awaiting the governor’s signature. Several of these laws require kits to meet a recognized safety standard, and some name UL 3700.

UL 3700, the Outline of Investigation for Interactive Plug-In Photovoltaic Equipment and Systems, was published in December 2025, and UL Solutions began certifying products to it in January 2026. It exists because rooftop solar standards weren’t written for equipment that homeowners plug in themselves. The safety problems are specific:.

Hoymiles states the HiFlow Pro was built around these challenges and shuts down in 135 milliseconds if it’s unplugged or the grid fails. For comparison, Maine’s law requires shutoff within 0.2 seconds.

Here is the part that matters most for buyers. UL 3700 evaluates a complete system: the microinverter, how it connects, relevant accessories, and the overall configuration, not the microinverter alone. That means compliance for one part doesn’t automatically establish that the whole kit has been certified.

It has been revealed that the HiFlow Pro indicates how this works in practice. Hoymiles describes the microinverter itself as UL 3700-compliant, and it has been widely reported as the first plug-in microinverter in the US to reach that point after its July 15, 2026 launch. The complete HiFlow Pro system has not yet finished certification. Hoymiles notes it is pursuing it and expects the process to wrap up in December 2026.

Industry observers note that the HiFlow Pro is a plug-in microinverter, so the panels are bought separately or as part of a kit. Per Hoymiles, up to four units can run on separate outlets for up to 1,200 watts, depending on state and local rules, and models range from 360 W to 500 W to fit different legal limits. The company lists a peak efficiency of 95.5%, a NEMA Type 6 enclosure, an operating range of -40°F to 149°F, a 12-year standard warranty, and monitoring through the S-Miles Home app. It also cites 250,000 hours of reliability testing, and says TIME named the product one of its Best Inventions of 2026.

According to recent reports, follow the manufacturer’s instructions. Plug the unit directly into a wall outlet, not a power strip or extension cord, and use a GFCI outlet if you’re running a larger system on one circuit.

Hoymiles estimates up to 721 kWh a year from a single 360 W unit, modeled at 5.6 peak sun hours a day and about 2,403 kWh from a four-unit system. At 38 cents per kWh, a rate found only in the highest-cost regions, the tech giant estimates savings of up to about $247 per unit per year and a payback of roughly two years. By our math, that figure assumes about 90% of the power gets used inside the home. Actual savings depend on how much electricity you use during daylight hours, and surplus power may flow to the grid without credit unless your utility offers net metering.

Recent findings suggest that for context, EnergySage puts typical savings from a plug-in kit at $13 to $20 a month at the national average rate of about 18 cents per kWh, and $40 to $50 a month in high-rate states like California, New York, and Hawaii. Two limits are worth knowing: the system reduces your bill but doesn’t replace the grid, and it won’t provide backup power in an outage. The federal homeowner solar tax credit also ended on December 31, 2025, so payback estimates shouldn’t assume one.

Sources indicate that rules vary by state, and sometimes by utility. Hawaii, with some of the country’s highest electricity prices, isn’t among the ten states with a plug-in solar law, so renters and condo owners here should check with Hawaiian Electric and their association before buying. Even in states with laws, utilities may ask for notice, and landlord and HOA protections differ.
