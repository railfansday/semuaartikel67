Latest Update: World of Warcraft: Forever isn't mere nostalgia bait - it's rebuilding the MMO social architecture that Blizzard spent years dismantling

According to recent reports, I don't play video games regularly anymore. Not because I've lost interest in them, but because I no longer make the time, the premium on which has seemed to exponentiate with age. I realised, however, in 2019 that I was a kind of sleeper agent: whenever I heard the opening notes of Legends of Azeroth, the original World of Warcraft login screen music, I would feel the reactivation of a latent part of my mind laid down when I was 10 years old. And so, like a form of possession, whenever Blizzard announces a new legacy iteration of the original WoW, no downpayment in time nor money is too great to prevent me from returning.

Here I am, then, at the launch of World of Warcraft: Forever, seven years after Blizzard first revived the original 2004 game with World of Warcraft Classic in 2019. Once more I set foot on Durotar, to spend as much time as I can levelling up a upcoming Orc Warrior before I am forcibly unplugged by either my boss or, more likely, my wife, and so far it's been a welcome return to some of the frictional - but more involving and consequential - socialising that WoW's decades of updates had previously sanded down.

According to recent reports, my relationship with Azeroth began in 2007, when I was ten years old. As soon as I inserted the CD-ROM, I knew that I was going to have to vastly increase my timeshare on the family desktop. For the next six years, school attendance, sleep, and quality family time suffered immensely as I battled to balance my latest parallel existence as a member of the Orcish Horde.

Industry observers note that it's unremarkable now, but the fact that characters created at WoW's launch are now 22 years old and can still be played in the latest iteration of the game would have been almost inconceivable to many developers at the turn of the century. In the late 1990s and early 2000s, the video game industry was overwhelmingly organised around discrete releases expected to have relatively short lives. For most games, commercial success was concentrated in a brief period surrounding their launch, after which sales declined and eventually ceased. WoW's extraordinary success, and exceedingly long half-life, demonstrated the commercial possibilities and durability of a different model: an evolving, immersive digital environment that players paid to inhabit, year after year. Rather than simply purchasing a finished product, players entered a world that would continue to grow around them. Upcoming players, after buying the game (and then its subsequent expansions) and paying for a subscription, could begin their adventures in the same world as those who had been there from the beginning, making their way through its ever-expanding geography and history.

The persistence of Azeroth has been the main driver of its abiding allure for me. I have returned to the game time after time, often just to be enveloped in an audiovisual environment that I know so intimately, and which was so formative in my life. But as the WoW universe was continually developed, the game became increasingly unrecognisable from the version I skipped school to play on Windows XP.

Sources indicate that what I wanted from the game had also changed. In a turn that would have been completely anathema to my teenage self, I no longer cared about reaching the endgame or having the most competitive character. I stopped caring about the latest raiding content or PvP rankings. Instead, the elements of the game I had once feverishly tried to skip over, like levelling, professions and story, became, ironically, the only parts of the game that I yearned for.

It has been revealed that as time unspooled, WoW naturally needed updates to stay fresh. This meant overhauling many of the unwieldy elements of its original design. Much of this was described as 'streamlining' the player experience, making the game less tedious by reducing downtime and, most critically, reliance on other players.

Recent findings suggest that the original Azeroth emulated the real world in ways that few contemporary games do. Firstly, it was openly unbalanced, with some classes vastly stronger than others, some of which were borderline unplayable. Secondly, it required extraordinary amounts of patience and downtime to navigate. Most importantly, though, it required collaboration with others to get the most out of it. In order to access the latest content, players would have to self-organise into specifically composed groups as big as 40 players large, and schedule lengthy chunks of time each week to make progress.

It has been revealed that over time, much of the original social architecture that forced player collaboration was stripped from the game. Adventurers no longer needed to search for and group up with other players from their own server to complete rewarding content. Automated matchmaking systems increasingly did the work for them.

I remember many late nights when I was prevented from doing something quite modest because there were simply not enough players online, or not enough of the right classes. With my ambitions frustrated, I would often have no choice but to log off and try again in the morning. These frustrations were, I only learnt in retrospect, an notable byproduct of inhabiting a world that did not exist solely for my benefit. Other people had their own schedules, priorities and ambitions, and my experience of the game depended on those contingencies.

The changes to WoW followed the trajectory of the wider online gaming industry, where players could increasingly be matched instantly with others at any time of day. These were changes that genuinely respected players' time, but the texture and immersion of the game world paid a hefty price. In making the game more convenient, Blizzard had also made it less necessary, and in some respects less possible, to participate in the social world that had once made Azeroth feel so alive.

It has been revealed that it became increasingly clear that there was a subset of players, like me, who weren't interested in seeing an old dog learn new tricks, and just wanted to spend time with the old girl we'd raised from a pup. Even if she did walk slowly, and was a bit blind.

Industry observers note that as the years went on, many players moved on with their lives, even if they continued to carry some indelible residue of Azeroth with them. Others created unofficial instances of the original game, known as private servers, and relived those infant years with a knowledge of the world that they could scarcely have imagined possessing when they first entered it. As these servers became increasingly popular, Blizzard, originally very reluctant, eventually introduced Classic in 2019, an official revival of the game in something approaching its original 2004 form. Since then, legacy versions of WoW have become popular enough to exist alongside the modern game as a major branch of the franchise.

According to recent reports, re-releasing these legacy versions has been a considerable commercial success. But it also exposed a problem with the project of preservation. The original game, because it is so timeworn, has effectively been 'solved.' Players know almost everything about it, including its most obscure idiosyncrasies, and have found ingenious ways of exploiting them. The uncertainty that once made Azeroth feel so vast and mysterious has been replaced by an extraordinary collective expertise. When newly introduced content was added to the game, even the best guilds would spend weeks making no progress. During those early years, players at Azeroth's frontiers had to innovate to find creative ways of tackling novel and seemingly intractable problems.

There are clear limits to simulating a turning back of the clock. Players like me slowly realised that we didn't simply want to return to an earlier, simpler period in Azeroth. We wanted something impossible. Like the characters in Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind, we wanted to forget what we already knew so that we might experience our birth in Azeroth for the first time all over again.

WoW: Forever is an attempt to solve this problem. Like Old School RuneScape, Forever's undeniable forerunner and a considerable success in its own right, it is an expanded re-release of the original game that imagines an alternate future for Azeroth: one in which the game continues to evolve without abandoning the original texture and mechanics, and one that preserves the social architecture that made it so enriching in the first place.

As I write this, my Orc Warrior is sitting at a Campfire, a new mechanic that allows players to establish communal campsites and contribute profession-specific items that confer temporary bonuses to themselves and others. To receive a benefit from Campfires, adventurers are forced to sit together, a deliberate attempt from Blizzard to encourage interactions between players. It is too early to tell how effective this socialising mechanism will be in the long run, but nearly everyone in my experience so far has been eager to shoot the breeze together. I have already shared a number of conversations with other players, most of whom are thirty- or forty-somethings on a similar nostalgia quest as me.

I am playing the game on a laptop using a trackpad, caring very little about efficient or optimal progression. Although I suspect a substantial proportion of the player base shares my approach, not everyone has approached the game so casually. At least five players have already reached maximum level, and many others will have meticulously theory-crafted prepares designed to skip straight to the endgame. This is a good thing, and the game should be able to support players with different goals. It's important that the game rewards time and effort, but I hope that the modest amount of time I am able to commit will allow me to feel like I am on my own meaningful adventure, and not a weak player who is trailing behind.

Many in the community have come to believe, perhaps wishfully, that Forever might escape the trap of vertical progression, in which each latest wave of content supersedes and renders older content redundant. Instead, the promise of horizontal progression, where new content expands the range of meaningful activities and rewards available without continually raising the ceiling of character power, has been latched on to by members of the community, even though Blizzard's messaging in this regard has not been totally clear.

For players like me, the appeal of horizontal progression is that Azeroth might once again become a place to inhabit, rather than a race to finish. I want to be able to wander off, pursue some obscure profession, or spend an evening doing very little of consequence without feeling that the rest of the world has left me behind. I know I don't have the time to commit to the endgame hamster wheel, and whether Forever will produce a gameworld that meaningfully accommodates players with such different ambitions remains to be seen. But for now, at least, it feels good to be home.
