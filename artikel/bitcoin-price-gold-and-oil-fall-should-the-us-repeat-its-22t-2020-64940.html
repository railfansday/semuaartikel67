In a recent development, bitcoin price is getting hammered in the aftermath of the FOMC minutes. Gold isn’t doing much better; oil and stocks have joined the sell-off. The markets are already nervous about the Federal Reserve’s next decision, and yesterday’s FOMC minutes gave us another reason to worry: policymakers are still talking about raising interest rates.

Almost all officials saw further hikes as appropriate under their baseline outlook. Two even floated the possibility of a bigger increase if inflation refuses to cool.

According to recent reports, this is a nasty combination for markets. Bitcoin fell around 3.2% over 24 hours, gold futures dropped more than 3%, and WTI crude lost around 3%. The dollar climbed to a 19-month high. Wall Street was mixed, though, with the Nasdaq finishing higher while the Dow and S&P 500 slipped.

Here’s the comparison. In March 2020, the Fed was slashing rates, buying bonds and throwing emergency liquidity at a financial system under immense pressure. Congress approved a $2.2 trillion rescue package to keep households and businesses afloat.

Today, we are watching markets fall while the Fed is possibly making money more expensive.

Sources indicate that october’s FOMC minutes weren’t exactly what markets wanted to hear. The Fed held rates at 3.75%–4% during its October meeting, but almost all participants believed further increases would likely be appropriate. Two officials also said a hike larger than the usual 25 basis points could be considered if inflation data came in hotter than expected.

Industry observers note that and there was more in those minutes than just interest rates. Officials raised concerns about hedge fund leverage and the growing debt tied to artificial intelligence investments. Some financing arrangements could become a problem if investors start questioning enormous AI spending, and whether it will generate the returns companies expect.

In a recent development, the risk is forced selling. Leveraged funds facing losses or investor withdrawals may have to dump assets rapidly, potentially pushing prices down further.

It has been revealed that gold has its own problem. A stronger dollar and higher interest rates make it less attractive to investors who can earn a return elsewhere. Bitcoin price, meanwhile, remains vulnerable when traders cut exposure to speculative assets.

The Fed hasn’t declared a financial emergency. Its minutes actually describe the banking system as resilient. But officials are clearly paying attention to risks that could amplify a market shock.

In a recent development, march 2020 was brutal. COVID-19 lockdowns brought businesses to a standstill. Investors rushed for cash, stocks collapsed, and Bitcoin briefly slipped below $4,000 after sitting near $10,000 just weeks earlier.

Industry observers note that the Federal Reserve didn’t wait around. It cut rates by 50 basis points on March 3, then delivered another 100-basis-point cut on March 15. The federal funds target range dropped to 0%–0.25%.

Recent findings suggest that on March 23, the Fed unveiled unlimited purchases of Treasury securities and mortgage-backed securities, promising to buy whatever amounts were necessary to support market functioning. It also introduced emergency lending programmes to keep credit flowing.

Recent findings suggest that president Donald Trump signed the $2.2 trillion CARES Act on March 27. The package included direct payments to eligible Americans, expanded unemployment benefits, and assistance for businesses struggling to survive lockdowns. The Paycheck Protection Program gave small businesses access to loans designed to help them keep employees on payroll.

The response was enormous, but the economy was facing an extraordinary shutdown. Millions of Americans had lost their jobs, businesses were closing, and normal economic activity had practically disappeared.

Markets eventually bounced back. The S&P 500 recovered its pre-pandemic record by August 2020. Bitcoin climbed above $12,000 during the same month.

The current US economy isn’t in the same position as it was during the pandemic. Inflation remains above the Fed’s 2% target, and policymakers are discussing rate increases rather than cuts.

Slashing rates to zero and flooding markets with liquidity might help asset prices. It could also make inflation worse. Another multitrillion-dollar spending package would face an entirely different argument in Congress, especially without a pandemic-style economic shutdown.

The Fed doesn’t have to choose between doing nothing and repeating the 2020 rescue. If banks struggle to fund themselves or credit markets start freezing, it can step in with emergency lending and liquidity operations. That’s a much narrower response than another round of unlimited bond purchases.

The Fed’s concerns about leveraged hedge funds and AI financing could become more relevant if investors start selling indiscriminately. A market correction is one thing. Forced selling spreading through the financial system is another.

In a recent development, at the moment, there’s no evidence that we’re facing another March 2020. The banking system remains resilient, and falling Bitcoin prices alone won’t trigger a government bailout.

It has been revealed that but the Fed has a problem that didn’t exist in quite the same way during the pandemic. It may need to protect financial stability without giving up its fight against inflation. In 2020, Washington opened the taps. This time, we wait and see, and Bitcoin price will survive.
