Housing groups cheer Senate passage of milestone permitting bill

🌐 Source: Housingwire • 📅 20 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 5 min read (999 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The U.S. Senate passed the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act (BAAJA) on Thursday night in a 62-33 vote, advancing legislation designed to streamline federal permitting for a broad range of projects, including housing.

The permitting reform bill aims to cut regulatory delays and lower costs for major development projects in housing, energy infrastructure and transportation. It is likely one of the last bipartisan measures this Congress will vote on before the 120th Congress is sworn in shortly after the latest year.

Pro-housing groups strongly support the bill, but many environmental organizations oppose it, arguing that it would roll back necessary environmental protections and promote more fossil fuel consumption.

BAAJA now heads to the U.S. House of Representatives, which is widely expected to vote on the measure before the upcoming Congress is sworn in on January 3. However, the clock is ticking, as the House is already on Thanksgiving recess. That leaves about a month for the House to either pass the Senate’s version or reconcile any differences between the two chambers.

If the bill doesn’t reach President Trump’s desk by January 3, the process would have to start over with the new Congress, which would likely change some of its provisions.

Many affordable and multifamily projects that rely on federal funding or assistance face regulatory hurdles that most privately financed housing developments avoid.

These projects include those backed by the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD), the Federal Housing Administration (FHA), and the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA). Examples include housing built using public housing assistance, Community Development Block Grant (CDBG) funding, USDA housing loans, disaster relief funds and more.

Most notably, these federally backed housing projects must go through National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) and National Historic Preservation Act (NHPA) reviews that don’t apply to most private- sector development projects.

Sources indicate that these reviews, critics argue, add an extra regulatory burden that can increase project timelines by a year or more. Critics further contend that NEPA and NHPA, while well-intentioned laws, are often used by NIMBY groups to delay or kill affordable housing development.

In response to these concerns, BAAJA would streamline NEPA reviews for housing development by:.

These changes would strengthen and expand provisions in the HOME Reform Act of 2025, a bill included in the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act that exempted small federal housing projects of 15 units or less from NEPA review.

BAAJA would similarly streamline NHPA reviews and narrow which projects trigger a review, allowing redevelopment projects involving older buildings to move forward more promptly.

Industry observers note that additionally, the bill would streamline Clean Water Act reviews, in effect speeding up federal wetlands permitting by shortening review timelines and limiting certain EPA interventions and legal challenges. It would also shorten Endangered Species Act consultation timelines and give states a larger role in federal reviews to reduce permitting delays for residential developments on land with protected species or habitats.

Recent findings suggest that support for BAAJA spans much of the housing industry. Earlier this month, the National Association of Home Builders, YIMBY Action, Up for Growth Action and dozens of other housing organizations and homebuilder groups signed a letter urging the Senate to move the permitting reform package forward.

On Friday, National Association of REALTORS Executive Vice President and Chief Advocacy Officer Shannon McGahn stated in a statement she supports the legislation’s goal of streamlining “permitting processes and reducing unnecessary regulatory burdens that drive up development costs, delay construction and limit housing supply.”.

“These barriers affect everything from starter homes to multifamily rental housing, making it harder to meet the needs of buyers and renters alike,” McGahn mentioned.

NAHB further argued that BAAJA would reduce land development costs and permitting delays, while also improving multifamily builders’ access to federal housing finance programs. In a statement following the Senate’s vote, NAHB Chairman Bill Owens mentioned that the bill “will help builders deliver the affordable homes families need.”.

In a recent development, “By cutting the regulatory costs and delays that drive up the price of construction, BAAJA will expand housing supply, lower costs for buyers and renters and increase access to affordable rental housing. With so many families priced out of the market, the House must pass this bill without delay,” Owens noted.

Industry observers note that housing is only a fraction of what BAAJA targets. It mainly aims to streamline the development of a wide range of federally backed projects, including:.

As it does for housing, BAAJA would scale back NEPA and other environmental reviews for these federal projects, while also including other measures to shorten permitting and approval timelines.

In the Senate, 47 Republicans and 15 members of the Senate Democratic caucus voted in favor of the bill.

Senators Mike Rounds (R-S.D.) and Josh Hawley (R-MO) were the only Republican Senators to vote against the legislation, largely because they feared it would undermine states’ rights.

Meanwhile, 31 Democrats voted against it. The legislation has drawn significant opposition from environmental groups concerned that its permitting changes could weaken environmental protections and accelerate fossil fuel emissions.

Sierra Club Executive Director Loren Blackford, for example, argued that the bill “furthers the buildout of fossil fuels, strips away power from communities and Tribes, threatens our clean water and public lands and endangers wildlife.”.

Other critics of BAAJA worry that the bill’s permitting reforms could accelerate the development of more data centers, including on public lands.

Sources indicate that despite the ongoing opposition from environmental groups, BAAJA has bipartisan support in the House. For example, Rep. Scott Peters (D-CA), who backs the bill’s permitting provisions, countered these concerns by noting that it would also fast-track renewable energy development. He reiterated his support for the bill after it passed the Senate.

“BAAJA lowers costs and lowers emissions. It’s that simple. It’s unimaginable that after this month’s election, when the entire country demanded lower costs, we would whiff on the opportunity right in front of us,” Peters stated in a statement.
