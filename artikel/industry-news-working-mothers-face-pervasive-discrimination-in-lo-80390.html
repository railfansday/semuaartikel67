📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Despite the contemporary art world’s increased attention to women’s experiences and the politics of care in its public-facing programming, working mothers employed by London’s galleries and auction houses face widespread discrimination, according to a latest study. As reported by the Art Newspaper today, a survey of 50 employees working in the UK capital’s commercial art sector has found that, for many, having children comes at considerable professional cost.

The study, conducted by Kate McMillan and Cleo Roberts-Komireddi and published today in the European Journal of Cultural Studies, looked at the extent to which pregnancy and maternity affected career progression among art world workers. Moving beyond previous studies centered on fine artists or senior directors, it focused on lower-paid, non-artist roles such as sales, administration, and marketing. In a recent development, roberts-Komireddi and McMillan’s research, which also included 12 in-depth interviews, reveals an industry “marred by implicit and explicit expectations that often result in workers needing to choose between their careers and being parents,” they write. Three quarters of respondents felt that motherhood had impeded career advancement, while 20 percent remarked they had been explicitly denied promotion.

Sources indicate that the authors found poor and sometimes illegal practices across the industry, including employees being “managed” out of their roles while on maternity leave. Such practices, they note, are made possible by a lack of formal human resource structures—only half of the respondence worked for organizations with dedicated HR staff, and just 60 percent noted their workplace had a specific parental leave policy. They additionally blamed the art market’s pervasive culture of secrecy.

Class and wealth also play a role in shaping employee experience, the study suggests. For junior employees on modest salaries, the cost of childcare can make travel and attendance at evening events difficult and increase the likelihood of discrimination on the job. At the same time, a lack of resources makes challenging such discrimination daunting, with many women instead choosing to take a less senior position, move into freelance work, or leave the art world.

According to recent reports, roberts-Komireddi and McMillan’s study spanned small galleries to multinational auction houses and found problems across the board. As they say in their report, “A lack of accountability pervades the sector.”.
