Director Andrey Zvyagintsev has worked with supervising re-recording mixer Andrey Dergachev for 20 years, since the director’s second film, “Banishment.” On each project, including their most recent portrait of a rotten marriage in a rotten world, “Minotaur,” they’ve tried to improve the sound design—using it as a hook that makes the film’s world feel more vital and alive for the audience.

According to recent reports, zvyagintsev and Dergachev generally strive to preserve as much authentic production sound from performances as possible. “On my third movie, ‘Elena,’ we recorded the live sound from shooting, and it’s maybe two or three phrases we had to rerecord, but otherwise the whole sound that you hear is fully recorded during the shooting,” Zvyagintsev told IndieWire on an upcoming episode of the Filmmaker Toolkit podcast. “Our main purpose is to keep as much sound from the shooting as possible and not to add anything extra, and it was the same for ‘Minotaur.’”.

But for the moment that Gleb attacks Anton, and its aftermath, it was crucial to Zvyagintsev that the world beyond the apartment start intruding on Gleb — that even though he wants desperately to cover up what he’s done and make sure no one sees or learns about it, we hear dozens of ways in which he isn’t alone. That required a lot of work outside of the studio to record the background noises Zvyagintsev and Dergachev could then layer into the scene.

“We were creating [background] sounds of the kids playing football in the backyard. We would spend the whole day recording the kids, how they’re speaking, the way they’re playing, people passing by on the street,” Zvyagintsev noted. They also recorded different sound layers from Gleb’s luxury SUV, which they could deploy to create maximum panic in both Gleb and the audience.

“The car signals, the leaves on the street, the birds — they all pronounced, and [emphasized] something. So we were modulating all those layers that we recorded, and then we combined them to create the atmosphere and to put it all in one place for the scene,” Zvyagintsev said.

There’s atmosphere from outside and from inside, as Zvyagintsev and Dergachev refused to let Gleb have a moment of peace on the soundtrack — until, as with all the cruel jokes of “Minotaur’s” filmmaking, he gets it in the worst way, at the worst possible moment. There’s a terrible silence once he’s struck Anton on the head with his own camera. And then the world comes rushing back in, even louder than before.

“We were trying to create this volume of sounds right after the hit. And then, when Anton is falling, there is this complete silence. And then somewhere far away we hear the car alarm and even more sounds that create this volume. This is what we were looking for,” Zvyagintsev mentioned.

It was key to finesse the sound design of the sequence so as not to overplay it. Zvyagintsev initially wanted to add music to the moment, but had to hold himself back. “It was really tempting,” Zvyagintsev remarked. “My composer Evgueni Galperine even wrote the music, which we added. But when we saw the result, we realized, and I understood that no, no, it has to be completely silent.”.

Silent as the grave, one might say. But it’s the sound design, the modulation between loudness and silence, that makes the moment so horrifying. Zvyagintsev and Dergachev did it by hand, just to make sure of it.

“Minotaur” is now playing in select theaters.
