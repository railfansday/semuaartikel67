I Tried Building My Own Interactive World in Google Genie, but Real-Time AI Engines Aren't There Yet

It has been revealed that beyond the Gemini assistant, Gemini Notebook, and various other AI offerings, Google also develops Genie, a world-building engine intended to help you create immersive visual experiences and games using only text prompts. Think of them like AI videos you can interact with and move through.

In a recent development, these promises point to the future of AI in all kinds of ways—from robotics to world sims—but the tool requires a lot of computing power, and access is limited for the time being. You can dive in now if you're on one of the Google AI Ultra intends ($99.99 or $199.99 a month), or join the waitlist if you're a Google AI Pro user ($19.99 a month). But if you've got access, is it worth the effort (and money)? I put it to the test.

Sources indicate that it's no exaggeration to say you can build an entire virtual world in Google Genie. It's going to be a more basic, less developed, and more chaotic world than the one in, say, GTA 6, but you can do it. Think of it like generating an AI video from a prompt, but you can then walk through the video and go as deep as you like, taking turns along the way.

Everything is rendered in real time, at a resolution of 720p and at 24 frames per second. That's not to say the engine is storing a massive 3D map in memory, however—after a minute or so, it's going to start to forget the places you've already seen. And while the basic layout will stay fixed, you might notice disappearing trees or changing colors.

Google's official line is "visual details and object permanence can still shift or drift over time" in Genie 4, so it's a known limitation. The tool is presented as-is, with the intention to develop it further over time as models improve, and it's aimed at filmmakers, historians, designers, scientists, robotics engineers, and game developers. Or indeed, anyone who just wants to get lost in a world that isn't real.

According to recent reports, some of the demos you'll find as you step through the Genie 4 portal give you a great idea of the possibilities. There's a "Dino Field Guide" that, you guessed it, lets you wander safari-style through a prehistoric habitat. All the flora and fauna are neatly rendered, and you even get dinosaur quiz questions along the way—a nod to Genie's potential as an educational tool.

According to recent reports, there's a "Villa Giallo" mini-game, which involves searching a haunted 1970s Italian villa and clicking on objects as you go; these objects act as clues to help you progress to the next room. I was also intrigued by a "White House: Code Red" experience that takes you into the West Wing during a "high-stakes national crisis." I couldn't quite figure out what the crisis was, but I definitely felt the highness of the stakes.

Someone's even built a "GTA 7" engine that drops you into a bright and vibrant cityscape. The slow speed and warped physics of the experience suggest Rockstar Games has nothing to worry about just yet—check out the disappearing cars and backwards-driving cops in the video above—but it's impressively detailed all the same, considering it all comes from a few text prompts.

According to recent reports, having checked out a few demos, I felt ready to put Genie 4 to the test myself. Eager not to infringe on any IP, I decided to generate a world where I wouldn't mind getting lost for a few minutes—somewhere I could get away from the stress of my day and enjoy some warmer weather.

Sources indicate that I started with the prompt "Create a fully immersive, photorealistic 3D environment showing a pub in an English village in the middle of a hot and sunny day. The pub is surrounded by country lanes, old buildings, and woods, and a handful of villagers are walking around and drinking outside the pub. Use a first-person view and use a quiet background of birdsong and wind rustling in the trees.".

In a recent development, it took a couple of minutes for my peaceful, idyllic world to be ready, and then I was immediately able to navigate through it using my keyboard and mouse. Once you're in your world, you are suggested certain prompts you can make use of, like strolling into the pub or getting the villagers to make a toast, and these worked well—evidence that your virtual world really is interactive (to a point).

Unsurprisingly, the usual AI video tells are in abundance: Everyone looks generic, the physics often go sideways, the text on signs is gibberish, and details don't remain fixed. Do a full 360-degree spin around, and you might find that not only have all the customers in the pub changed their appearance, but you're also at an entirely different pub.

Sources indicate that the best feature is the ability to make real-time edits: I summoned both a storm and a cricket pitch with a few text prompts, though Genie decided to put a hut in the middle of the cricket pitch for some reason. I think I was most impressed by the moment when a cricketer walked past me and commented on the rain, providing hints of how this kind of tech could expand or augment games and virtual experiences in the future.

That remarked, even at v4 of Genie, this tech is still very much in the experimental stage, and while I can see the potential, it also shows just how difficult a task world-building is for an AI engine. Without 3D maps and proper physics engines as a foundation, you'll encounter a great deal of hastily rendered slop as you explore your environment. Even if you're a prompting pro, for the time being this is something to mess around with, rather than use in a practical way.
