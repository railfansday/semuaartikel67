Digital Outages Are Rampant All Across America. Is AI to blame?

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that coincidence is not causation, and yet the urge to ascribe causative power to random events is a typically human characteristic. Take the case of the man who snaps his fingers every five minutes. When a neighbor asks why, he replies, “To keep the elephants away.” “But there are no elephants anywhere around here,” the neighbor points out. “See?” the man says. “It’s working!”.

It has been revealed that last week, people who rely on Microsoft Outlook to manage their lives suddenly found it was not working properly. The next day, the FAA issued a rare “ground stop” order because pieces of the digital system that air traffic controllers rely on failed.

Industry observers note that the following Monday, four of the country’s largest banks found their computer systems were malfunctioning, and United Airlines experienced a computer outage that exacerbated the travails of long suffering passengers still trying to recover from the “ground stop” fiasco. Then on Tuesday, the National Security Agency’s internal communications network went offline, and Apple web services were disrupted.

It has been revealed that according to the New York Times, Senator Josh Hawley, a Republican from Missouri, told reporters last week, “I think we ought to be briefed thoroughly on what is actually happening here. If this is all a coincidence, it’s like the greatest coincidence in the history of the world.”.

The president of the United States offered this reassuring statement: “It’s life. When you’re major and powerful and have all the money, things like this happen. It’s fine.”.

Not surprisingly, with all the talk about AI taking over the world and employees quitting their jobs to warn people of the danger, quite a few people are wondering if AI is responsible for all these digital disruptions.

According to recent reports, for some Americans, the timing and scale of the outages made it hard to believe that sheer coincidence was the explanation. In a curious way, AI is involved, but not in the way you might think.

Ever since the digital revolution began more than 50 years ago, the computers we depend on have only been able to do what humans tell them to do. Those instructions are created by coders — people who use the programming language of the day that tell computers what to do and how to do it.

It is well known in the trade that millions of lines of bad code exist. It is a phenomenon known as “garbage in, garbage out” or GIGO. Digital firms employ phalanxes of people to check the code their coders write, but it is a tedious process that often misses even glaring errors. Curiously, the most effective way to find bad code seems to be by using artificial intelligence, which can scan 10,000 times more lines of code in a given amount of time than a human can do.

Industry observers note that tech startup Calif stated in a blog post this week that, using AI, it had found a critical flaw in Java4Shell, which leaves much of the internet exposed to cyber attacks if it is not corrected. Java is a popular programming language used by most Fortune 500 companies, according to Politico.

“Banking, aviation, telecommunications, nearly every critical sector runs on Java, including national security systems,” Calif wrote. It calls Java “the beating heart of the modern economy.” Oracle, the developer of Java, released a patch for the vulnerability Tuesday. South Korea’s intelligence agency reportedly sent a separate security advisory earlier Tuesday to public institutions, warning them to fix the issue and prevent hackers from exploiting the Java flaw.

According to recent reports, the Java vulnerability comes weeks after a separate major vulnerability was discovered in another popular programming language, PHP, which powers up to 70 percent of the internet. At the time, governments worldwide advised organizations to take their sites offline to patch the vulnerability and prevent breaches.

Recent findings suggest that director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton said on Fox News that ongoing efforts to patch the PHP vulnerability took an internal National Security Agency communications system offline Tuesday. He added that AI is making it easier to find such vulnerabilities, which can take years to fix without using the power of AI to find them.

Sources indicate that peter Avery is the chief information and security officer for Velocity Security, a cyber security firm in Michigan. He told the New York Times he was skeptical that the events of last week were connected. “I like facts,” he noted. “And until now, these are all separate concerns.”.

It has been revealed that drawing patterns where they do not yet exist, he mentioned, is human nature. It’s part of the need to make sense of things, he said, but can lead down a dark road. “When you start to live in that world where everyone’s a liar and no one is telling the truth, you are starting to live in fear.”.

Recent findings suggest that maybe snapping your fingers every five minutes is our best defense against cyber criminals stealing our data and crashing our online persona. That’s not a particularly comforting thought.
