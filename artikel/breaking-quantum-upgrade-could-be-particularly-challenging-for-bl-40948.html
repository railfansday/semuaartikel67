Breaking: Quantum Upgrade Could Be 'Particularly Challenging' for Blockchains: IOSCO

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Financial markets, including crypto, need to start preparing now for quantum computers capable of breaking the cryptography that secures them, according to the world's main forum for securities regulators.

According to recent reports, A "sufficiently capable" quantum computer could undermine widely used public-key cryptography that supports critical functions across financial markets, the International Organization of Securities Commissions (IOSCO) noted in a report published on Thursday. The cyber readiness challenge posed by quantum computing "is immediate and requires action," it said.

"Practical quantum computers capable of compromising widely used cryptography do not yet exist, but the scale and duration of the transition mean the financial sector should start preparing today," IOSCO Board Chair Jean-Paul Servais noted in a statement.

Sources indicate that the 63-page report cited conservative estimates that such machines could emerge between 2030 and 2035. Several industry participants consulted by IOSCO went further, arguing that firms should prepare for the possibility that they arrive within five years, potentially accelerated by AI.

According to recent reports, IOSCO said the migration may be particularly challenging for blockchains, because cryptography is embedded in their core architecture and any change requires coordinated action across decentralized networks. Public blockchains such as Ethereum, Solana and Tron rely heavily on elliptic curve cryptography to verify identities and validate transactions, and a powerful enough quantum computer could derive private keys from exposed public keys, a scenario known as "Q-Day.".

Swapping in quantum-resistant signatures comes at a cost. Signatures under ML-DSA-44, a post-quantum standard from the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology, are about 2,420 bytes, roughly 35 to 40 times larger than today's 64-byte elliptic curve signatures, the report remarked. It cited evidence from a quantum-resistant Solana testnet that saw throughput fall by about 90% when post-quantum signatures replaced existing ones.

Industry observers note that because the cryptography sits in transaction formats and consensus rules, replacing it requires hard forks, and decentralized networks have no way to force quick adoption, making migration "slow and fragmented," IOSCO said. Accounts and smart contracts still controlled by vulnerable keys could stay exposed after a network adds quantum-resistant options, and the path to quantum resistance for Bitcoin wallets remains uphill.

Recent findings suggest that wallet makers face their own trade-offs. SHRINCS, the leading candidate for a post-quantum Bitcoin signature scheme, offers "stateful" signatures that are about 10 times smaller but can expose funds to theft if keys are reused beyond a set signing budget, Trezor CTO Tomáš Sušánka has remarked. Trezor is considering making the larger, safer stateless option the default, and hardware wallets will also need post-quantum cryptography for firmware and device authentication, he added.

The report distinguished "on-spend" attacks, in which an attacker races to exploit a public key revealed by a pending transaction, from "at-rest" attacks on keys left exposed for longer. Dormant or abandoned coins pose a particular problem where their holders can't move them. Crypto firms have formed a quantum alliance as some 6 million BTC sit in exposed addresses, and Bitcoin developer Cory Fields has proposed "quantum landmines" to make attacks on such coins costly.

Recent findings suggest that layer-2 networks are not insulated either. Optimistic rollups could face forged signatures during their dispute windows, typically seven days on Ethereum, and many zero-knowledge rollups use SNARK proofs that rely on elliptic curves. Hash-based STARK proofs are more resistant, but a rollup is only as safe as the layer-1 contracts, bridges and governance keys it depends on, the report mentioned.

Recent findings suggest that developers are already working on fixes. Researchers have proposed several ways to make Bitcoin quantum-safe, StarkWare has shown a quantum-safe Bitcoin transaction, and Ethereum co-founder Vitalik Buterin has stated the network is betting its future on quantum resistance.

Sources indicate that IOSCO remarked firms should migrate to post-quantum cryptography and build "crypto-agility," the ability to update cryptographic systems without major redesign. Quantum key distribution could complement those measures on selected high-security channels, it added.

Industry observers note that most preparations across financial markets are still at an early stage, focused on awareness, governance, training and identifying vulnerable systems, according to the report, which drew on a March 2026 survey of members of IOSCO's Fintech Task Force and discussions with experts. Some respondents described "quantum-procrastination," in which firms put off action while waiting for clearer regulatory signals or wider adoption.

Recent findings suggest that no surveyed jurisdiction reported operational deployment of quantum computing in regulated financial services. The watchdog also urged regulators to act against "quantum washing," in which firms overstate the role, maturity or performance of quantum technology. IOSCO said it will treat quantum as a priority in 2027, focusing on raising awareness across the financial ecosystem.

Sources indicate that quantum hardware is advancing promptly. IBM has claimed a trusted quantum advantage, and Google has said it has cracked some of the field's biggest bottlenecks.
