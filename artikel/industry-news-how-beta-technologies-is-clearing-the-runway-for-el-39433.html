Recent findings suggest that beta Technologies, one of the top three U.S. electric aircraft makers, is aiming first at regional cargo and medical transport, before pursuing the passenger market. Shown here is Beta’s Alia A250, for vertical takeoffs and landings.

In the cockpit of Beta Technologies’ Alia CX300, pilot Chris Caputo steers onto Runway 33 at Patrick Leahy Burlington International Airport in Vermont. Cleared for takeoff, he releases the brakes and nudges the throttle forward.

Sources indicate that at the back of the aircraft, the five-blade pusher propeller snaps awake, and the Alia bolts forward. There’s no shriek of a turbofan spooling up, no shudder of a piston engine, and no wait for thrust. Just the low hum of an electric motor and a shove of instant acceleration.

It has been revealed that within a few seconds, we’re at 85 knots (almost 160 kilometers per hour), and Caputo rotates the nose upward. The runway drops abruptly away as we climb. Gaining speed and altitude, we head northwest over Lake Champlain. Through the wraparound canopy, I watch the hunched shoulders of Vermont’s Green Mountains shrink under the right wing, with Newly introduced York’s taller Adirondacks to the left.

Caputo, a former Air Force fighter pilot, flicks the control stick hard left, then right. Despite its 15-meter wingspan, the Alia feels snappy, its fly-by-wire system automatically coordinating aileron and rudder inputs. The machine, he claims, is much simpler to manage than the Cessna 182 I flew around Australia in my student-pilot days.

Sources indicate that what’s more, our half-hour flight in the Alia will consume only about US $9 worth of electricity. The same flight in a comparable Cessna 208 Caravan would burn roughly 25 to 30 gallons of Jet A fuel, costing about $210 to $255 at press time. “Even at full throttle, we’re not burning any fuel up here,” notes Caputo, as he takes the Alia into a sweeping left turn and heads south over the lake. “We’re not polluting the environment. [There’s] no noise pollution.”.

At Beta’s South Burlington, Vt., facility, workers assemble the body of an Alia A250. The company says it has an order and option-to-buy backlog of nearly 1,000 aircraft.Beta Technologies.

In a recent development, this ride is happening during an unusually lively time for electric aviation. The promise of lower operating costs and cleaner flight has pushed electric aircraft from a fringe experiment to one of the aviation industry’s most closely watched frontiers. After years of renderings, prototypes, and overly optimistic timetables, companies are now flying full-size aircraft, submitting hardware to regulators for certification, building factories, and testing routes with prospective customers.

Industry observers note that among the top three U.S. developers of electric planes, Archer Aviation and Joby Aviation are concentrating on piloted vertical-takeoff-and-landing air taxis, otherwise known as eVTOLs. Beta Technologies is taking a broader—and in some ways more innovative—path into the market. Rather than betting first on air taxis, Beta is looking to enter electric aviation through short-haul freight planes that use conventional runways, fed by an open charging network that’s designed to make electric aircraft—both Beta’s and its competitors’—part of a working transportation system.

To that end, the company is developing two variants of its Alia airframe: the runway-based CX300 I’m flying in and the A250, which adds four lift propellers for vertical takeoff and landing. Beta states its test pilots have flown the Alia family more than 300,000 kilometers, including doing multiple U.S. crossings and overseas demonstrations. The company claims an order and option-to-buy backlog of 991 aircraft.

In a recent development, “We’re taking a pragmatic stepwise approach for both certification and market entry,” notes Beta’s CEO and founder Kyle Clark. “We’re ready to go today with medical and cargo aircraft that fly from airport to airport.” Next up, he says, will be passenger aircraft with conventional takeoff and landing, followed by cargo eVTOLs, and finally passenger eVTOLs.

Beta’s 17,500-square-meter production facility sits beside the Burlington airport and looks more like a giant sunlit workshop than a conventional airplane factory. Geothermal heat pumps help heat and cool the structure. The roof will eventually hold a megawatt of solar panels. On the floor, technicians make composite structures and join aircraft sections beneath American and Pride flags.

In a recent development, electric-aircraft makers compensate with redundant motors and electrical channels, but most leading designs also have wings to allow gliding. In Beta’s A250, that means that if all else fails, the pilot still has a shot at reaching a suitable landing spot.

Archer Aviation’s Midnight has six tilting propellers and six dedicated lift props. Beta’s A250 keeps its four lift propellers horizontal and uses a separate pusher motor for forward flight.

Sources indicate that in Beta’s home state of Vermont, the electricity produced in the state comes almost entirely from renewable sources. Of course, electric aviation’s climate advantage depends not only on how clean the power grid is, but on how efficiently the aircraft uses energy. The turbines in conventional jet engines are marvelous machines, but they throw away a lot of energy as heat. Plus they need to carry the hardware to manage that heat—inlets, ducts, radiators, oil coolers—and the drag that comes with pulling air through all of it.

Industry observers note that by contrast, an electric design, with no combustion, gearbox, or oil circuits, avoids much of that energy burden. The result is an aircraft that is more than twice as “slippery” in terms of its lift-to-drag ratio. Batteries will never come close to the fuel density of petroleum fuels. But because electric drivetrains are much more efficient than combustion engines, a battery doesn’t necessarily need to match the energy density of petroleum fuel to be useful.

It has been revealed that while the motors on an electric aircraft provide the drama at takeoff and landing, the batteries set nearly every boundary: the range, payload, charge time, cooling load, reserve energy, and, ultimately, useful life. For now, the hardest limit on electric aviation is not imagination, software, or even motors. It is the stubborn weight of stored energy.

Recent findings suggest that “One thing you notice about [electric] airplanes is that they are all small,” states Cecutta, the aviation analyst. “They are not small because someone has done a market study and decided that is the optimal size. They are small because you cannot make them massive. This limitation is from the batteries.”.

In a conventional aircraft, fuel accounts for a significant percentage of the takeoff weight. But that weight gradually disappears during flight. In an electric plane, the battery packs also account for a lot of the weight; in Beta’s CX300, they’re close to half the aircraft’s mass. But a drained battery at landing weighs the same as a fully charged one at takeoff. That means the rest of the aircraft must be light enough to lift a power source that never gets lighter, yet strong enough to endure vibration, turbulence, hard landings, and years of fatigue. The more engineers push to save weight, Datta notes, the more flexible the airframe becomes, making the wings and other structures more prone to bending, twisting, and vibration. In that sense, the battery problem becomes a structural problem, too.

According to recent reports, beta’s CX300 carries five liquid-cooled battery packs of about 45 kilowatt-hours each, giving the aircraft roughly 225 kWh of rated capacity, though not all of that may be usable in flight. Each pack weighs 255 kg and contains 2,772 cylindrical cells. Altogether, about 14,000 cells sit beneath the occupied cabin, each one a tiny storehouse of energy, heat, and potential trouble.

According to recent reports, the burden of the battery explains much of what I see elsewhere in Beta’s aircraft: the 15-meter wingspan, the composite structure, and the almost doctrinal effort to leave out every piece of hardware and creature comfort that’s not essential to the mission. The CX300 even does without flaps, which most aircraft use to generate extra lift at low speeds, so it must take off and land unusually fast for an aircraft of its size.

Industry observers note that on Beta’s battery line, commercially supplied standard 21-millimeter-diameter, 70-mm-long cells are tested and assembled. Cells become modules, modules become subpacks, and eleven subpacks become a 45-kWh pack. Cooling plates manage heat during normal operation, while passive barriers and electrical isolation are designed to contain the ugly edge cases that Beta’s testing process intentionally triggers.

It has been revealed that the modular design of the packs will give Beta’s planes a path to better batteries as the cells and battery chemistry improve. Caputo tells me that the company is already evaluating higher-density cells at its battery testing facility in nearby St. Albans, Vt. More watt-hours per kilogram could eventually mean more range, more payload, or both.

According to recent reports, what sets Beta Technologies apart from its competitors is the breadth of its ambitions: It’s not just building electric aircraft. It’s also cultivating a broader ecosystem to nurture the growth of electric aviation.

Sources indicate that for instance, Beta intends its motor business to extend beyond its own aircraft. The firm has delivered lift motors for Eve Air Mobility’s proof-of-concept aircraft and has a contract to supply up to $1 billion in pusher motors as Eve’s program develops. In addition, Beta is selling flight-control computers and software to Horizon Aircraft. It is also producing propulsion systems for the military, as well as the MV250, a hybrid-electric autonomous aircraft for defense missions.

Sources indicate that further evidence of Beta’s far-ranging plans is visible near the battery line in the South Burlington facility, where workers are readying a few of the company’s 1.5-meter-tall Charge Cubes for shipping. Intended for installation at airports, each cube takes 480-volt AC from the grid and can deliver up to 320 kW at as much as 1,000 volts DC. According to Beta, charging either model of its Alia aircraft takes about 35 minutes, from the minimum charge required under visual flight rules to full state. A separate thermal-management cube circulates water-glycol coolant through the aircraft to keep the batteries from overheating during charging.

The charge cubes use the Combined Charging System (CCS), an open standard increasingly shared across the electric transportation industry. Beta’s strategy resembles Tesla’s decision to treat charging as part of the vehicle business, with one key difference: Beta is courting other aircraft makers, airports, and operators to use its technology. Archer, Atlantic Aviation, Signature Aviation, and government agencies have purchased or installed Beta’s charging equipment. As of October, Beta has 138 fast-charge locations, with additional sites in permitting or construction.

In a recent development, for charging beyond the fixed network, Beta offers a wheeled 40-kW Mini Cube and a 65-kW Mini Cube+ that can ride inside an Alia and plug into more modest electrical service. The Mini Cube can also charge cars, buses, and trucks—any vehicle that uses the CCS standard.

Sources indicate that back above Lake Champlain, the CX300 levels off, and the motor recedes to a faint hum. Then Caputo switches it off, and there’s nothing but the rush of wind around the canopy. The aircraft glides on its long wings while the coasting propeller turns the motor into a generator, feeding about 9 kW back to the battery.

Then we turn to the east and slip into the landing queue behind a National Guard C-130 Hercules, and Caputo powers and slides the throttle forward. The motor springs to life again, pushing the airplane, and perhaps aviation itself, into the future.
