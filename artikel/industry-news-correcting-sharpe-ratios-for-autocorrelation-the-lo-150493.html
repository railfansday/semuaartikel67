📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that the Sharpe ratio's standard error formula assumes i.i.d. returns. That assumption is almost always false for real strategies: smoothed marks, overlapping holding periods, illiquid positions, and momentum effects all leave a lag-1 autocorrelation in the return series. When returns are autocorrelated, the effective sample size is smaller than the nominal one, and significance tests fire far too often. With rho = 0.2, a nominal 5% test rejects the null at 18–25% in simulation. That is not a small calibration error; it is the difference between "maybe real" and "coin flip.".

The fix is cheap: correct the Sharpe ratio by the Lo factor before you run any significance test.

Industry observers note that for most daily-frequency strategies, lag-1 dominates and the closed form above is enough. Use the general form when you have overlapping windows (e.g., 5-day returns sampled daily), where lag-k dependence out to k = 4 is structural, not noise.

Do not eyeball this from a chart. Compute it, and compute it on the return series you actually test, not on cumulative PnL. Cumulative PnL is a random walk under the null and will show high autocorrelation regardless of the strategy.

Two caveats on negative rho specifically:.

The autocorrelation correction is the single most common missing step in strategy evaluation, and it interacts badly with multiple testing.

Consider a grid search over 200 parameter combinations. You keep the best in-sample Sharpe. Two things then compound:.

Sources indicate that the result is a strategy that looks significant at p < 0.01 on the raw numbers and is pure noise. The autocorrelation correction does not fix selection bias on its own, but it is a prerequisite. Deflating a Sharpe that was never corrected for dependence deflates the wrong quantity.
