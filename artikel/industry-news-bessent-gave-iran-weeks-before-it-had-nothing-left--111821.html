According to recent reports, U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent joins 'Sunday Morning Futures' to discuss the Trump administration's economic sanctions against Iran, predicting a swift collapse of the regime's economy. Bessent also addresses the Iranian death threats that forced his family to relocate to a military base and weighs in on the compliance failures at Nvidia amid its ongoing espionage probe.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent warned in September that Iran would have "nothing left to trade" within two weeks.

Nearly two months later, the Trump administration is still targeting the overseas financial networks Tehran has used to keep money moving despite sweeping U.S. sanctions in the midst of ongoing war.

On Monday, Treasury unveiled another round of sanctions targeting more than two dozen individuals and companies accused of helping Iran move billions of dollars through financial networks spanning Dubai, Hong Kong and Turkey.

Bessent has repeatedly warned that Iran is nearing economic collapse, describing successive crackdowns as steps toward cutting off the regime's remaining financial lifelines. Yet each new round of sanctions has exposed more of the networks Tehran has used to keep doing business abroad — even as mounting economic pressure has failed to produce a diplomatic breakthrough.

Secretary Scott Bessent speaks to members of the media outside the White House in Washington, D.C., on Oct. 22, 2025 (Aaron Schwartz/CNP/Bloomberg via Getty Images).

While the administration claims its blockade has largely stopped Iranian oil exports, Monday's sanctions detail how Tehran has relied on currency dealers, trading companies and middlemen to conduct business abroad.

Treasury identified approximately $14.7 billion in financial activity potentially linked to Iranian shadow banking between January 2025 and August 2026, underscoring the scale of the networks Washington is now targeting.

Sources indicate that just three days earlier, Treasury declared that Iran had exhausted its seaborne oil supply outside the U.S. blockade, saying no newly introduced oil had been loaded onto vessels since the launch of Operation Economic Outcast in August.

"The regime has exhausted its seaborne oil supply, and it may never see the revenue from the oil it has already sold. The ship is sinking," Bessent said Friday.

According to recent reports, in a response to Fox News Digital, Treasury stated Iran has not loaded a single barrel of oil onto vessels since Aug. 25, 2026, and claimed Tehran is struggling to access foreign currency and recover proceeds from previous oil sales.

The department also provided a report showing the Iranian rial has lost roughly 25% of its value since August, while inflation reached 90% in September. Treasury stated it is continuing to target alternative financial channels, including cryptocurrency exchanges, as Tehran looks for ways around U.S. restrictions.

But even as the administration claims success in cutting off newly introduced oil shipments, Treasury is still pursuing Iranian operatives accused of collecting billions of dollars from previous sales. Friday's sanctions targeted four individuals involved in recovering oil revenues held overseas, some of which Treasury mentioned had been diverted by middlemen for personal gain.

Bessent's September prediction was hardly his first warning that Tehran was approaching an economic breaking point.

Recent findings suggest that months earlier, he said U.S. sanctions were "asphyxiating" Iran's economy and warned that the country could soon be forced to shut down oil wells as storage facilities filled up.

By late August, Bessent was predicting Iran's economy could collapse within "weeks or months," as the administration launched Operation Economic Outcast, a campaign aimed at cutting off the regime's remaining revenue.

The warnings continued in October, when Bessent projected Iran's economy could shrink by more than 25% over the year ending in March 2027. Earlier this month, he described the country as experiencing one of the fastest economic collapses in history.

According to recent reports, the administration had increasingly turned to economic pressure after months of military strikes failed to force Tehran into an agreement. Washington reimposed its naval blockade in July after an interim deal collapsed, and Secretary of State Marco Rubio noted in September that the price Iran would pay going forward would be primarily economic, although military action remained an option.

According to recent reports, but neither the military campaign nor the mounting economic pressure has produced a broader agreement. Iran continues to demand sanctions relief, access to frozen assets and an end to the blockade, while Washington is pressing Tehran for concessions on its nuclear program and regional activities.

Despite months of escalating pressure, Treasury is still identifying overseas financial networks that have allowed Iran to conduct business outside traditional banking channels.

Iran has spent decades developing ways around American financial restrictions, relying on middlemen, shell companies and foreign currency exchanges to keep trade flowing even as Washington moves to cut off its access to international markets.
