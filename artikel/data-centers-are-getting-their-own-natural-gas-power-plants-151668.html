Data centers are getting their own natural gas power plants

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that one of the biggest roadblocks to starting up a data center is getting approval to connect to the electric grid—a wait that could last as long as a decade. Some developers aren’t willing to wait and are instead opting to build their own off-grid, or behind-the-meter, gas power plants to fuel the AI boom.

Recent findings suggest that xAI’s Memphis campus has famously used dozens of natural gas turbines, some of them without permits, as far back as 2024. At the time, that facility was a rarity, and it faced scrutiny from regulators. It remains the subject of ongoing legal battles. But the strategy of going off-grid for power has since become a lot more common.

As of October 2026, data center developers have declared aims for more than 100 behind-the-meter gas power plants at facilities in the US, according to data from Cleanview, a market intelligence organization. The facilities have a combined planned capacity of over 115 gigawatts, more than double the peak power demand of California. The vast majority of these announcements have come since the beginning of 2025.

It’s possible that not all these facilities will be built; only a small fraction are under construction or operating today. But there’s a growing trend of large data centers looking to build their own power supply, and this could shape the grid—and its climate impacts—for decades to come.

Building your own power plant is generally more expensive than just hooking up to the grid, but the AI rush has made developers willing to pay a significant premium to get online faster. “The biggest motivator for on-site power right now from data centers is speed,” indicates Musfika Mishi, an associate at BloombergNEF, an energy consultancy.

When developers want to build a newly introduced industrial facility—like a data center—that requires a lot of electricity, they need to request a grid connection from a local utility or regional grid operator. That request kicks off a yearslong process in which grid planners study the existing grid to understand what would need to be built to safely deliver power to the facility without compromising reliability. Upgrades might be required, like building new transmission lines or larger transformers, and the developer might be on the hook to help pay for them.

Industry observers note that rather than waiting for that process to play out, developers can opt to instead build their own power plants. These facilities are referred to as “behind the meter” because they sit on the customer’s side of a utility meter—supplying the building directly, rather than relying on a utility via a grid hookup.

“This shift in the data center developers’ power strategies towards off-grid gas raises some vital questions about how data centers will be powered going forward,” states Brendan Pierpont, director of electricity at the think tank Energy Innovation.

Behind-the-meter doesn’t always mean being totally disconnected from the grid, however. Some facilities may build such plants as a temporary measure, eventually connecting to the grid and tapping their own power plants only as backup. “It’s really just a very expensive way to bridge that maybe five-to-seven-year timeline gap until these data centers can get a grid connection,” Pierpont says.

Sources indicate that even if gas plants are pitched as a near-term solution, building upcoming fossil fuel infrastructure could lead to longer-term effects, since an operator might choose to continue running plants once they’ve spent money building them. “Even though data centers say that it’s a grid-bridging solution, the probability that it is going to be in use much longer is a real risk, and that leads to fossil fuel lock-in,” Mishi says.

The on-site gas rush could have major implications for greenhouse-gas emissions. Today, the US grid is still reliant on fossil fuels, but nuclear and renewables together make up about 40% of electricity generation. That means making a grid connection is generally a lower-emissions option than relying entirely on an on-site gas plant. (That’s not the case everywhere—India, for example, is expecting to see a data center boom in the next decade, and its grid is largely served by emissions-intensive coal-fired power plants today.).

Utilities are already planning to build more natural gas power plants on the grid to meet rising electricity demand. Equipment manufacturers are struggling to keep up with the surge, and it’s pushing developers of behind-the-meter plants toward less-efficient alternatives.

Today, large natural gas plants generally use combined-cycle turbines. These capture heat to generate additional electricity, making them more efficient than single-cycle turbines, which are generally cheaper to build. Very few of the planned behind-the-meter facilities, however, will use combined-cycle turbines, Mishi indicates. That means these off-grid plants will likely produce more greenhouse-gas emissions than even other natural gas resources.

BloombergNEF is also tracking on-site natural gas plants at data centers, finding 181 gigawatts’ worth of unveiled projects globally, with 90% of that capacity designated for the US. If all planned behind-the-meter gas plants for data centers in the US go ahead, they could emit 345 million metric tons of carbon dioxide equivalent each year by 2035, according to a BloombergNEF report. That’s 152 million metric tons more than if those data centers pulled power from the US grid—a difference greater than the total annual emissions of the Netherlands.

Sources indicate that and while it might seem logical that data centers bringing their own power could ease communities’ energy bills, those facilities going off-grid doesn’t protect prices for everyone else, states Matthias Fripp, director of global policy research at Energy Innovation. More demand for natural gas could push its price higher, he explains, which could in turn raise rates for the roughly 60% of American households that use natural gas for heating and cooking. That effect may also trickle into electricity prices.

There’s still a lot of uncertainty about how much of this gas rush will come to fruition, so this future isn’t set in stone. Today, just 4.9 gigawatts of behind-the-meter gas projects tied to data centers are operating, and 15.8 gigawatts are under construction, according to Cleanview’s data.

Industry observers note that creative solutions could also help limit the climate burden of the on-site gas buildout, BloombergNEF’s Mishi states. Building gas alongside on-site renewables or energy storage could help limit how often a gas plant needs to fire up, she says, though the economics of building multiple energy sources can be tricky.

In a recent development, while the grid is struggling to keep up with demand, building more renewables could help, Fripp adds. In a recent study, his team found that building renewables and energy storage to meet rising electricity demand could be reliable and cheaper than relying on fossil fuels.

It has been revealed that “These data centers want a lot of power, and they want it fast, and the grid is not well equipped to provide that,” Fripp says. “There are potentially good solutions and bad solutions … Rushing off the grid and putting in your own behind-the-meter gas is probably a bad way, because it’s still going to raise other people’s bills, and it’s not great for the environment.”.
