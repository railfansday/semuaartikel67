AI is changing software engineering — and some developers miss the struggle

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that when Gemma Rodgers began her computer science degree in 2002, software engineering was seen as a future-proof and lucrative career. It appealed to people who enjoyed getting into the weeds of code, tracking down bugs and building programs from scratch.

It's an industry that has always moved fast, Rodgers, a software developer based in the U.K., told CNBC. But the arrival of artificial intelligence has accelerated the pace, with AI tools now part of the daily work of every software engineer she knows. In a 2026 Stack Overflow survey, 83% of software developer respondents said they use at least one AI tool.

As AI coding tools help engineers write, debug and test code faster than ever, some are mourning the job they used to have. Rodgers has always loved the sense of accomplishment that comes from piecing together different parts of code to find a solution, particularly when she's spent days, or even weeks, trying to fix a stubborn bug.

"The sense of joy and fun that I used to experience by having a challenge and then having to work that out, it's been removed somewhat," she said. "Now you can sometimes do it in a few prompts, and it's great — but it's a breadcrumb of joy, not a full meal of joy.".

Rodgers is not alone in feeling nostalgic about the not-so-old days. Software engineers have taken to social media to describe missing their old way of working, when they could get into a flow state with code for hours at a time.

It has been revealed that still, AI tools can be useful and have lowered the barrier to entry significantly. "It's becoming an industry where a lot of people have been able to thrive who wouldn't necessarily have been able to thrive 10 years ago, five years ago, even three years ago," Rodgers stated.

It's also notable that the core skills engineers have traditionally learned on the job are not lost, she noted. AI tools and internet services can go down unexpectedly, and engineers still need to know how to investigate problems and work through solutions themselves.

"The sorts of skills that we were taught years ago are kind of being lost because [engineers are] having to just churn work out," Rodgers added.

The job itself is changing, too. James Broadhead, chief technology officer of AI decision-making company Taktile, told CNBC that engineers who might previously have spent three weeks building a prototype can now generate 10 in an afternoon. The hardest part of that, he remarked, is "having the judgment to decide which of those are any good.".

"The [AI] agents are spitting out so much code that [engineers] haven't read it all anymore," Broadhead remarked. "It used to be you'd build up a sense of comfort and awareness of how the whole code base was structured, but now the whole thing changes all the time.".

Joshua Wöhle, CEO of London-based AI training tech giant Mindstone, told CNBC he sees a growing divide between workers who know how to use AI effectively and those who don't.

Industry observers note that the industry used to talk about "10X engineers," he noted, who were dramatically more productive than their peers. Now, AI is widening that gap even further to produce "100X engineers.".

It has been revealed that wöhle believes companies will increasingly have to decide whether to help employees catch up with AI or prioritize those who can get the most out of it. But he also understands why some engineers might struggle to adapt.

The people who once excelled at the job were often those who loved writing and working through code, he said, and those are not necessarily the current top performers.

Industry observers note that "That's the grief that is very, very real," Wöhle said. "The thing you love doing is no longer the thing the firm should be paying for, to a degree, because it's no longer adding the value to the company that it used to.".

In a recent development, but not every engineer sees the move away from hands-on coding as a bad thing. Chris Sees, a software engineer of more than 20 years and CEO of London-based virtual office company Hoxton Mix, told CNBC that AI gives developers more time to tackle interesting problems. He views AI as a capable junior engineer that still needs supervision.

According to recent reports, "The important thing is knowing what to delegate and when human judgement is needed," Sees mentioned. "We'd be silly to think AI gets everything right, and I know I get a lot of satisfaction out of proving and spotting where it has gone wrong.".

Even so, Sees worries about what happens to the next generation of engineers if AI takes over the routine coding and debugging through which they traditionally learned. Those tasks are "where you learn lessons and build scar tissue," he noted, and companies need to make sure AI becomes part of the learning process, rather than a shortcut.

For Rodgers, what matters is having control over how she uses the technology. As a self-employed developer, she can use AI to automate the parts of a project she finds tedious, while keeping the more interesting problem-solving for herself.

According to recent reports, she believes there's something vital about working through a difficult problem rather than immediately outsourcing it to technology. Friction and discomfort, she noted, are vital parts of the human experience.

According to recent reports, "As humans, we need to feel accomplished in something and part of accomplishment requires challenge," she stated. "Unless we are able to work that through in so many different aspects of life, then you never get the full force of the good feeling on the other side.".
