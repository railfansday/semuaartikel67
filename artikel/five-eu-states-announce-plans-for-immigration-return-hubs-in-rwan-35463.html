Germany, Austria, Netherlands, Denmark and Greece likely to face challenges to policy criticised as ‘grave moral failing’.

Five EU member states have unveiled intends to set up an offshore centre in Rwanda for people with no legal right to stay in Europe, in a pilot scheme that is expected to face legal challenges and has been denounced as “a grave moral failing”.

The five countries – Germany, Austria, the Netherlands, Denmark and Greece – are the first to seek to use the EU’s newly agreed returns law that allows people who have exhausted all avenues of appeal against their removal from Europe to be sent to a third country.

In a statement released on Monday the five EU countries mentioned “concrete negotiations with partner country Rwanda will begin for implementation”, saying the scheme would make “an important contribution to further curbing illegal migration, dismantling the business model of smugglers and relieving the burden on our population”.

It has been revealed that officials hope to send 100 people to Rwanda from early next year to test the model. The scheme will apply to adults whose claims to asylum have been rejected or others with no legal right to live in the EU.

In theory, according to diplomatic sources, migrants would stay in a reception centre in Rwanda for a couple of months, before choosing to integrate into life in the Great Lakes country or return to their home nation.

Unlike the failed scheme pursued by the British Conservative government in Rwanda, or Italy’s agreement with Albania, it will not apply to people pursuing asylum claims.

It has been revealed that the reception centre, which would be an open facility, was not intended to house people long term but to accommodate regular flows of people, the countries remarked. Diplomatic sources said the EU countries were not using British-funded facilities built in Kigali.

The governments involved argue it will protect the European asylum system while removing the incentive for irregular migrants to come to Europe.

Denmark’s immigration minister, Morten Bødskov, whose government has pioneered a tough line on migration widely emulated across the EU, noted it was “a huge breakthrough”.

The German interior minister, Alexander Dobrindt, stated: “Return hubs offer a positive approach for people without the right to stay and with no prospects in Europe, whose countries of origin do not cooperate in the repatriation process.”.

Industry observers note that andy Burnham told reporters last week that the UK had not had direct discussions with European governments involved in return hubs but “we will watch that with interest, learning from what went on in the past. The public want us to do whatever we can to deal with illegal immigration.”.

Critics have highlighted the ethical difficulties of striking an agreement with Rwanda’s repressive government, which was found in 2024 by the US state department to allow state security forces and civilian officials to commit human rights abuses with impunity, while also raising concerns about freedom of expression and forced labour.

Spain’s socialist prime minister, Pedro Sanchez, remarked on Monday the move had no “future or humanity”. “I don’t see any future or humanity in that. It’s not the solution,” Sanchez, an outspoken defender of immigration in a Europe that has tilted to the right in recent years, told Telecinco television.

Sources indicate that the French Green MEP Mélissa Camara denounced the agreement between the five countries and Rwanda as “an agreement of shame” and “a grave moral failing”. She stated: “Under this agreement, people will be deported thousands of kilometres away from the European Union, with no guarantees regarding the compliance with their rights.”.

Industry observers note that alain Berset, the secretary general of the Council of Europe, the continent’s human rights body, stated it was not possible “to be so precise a priori” about what any return hubs proposal meant. Speaking to the Guardian before the announcement, he said all members of the Council of Europe were committed to the European convention on human rights “and this must be the lighthouse” for all discussions on return hubs.

“On concrete cases, then we will have to see if this is compliant with the convention,” he stated. He stressed that “all countries must be able to define their migration policy”.

Officials involved in setting up the migration centres expect legal challenges, but say the scheme is grounded in human rights law.

Industry observers note that rights organisations say the scheme is bound to fail. “Member states may call them ‘return hubs’ or even ‘welcome centres’, but no euphemism can hide the violence of uprooting people from the life they’ve been building in Europe, and sending them to a country they don’t even know, without family, networks or meaningful prospects,” remarked Chiara Catelli of Picum, a pan-European organisation that campaigns for the rights of undocumented migrants.
