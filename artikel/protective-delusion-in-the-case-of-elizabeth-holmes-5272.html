Protective Delusion in the Case of Elizabeth Holmes

The documentary You Can See Everything has sparked debate about whether incarcerated entrepreneur Elizabeth Holmes is delusional or manipulative. Delusions are fixed, false beliefs. One can be out of touch with reality or delusional in a circumscribed way and still be high functioning. A "manipulator" exploits others in a calculated way.

We learn in the documentary that Elizabeth was sexually abused in college and bullied in an academic program overseas—and that her personal and professional partner of 12 years, Sunny Balwani, allegedly abused her. Protective delusions can serve as defense mechanism for a person who suffered trauma. In some cases, they are adaptive and even a survival tool. Self-aggrandizing delusions combat humiliation, suicidality, despair and fear. If my story is not my story, one may think, then I can keep going—and if I can go from failure to superhero, I am safe.

Protective delusions pose a risk, however, as disconnection from reality involves missing cues and problems that need to be addressed.

By age 19, Holmes was living the dream. She and Balwani built Theranos into a billion-dollar company. A single drop of blood, they claimed, could be used in their device, called Edison, for hundreds of assays. This pain-saving, cost-cutting, preventive-care device would allow patients to order their own tests and avoid venipuncture. Early detection would foster greater health. Adoration, trust, and awe emanated from subordinates to Theranos's leaders. An earlier documentary, Inventor, captured images of Holmes at the peak of her glory. Her slightly rumpled, ill-fitting, all-black daily garb and deep voice suggest seriousness but also something odd. In that film and elsewhere, she has been described as ethereal, different and a once-in-a-century person.

According to recent reports, holmes's invention created a savior-like persona for her. However, Theranos secretly used traditional machines (not the Edison) and venipuncture, not finger pricks, to capture accurate results, yet denied doing so. Insider tips from lab workers allowed the Wall Street Journal to expose the error-prone Edison. The firm crashed, Holmes and Balwani were convicted of wire fraud and sentenced to 10-years plus in prison.

Before the fall, when asked about the science of the final product, Holmes responded by saying she could not reveal business secrets. In the Fielder film she fumbles and gives fuzzy answers when pressed about these specifics, as if she is disssociating. She still believes in her invention. Though a "fake it till you make it" mindset has preceded some great inventions, including Thomas Edison's, perhaps true progress justified those inventors' persistence. Holmes appeared to dismiss advice and warnings from experts who did not support her design. Some insisted that success was impossible in its present form. Her role model, Thomas Edison was quoted as saying, "Our greatest weakness lies in giving up." It may have felt empowering to identify with him and do as he did.

It has been revealed that the Edison may have been ill-conceived from the get-go. Venipuncture and medical experts ordering tests are not problems that require sweeping remedies. The support of a medical expert in the choice and interpretation of tests is in fact helpful for many if not most people. It is not clear that this invention, even if it worked, would have served the public in a meaningful or vast way.

What was going on in Elizabeth Holmes's mind then and now? Even if she is manipulating, what is the benefit of deceiving others now? She owes $450 million and so she is worth zero. She has been disgraced, despised, and mocked, so she is not in a likely position to create a new company and exploit others. Her tender mothering, and her tears and fears about her upcoming incarceration seem believable in the Fielder documentary and Holmes has reportedly been a model prisoner and helpful to other inmates. Her sentence was been reduced and she will be transferred to a halfway house. Former inmates have said kind things about her.

Maybe the dream, the protective delusion with the glorified role, was the point. Holmes repeatedly claims it was never about the money. Still, ignoring expert advice is somewhat irrational. Surrounding yourself with worshippers is dangerous. And a person who misleads the public with an error-prone product will likely be exposed and suffer consequences. Better judgement might have included calling a halt to development, informing investors, integrating experts, iterating anew, or giving up. Holmes destroyed her own life, in a big way, at least as much as anyone else's.

Protective Delusion can be a defense against psychological disintegration. Though it is a form of disassociation, it is stabilizing. Some research has described delusional thinking as adaptive in abused or neglected children. Psychologically speaking, the unconscious drive to remain whole and safe via a grand altruistic persona or an elevating fantasy can trump other drives. The grandiosity can include sweeping altruism.

Industry observers note that poor judgment, insight, and reality testing are associated with delusional thinking. We cannot know if trauma led to protective delusion for Holmes. What was she like before all this happened? Was there a personality change? Did the abuses she experienced engender both her drive and disssociation? Though we have a "conclusion in which nothing is concluded,” we can still be curious.
