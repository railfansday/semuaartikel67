Recent findings suggest that chris Radkowski is a governance, risk, and compliance (GRC) expert at Pathlock. He brings extensive experience in enterprise security, application access governance, and compliance, including nearly 17 years at SAP. His work focuses on how organizations manage access risk as business systems evolve and AI agents take on more operational responsibility.

Sources indicate that pathlock provides identity and access governance for ERP systems and other business-critical applications. Its platform combines access risk analysis, compliant provisioning, access certification, segregation-of-duties controls, and continuous monitoring to help organizations govern human and non-human identities and maintain audit readiness.

You spent nearly 17 years at SAP leading governance, risk, compliance, and identity initiatives before joining Pathlock. How has your view of access governance evolved as enterprises move from managing human consumers to managing AI agents that can independently interact with critical business systems?

My time at SAP was about mastering a known problem of humans interacting with applications, requesting access, getting provisioned, and managers periodically reviewing whether that access still made sense. It was complex, but the actors were predictable.

AI agents break that model and expose upcoming risks for organizations. They’re the fastest-growing class of non-human identities, and they don’t request access the way a person does. They’re granted broad, standing access and then act on it hundreds or thousands of times faster than any human could. That changes the core question of governance. For decades, we focused on controlling who could get into a system. With agents, the harder problem is understanding what happened after access was granted because an agent can execute an entire chain of transactions before anyone notices something’s off.

Pathlock research backs this up. More than half of organizations (52%) can’t fully verify what their AI agents are doing across business systems, and roughly half (48%) can’t trace agent activity end-to-end. That’s a governance gap is tolerable with machines because you had time to catch a bad decision. However, it’s not tolerable at machine speed.

Recent findings suggest that traditional identity governance was largely designed around employees, contractors, and administrators. What fundamentally changes when the “identity” requesting or exercising access is an autonomous AI agent?

Everything about the trust model changes. Traditional identity governance assumed a person behind every request. Someone with a job function, a manager, a reason to need access, and a limited number of actions they could physically take in a day. You could build controls around that, such as role-based access, periodic reviews, segregation of duties, all calibrated to human speed and human judgment.

Recent findings suggest that an autonomous agent doesn’t fit that model. It’s not requesting access the way an employee does. It’s often granted broad, standing permissions up front so it can do its job across systems. And once it has that access, it can act far faster than any person could, executing chains of transactions, modifying records, approving workflows, all without a human in the loop at each step.

According to recent reports, that breaks several assumptions governance was built on. First, who is requesting access stops being the interesting question. An agent’s identity doesn’t always map cleanly to a single person or role, and the same agent might act on behalf of different business processes at different times. Second, and more notable, oversight shifts from access to action. It’s no longer enough to know an agent is authorized to touch the finance system. You need to know what it did there, whether that was appropriate, and whether you’d be able to reconstruct that trail after the fact. Furthermore, AI agents that act on behalf of the user are even harder to detect, since the activity appears as if the human user was behind the UI.

It has been revealed that pathlock research highlights most organizations aren’t there yet. 79% have no dedicated AI governance team, and roughly half can’t fully verify or trace what their agents are doing across systems. That gap is really the core of the challenge. Governance built for human speed and human judgment simply hasn’t caught up to identities that act on their own, continuously.

AI agents may need broad access across ERP, finance, HR, procurement, and other enterprise systems to complete complex workflows. How can organizations give agents enough authority to be useful without creating excessive privileges or latest security vulnerabilities?

This is really a least privilege problem at machine scale, and it’s the tension every organization deploying agents will run into. The instinct is to over-provision and give the agent broad access up front, so it never hits a wall mid-workflow. That’s exactly how you end up with an agent that can approve a vendor payment, alter that vendor’s banking details, and close out the transaction, when it only ever needed to do one of those things.

It has been revealed that the fix is fundamentally similar to what we’ve always preached in access governance. Segregation of duties, least privilege, entitlements scoped to the task and the moment rather than standing indefinitely, but it must be automated and enforced continuously, because no human team can review permissions at the speed agents operate. An agent handling invoice matching doesn’t need open access to the general ledger, it needs exactly what that workflow requires, for exactly as long as it’s running. And the same SoD principles that apply to people apply here too. An agent that can both initiate and approve a transaction has recreated a classic violation, just faster.

None of that matters without traceability. Visibility into what an agent did has to be a first-class requirement, not something bolted on after an incident. Done well, tightly scoped, well-governed access makes an agent more useful, because its output can be trusted without someone double-checking everything behind it. The organizations getting this right are building governance into the deployment from day one.

Sources indicate that segregation of duties has traditionally prevented a single person from controlling conflicting parts of a business process. How should companies rethink segregation-of-duties policies when AI agents can execute multiple steps of a workflow autonomously?

Recent findings suggest that traditional SoD assumed one person, one role, and a workflow slow enough that a second person would naturally check the first person’s work at the next step. Agents dissolve that assumption. A single agent can be built to handle requisition, approval, and payment in one continuous run, which means the conflict controls we relied on for decades can simply be automated away, not by intent, but by convenience.

The rethink starts with applying the same logic to agents that we’ve always applied to people. No single identity, human or machine, should control both sides of a transaction that’s meant to have independent checks. In practice that means mapping SoD rules at the task level rather than the role level, so an agent that initiates a payment is structurally barred from also approving it, even if both actions technically fall under one automated workflow. It also means treating agent-to-agent handoffs as seriously as human handoffs. If one agent approves what another agent submitted, you need assurance that’s a genuine, independent check and not two instances of the same logic rubber-stamping each other.

Sources indicate that the harder shift is monitoring. With people, SoD violations usually surface in periodic access reviews. With agents acting continuously, you need conflict detection running in real time, evaluating what an agent is doing across a workflow as it happens. Companies that only think about SoD at the point of granting access are already behind. The conflicts that matter now show up in execution.

Should enterprises treat AI agents, bots, and other non-human identities differently from human users when it comes to provisioning, access certification, monitoring, and revoking privileges, or should essentially the same governance framework apply?

Industry observers note that the underlying principles should be the same. Least privilege, segregation of duties, certification, and the ability to revoke access on demand. What must change is how those principles get implemented, because agents don’t behave like people.

Provisioning must be scoped to the task and granted in the moment. Certification can’t stay a quarterly checkbox either. A person’s role changes slowly; an agent’s effective access can shift with every task, so certification needs to be closer to continuous. Monitoring is the biggest gap today. We built audit trails around human behavior, and those baselines don’t hold for something executing thousands of actions an hour. Revocation must be instant and complete, since an agent with even a day of residual access at machine speed is a very different exposure than a slow offboarding for a person.

So, it’s less about a separate framework for agents and more about applying the same one with a much higher bar for speed and precision, since gaps close in on you far faster than they did with human identities.

Recent findings suggest that permissions tell security teams what an identity can do, but AI agents introduce a greater need to understand what they actually did. How vital will continuous behavioral and transaction-level monitoring become as agentic AI adoption grows?

It’s going to become the center of gravity for governance. Permissions were always the easier half of the problem. They’re static, auditable, and change infrequently. What an identity does with those permissions was the harder half even with humans, and we mostly got away with lighter monitoring there because people move at human speed and leave a paper trail as they go.

Agents remove that cushion. An agent can be perfectly entitled and still cause damage, not by exceeding its permissions, but by using them in a way nobody anticipated, executing a sequence of legitimate actions that adds up to a bad outcome. That’s why the question must shift from “was this authorized” to “was this appropriate,” and you can only answer that by watching behavior and transactions as they happen.

Recent findings suggest that this is also where the traceability gap we’re already seeing becomes dangerous. Today, close to half of organizations can’t trace agent activity end-to-end, which means if something does go wrong, they’re reconstructing it after the fact rather than catching it in progress. As agentic adoption grows, that’s not a sustainable position. Continuous, transaction-level monitoring is what turns an agent from a black box you hope is behaving into a system you can stand behind, with a real record of what it did and why.

Sources indicate that cloud migrations and moves to platforms such as SAP S/4HANA can significantly reshape roles, integrations, and permissions. Where do you see access risks most commonly emerging during these transformations, and how can organizations prevent legacy access problems from being carried into the upcoming environment?

The biggest risk is siloed governance during the migration itself. In a legacy environment like ECC, GRC often sits separately from the technical teams running the migration, and when that happens, organizations carry years of accumulated roles and risk straight into the newly introduced system rather than redesigning them. Our 2025 Digital Transformation & Access Risk Report found that 52% of organizations don’t blueprint GRC controls early in the migration project. That’s the root cause of most of what goes wrong.

Sources indicate that people tend to treat a move to S/4HANA as a technical upgrade, when it’s really a full business transformation. Roles get restructured, integrations multiply, and new transaction codes get introduced, so this is the moment to redesign access from scratch. Once bloated roles and stale SoD conflicts get carried into the new environment, they become far harder to unwind, because now they’re embedded in a system everyone assumes was built cleanly.

It has been revealed that preventing that means bringing GRC into the migration from day one. That includes running SoD analysis and access risk assessments in parallel with the technical build and giving teams real-time visibility into access risk throughout the transports moving from development through QA and into production. Organizations that treat governance as part of the transformation itself come out the other side with a system that’s easier to audit and maintain.

Many companies still rely on periodic and often manual user-access reviews. As the number of human and machine identities grows, do you expect access governance to shift toward continuous, automated controls rather than point-in-time certification exercises?

Recent findings suggest that yes, and I’d say it’s less a prediction than something already underway. Periodic, manual user access reviews were built for a world where the number of identities was manageable and roles changed slowly enough that a quarterly or annual checkpoint made sense. Neither of those conditions holds anymore. The sheer volume of human and machine identities has outgrown what any team can meaningfully review by hand, and a lot of organizations are still running that process through spreadsheets and email approvals, which was already a strain before agents entered the picture.

Sources indicate that point-in-time certification also tells you someone’s access was appropriate on the day it was reviewed but notes nothing about the 90 days in between. With the pace at which access changes now, especially with machine identities that pick up new entitlements as workflows demand them, that gap is where real risk accumulates undetected.

It has been revealed that continuous, automated controls close that gap by evaluating access and usage as conditions change. That means the review becomes exception-based rather than exhaustive, with automation surfacing what’s changed or looks anomalous so people can focus their judgment where it’s needed instead of rubber-stamping thousands of unchanged entitlements. Organizations still doing this manually are working harder than they need to and are most likely to miss the access risk that matters.

Boards and senior executives increasingly have oversight responsibility for cybersecurity and compliance, but identity risk can be highly technical. What metrics should leadership teams track to understand whether access to critical enterprise systems is actually creating material business risk?

Industry observers note that boards don’t need technical details, just metrics translated into business risk. A few matter most. Open segregation-of-duties violations in finance, procurement, and payroll since those map directly to fraud risk. The percentage of identities, human and machine, covered by certification and monitoring, since ungoverned access is a blind spot regardless of how strong other controls are. And time-to-revoke, which indicates how long exposure windows stay open after someone leaves or a role changes.

I’d also add visibility into AI agent activity specifically, since roughly half of organizations still can’t fully verify or trace what their agents are doing. Together, these tell leadership whether access is genuinely governed or just assumed to be.

Looking ahead several years, as autonomous agents become capable of initiating transactions and making operational decisions across enterprise applications, what will a mature governance model for AI agents need to look like?

According to recent reports, A mature model must treat agents as full participants in governance. That means every agent has a clear identity, a defined purpose, and entitlements scoped to that purpose rather than broad standing access granted for convenience.

Access must be dynamic, expanding and contracting with the task rather than sitting as a permanent grant, and segregation of duties must be enforced structurally, so no single agent can both initiate and approve consequential actions. None of that works without continuous, transaction-level monitoring, since permissions only tell you what an agent can do, not what it did, and that distinction becomes the whole ballgame once agents are making operational decisions on their own.

Accountability also must be built in from the start. Every action needs to be traceable to the agent that took it, the data it acted on, and ideally the reasoning behind the decision, so organizations can explain outcomes after the fact rather than reconstruct them under pressure. And governance can’t sit solely with security teams anymore. It has to be a shared responsibility across business, risk, and compliance functions, with board-level visibility into how much operational authority agents hold.
