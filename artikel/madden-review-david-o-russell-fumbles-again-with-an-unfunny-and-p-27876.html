‘Madden’ Review: David O. Russell Fumbles Again with an Unfunny and Purposeless Football Biopic

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

“Madden” is technically a David O. Russell film, but all involved seem to want to downplay that fact. Amazon MGM Studios, in their infinite wisdom, has decided to emphasize star Nicolas Cage’s performance as the larger-than-life NFL coach and sports broadcaster in its scant promotional material. While the trailer includes an obligatory “From the director of ‘Silver Linings Playbook’ and ‘American Hustle’” title card, the poster features Cage and Christian Bale, who plays Oakland Raiders coach Al Davis, flashing their Super Bowl rings, and no mention of Russell. Industry observers note that to be fair, this isn’t the worst promotional strategy. Russell may be a problematic figure, but it wasn’t so long ago when starring in one of his films almost guaranteed an actor awards attention. During a brief five-year period in the 2010s, his work garnered Oscar wins for Christian Bale and Jennifer Lawrence, and nominations for Amy Adams, Bradley Cooper, and Robert De Niro. Putting aside the uneven quality of his work, Russell certainly afforded actors the opportunity to “make choices” in lavish studio productions, which often amounted to adopting broad accents and wearing flashy costumes, and receive acclaim for their efforts. It’s probably why people keep working with him in spite of the numerous incidents of on-set mistreatment (on top of a sexual assault allegation) that have continued to this day.

Russell may shifted to streaming but his commitment to allowing actors to go substantial or go home remains sacrosanct. In “Madden,” Cage wore 30 pounds of prosthetics on top of the 25 pounds he gained for the role. He certainly resembles the man, at least if you ignore Cage’s actual face. Ditto Bale, who adopts Davis’s Noo-Yawk accent and bold fashion sense. But it’s all in service of a film so milquetoast that it will make you beg for the sheer personality exhibited in “Amsterdam,” which at least had the decency to have a narrative and occasionally spark tension and generally be about something.

Recent findings suggest that here’s what happens in Russell’s eleventh feature: head coach John Madden leads the Oakland Raiders to finally beat their longstanding rivals Pittsburgh Steelers in the 1976 AFC Championship, after which they win the Super Bowl. Following his diagnosis of a bleeding ulcer in his stomach, Madden permanently retires from his coaching career to be with his family. Realizing he’s a stranger in his own home after spending too much time away, he quickly transitions into sports broadcasting where he becomes a television mainstay. To secure his legacy and pass on his legendary playbook, he eventually lends his likeness to EA Sports for the “Madden” video game franchise, which becomes one of the most profitable on the market. He then lives happily ever after.

In a recent development, if that sounds like a film with little-to-no conflict, you would be correct. Part of the problem is that Madden, while being an American institution and a legendary NFL coach, isn’t really suited to be the subject of a biopic, largely because the details of his life are fairly free of drama or intrigue. He’s the kind of figure who should be the lead of a Saturday-morning cartoon where he helps kids solve mysteries using the power of his football mind. Rather than impose a zany plot onto his life or make up outlandish shit for entertainment purposes, which he’s known to do, Russell dutifully relates the major events in his life.

Sources indicate that the bare-bones emotional foundation of “Madden” takes the form of the two loves in the eponymous character’s life. The first is with his wife Virginia (Kathryn Hahn), who longs for John to recommit to fatherhood and their marriage, at least until it becomes clear that he needs football to be a functioning human being. Hahn’s established joie de vivre ensures Viriginia is slightly more interesting than an archetypal long-suffering wife character, but, alas, her general lack of chemistry with Cage dampens their tender scenes together, and their intermittent marital troubles are too fleeting and half-hearted to make any dramatic purchase.

The second, and arguably most pivotal, is with Davis, whose slightly-problematic bromance with Madden serves as the film’s primary source of drama. Russell’s film briefly flirts with an interesting thread by portraying Davis as a devil on Madden’s shoulder, someone who seemingly wants him to continue coaching forever, his health and family life be damned. He constantly tries to pressure Madden into returning to the league long after he retires in the vein of an old buddy who wants you to stay out for “just one more beer.” Despite his size, Madden frequently shrinks in Davis’s presence, who knows just what to say to get him to do what he wants.

But while Russell gestures towards connecting Davis’s “maverick” professional style, which made him uniquely qualified for the world of sports ownership but nevertheless created controversy within the league, and his friendship with Madden, he ultimately chickens out from characterizing him as a truly malign influence. (“Madden” just isn’t set up to be a film with a villain or an antagonist of any kind.) Instead, Russell depicts John and Virginia Madden and Al and Carol (Sienna Miller) Davis as tightknit couple friends, admittedly a type of relationship that rarely receives the big-screen treatment, whose ups and downs are manageable because, well, they really just love each other.

Recent findings suggest that it’s worth noting that “Madden” is technically a comedy. It certainly presents as one, featuring well-proven settings for laughter in sports comedies like the sidelines, the locker room, and the announcing booth. Intermittent jokes pop up in these spaces, though I’m genuinely hard-pressed to remember anything specific because much of it suffocates under Cage’s affectionately-caricatured performance. Russell trusts that the sight of the actor in a full-body fat suit is enough for audiences. Granted, Cage seems to palpably enjoy embodying Madden’s flamboyant energy and imposing figure, even if his inconsistent voice acting, complete with slurred speech and vowel-swallowing, occasionally threatens to tip into outright cruelty. (“Madden” features the real article speaking at the film’s end and it’s downright absurd how much Cage exaggerates his diction.).

It has been revealed that if there’s one semi-sophisticated bit in the film, it’s that Madden’s one-track mind sees the world entirely through football strategy. He literally can’t write “XOXO” to his future wife in their yearbook without turning it into a play design. But Russell doesn’t really develop the idea any further and instead defaults to broad humor that tends to blend together and subsequently die on the vine. These often materialize as sight gags, like Bale’s increasingly ridiculous haircuts, or John Mulaney, who plays Electronic Arts founder Trip Hawkins, costumed to resemble Steve Jobs. Sometimes it amounts to Shane Gillis delivering Shane Gillis-style lines, sometimes with a perm or donning a wizard’s outfit, or former running back Marshawn Lynch dressed like a nerd.

Sources indicate that on paper, Russell is actually a neat fit for a film like “Madden.” He made his bones directing abrasive ensemble screwball comedies. He’s directed period biopics, including one that’s also a sports drama, and one of his most acclaimed films involves football fandom. Sadly, “Madden” features few Russellisms beyond tacky ’70s and ’80s fashion and some mild domestic discord. He’s replaced his typically messy, chaotic atmosphere for an overly genial one.

Industry observers note that anyone who has some familiarity with screen passes or end-arounds likely learned it downwind from Madden, who used whiteboards to make complicated play schemes accessible to the viewing public. His impact extends well into the realm of pop culture, like the Miller Lite commercial where he breaks through the wall, the custom-manufactured bus in which he drove around the country due to his well-documented fear of flying, and his famous turducken. “Madden” covers all of that material without offering any insight other than the security of the man’s legacy. The Russell who made “I Heart Huckabees” has long faded away, but it’s still a shame that he’s been reduced to directing one of the oddest and blandest examples of brand management in recent memory.

Amazon MGM Studios will release “Madden” in select theaters and on Prime Video on Wednesday, November 18.
