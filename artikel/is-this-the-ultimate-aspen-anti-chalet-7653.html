Is This the Ultimate Aspen Anti-Chalet?

Above: The primary bathroom of a home in Aspen, Colorado, designed by Rafael de Cárdenas with architecture by SAOTA. Custom tub with Dornbracht fittings; ceramic vase by artist Kazunori Hamana; side table by Rafael Prieto.

“We’re having a crisis of taste,” architect and designer Rafael de Cárdenas is in the midst of saying. “There’s a sameness. It’s all a historical mishmash.” He’s at breakfast in Manhattan drinking a cinnamon latte (the restaurant did not have the turmeric he requested). He has already completed his run in Central Park and his gratitude list in his Day One journal app, and he’s on day 735 following a visit to the Hoffman Institute—the famously mysterious weeklong “personal growth” retreat. “I feel like we’re in The Handmaid’s Tale,” he says. “AI is happening, but we’re going to pretend we’re designing manor houses in the English countryside in the 19th century?” Later he adds, “I have never been interested in recreating the past.”.

According to recent reports, so when longtime client Rachel Dorrell asked him to create a mountain house in Aspen for her growing family, no one expected a typical ski chalet. From her first visit to the plot of land, she realized the views of the town and the slopes beyond would be unmatched. Today those “crazy views” are framed by windows so large it’s hard to tell where rooms end and the outside begins. De Cárdenas, who collaborated with SAOTA architects to create the modernist structure, set about realizing Dorrell’s vision of “Colorado, but refined,” as she puts it. “Glamorous ’80s apartment” is how he describes the results.

“The mood board was Angelo Donghia,” that great of American design who worked with the likes of Liza Minnelli. “And Beetlejuice, always Beetlejuice! And Mickey Rourke’s apartment in 91/2 Weeks.” A cheetah-print rug sits in the library, while sculpted white custom sofas anchor the immense yet minimalist living room. “So many people have asked, ‘Shouldn’t there be art above the fireplace?’ ” de Cárdenas notes. “I’m like, ‘Why?’ ”.

Following the rules has never been his style. It has been 20 years since he debuted his design firm, after studying at UCLA and assisting architect Greg Lynn at Vienna’s Angewandte institute and MAK museum, then returning to his native Newly introduced York. The son of an erstwhile fashion PR executive and an actuary, de Cárdenas aspired to work for David Rockwell or for Alex de Betak’s fashion production company, where he once applied for a job. In the end he did a stint at Calvin Klein and worked as a production designer for films and commercials. Then a friend asked for help redoing a kitchen, something he did “kind of at night,” de Cárdenas remembers. Next came his first real client: actress Parker Posey. It was a revelation. “She was the first person I ever had to make window treatments for. I was like, ‘What the f***?’ I remember looking at a window and being like, ‘What is this supposed to look like?’ I didn’t understand that residential interior design is, essentially, fabric.”.

It has been revealed that around the same time, he landed his first major corporate client, Nike, and did the design of aNYthing, a Lower East Side boutique that became a nexus for skaters, artists, and musicians in the late 2000s. “No one was doing stores like that at the time,” claims creative director Jen Brill, who worked with de Cárdenas on several Nike projects. “There was nothing minimal about it. It was loud, it was fun. It was the first store I saw that was like retail theater.”.

Another friend, Felix Burrichter, a curator and the founder of Pin-Up architecture magazine, recalls de Cárdenas’s early fascination with the Memphis Group architects (“when it was still considered bad taste,” Burrichter says) as well as his willingness to experiment with materials, such as applying duct tape patterns to the walls of a Jeffrey Deitch exhibition in Rome, or wrapping an Ikea chair with fluorescent gaffer tape. “For the aNYthing store, I physically built a lot of those things, because there was no one to do it and no budget,” de Cárdenas says. He sometimes laments not being so hands-on anymore, now that he has a team of 18 based in Newly introduced York and Paris. “There was a lot of energy in those projects.”.

“His work is like Limelight–meets–Park Avenue,” says Burrichter, who calls de Cárdenas the heir to ELLE Decor A-List designer Peter Marino, a maestro who works with significant private clients alongside luxury behemoths such as Dior and Chanel. De Cárdenas may have moved from duct tape to gold leaf applied by European artisans, “but there is always that tension in Raf’s work,” Burrichter says, “the binary between decorating and being an architect, between uptown and the club.”.

Dorrell, for her part, became aware of de Cárdenas a decade ago, when she and her husband purchased a home in the West Village that he had staged. “We were like, ‘Who did the interiors and who did the art? It’s perfect.’ ” She indicates they were drawn to the ’70s vibe and the peachy hues, an unusual shade at the time. “We didn’t change a thing.” Since then they have worked together on homes in Austin, Newly introduced York, and right now their largest yet, in Palm Beach. Dorrell sends “rapid-fire” texts of inspiration ranging from tiled walls at Lake Como’s Villa d’Este to barstools in Paris. “Raf cracks me up—we are brutally honest with each other,” she says, explaining that her taste skews more traditional, while his is often edgier.

In a recent development, “Some of my projects have moments that are ugly, and they excite me,” he states. “Because something truly excellent, something that is pushing boundaries, is not going to land pleasurably at first.” He points to Madonna, a frequent touchstone and source of inspiration for him. “The first time you saw Blond Ambition, the first time you saw Madonna with a high ponytail and with the headset, it was a little jarring. The thick eyebrow and the cone bra.” He often finds himself thinking, Where is that moment now?

It has been revealed that despite his concerns about the flattening effect of the algorithm, he’s still happy he’s in the business of taste. “I can’t believe I was lucky enough to land here, because I didn’t know what interior design even was,” he says. “I like my job. I’m grateful for my job. I want people to say, ‘He’s good at his job.’ ”.

The Dorrells might concur: “ ‘We’re not doing another project,’ ” Rachel recalls her husband saying about Aspen. “Now he’s like, ‘Thank god,’ because it’s so awesome.”.
