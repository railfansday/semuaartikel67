That waterlogged atmosphere means that even without El Niño, there’s been less of a distinct rainy season in California, and more rapid flip-flop between intense episodes of rain dumped on the ground followed by periods of heat and dryness. “It’s these strange, erratic patterns that we’ve never seen before,” she claims. And the state is bracing for how the Super El Niño will clash with this new climate normal.

1997 was the year that the term “El Niño” shot to global fame. It was the most powerful El Niño in recorded history, and the first to be scientifically monitored in real time, continuously tracked by satellites and breathlessly reported on by news outlets. El Niño’s rise to household name status was even cemented in pop culture by an appearance on Saturday Night Live, in a classic sketch by comedian Chris Farley.

But the event was no laughing matter. It caused global devastation during its brief reign. El Niño events form as westward-blowing trade winds over the eastern tropical Pacific Ocean weaken, allowing warm water to accumulate at the surface. Those waters can then release massive amounts of heat to the atmosphere, altering the position of the Pacific jet stream and helping to disrupt global weather patterns.

In a recent development, in 1997–1998, global temperatures spiked by 1.5 degrees Celsius on average. Along with the torrential rains in California, the event brought brutal drought to Southeast Asia and deadly summer floods to central Europe and Peru. Record-breaking floods also hit East Africa, which in turn triggered a surge in mosquito-borne Rift Valley fever. The oceans were so warm that 16 percent of the world’s reefs died.

In all, the El Niño–related weather extremes were linked to more than 22,000 fatalities around the world, including nearly 200 in the United States, and an estimated $5.7 trillion in global economic losses.

In a recent development, but there is a general overall pattern to how El Niño tends to affect weather around the planet, Di Liberto indicates. For example, there tend to be more tropical cyclones in the Pacific Ocean, and fewer in the Atlantic — a pattern also observed in 2026.

California’s water management system was also originally designed specifically to simply shunt excess water away from residential areas, rather than try to capture the water or help it infiltrate into aquifers, Grenier notes. But that system worked for a different climate, one that wasn’t so prone to “weather whiplash” — wild, sudden swings between extremely wet and extremely dry conditions.

Alongside punishing, drenching back-to-back storms called atmospheric rivers, the state has also experienced frequent episodes of drought, including two recent severe events, one lasting from 2012 to 2016 and another from 2021 to 2025.

And the heavy rains don’t necessarily help; the torrents of water mostly run off across the surface instead of infiltrating soils to reach the underground aquifer.

Such hydroclimate whiplash isn’t unique to California by any means. It’s a global problem, and it’s becoming more common. A small increase in air temperatures can give a big boost to the atmosphere’s capacity to hold water vapor, increasing that capacity by about 7 percent for every 1 degree C of temperature increase. All of that water vapor has contributed to an increase in extreme precipitation events around the globe since the mid-20th century.

Precipitation is just half of the problem. Rising air temperatures also make the air “thirstier,” causing it to draw even more moisture out of vegetation, soil and surface bodies of water during drier periods — which makes the land more vulnerable to severe drought and wildfire.

It’s now clear that these hydrodynamic shifts have been increasing the frequency of weather whiplash events around the world over the last 75 years or so, scientists reported in 2025. And that whiplash is likely to increase as the world warms more.

Kelvin waves are massive, slow-moving swells of warm ocean water, part of the complicated interplay between wind and water that produces the El Niño Southern Oscillation, the overall weather pattern that includes El Niño and counterpart La Niña events, states Josh Willis, an oceanographer at NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Pasadena, Calif.

According to recent reports, as prevailing west-blowing winds weaken over the tropical eastern Pacific, Willis says, water normally pushed west by the winds instead sloshes back eastward.
