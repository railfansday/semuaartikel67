According to recent reports, the world may not have been champing at the bit for another “Meet the Parents” sequel with quite the same enthusiasm that defined the hype for “GTA VI” (pretty close, tho!), but anyone who’s been itching for another crazaaaazy weekend between neurotic nurse Gaylord “Greg” Focker and his imperious ex-CIA father-in-law Jack Byrnes has had to wait even longer for their prayers to be answered. Was it worth it?

It has been revealed that well, let’s do a quick comparison between two cultural phenomenons that I’m definitely not just lumping together in a desperate — some might say “heroic” — attempt to write an involving review lede for a movie that never makes even the slightest effort to earn your attention. Sources indicate that ahem: On the one hand, it took 13 years for Rockstar Games to produce another numbered installment of their marquee franchise, during which time they leveraged cutting-edge technology and unprecedented financial resources in order to radically iterate upon a proven formula so that the firm’s Baudrillardian allegory of American pathos would map onto an era that has become too shattered for more coherent sense-making. On the other hand, it took 16 years for the combined power of Universal and Paramount (a Skydance Company) to will another “Focker” movie into existence, during which time someone realized that it might be kinda funny if Robert De Niro said the words “Jamba Juice.”.

It has been revealed that and in fairness, they weren’t wrong. A Robert De Niro character shouldn’t know what Jamba Juice is, let alone want to drink there! Not only that, but he correctly emphasizes the “JAM” of it all, the formal precision of which suggests that Jack — a red-blooded Boomer who’s recently become an exercise-obsessed vegan health nut — has developed a sincere degree of respect for the smoothie chain and its various blended offerings, even if it’s taken him 16 years to do so.

Given that John Hamburg’s “Focker In-Law” brings back so much of the franchise’s original cast almost three decades since they first played these roles, the film benefits from a Linklater-esque sense of temporal continuity that invites us to imagine the time between movies as a living space. Don’t be fooled by the gruel-thin story, the parade of self-amused callbacks, or the painfully contrived jokes that substitute for real emotion, this isn’t an opportunistic cash-grab meant to capitalize on our collective appetite for big screen comedies — it’s a family reunion.

It has been revealed that the expectations for a legacy sequel are much lower than they might be for the most expensive video game in history, especially in the context of a franchise that’s sustained by the idea that the surname “Focker” sounds almost indistinguishable from the word “fucker.” (Don’t be embarrassed if you hadn’t put that together before — it’s subtle, and film critics are professionally trained to identify such things). “GTA VI” had to revolutionize interactive entertainment in order to be considered a success. “Focker In-Law” just has to deliver more of the same. It does that over and over and over again; I don’t know about Jack’s nipples, but Greg (Ben Stiller) can certainly milk an old joke until it turns to dust in his hand.

The cinematic equivalent of half-rotten Thanksgiving leftovers that you’d only eat in lieu of having any other food in the house, “Focker In-Law” might be an increasingly rare significant-screen comedy, but it’s endowed with the “whatever”-level apathy of the worst straight-to-streaming junk. Depressingly, that feels like a systemic failure more than a creative one, as Hamburg is a very funny guy who directed several of the better comedies of this century’s pre-Netflix era (e.g. “Along Came Polly”), and he’s far from the only proven talent who gets wasted here.

The basic story — for which Hamburg shares credit with Austen Earl and, curiously, Ilana Glazer — offers a natural riff on the franchise’s initial premise: Greg might wear Radiohead t-shirts and sport facial skin tighter than any scene in the movie around him, but he’s now older than Jack was during the first movie, which means he and his wife Pam (Teri Polo, making the easiest money of her life) have aged into the “Parents” who are due to be “Meet”-ed when their son Henry (an overqualified Skyler Gisondo) brings his massive-eyed latest girlfriend Olivia (Ariana Grande) to Jack’s 80th birthday celebration.

Industry observers note that the joke, and it’s not a bad one, is that Greg — a beta male bozo who’s desperate to make his children and their partners feel welcome — immediately bumps against his probable daughter-in-law, while Jack — a dominant and unyielding patriarch whose “Circle of Trust” is harder to crack than a captured enemy spy — cottons to her from the moment she gets to his house. It helps, of course, that Olivia is an ultra-rigid life coach and former hostage negotiator who shares Jack’s taste for military discipline, as well as his knowledge of different polygraph technologies. Why she’s drawn to a softboi like Greg’s son remains unclear, but the “Wicked”-like theatricality of Grande’s performance is farcical enough for such open questions to be their own reward (it makes sense that her character would be so emotive and intentional), and at the end of the day what matters is just that Greg has a reason to feel rejected all over again.

Industry observers note that he doesn’t take it well! In fact, he acts like a massive asshole about it to a degree that steers the movie away from cringe comedy comfort food and closer towards a character study about a malignant narcissist instead. Stiller is a maestro of self-involved unpleasantness (his work in “Greenberg” and “The Meyerowitz Stories” has never gotten the recognition it deserves), but that kind of toxicity is far more unpleasant in the context of a film that thinks it’s cute and/or easily redeemable.

According to recent reports, in “Meet the Parents,” Greg got into all sorts of wacky trouble because he meant well and cared so much about seeming worthy of Jack’s daughter. In “Focker In-Law,” the hijinks stem from the fact that he’s a scheming monster who’s trying to sabotage his son’s relationship so that Henry might stay closer to home. The clean and simple fun of watching good intentions curdle into misunderstood chaos has been replaced by the dull ache of watching someone try to pass off diagnosable psychosis as the stuff of routine parental anxiety.

Alarm bells start ringing from the very first comic setpiece, in which a classic setup — Greg tries to cut the airplane bathroom line by sneaking from coach into first class — is almost completely undone by the logic gaps that have come to define the character. Why does he impulsively steal Olivia’s birth control out of her bag before he even knows who she is, and how is it possible that such an overbearing dad hasn’t even seen a picture of his son’s very serious upcoming girlfriend? Such questions aren’t really worth answering, and Hamburg at least has the courtesy to distract us from them with a fly-by cameo by Cole Escola, but the vibe is all the way off by the time we even touch down in New Jersey for the main event, and nothing that happens there is remotely inspired enough to redeem the pervasive sense that no one can remember why “Meet the Parents” became a franchise in the first place.

Recent findings suggest that was it Owen Wilson’s amusingly oblivious performance as Pam’s charmed ex-fiancé, Kevin? There’s only one way to find out! Was it Jack’s penchant for lie-detector tests or his affinity for unlovable pets? Let’s give them both a shot and see what sticks. (I was amused by the German shepherd he trains in this film, who sets up a very good joke accusation that I don’t want to spoil here). Maybe it became a phenomenon because people crave manic setpieces that end in a plume of human ashes — we should run that back just in case. Anything to avoid the obvious and enduring coziness of a “home for the holidays” movie, which “Focker In-Law” inexplicably forfeits by setting its story at the height of summer.

It has been revealed that the ballad of Gaylord Focker certainly didn’t become a thing because of Greg’s daughter and her long-term boyfriend, whose presence this movie struggles to accommodate despite the fact that Beanie Feldstein and Eduardo Franco are both eager to help liven things up. In fairness, why open the floor to potentially amusing upcoming characters when you can spend half the film’s runtime agonizing over Greg’s uncomfortably demented attempts to steal the engagement ring that Henry wants to give to Olivia? Some opportunities are just too good to pass up, I suppose.

According to recent reports, that “Focker In-Law” maintains any of its series’ long-faded charm can be attributed to De Niro, who sleepwalked through the previous two sequels but feels revived by Jack’s newfound looseness in this one. The actor delights in playing off Grande’s type-A eagerness, just as his character takes a muted pleasure in how much Olivia causes Greg to crash out. In a comedy as flat, stilted, and generic as this one, De Niro’s still-inimitable star power is almost enough to save the movie from its own reheated cynicism. “JAM-ba Juice.” They actually just go by “Jamba” now. The franchise’s juice is long, long gone.

Universal Pictures will release “Focker In-Law” in theaters on Wednesday, November 25.
