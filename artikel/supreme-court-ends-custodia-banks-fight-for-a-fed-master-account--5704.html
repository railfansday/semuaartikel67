Supreme Court Ends Custodia Bank’s Fight for a Fed Master Account, Leaving the Fed Free to Deny Crypto Banks Access

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The U.S. Supreme Court on Monday denied Custodia Bank’s petition to review the Federal Reserve’s refusal to give the Wyoming bitcoin bank a master account, ending chief executive Caitlin Long’s years-long fight for direct access to the central bank’s payment rails.

The case, Custodia Bank, Inc. v. Federal Reserve Board of Governors, No. 26-62, is listed under “certiorari denied” on the Court’s Monday order list. The denial leaves in place a Tenth Circuit ruling that regional Federal Reserve Banks have discretion to reject even eligible applicants, and it closes the last court path Custodia had to force the Fed’s hand.

A master account is a bank’s account at a Federal Reserve Bank, and the entry point to Fedwire and other payment services. Without one, a state-chartered bank has to rely on a correspondent institution to move money.

Custodia is a Wyoming special purpose depository institution, a charter that bars it from making loans and requires dollar deposits to be fully reserved in liquid assets. After receiving that charter, it applied for a master account at the Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City in October 2020. In January 2021, the Kansas City Fed confirmed the bank was legally eligible and told it there were “no showstoppers.” In August 2021, Custodia also applied for Federal Reserve membership.

While those requests sat pending, the Board of Governors in August 2022 published guidelines for evaluating master-account applications. The rules put firms that are neither federally insured nor supervised by a federal banking agency into “Tier 3,” the group that “generally receive[s] the strictest level of review.” Custodia fell into that bucket.

On January 27, 2023, the Kansas City Fed denied the master account, citing an “unprecedented” business model “narrowly focused on crypto-asset activities” and concluding the risks were “highly likely” to be “inconsistent with safe and sound banking practices.” The same day, the Board denied Custodia’s membership application, saying approval “would be inconsistent with the financial, managerial, and corporate powers factors.”.

It has been revealed that custodia had already sued the Fed and the Kansas City Fed in June 2022 over the delay. On March 29, 2024, U.S. District Judge Scott W. Skavdahl in Wyoming ruled that the Federal Reserve Act gives Reserve Banks discretion to grant or deny master accounts, dismissed Custodia’s Administrative Procedure Act claim for lack of a final agency action, and granted the Kansas City Fed summary judgment.

In a recent development, the Tenth Circuit affirmed on October 31, 2025, in a 2-1 decision Bitcoin Magazine covered at the time. Judge David Ebel, joined by Judge Veronica Rossman, held that Section 342 of the Federal Reserve Act, which indicates a Reserve Bank “may receive” deposits, is discretionary, and that the Monetary Control Act of 1980’s instruction that Fed services “shall be available” to nonmember banks is a pricing rule, not a right to an account.

It has been revealed that tymkovich dissented. “We are bound by the ordinary language of the statute and, in my view, shall means shall,” he wrote. The 1980 law, he said, “mandates access to the Fed’s payment services for all nonmember depository institutions.” On March 13, 2026, the full Tenth Circuit denied rehearing, with Judges Hartz, Tymkovich and Eid voting to grant. Being denied a master account, Tymkovich wrote in that later dissent, is “akin to a death sentence.”.

Custodia’s July petition, filed by Kannon Shanmugam, asked whether the Monetary Control Act “gives presidents of regional Federal Reserve Banks discretionary authority to deny master accounts” to legally eligible nonmember banks. The Board and the Kansas City Fed opposed review. The Court distributed the case for its Nov. 13 conference and turned it down today.

Sources indicate that the practical effect is that a state charter, even one that makes a bank legally eligible, does not guarantee a door into the Fed’s payment system. Crypto-focused banks and other novel charters now have little judicial recourse, at least in the Tenth Circuit, if a Reserve Bank turns them away. Critics have long described that pattern of exclusion, and of banks cutting off crypto firms, as Operation Choke Point 2.0.

Neither Custodia nor Long had commented on the Supreme Court’s denial as of 10:07 a.m. ET Monday.
