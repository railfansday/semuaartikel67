Database Leaks Expose 3.5 Billion Usernames, Passwords, and Documents

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

More than 3.5 billion usernames, passwords, national identity numbers, and documents were left exposed to anyone online in two unrelated databases, according to latest findings shared with WIRED. The massive troves included data from people in dozens of countries. One seemed focused particularly on Chinese citizens and included more than a billion China-linked records.

The findings are the latest in a seemingly endless series of revelations about incidents in which inadequately secured data puts people’s personal information at risk. But the two examples also provide a window into the scale of current cybercriminal data collection—both information that attackers collect by assembling and organizing other datasets, and troves they amass using info-stealing malware.

“Personal data is personal data anywhere in the world. At this point, personal data for pretty much every American is out there somewhere, and if we look at China or elsewhere, they have the same kinds of problems and security risks,” says Greg Pollock, the director of research at security manufacturer UpGuard, who found one of the exposed databases. “The cybercriminal world is reaping the benefit of decades of bad information security practices.”.

In a recent development, the two exposed databases are unconnected but were found at around the same time by two different researchers. Pollock’s discovery contains more than 1.5 billion aggregated documents and details of predominantly Chinese individuals and businesses. The second trove, found by Black Hills Information Security researcher Jeremiah Fowler, contains around 2 billion login credentials, with hundreds of millions linked to user accounts for mainstream United States tech platforms.

According to recent reports, exposed personal data, including usernames and passwords, has always been instrumental for scammers and cybercriminals. However, as AI cybersecurity capabilities fuel faster vulnerability exploitation, more effective scamming, and ransomware attacks, access to personal data and account credentials can be more key than ever to attackers.

Industry observers note that the data discovered by Pollock includes 1.64 billion documents representing personal information from at least hundreds of millions—if not around a billion—people, most of them seemingly in China based on included national ID numbers. According to his analysis, the unsecured database held at least 840 million “distinct” national ID numbers, 300 million phone numbers, around 11 million student enrollment records, and nearly 8 million business registrations. From a sample of the data, both children and deceased individuals were included in the files, Pollock notes.

In a recent development, the China-linked data sat unauthenticated and openly accessible on the open internet in what seemed to be some sort of cybercriminal or gray market infrastructure. Pollock was unable to trace an owner or find contact information to notify anyone about the exposure, but as he was investigating the leak, the database became inaccessible—seemingly as part of a ransomware attack or some sort of destructive campaign. He says the database was actively being updated until the week before he discovered it.

In a recent development, pollock, who has investigated data privacy and breaches for many years, states that he attempted to validate the data during the time it was accessible, but he can’t be certain about its fidelity or whether the same trove has been circulated elsewhere. It is also possible that it contains duplicate records, though Pollock attempted to check for this. He emphasizes that it seemed like the data had been analyzed carefully for maximum value to its owner.

In a recent development, “Someone had done some work to bring together a bunch of data and then normalize it to make it useful,” he indicates. “There’s no legitimate source on Earth that would have all of these things structured in this way, but if you had all of these breaches and brought them together, that would be valuable to an attacker.”.

In a recent development, the larger of the two exposed databases, found by longtime security researcher Jeremiah Fowler, contained around 2 billion credentials and appeared to be data stolen by info-stealer malware, which can collect all login details being used on an infected machine, and collections of other data from potential cybercriminal activity. There is no indication that individual companies or institutions were breached to compile the data, Fowler says.

“After finding a couple of different repositories for info stealers and malware, I have a search signature to look for them,” Fowler explains to WIRED. “When this popped up, I could not believe just how substantial it was.”.

Recent findings suggest that fowler says there were 8,471 folders in the exposed database containing plaintext credentials and account details. Some of the folders were named by country, including the US, UK, France, Germany, and dozens more. He found hundreds of millions of mainstream email accounts, tens of millions of .gov, .edu, and .org email addresses, more than 100 million social media accounts, and tens of millions of ecommerce accounts.

Fowler could not determine who owned the database, but he contacted the hosting tech giant, which removed it from the internet. It’s unclear how long it was online or if anyone else accessed the information.

Sources indicate that the enormous scale of the two exposures could create a sense of hopelessness that individuals are powerless to defend their private information. And there is a public understanding about the extent of data theft across the web. But both researchers point out that after years of this escalating situation, protections like using password managers and enabling multi-factor authentication really have broadly proved effective at keeping attackers out of consumer accounts, even if they aren’t silver bullets against every kind of attack.
