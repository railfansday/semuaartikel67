Meet ‘egoviruses’: the surprise microbes living in the guts of millions of people

It has been revealed that roughly 180 million people could be carrying previously unknown giant viruses inside their guts. Microbiologists say that these ‘egoviruses’ could be the missing link between giant viruses that infect single-celled organisms and distantly related poxviruses, such as those that cause smallpox and mpox, which can infect people and animals directly. The findings are published in Nature1.

Large and giant viruses in the phylum Nucleocytoviricota are a vast group of DNA viruses — some are so big that they can be seen under a light microscope — that mostly infect single-celled organisms in the environment.

According to recent reports, poxviruses are an outlier in the giant-virus world because they can directly infect humans and animals and their origin is a mystery, says Fasséli Coulibaly, a structural biologist at Monash University in Melbourne, Australia. Egoviruses could be the evolutionary bridge between microorganism-infecting giant viruses and pathogenic poxviruses, he says.

According to recent reports, tom Delmont, a microbial ecologist at the French National Centre for Scientific Research in Paris, and his colleagues discovered egoviruses while combing through online libraries of metagenomes. These contain the genetic information of all the organisms in a particular environment, which means that researchers can hunt for microbes without having to cultivate them. When the team scanned for a single gene found in most nucleocytoviruses, they discovered egoviruses — the only type of nucleocytovirus that is prevalent in the gut.

They found egovirus DNA in gut samples from people, buffalo, goats, chickens, deer and pigs. Six clades were unique to people. One of these clades is estimated to be present in roughly 1% of the global population, on the basis of the presence of at least one of two egovirus proteins in the samples they analysed.

The researchers detected these proteins in samples spanning a large proportion of the human population, from the Hadza, a hunter-gatherer group in Tanzania, to populations in Europe, the United States and South America, states Delmont. That suggests that egoviruses have been inhabiting the human gut for a long time, he adds.

Recent findings suggest that that egoviruses are found in many animal species, including people, and are widespread around the world shows that giant viruses are more diverse than previously thought, indicates Subir Sarker, a molecular virologist at James Cook University in Townsville, Queensland. “This is a really groundbreaking study,” he adds.

To identify potential hosts for egoviruses in the gut, the team compared the amount of genetic overlap between egoviruses and organisms with a nucleus. The researchers found the most overlap with single-celled organisms that help ruminants, such as cows, to digest their food.
