Hackers Now Want Your Browser Sessions More Than Your Passwords, but You Can Stop Them

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that there’s a very organized dark web supply chain that attackers use to trade in stealer logs. Russian Market has effectively replaced Genesis Market as the new storefront for black hat cybercriminals, although much of the market has also shifted to private channels on Telegram.

It has been revealed that according to threat analysts at DarkOwl, approximately 180,000 stealer logs were sold on Russian Market in just the first half of 2025. What's more, Russian Market is a well-designed storefront that customers can access even without Onion links or the Tor browser. That means anyone could go there and buy a bunch of stealer logs to start attempting account takeovers at scale.

Recent findings suggest that meanwhile, Flare Research estimates that this isn’t even close to the actual scale of the threat, because 90% of stealer logs now surface privately on Telegram. There, automated chatbots let scammers shop for stealer logs like it’s Amazon, with options to filter by country, app, and account privilege. Logs sell for anywhere between $5 and $500 a piece, depending on the estimated value of the target accounts.

It has been revealed that the easiest way to go about this is to take a look through all your active devices and their locations in your account settings on most websites. If you see a device or location you don’t recognize, you are likely the subject of an ongoing breach. However, keep in mind that this may not always be a reliable method if you use iCloud Private Relay or a VPN. Still, it's generally best practice to log out of all active sessions every once in a while and perform a fresh login to invalidate any unauthorized sessions.

Recent findings suggest that reset the passwords on your email account, enable two-factor authentication for added protection, and sign out of all sessions before doing a fresh login. Your email inbox is the single point of entry for most attacks because that’s where most websites send their verification codes or magic links.

Sign out of all active sessions on every account that you suspect is compromised. This will log out any attackers who are currently on a hijacked browsing session.

Change your passwords on every platform in order of priority. Start with banking websites, cloud storage providers, and password managers first.

Sources indicate that look through each compromised account’s third-party integrations and app permissions. Revoke access if you can’t recognize an app, or it looks suspicious. Also make sure to rotate any API keys that you’re presently using, since those may have been compromised as well.
