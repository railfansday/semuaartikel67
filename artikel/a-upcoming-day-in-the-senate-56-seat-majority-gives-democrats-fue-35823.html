Party wins biggest majority since Obama and one unthinkable two years ago – now the hard work of investigating and lawmaking begins.

It’s official: when Democrats take control of the Senate next year, they’ll have 56 seats, their largest majority in nearly two decades and one that appeared unthinkable at the outset of Donald Trump’s return to power two years ago.

The landslide victory was confirmed on Tuesday, when the lengthy ballot-counting process in Alaska ended with Democrat Mary Peltola defeating the incumbent Dan Sullivan, who became the ninth Republican to see their seat taken by the opposition in a midterm election that produced a political earthquake.

“Mary Peltola’s victory last night was an exclamation point on what has been a great election year,” said Chuck Schumer, the top Senate Democrat who is angling to serve as majority leader next year.

Recent findings suggest that “The American people have given us their votes. Now we have to earn their trust.”.

Schumer and Hakeem Jeffries, who on Wednesday was nominated by his party to serve as speaker of the House after Democrats won a decisive majority in that chamber, have made clear they plan to pursue an aggressive agenda of investigating and lawmaking once the latest Congress starts next year.

Recent findings suggest that “We have to lower costs. We have to end this corruption of the most corrupt administration in American history and we have to end the chaos that is so, so prevalent in this administration,” Schumer remarked.

Industry observers note that the Democrats’ 56-seat Senate majority will be the biggest for either party since the first two years of Barack Obama’s presidency, when his allies briefly controlled 60 seats in the chamber. The caucus will be made up of long-serving lawmakers from blue states, as well as latest arrivals from states that have not sent a Democrat to the Senate in decades, such as Texas, South Carolina and Mississippi.

Its a substantial accomplishment for the party, two years after voters ousted them from control of the Senate and sent Trump back to the White House. Their path to the majority this year required gaining at least four seats, the majority of which were from states that had backed the president in 2024. It appeared to be a tall ask, but in the end, Democrats won more than twice that number, as voters soured on Trump’s handling of the economy, immigration and the war with Iran.

Large as it may be, the new Senate majority will be bound by the same restrictions that have snarled both parties under divided government, and even when they have had full control of Washington.

Trump has two more years left in his second term, and one of the first things he did after it became clear that Democrats had won control of Congress on election night was post on Truth Social an image of a red cap with the word “VETO” written on it. Democrats have vowed to try to pass bills anyway, though they would need a two-thirds majority in both chambers to override his vetos – numbers that will require cooperation with Republicans.

In a recent development, nor is the Democratic majority big enough to get around the Senate’s filibuster without at least some Republican votes. Over the past two years, the dynamic was reversed, with Democrats halting party line efforts by the GOP to pass bills imposing new voting restrictions, among other measures.

That’s assuming they stay united themselves. So far, party leaders have signaled that they’ll tackle issues on which Democrats appear to have reached a consensus, such as recreating subsidies for Affordable Care Act health prepares, or trying to force repeal of Trump’s tariffs.

But first, Senate Democrats must sort out their own internal affairs. Maryland senator Chris Van Hollen last week announced a challenge to Schumer for the role of majority leader, and the caucus will take a behind-closed-doors vote on 1 December to settle the matter.

Recent findings suggest that other issues that were fraught on the campaign trail are sure to come to the forefront after the 120th Congress is sworn in on 3 January, including the question of aiding Israel. New arrivals including Abdul El-Sayed of Michigan and Troy Jackson of Maine have accused the country of carrying out a genocide in Gaza, and over the past year, many of the party’s sitting lawmakers backed efforts to halt shipments to Israel of bombs and bulldozers.

The country still has allies among Senate Democrats, and over the summer, Pennsylvania senator John Fetterman warned that he’d consider leaving his party if it became “anti-Israel”. Had he done so, that would have made it even harder for the Democrats to reclaim the chamber’s majority.

But with their sweeping wins in the midterm, Fetterman’s objections may carry less weight.

“We’ll still need several Republican votes to pass things, controversial things. But it certainly gives us the strength to … make some proposals that haven’t been made in a long time, whether it’s healthcare costs or anything of that nature,” Dick Durbin, the No 2 Senate Democrat who is retiring at the end of the year, mentioned in an interview.

“It’ll be a upcoming day in the Senate. I’m sorry I won’t be here.”.
