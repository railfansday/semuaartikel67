As Power Demand Surges, Energy Is Moving Closer To The Edge

Darren H. S. Tan. Chief Executive Officer at UNIGRID.

​Over the summer, Europe suffered a heat wave that strained its power grids, simultaneously increasing electricity demand and forcing some power plants to shut down.​.

This isn’t the first time extreme temperatures have impacted the grid’s ability to deliver power. Many places around the world have experienced record-high or record-low temperatures in recent years, and the demand for heating or cooling reached levels that existing energy infrastructure wasn’t designed to handle. ​.

Sources indicate that the problem is being made worse by the construction of hyperscale AI data centers, which require massive amounts of electricity and further strain the grid. Completely rebuilding the grid to meet modern demands has proven fiscally impractical for many governments, forcing many places to solve the problem on the local level using distributed generation and storage.

Sources indicate that energy infrastructure across the U.S. and Europe was built over a century ago, when energy needs were much different. ​.

Even so, studies have shown that we use only about 40% to 55% of the grid on average, and that’s by design, so it can handle spikes when they occur. But recent extreme weather events and data center demand are causing power needs to exceed even that generous buffer.​.

For example, the Electric Reliability Council of Texas (ERCOT), which manages electricity in Texas, has mentioned its interconnection queue right now includes over 1,800 projects totaling 474 gigawatts of power, over five times the state’s record peak electricity demand. Ninety percent of those requests come from data centers. ​.

The cost to completely rebuild the grid to meet today’s needs is astronomical, with some estimates putting the total replacement cost in the trillions. Because of the potential cost and the fact that the aging grid is a chronic rather than an urgent problem, it receives patchwork upgrades as needed and as government budgets allow. ​.

With a full rebuild out of the picture, several strategies are in play to work within the system we already have. When demand approaches available supply, energy companies offer rebates for reducing usage at peak times, implement peak pricing or, in more extreme cases, resort to rolling blackouts, which are planned outages to alleviate the demand during peak periods. ​.

Beyond the grid itself, some areas are reducing their dependence on the centralized infrastructure by moving toward local power generation and storage.

Industry observers note that battery energy storage systems (BESS) can store electricity and discharge it when demand exceeds the grid’s capacity. ​.

It has been revealed that they are already showing promise in several states, such as California, where battery storage capacity has reached over 21,000 MW, equal to about one-third of the state’s peak demand. Eighty percent of that capacity is represented by grid-connected batteries, but smaller batteries are also enabling residents to increase their own energy storage capacity at home.​.

In a recent development, as these batteries become cheaper and more reliable, it’s not out of the realm of possibility that these energy storage systems become a standard household appliance in the future, much like a refrigerator or washing machine is today. They can also play a role in energy needs for small businesses.

In a recent development, being able to store energy and use it in an emergency is only half the equation. The other half is being able to generate electricity independently of the grid. ​.

According to recent reports, this isn’t a concept that should be unfamiliar to many, as many organizations and homes already have backup generators or solar panels. Although these methods can keep small businesses and homes powered up, they have their disadvantages. Generators are noisy, pollutive and can be dangerous, while solar has significant upfront installation costs. ​.

In a recent development, plug-in solar is emerging as another option for generating electricity locally that has already seen success in Europe and is starting to gain support in the U.S. as well. Instead of being installed on a rooftop, these smaller panels can be mounted on a balcony or a small section of a yard or business property and plugged into a wall outlet to help power homes and small businesses. ​.

In a recent development, these systems cost significantly less than traditional solar and are portable, so people can move them around wherever they’re needed. These benefits open up power generation to a whole new segment of the country: commercial and residential renters alike who might never invest in a hardwired battery system or solar installation.

As energy demand continues to increase, expanding traditional grid infrastructure will remain important, but those projects can be expensive and take years to complete. Distributed generation and storage offer another way to increase resilience in the meantime. ​.

By generating and storing more electricity closer to where it will be consumed, communities and business leaders can help reduce their reliance on the centralized grid during peak demand.​.

We’re unlikely to solve growing power constraints with any single technology, but as local generation and storage become cheaper and more accessible, they can play a larger role in managing peak demand and making better use of the grid infrastructure we already have.​.
