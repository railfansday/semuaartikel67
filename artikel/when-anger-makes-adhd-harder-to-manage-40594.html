When Anger Makes ADHD Harder to Manage

In a recent development, anger can lead to increasing symptoms of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). When a person is angry, it’s hard to sit still, which is already a symptom that many people with ADHD experience. When you’re angry, your mind can become clouded, leading to careless mistakes and not listening to others because you are distracted. Stan’s anger leads not only to concerns in his relationship but also to impairments in functioning in the workplace.

At work, Stan interrupts others and talks too much, leading to difficulties with others when his colleagues oppose his proposals. His anger leads him to quickly blame others, and before he knows it, he’s accusing others of dropping the ball. What follows next might be called an “ADHD triple play.” Instead of a pitcher getting three players out on base, he alienates three of his colleagues in vital roles for ensuring timely completion of a project.

Recent findings suggest that stan’s anger, in part, creates the ADHD symptoms of talking too much and not waiting his turn or listening to others. These are symptoms of ADHD, showing how ADHD can exacerbate anger. He interrupts Rob without listening to him (not waiting his turn), insults Clara rather than listening to understand her perspective (not listening), accuses Lena of not responding to emails (talking too much), and doesn’t give her an opening to explain her higher priorities and apologize for the delay.

Recent findings suggest that so, how do ADHD symptoms lead to anger? In Stan’s case, his ADHD often sets the backdrop for instigating his anger. At work, he doesn’t always listen closely to updates about current projects during check-ins with his colleagues. Because he missed information, he is more likely to accuse his colleagues of things they didn’t do or of dropping the ball on things they actually did do.

According to recent reports, you may feel forgetful at times. You might be guilty of hearing what a colleague said but then forgetting who said it, and you may find yourself getting angry at someone who wasn’t even involved. Or maybe you have a habit of butting in on others' conversations. Impulse control is a defining feature of ADHD, so butting in on others can lead to harsh comments from others, which can make them more angry. Then, as your anger alienates others, you might increasingly find yourself on the outside of conversations. You may sense the walls others put up, which can increase your anger at feeling left out. People might tiptoe around you to avoid your anger. It can only take one blow-up for others to have their radar up in every interaction, creating uncomfortable dynamics. This can lead to not being able to let go of the anger, which interferes with focus and listening to others.

One way to stop this vicious cycle is to learn skills for feeling the anger without taking it out on others. A simple principle of practicing radical acceptance starts with knowing you can’t control other people’s behavior.

According to recent reports, the recommendation to choose acceptance is fundamentally hard. Trying to change the other person means you have not accepted the person or the stark reality that while you can influence other people, you can’t change them.

This is so hard that it can take a lifetime to learn this lesson. There are stages of life where there are grey zones. If you have a rebellious teenager, how much can you accept behavior that can lead to serious consequences? There are clearly areas where you have influence and can work to change the other person, such as parenting, teaching, and coaching, where your job is to identify blind spots and work for change.

Sources indicate that research demonstrates that radical acceptance helps people to reframe negative events, which likely strengthens their coping skills (Segal et al., 2023). Keep in mind, radical acceptance does not mean approving of other people’s behavior. You may have valuable contributions to make, but your ADHD symptoms may cause others to dismiss you as a hothead. If this leads to failing to be persuasive, you may begin to feel ineffectual, like no one is listening to you, which can fuel more anger and resentment. Radical acceptance can help you acknowledge how frustrating it is to feel dismissed and choose a response that gives your ideas a better chance of being heard.

Segal, O., Sher, H., Aderka, I. M., & Weinbach, N. (2023). Does acceptance lead to change? Training in radical acceptance improves implementation of cognitive reappraisal. Behaviour Research and Therapy, 164, 1–9. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.brat.2023.104303.
