Dissolved organic matter changes little as water crosses Antarctica’s continental shelf, offering new insight into the ocean’s carbon cycle.

A recent study suggests that dissolved organic matter changes little as water crosses the Antarctic continental shelf and sinks toward the deep ocean.

Despite abundant marine life and shifting water masses, researchers found little change in its amount or chemical composition.

The study drew on water samples from two expeditions aboard the Polarstern, an icebreaker operated by the Alfred Wegener Institute, Helmholtz Centre for Polar and Marine Research (AWI).

It has been revealed that ocean currents move water around the globe in a vast circulation system. The Antarctic continental shelf plays an essential part.

Industry observers note that deep water arrives from the north and rises onto the shelf. Extreme cold and sea ice formation make it denser.

This heavy water then sinks into the deep ocean. It helps form Antarctic bottom water, the coldest, densest, and one of the most voluminous water masses in the world’s oceans.

As the water crosses the shelf, it passes abundant marine life. Researchers wanted to know if these processes change the carbon before the water sinks.

The answer matters since rising temperatures and declining sea ice could change how the Southern Ocean stores and transports carbon.

Recent findings suggest that the southern Weddell Sea shelf is a key place for Antarctic bottom water formation. It is remote and hard to reach. Studying it depends on specialized research vessels like the Polarstern.

In a recent development, dissolved organic matter plays a crucial role in the global carbon cycle. It is released during the production and breakdown of marine life.

Recent findings suggest that this matter holds tens of thousands of different compounds. Some are rapidly eaten by microbes, while others persist for a long time.

Industry observers note that it is the largest reservoir of organic carbon in the ocean. Understanding how it changes and moves is key to predicting the ocean’s response to climate change.

It has been revealed that the team analyzed water samples from two Polarstern expeditions to the southern Weddell Sea (2016 and 2021). To find out whether the carbon changed, the team used several complementary methods.

Industry observers note that the Institute for Chemistry and Biology of the Marine Environment (ICBM) at the University of Oldenburg has a distinctive collection of advanced analytical instruments. These allow detailed study of the molecular make-up and structure of complex organic matter mixtures.

State-of-the-art analytics at AWI’s MICADAS (Mini Carbon Dating System) facility helped the team determine the age of the carbon.

The shelf sees intense seasonal blooms of phytoplankton, so scientists expected freshly produced organic matter that microbes can readily break down to leave a clear molecular imprint on the sinking water.

Instead, they found almost no detectable change in the amount or composition of dissolved organic matter carried into the deep ocean.

The key to this paradox is time. Dense waters stay on the Weddell Sea continental shelf for an unusually long period, between six and twelve years.

During this time, the water also circulates beneath the large Filchner–Ronne Ice Shelf.

The researchers suggest that this extended stay gives microbes time to break down newly produced organic matter, converting its carbon into dissolved inorganic carbon before the water leaves the shelf.

“Our study reveals that the molecular properties of dissolved organic matter are conserved during deep-water formation in the southern Weddell Sea,” noted lead author Gonzalo Gomez-Saez from AWI.

“Our findings highlight dissolved organic matter as a stable component of the regional Southern Ocean carbon cycle.”.

It has been revealed that “Dissolved organic matter is a huge and complex store of carbon in the ocean. By examining its molecules in detail, we can investigate how it changes as water moves through the Antarctic continental shelf,” added senior author Thorsten Dittmar from ICBM.

The findings suggest that this large carbon store changes little during water movement and transformation on the southern Weddell Sea shelf.

“Global warming is now reaching the Antarctic shelf seas. If the large reservoir of dissolved organic matter is scarcely affected by processes on the shelf, this suggests that this carbon reservoir could remain largely unaffected by climate change in this region,” stated study co-author Moritz Holtappels from AWI.

But not all ocean carbon behaves the same way.

Dissolved organic carbon remained largely stable in this study, while other forms of carbon may respond differently to environmental change.

That includes organic particles sinking to the seabed and dissolved inorganic carbon in seawater. These may react more to changes in temperature, sea ice cover, and marine life.

Researchers will keep studying how different forms of carbon respond to environmental change.

One effort is Antarctica InSync, an international initiative bringing scientists together to study the rapidly changing Antarctic environment.

Oceanographer and co-author Alexander Haumann will help lead a Polarstern expedition in 2028.

The team will investigate how carbon moves through the Southern Ocean during the Antarctic winter.

These observations will help scientists understand how climate change may affect the ocean’s ability to transport and store carbon.

Intense biological activity does not necessarily leave a lasting imprint on the dissolved organic matter carried into the deep ocean.

In the southern Weddell Sea, the water’s long journey across the shelf may help explain why.

Microbes get years, not weeks, to finish the job.

That stability is good news in one sense. This carbon reservoir may hold up as the region warms.

In a recent development, but it is not a blanket reassurance. Other carbon pools could shift.

It has been revealed that the team’s next steps will probe those differences. For now, the southern Weddell Sea offers a rare case of carbon holding steady in a changing world.

The study is published in the journal Nature Communications.
