I Used to Counteroffer My Best Employees With More Money. Now I Rely on These 3 Strategies.

Sources indicate that like all business leaders, I have had some of my top performers tell me they were leaving. There is no way to sugarcoat the feeling. It’s like a kick in the belly — and in the wallet.

Sources indicate that according to statistics reported by HR Morning, replacing an employee costs upwards of 200% of what they were making. And that is probably a conservative figure, given that losing a valuable team member can cause a ripple of lowered productivity and tabled projects as people scramble to pick up the ex-employee’s work.

Recent findings suggest that given these facts, I used to follow the standard pattern of attempting to keep talented team members by offering them higher compensation, bonuses or organization equity. It was a tactic that almost always worked, but it didn’t create loyalty. Instead, it made those people price-sensitive, and they eventually found organizations willing to pay more, so they left anyway.

In a recent development, I am not suggesting that it is wrong to counteroffer someone’s resignation with money. However, it’s reactive and arbitrary. Unless you have put a cap on how high you are willing to go, your negotiations will begin to feel like a moving target that’s out of your control. Again, you may end up delaying someone’s departure, but you won’t keep them forever unless you put more proactive measures in place.

Sources indicate that some positions in your organization drive enterprise value and some don’t. McKinsey and Company suggested in an older article that these will probably make up about 5% of your workforce — and contribute 800% more than others across your organization.

Recent findings suggest that over the years, I’ve found that I could typically name fewer than 10 roles that would materially impact my business if they remained vacant for an extended period. My recommendation is to name those roles and then create retention intends for each of them.

What are some focused blueprint ideas? Deferred compensation structures allow team members to grow their money tax-free as long as they stay with your organization. That way, you won’t inflate your overall payroll and can concentrate your resources where they matter most. This type of long-term incentive can be tangibly meaningful because it’s tied to tenure and not merely performance. Since it’s only applicable to a small group of high-value employees, you aren’t raising your compensation across your organization, either.

Recent findings suggest that I once considered using equity as a major retention lever. Why not? It felt right and seemed to be a no-brainer way to get people to stick around and contribute to our organization’s growth. Yet when I mapped out the risks and rewards, I realized that giving away equity has massive long-term costs.

Here’s the matter: equity permanently dilutes your ability to use equity in other ways later. For example, you can’t bargain with equity with potential investors if a chunk of it has been given to your employees. Going the equity route also complicates your organizational governance, which can make your financial picture blurry.

According to recent reports, rather than offering equity to someone who wants to leave, explore non-equity alternatives. Treat equity as a last resort that you’ll only consider if you think it will bring out an employee’s long-term ownership behavior. And be open to talking about this with a professional who has helped other businesses design executive benefit and compensation structures that retain key people while protecting tax exposure and equity. Looking for a resource to get your feet wet? Colin Steinberg, a partner at Institutional Architects, frequently shares advice on executive planning and related topics.

It has been revealed that there are many business-friendlier alternatives to simply handing an employee who’s about to leave more money or equity. As mentioned before, you might provide deferred compensation, a way to delay income that benefits both you and the team member.

Recent findings suggest that you might also look into supplemental executive retirement plans. Chances are strong that you already offer retirement benefits. Could you offer additional ones directly tied to both tenure and performance? Supplemental executive retirement plans help C-suite employees see meaningful future value in putting down roots, which SmartAsset explains can help trim churn among your highest-paid performers.

Industry observers note that when structured appropriately, these types of retention strategies avoid immediate financial strain on your balance sheet. They also allow you to turn retention tools into ways to improve your tax efficiency, reduce volatile compensation models and retain control over your equity.

I firmly believe that founders who lose their most valuable people (but are otherwise building solid, fair companies) don’t have a problem with their retention. They have a problem with the way they’ve structured their long-term compensation packages for their most impactful roles. If you want to get real leverage as an employer, you need to develop smarter systems that incentivize your key players without losing your ground.
