Only the Seminole Tribe can offer online gambling in Florida—and they're furious at DraftKings

Last week, DraftKings asked a federal court in Florida to take over a lawsuit recently filed against it by the Seminole Tribe, opening another front in the nationwide online “swaps” debate.

This Florida case adds to an already complex subplot involving Indigenous tribes, some of which are challenging prediction markets, while others are trying to get in on the action themselves.

In the latest lawsuit, the Seminole Tribe contends that two of DraftKings’ current online offerings—the fantasy sports-style “Pick6” and the “Super App” sportsbook—are “illegal” in Florida.

The tribe argues that every such “wager that Defendants now accept through their illegal sportsbook siphons away revenue that funds both the Tribe’s and Florida’s public programs and government services.”.

Neither DraftKings nor its attorneys immediately responded to Ars’ request for comment. However, in a court filing, DraftKings says that its products available to Floridians are merely “fantasy sports and the trading of [financial] derivatives” and not gambling.

DraftKings notes that its “licensed sportsbook” operates in other states but “not in Florida.”.

Multiple companies, including DraftKings, claim that their “swaps” (a type of financial derivative) are, in fact, properly governed by a federal agency, the Commodity Futures Trading Commission. Therefore, DraftKings argues, states cannot regulate this activity as gambling.

The Seminoles, a sovereign nation and a federally recognized tribe, have a deal with the State of Florida, known as the 2021 Gaming Compact. That 30-year contract allows the tribe to maintain what the tribe states is the “exclusive right to offer online sports betting throughout the State of Florida.” In exchange, this betting regime is regulated, and Florida takes a cut.

But since that contract was signed, attorneys for the Seminole Tribe say in the complaint, DraftKings has pursued years of court challenges and a failed ballot measure designed to break into the Sunshine State.

As of 2026, DraftKings launched “Pick6” in Florida, a game where individuals can put money down while predicting specific outcomes—for example, whether Victor Wembanyama of the San Antonio Spurs will score more or less than 28.5 points in his next basketball game.

“Based on DraftKings’ representations, Florida consumers may predictably but incorrectly conclude that Pick6 is a legal product in Florida. It is not,” the complaint states.

Sources indicate that “Pick6 is illegal ‘gambling’ under Florida law. DraftKings operates Pick6 without a license, without state oversight, and without any consumer protections Florida requires as a condition of legal sports wagering.”.

In June 2026, DraftKings launched what it calls a “Super App,” which the Seminole Tribe argues is a “one-stop shop for illegal gambling,” as it provides an interface that is “in the same format used by every traditional, regulated sportsbook.”.

Florida-based professor Robert Jarvis of Nova Southeastern University studies gambling law, and he told Ars by email that he didn’t “buy the argument” DraftKings put forward that its products cannot be regulated under Florida gambling law.

Jarvis also noted that the tribe caught a break, too, with their randomly assigned federal judge.

“Not only have they had very good luck in federal court in previous cases, the federal judge assigned to the case is Ed Artau, who previously served as a Florida state trial judge (2014–20) and a Florida state appellate judge (2020–25),” he wrote. “Thus, in a sense, the Seminoles get the best of both worlds: a federal court run by a Florida state judge.”.
