It’s not just you: Durable goods are getting less reliable, from sputtering cars to glitching smart TVs

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

"They just don't make things like they used to." The phrase, formerly exclusive to grandparents, is now used by consumers of all ages.

In a recent development, across the United States, durable goods like cars, appliances and electronics are becoming less reliable. In the automobile industry, owners of 3-year-old models reported an average of 204 problems per 100 vehicles as of Feb. 12, the highest level since 2022, according to a report from consumer intelligence company J.D. Power. Reliability is a growing concern for dishwashers and front-load washers too, respectively experiencing an average of 92 and 91 problems per 100 units as of Oct. 29, according to a similar J.D. Power report.

Consumers have noticed: Shoppers dealing with high inflation rates and added tariffs on imported goods are unsatisfied with all kinds of products, from cars and tech devices to furniture and kitchen gear. Just over three-quarters of Americans say they believe companies often sell lower-quality products and services at higher prices, according to an Axios and Harris Poll survey published in May 2025.

It has been revealed that to be sure, reliability and durability aren't the only ways to define a product's quality. Aesthetics and performance matter to consumers, too. Your television today may not last as long as the one you grew up with, but it's probably larger and thinner with better picture definition — and it likely costs much less, when adjusted for inflation, says Justin Sirotin, an industrial design professor at the Rhode Island School of Design. Indeed, the average price of a television was roughly $622 in 1996 adjusted for inflation, whereas a 55-inch LCD model now costs $232 on average.

But because they're penny-pinched, consumers are becoming more focused on finding high-value products — ones that perform consistently and reliably — than on price, according to a 2025 Deloitte report.

In a recent development, unfortunately for shoppers, some businesses are also economically squeezed, and may manufacture products with cheaper, less durable parts to keep their profits growing, Sirotin claims. Plus, we've all gotten used to sleek products with impressive features at affordable prices, and when businesses have to deliver on those aspects to grow demand, longevity can drop down the list of priorities. Manufacturers may prioritize quality on the external parts of a product — like the picture quality on a television — and get their cost savings with cheaper internal components that you won't initially notice, says Keyana Sapp, an investigative journalist and creator of the Worse on Purpose newsletter.

In some cases, a product's durability may even be part of a "planned obsolescence," wherein businesses intentionally give goods a certain lifespan so customers need to buy replacements within a predictable timeframe. Planned obsolescence isn't necessarily rampant, and it's hard to prove that any individual company purposefully designs products to fail, Sapp indicates.

But historically speaking, it's not entirely a conspiracy theory. In 1924, major light bulb manufacturers formed an international coalition and agreed to lower and standardize the lifespan of incandescent light bulbs to 1,000 hours. The cartel broke up during World War II.

According to recent reports, you can, of course, try extending the longevity of products you've already bought by paying to repair them, rather than getting a brand-upcoming replacement. But as devices like vacuum cleaners and headphones evolve technologically, they require increasingly specialized tools and labor, claims Shahram Mokhtari, lead teardown technician at online repair community and e-commerce site iFixit. Specialized screws, glued components and limited access to repair information can make some products difficult or impossible to fix, the Federal Trade Commission reported in May 2021.

Europe has right-to-repair regulations that require manufacturers of certain products to provide access to replacement parts, tools and information. Only nine U.S. states have similar legislation for consumer electronics.

If you want to know how long a product might last before you buy it, look up its reviews or do some research to understand if it's complicated to fix, Mokhtari recommends. Or, if you have the budget, you can prioritize higher-priced products that might bring both initial quality and long-term longevity. This may require intentional long-term planning for some consumers — even as online shopping has made impulse buying easier, Sirotin notes.

In a recent development, "It takes a lot of intentionality by the consumer to say, 'OK, well, is this chair really a chair that I want to have for 25 years? Or is this the chair I'm going to buy and sit in for six months, and then I'm just going to get another chair?'" claims Sirotin.
