Breaking: I Took a Mystery “Decision Detox” Trip With Zero Planning. Here’s What Happened.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

From the treehouse-like deck outside my hotel room, I took in the lush cluster of live oak and eucalyptus trees all around me, the whisper of wind and chirp of unknown birds, and the small sliver of Pacific Ocean peeking through the foliage-lined horizon. I was completely at peace. The best part: I hadn’t made a single decision for the entire weekend. In fact, until just a couple days prior—the day of my trip departure—I had absolutely no idea where I’d be on this gorgeous Sunday morning.

Industry observers note that the morning of my trip, I tore open a sealed envelope that held all the information about the hotel where I’d be staying (Chaminade Resort and Spa), my itinerary (forest therapy, spa treatments, dining at The View and Lago di Como), and even the destination itself (Santa Cruz, California). This was all part of the “mystery travel” experience I eagerly agreed to, facilitated by Benchmark Resorts & Hotels and Pack Up + Go, an agency that prepares completely curated surprise vacations. Here was the deal: I filled out a survey about my preferences (my priorities while traveling, the types of hotels I liked) along with budget, then they planned the entire trip, and I wouldn’t find out details (beyond some notes about weather and packing suggestions) until I opened my envelope the morning of my departure.

As an avid traveler, eldest daughter, and strategic planner (particularly in the context of trips), I thought this format might stress me out—but I found there was a sense of freedom and lightness in not pouring into pre-trip research or curating my own itinerary. All I had to do was show up. This brand of mental surrender actually speaks to a rising travel trend: “decision detox.”.

According to recent reports, modern life can often feel like an endless series of choices. Between social media, the internet, and now AI, “we are inundated with information and access to it,” states Morgan Cutlip, PhD, psychology educator, author, and researcher. “This sends us regularly searching for the ‘right’ way of doing things rather than trusting our instincts or talking to a friend and doing what they do.”.

Industry observers note that and that absolutely applies to trip planning. “When there are so many options for something like a vacation, the more information you consume and the more reviews you read, the more uncertain you can become about your decision,” she says. “Searching out accommodations, looking up flight options, thinking through food—it becomes a ton of micro decisions packed into this one major thing that feels like it’s supposed to be extra magical, extra fun, and low stress, so the stakes feel high.”.

In a recent development, in fact, while vacations are meant to be a time to unwind and enjoy life, a 2025 survey of 1,500 Americans from YouGov found 70 percent of U.S. vacationers find their booking experience to be stress-inducing. That’s where “decision detox” trips come in, where you completely relinquish control of trip planning. The intention here is for travelers to take a break from the mental overload of everyday life and never-ending choices, in order to take a true vacation.

It has been revealed that this travel trend is on the rise. A research report published by Lemongrass, a certified B-Corp marketing agency, found a clear shift in traveler preferences for curated, all-inclusive itineraries that remove planning fatigue; a desire to visit quieter, less-crowded destinations; and a pull toward peaceful relaxation over highly stimulating experiences.

It has been revealed that and while traditional travel agents have long been planning accommodations and transportation, decision detox trips take that concept one step further with an emphasis on fully planned retreats and completely handled logistics. In my case, this meant a complete surprise trip and fully embracing the thrill of adventure that comes with not knowing your destination until the moment you depart.

“For some people, this might sound like handcuffs on a holiday, but for [people] burdened with making seemingly every decision in their families’ lives all the time, it’s heaven in the form of a pre-booked massage and meals that magically appear when desired,” Jools Sampson, founder and owner of Reclaim Yourself Retreats, wrote in the Lemongrass report.

Women are likely disproportionately affected by this kind of decision fatigue, as they tend to take on the lion’s share of trip planning. In fact, data from research organization Skift found that 82 percent of all travel decisions are made by women. This can be particularly draining for moms, who are constantly managing the logistics of life, states Cutlip, whose work often focuses on mental load discrepancies in relationships. “And if she’s the one planning it, she’s almost certainly coordinating with her partner too. Not being able to fully make decisions on her own, having to negotiate and talk each one through, adds yet another layer of work.”.

But no matter what role someone takes in a family structure, or what the underlying reason is for mental fatigue, leaning into decision detox travel can come with a whole slew of mental health benefits.

Recent findings suggest that “You eliminate all of the fatigue, overwhelm, and unnecessary stress that can come with planning a trip,” indicates Cutlip. “If you’re somebody who has the capacity to let go and you’re comfortable putting someone else in the driver’s seat, it frees you to focus on everything else a vacation requires: preparing to leave your home, getting work off, taking care of the pets, cleaning out the fridge, and then showing up ready to relax.”.

According to recent reports, benchmark decided to partner with Pack Up + Go after reading YouGov’s survey about traveler planning fatigue. “It’s a different way to approach a vacation,” says Evan Crawford, vice president of marketing for Benchmark Resorts & Hotels.

It also leads to travelers visiting locations they may not have otherwise thought to book—such as Woodinville, Washington, or the Columbia River Gorge, says Crawford. “Maybe you’ve never even heard of the place before, but now you can go there and get exposed to it and have this extraordinary experience.”.

And while a complete surprise vacation is one approach, it’s not the only way to try out a decision detox trip.

A reprieve from decision-making is at the heart of Duvine Cycling, which curates bike tour vacations that take guests everywhere from the vineyards of Burgundy to the Southern Lakes region of New Zealand.

Sources indicate that “Basically, all you have to do is show up on a trip. You don’t have to think about anything,” states CEO and co-founder Andy Levine. These active, planned trips inherently encourage people to unplug and make lasting connections with their fellow cyclists. “[We act as] their concierge, and we get to make these really cool things happen, all while on a bike.”.

Then there’s Selar, a sustainable polar expedition organization launching in early 2027. For anyone seeking a grand adventure—without all the intricate planning that often goes into these types of trips—Selar offers a chance to take a journey way up north with itineraries across Norway, Svalbard, and Greenland. Or take HX Expeditions, which, similar to a cruise model, offers all-inclusive voyages that cover excursions, meals, drinks, and gratuities—but unlike a classic cruise, these itineraries are led by onboard naturalists and historians, and their packages also include lectures, hands-on experiences, and expedition gear.

At Vermejo, a Ted Turner Reserve in Latest Mexico, each guest’s nature-focused itinerary is completely handled by their Reserve Ambassadors. There are more than 45 guided activities available, ranging from horseback riding through vast prairies, fly-fishing for Rio Grande cutthroat trout, or taking a UTV tour with the opportunity to spot elk, bear, and other substantial game.

Of course, numerous wellness resorts have been hopping on this trend for some time now, acknowledging that a true mental reset is intrinsically linked to less decision-making. BodyHoliday, an all-inclusive wellness resort in St. Lucia, asks guests to “give us your body for a week, and we’ll give you back your mind;” Sensei Lanai in Hawaii offers guided stays that cater to your individual health needs and goals; and at Renew Retreat at Calamigos Ranch in California, you can take part in a preset wellness retreat.

Sources indicate that there are also a number of retreats specifically designed for staying active. Take a running retreat with Run Wild, for example: these women-only trips are designed so you simply choose your pace and place of interest and select an already-set itinerary.

It has been revealed that the crux of these trips is rooted in analog experiences, which can help with the mental fatigue and technology overload we experience in everyday life. “I think people are really craving that opportunity to be in the moment, to be human,” states Crawford.

Sources indicate that surrendering complete control is never easy—but in the case of my trip to Santa Cruz, I found it wildly therapeutic. By not stressing about every detail, both before and during the trip, I was able to feel fully present with every item on my itinerary, including a hike through redwood forests, idly reading by the pool in my (pre-reserved) cabana, and savoring meals set to stunning views of the treetops and ocean beyond.

Sources indicate that because I opted for a road trip rather than air travel, the Pack Up + Go team even chose my rest stop on the way from Los Angeles (Paso Robles), along with recommendations for restaurants and shops to explore in the area. And this detailed research extended to my final destination in Santa Cruz, too: the team provided a meticulously curated list of shops, experiences, and more for me to explore during my final day in the area.

Before embarking on your own decision detox trip, take stock of whether this will feel freeing or cause you more stress. “My advice would be to reflect on whether you’re somebody who can actually let go of control,” indicates Cutlip. “If you’re going to turn it over to someone else and then micromanage the process, just do it yourself. If you can turn over control, fully embrace it and enjoy it.”.

For me, it was a practice in letting go of control and allowing outside help—something I think many of us could benefit from on occasion. And while I won’t necessarily be handing over the reins for every future trip I take, it was a beautiful opportunity to give my mind a bit of reprieve, to stop overthinking for a while, and simply take in the wonder of a beautiful place.
