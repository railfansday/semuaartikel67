Sonny's Corner Brand Identity Is a Masterclass in Branding

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Order builds the Sonny's Corner brand identity from a hand-lettered subway poster alphabet, one deep red, and a diamond that holds it together.

It has been revealed that the bar sits on the corner of Greenpoint Avenue and Franklin Street in Brooklyn, at 142 Franklin Street, on a spot that has served the neighborhood as a bar for almost a hundred years. Its previous tenant was the Pencil Factory, named after the Eberhard-Faber Pencil Factory across the street, and Order lets that history set the tone without turning it into costume. The name does double work. Sonny is one owner's newborn child, and the corner itself catches the best afternoon light in the neighborhood. The lockup indicates it plainly: a white diamond on a deep red field, the name set in chunky hand-drawn capitals that feel painted rather than typed.

The typeface is the whole idea. Order worked with Brooklyn type designer Ben Tuttle on Oppy Sans, a revival of the hand lettering Amelia Opdyke Jones drew for The Subway Sun, the New York subway etiquette posters that ran from 1918 to around 1965. Jones signed the posters as Oppy, and her letters carry the jokes and the warnings in the same loose hand. The revival keeps that hand-drawn character, and Order runs the single style everywhere: identification, signage, packaging, and everything between. One style, no supporting cast, so the voice never wavers. The specimen reveals the full alphabet with numerals and punctuation, and the letters hold their shape from a matchbook up to the shopfront.

According to recent reports, the color comes from the room itself. A deep red was chosen to match the wooden interior and the seating upholstery, and simple neutrals carry the rest. The diamond from the lockup repeats as a harlequin pattern across the matchbooks, the coasters, and the awning over the door. The same red runs through the pens, the caps, the tote bag, and the business cards, each one carrying the name small and straight. The motto on the window reads Where the sun always shines, a line that works three ways at once: the child, the typeface, and the corner itself, which lines up with Manhattan's 23rd Street corridor and catches the afternoon sun. If you know, you know.
