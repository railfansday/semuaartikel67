WASHINGTON — A part of the brain famous for listening is key for talking, too.

Sources indicate that data collected from people as they listen and try to talk show that this brain region, a small spot inside a larger area called Wernicke’s area, has a more expansive job than researchers thought when it comes to communicating. The results may help efforts to restore people’s voices. The results presented November 15 at the annual meeting of the Society for Neuroscience and published November 19 in Science.The study provides convincing evidence that this brain spot is critical for speech production, states neurologist and language researcher Jeffrey Binder of the Medical College of Wisconsin in Milwaukee. “My hope is that this study will help scientists and clinicians accept a more accurate interpretation of this focal area” as an early stop on the pathway to saying a word.

Recent findings suggest that A small part of the brain, just above the left ear, was first described in 1874 by a neurologist Carl Wernicke as necessary for making sense of speech. People with damage there had trouble comprehending words. But some people also had trouble producing them. “Sounds were swapped, words were replaced with near misses,” Edward Chang of the University of California, San Francisco, stated in a news briefing. “The basic question has been, ‘Why would damage to a hearing area of the brain break the ability to speak?’ That question has been open for 150 years.”The newly introduced research offers an answer: A small bit of Wernicke’s area (to be extremely specific, the left posterior superior temporal gyrus or lpSTG ) becomes active just before a word is spoken. “Every word that you have said today began in that small part above your left ear about a quarter of a second before you said it,” Chang said.

The finding adds to growing evidence that brain areas are more complex than people have appreciated, claims neuroscientist Leigh Hochberg at Brown University in Providence, R.I. “I believe that one of the things we are going to learn over the next five, 10 years of this research, is that each part of the brain that, we were taught as a med student or a grad student, does this, probably does that and a whole lot more.”.

Industry observers note that scientists measured brain activity in 28 people before they had surgery for epilepsy or tumors. They were able to see that this brain region kicked on as a person listened to words, and also just before they attempted to speak. Cells there had preferences for certain sorts of words, long ones, for instance. “It’s more active when you’re about to say the word caterpillar than when you’re about to say the word cat,” said Matthew Leonard, a cognitive neuroscientist at UCSF.

Electrical stimulation to this small bit of brain also interfered with people’s ability to talk. “What we found was that stimulation, really only in this lpSTG area, disrupted this speech production when it was delivered just prior to speaking,” noted UCSF bioengineer Alexander Silva.

It has been revealed that some efforts to make implanted devices that can help restore speech to people who no longer talk focus on the motor cortex, a part of the brain that controls the movements required for speech. This bit of Wernicke’s area offers an additional spot to consider, Silva said. “What if you could target a single brain region and gain access to the full spectrum of speech, from listening all the way up to speech production?”.

Experimental brain implants in two volunteers with paralysis were able to detect both what they heard and what they were trying to say, the researchers report in the study.

The discovery may help scientists better understand situations that involve both hearing and speaking, such as speech or comprehension differences in people with autism, or how children first begin learning to talk, Chang stated.
