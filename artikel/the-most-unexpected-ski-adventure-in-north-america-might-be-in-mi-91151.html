The Most Unexpected Ski Adventure in North America Might Be in Michigan

It’s breakfast in the main yurt, so the girl from the kitchen is again standing on a chair and rubbing the dome of a snow globe. Eyes shut tight, she shouts over the gathered skiers.

“Grant us snow, almighty spirits of winter,” she begins, raising the globe above her head. “By your power, give us powder. Amen.” She is our shaman.

“Amen,” we all murmur along and mean it.

The Midwest is a peculiar place for a ski pilgrimage. But on Michigan’s isolated Keweenaw Peninsula, known as “the fin,” Mount Bohemia has no shortage of true believers. And they’re not coming for the corduroy. This expert-only backcountry resort is ungroomed, technical, and seriously steep. Signs across the mountain are clear: NO BEGINNERS ALLOWED.

It has been revealed that while skiers scour the globe for powder, Michigan has been sitting on one of the country’s most unlikely stashes. Bohemia sees almost 300 inches annually, with no snowmaking. Here, bad weather is usually good news. Arctic air sweeping over Lake Superior’s warmer water produces storms that bury the Keweenaw. This lake-effect snow is remarkably light and dry—the kind you’d travel to Utah or Japan to find.

Industry observers note that on paper, Bohemia’s numbers seem modest: 900 vertical feet, 585 acres, and just two lifts. Yet over 90 percent of its runs are black diamond or better, including the region’s only triple-blacks. Boho has the untamed feel of backcountry skiing, minus the skinning. Its dozen or so named territories—mostly sprawling glade districts—each have distinct temperaments.

“It can take two or three visits before guests get a feel for the mountain,” states Vern Barber, Bohemia’s general manager.

Recent findings suggest that I spend one morning adrift in one of the territories, Extreme Backcountry, a half-mile-wide expanse of Bohemia’s toughest terrain. Its high-angle tree runs, cliff drops, and narrow chutes kept funneling me onto unexpected lines. Supposedly, somewhere out there is a frozen waterfall drop I never managed to find. I wasn’t going to hit it. I just wanted to look.

Industry observers note that now I’m farther out in Middle Earth, another territory, surfing Boho’s longest runs through twisting hardwoods. Less steep, but somehow more exhausting. This is where skiers come to explore, hunting for pockets of untouched powder.

Recent findings suggest that I stop at a trail marked “Helm’s Deep.” It took several attempts to find this place. Even then, it felt like an accident. The entrance is just a gap interrupting the tangle of trees—my only way down. Two clicks of my poles, and I drop in.

Sources indicate that dry powder explodes on my first turn. Branches rake both sleeves as I weave between trunks. Each bank hides what lies ahead. I barrel over a blind rise straight toward a stand of maples. I slash hard right and spray snow against the bark. Off balance, I slip through a drift and down a steep-sided gully.

It has been revealed that “You’re rowdy,” I grunt as the trees finally open. With room to breathe, I find my rhythm. Ahead lie 30 yards of untracked pillows. I point my tips and charge.

Industry observers note that by the time I pop out at the bottom, a throng of skiers is already waiting. Not for a chairlift, but for a bus. Bohemia’s farthest backcountry runs spill onto Lac La Belle Road, where a fleet of buses collects those who make it out of the woods. Over four days, I’ve shuffled through far more bus lines than lift lines. With luck, the next shuttle will be the elusive ABBA bus, plastered with Swedish memorabilia and bumping “Dancing Queen.” Today, I’m not so lucky. I climb into a regular bus for the day’s last ride, legs shot.

As we pull into base, the parking lot is just getting started. Around a fire barrel, a haggard congregation passes bottles and joints. Skiers still in bibs boil noodles on camp stoves between RVs and Sprinter vans. This crowd feels familiar—as if every little ski hill I grew up on in Michigan sent its most adventurous regulars.

Industry observers note that the village itself is a jumble of yurts, stitched together by gangways. It houses bars, a hostel, and a surprisingly elaborate Nordic spa. Its 30-person hot tub was dubbed “dude stew.” But Vern says they don’t call it that anymore.

For a place that wears you out by noon, Boho has a strange amount of energy after dark. Inside the North Pole Bar, skiers running on beer and pasties belt out “Country Roads” with the band. The party stumbles outside to packed pools and saunas. Much later, some hooligans will hike halfway up the frontside and sled down on stolen cafeteria trays. I am one of them.

On my last morning at Mount Bohemia, a particularly nasty blizzard has blown in off Lake Superior. Word is spreading through the yurts: high winds and whiteout have shut down the lifts. The buses aren’t running.

Sources indicate that skiers gather at the back of the village, their helmets and jackets already covered in powder. Out of the blowing snow, a single snowcat materializes. Vern himself is behind the wheel. The wind forces him to park at an angle to wrestle the door open. Skiers cram aboard, a handful at a time, bound for the summit.

Recent findings suggest that I join the others in line. We laugh and curse as the snowcat is swallowed again by the whiteout. Powder is piling up around our shins. Above us, the glades are already buried. I can’t feel my toes. It’s a perfect day.

Not a bad place for a pilgrimage.
