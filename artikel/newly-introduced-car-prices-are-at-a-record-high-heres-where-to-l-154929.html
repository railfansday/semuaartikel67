Newly introduced car prices are at a record high. Here's where to look for deals

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Consumers who are in the market for a latest car may find the latest pricing data somewhat sobering.

The average transaction price for a newly introduced vehicle reached an all-time high of $51,109 in October, up 2.1% from a year earlier, according to a report from Kelley Blue Book, an auto valuation firm. The amount excludes incentives, taxes and fees.

But for consumers who can't spend that much — or don't want to — the good news is that plenty of buyers are shelling out much less for a vehicle.

"The average is telling us as much about product mix as it is about affordability," Erin Keating, executive analyst at Cox Automotive, which owns Kelley Blue Book, stated in the report.

Sources indicate that of the five highest-volume car types sold in October, three — compact and subcompact SUVs, and compact cars — sold at prices far below the overall average transaction price, she said. For example, the average amount paid for a compact car in October was $28,030.

Additionally, sales of vehicles priced from about $28,000 to $38,000 climbed 9.6% from a year earlier.

"Consumers are still buying plenty of vehicles well below the headline average transaction price, even as strong demand for full-size pickups and other larger, well-equipped vehicles keeps pushing the average to latest highs," Keating stated.

The record price comes amid a 34% jump in the average cost of a newly introduced vehicle since October 2019, when it was $38,259, according to Kelley Blue Book data.

In a recent development, while pandemic-related supply shortages helped drive up prices earlier this decade, consumers' appetite for larger, more expensive vehicles like SUVs and pickup trucks has also pushed the average higher. Full-size pickups, for instance, sold for an average of $67,912 in October, according to Kelley Blue Book.

Yet buyers who would struggle to afford a $50,000-plus new car can still spend less and find deals in the current market.

For starters, automakers have been slower than usual to roll out 2027 models, at least in part because 2026 cars continue to sell well at current prices, remarked Ivan Drury, director of insights at Edmunds, an automotive research organization. In October, 76% of fresh-vehicle sales were for current-year models, compared with 59% a year earlier.

This slowdown in the annual model-year transition means more 2027 cars will hit dealer lots soon, which suggests dealers and automakers are motivated to clear 2026 inventory, Drury remarked.

But waiting until the last week of the year to buy — thinking dealerships are desperate to make the sale — won't necessarily get you a better deal, he remarked.

"You're not going to get massive savings by waiting," Drury remarked. "Everybody's busiest time of the year is between Christmas and Fresh Year's, but it's not like the best deals are then — that's a perception thing.".

Some manufacturers are offering incentives on certain 2026 models, which can reduce the cost of a new car by thousands of dollars.

In a recent development, for example, the 2026 Nissan Pathfinder, a midsize SUV with a starting price of $41,565 — which includes destination charges — comes with a rebate of $5,000 for qualified buyers through Nov. 30, according to Cars.com, an online automotive marketplace.

Just be sure to check your local dealerships to confirm participation for any manufacturer's incentives, experts say.

Look for cars that have been sitting on dealer lots for an extended time, Drury stated. In September, newly introduced cars took 58 days to sell, on average, according to Edmunds.

Generally, dealerships consider cars unsold for 60 to 90 days to be aging inventory, which may motivate them to offer a better deal, according to Autotrader.com, an online auto marketplace owned by Cox Automotive.

It has been revealed that "You'll find the vehicles that dealers definitely want to get rid of the most," Drury stated. Looking for older inventory will be "your quickest way to find a dealership that's thinking, 'Yes, I too want to get rid of this. Help me help you,'" he said.

Listings on some car-shopping websites include how long a car has been on a dealership's lot. You can also ask the dealership how long a particular car has been in its inventory.

Leasing may present an opportunity to get into a car with lower monthly payments than financing the same vehicle, depending on the terms of the lease.

In a recent development, however, fewer attractive leasing deals are available, a trend that started during the pandemic, Drury said. Plus, leases, which are typically for three years, usually come with mileage limits and charges for excessive wear and tear.

Nevertheless, leasing may make financial sense for a consumer who "gets that two- or three-year itch for a new car," Drury mentioned.

This is because after the first two or three years of making payments on a car loan — a time period when the steepest depreciation generally occurs — the car's value may be less than what is still owed on the loan, depending on the specific terms. This is known as being upside down or underwater, or having negative equity. In that case, the amount must either be paid off or added to the buyer's newly introduced loan.

"We see so many cars traded in that are 3 years old, and the customer is upside down by $7,000, $8,000 or $10,000 and they roll that into another loan," Drury mentioned.

You also may be able to find a good deal on a used car that's not too old.

In a recent development, the average transaction price for a 3-year-old vehicle was $32,039 in the third quarter, down slightly from $32,461 in the second quarter but 3.1% higher than $31,067 a year earlier, according to Edmunds. It's also higher than the average $27,133 list price for used cars of any age in October, according to Cox.

Industry observers note that however, these 3-year-old cars are selling for far less than they did when they were newly introduced — $16,562 below the original sticker price of $48,601, on average. That marks the largest dollar gap Edmunds has on record, surpassing the previous high of $16,252 in the last quarter of 2019.

Some 3-year-old models tend to take longer to sell than others, which may give buyers some negotiating power, according to Edmunds. For example, a 3-year-old Jeep Cherokee, with an average transaction price of $23,138 — compared with its original price of $42,263 — takes an average of 64 days to sell, according to Edmunds.

It has been revealed that additionally, a good credit score can help keep the cost of financing a car purchase down. The higher your score, the lower the interest rate you can get, generally speaking.

And, finally, if you're trading in a car, be sure to shop it around, Drury remarked.

In a recent development, "Get multiple offers. They can vary by thousands of dollars," Drury mentioned. "Have those offers in hand when you're at the dealership that has the next car you want to buy.".
