According to recent reports, in the 1980s and early '90s, if an action TV series wanted to succeed, the easiest way to do it was to give the hero a cool car. The Dukes of Hazzard had a Dodge Charger, Michael Knight (David Hasselhoff) drove around in a black Trans Am named KITT on Knight Rider, and in Magnum P.I., Thomas Magnum (Tom Selleck) looked like the coolest dude in the world behind the wheel of his bright red Ferrari.

'80s action TV got so wild that Airwolf went a step higher and made the vehicular hero a helicopter. Developed for CBS in 1993, Viper tried to recreate that success. Viper's plot was about a crime fighter who battles the bad guys while driving the new Dodge Viper sports car. Think Fast & Furious meets RoboCop, with a transforming vehicle doing the heavy lifting. There was only one problem: famed producer Stephen J. Cannell came up with a series carrying the exact same name.

The Dodge Viper reached buyers in 1992. It was a pricey sports car that not many could afford, but with its open roof, long hood, and wide front, it really did look like a viper ready to strike. A car like that might not be a huge seller on the mainstream market, but the style could surely be used to make money in other ways. Chrysler and Paramount Television worked on a deal that would make the Dodge Viper the star in its own titular CBS series simply called Viper.

Sources indicate that the premise for the TV action series was simple: in the fictional Metro City, crime is running rampant, leading to the police department forming a crime-fighting force known as the Viper Project. At the center of this was a bright red Dodge Viper that could turn into a militaristic, silver, armored car called "Defender." The series would be led by James McCaffrey as Michael Payton (Viper's version of Michael Knight), and the car's creator and the brains behind the operation, the paralyzed Julian Wilkes (Dorian Harewood).

However, the same year that Viper was set to debut, another show with the exact same title went into production. This one was led by TV producer and novelist Stephen J. Cannell, the legend who helped create iconic TV shows such as The Rockford Files, The A-Team, and 21 Jump Street. Both series had similar premises, with a cool hero behind the wheel of a cool car called a Viper as they took down the bad guys, but only Paramount had the rights to use the actual Dodge Viper. This led to the Chrysler Corporation trying to stop Cannell Studios Inc. from going into production with their Viper series through a trademark infringement lawsuit.

Chrysler contended that the red car being used in trademarks for Cannell's Viper looked very similar to the Dodge Viper. It wasn't going to be an easy task to stop it, as Cannell's show had already cast Michael Dudikoff in the lead and sold the syndicated series to over 50 stations. A month later, Cannell Studios caved by changing the name of their Viper to Cobra and making the car an old-school Shelby Cobra.

Cobra premiered in September 1993 with Michael Dudikoff playing Robert "Scandal" Jackson, an A.W.O.L. Navy SEAL hiding out in Alaska who becomes an underground crime fighter for the secret organization Cobra while barreling through the fictional Bay City in his cherry red Shelby Cobra (what a coincidence!). Sadly, Cobra, which co-starred James Tolkan (best known as the principal in Back to the Future) as Cobra director Dallas Cassell, and Allison Hossack as Danielle LaPoint, Dallas' daughter and Scandal's partner, only lasted one season, going off the air in May 1994.

Industry observers note that although Viper was no huge hit, it fared much better than Cobra. Viper ended up premiering on NBC instead of CBS in January 1994, only to be canceled by the network after one season. That could have been the end of it, but Viper survived, returning for a second season in syndication in 1996. However, it was with an almost entirely newly introduced cast, now with a CIA agent named Thomas Cole (Jeff Kaake) leading the Viper group.

After two seasons with Kaake as the lead, James McCaffrey returned for the fourth and final season, now switching things up by driving a blue Dodge Viper. Fittingly, the series ended in the spring of 1999, the perfect time capsule of late 20th-century action television in an era before the landscape completely changed and left the simple but effective use of cool cars behind.
