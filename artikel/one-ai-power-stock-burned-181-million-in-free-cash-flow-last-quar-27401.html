It has been revealed that the AI power boom is driving massive demand for power equipment manufacturers such as GE Vernova (GEV +1.29%) and Bloom Energy (BE -2.15%). It's also creating an interesting cash-flow divergence.

According to recent reports, in the third quarter, Bloom Energy burned $181 million in free cash flow, while GE Vernova generated $5.4 billion, well above its GAAP net income of $1.3 billion. The difference comes down to a crucial measure investors shouldn't overlook: working capital.

In capital-intensive businesses, profits are part of the story. How effectively a firm turns day-to-day operations into cash can tell us much more. Here's what investors should know about this key difference between GE Vernova and Bloom Energy right now.

Sources indicate that in the past couple of years, GE Vernova and Bloom Energy have benefited from powerful tailwinds driven by an unprecedented demand for on-site, reliable power solutions. After decades of modest electricity demand growth, data centers, industrial reshoring, and grid electrification are driving strong electricity demand.

In Q3, GE Vernova booked $29 billion in orders for heavy-duty gas turbines and other power equipment, against $12.6 billion in total revenue, and its backlog grew to $197 billion. Because hyperscalers and utility customers pay upfront deposits to reserve factory slots, GE Vernova saw contract liabilities (deferred revenue) jump from around $26 billion to $46 billion in the first nine months of this year.

In a recent development, on top of this, GE Vernova takes longer to pay suppliers than it does to hold inventory. Taken together, when orders surge, cash flows into GE Vernova well before product is delivered. As a result, its non-cash working capital (which subtracts payables and contract liabilities from receivables, inventory, and contract assets) has fallen, contributing significantly to its $5.4 billion in free cash flow in the period.

Sources indicate that bloom Energy saw free cash flow come in at negative $181 million due to the opposite effect. That's because Bloom is manufacturing and installing solid-oxide fuel cells faster than it bills customers, partly because it has offered customer-friendly milestone-based billing tied to installation and commissioning, rather than requiring upfront payments.

As a result, unbilled work absorbs almost all of its customer inflow deposits, and fuel cells sitting in finished goods drain its cash balance until they are delivered and billed.

GE Vernova and Bloom Energy have been major beneficiaries of AI adoption, although both stocks are experiencing significant volatility as investors reevaluate the thesis around the size and speed of the data center build-out.

For GE Vernova, its negative non-cash working capital position provides a financial cushion, as customers and suppliers pay for its expansion, boosting near-term free cash flow that outpaces earnings growth and reducing the need to raise capital.

For Bloom Energy, its positive non-cash working capital means it relies on timely execution and customer payments to grow its cash position. The tech giant is spending heavily on manufacturing and expanding capacity, along with customer-friendly milestone-based billing, which is eating into free cash flow today despite surging profits.
