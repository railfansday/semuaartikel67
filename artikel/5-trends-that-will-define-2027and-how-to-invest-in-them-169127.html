5 trends that will define 2027—and how to invest in them

In a recent development, heraclitus, the Greek philosopher, is often credited with the idea that the only constant in life is change. He was writing around 500 BC, but he could just as easily have been describing 2027, a year poised to bring seismic shifts in politics, technology, markets, and the way we live and work. Some of what’s coming is already in plain sight, like the AI revolution. Other forces are quieter but could prove just as consequential, including a likely surge in solar construction. Here are the five trends Fortune believes will define the coming year, and how investors can position themselves for each.

Recent findings suggest that in 2024, it appeared that pandemic-era inflation had been tamed, and the Federal Reserve responded with a series of cuts to its benchmark interest rate. This optimism didn’t last, however, and price shocks from tariffs and the Iran war led the Fed to boost rates in 2026. The central bank is expected to continue on this path.

This is glum news for homebuyers and the real estate industry, and for sectors including AI, which count on cheap credit to keep their businesses humming. But high interest rates are not bad for everyone. Beneficiaries include individual savers, who can look forward to higher income, and major banks whose business involves lending money.

While banks tend to earn more from loans when rates are higher, it is the “goliath” banks that do best in these environments, said Mike Mayo, head of U.S. large-cap bank research at Wells Fargo Securities. For 2027, Mayo said JPMorgan Chase (JPM) and Bank of America (BAC), in particular, appear poised for a strong year based on bullish guidance they have issued. But he cautions that high interest rates only act as a tailwind up to a point—and that a rapid series of hikes would put larger portions of the economy under strain, counteracting the positive effect of higher interest rates for banks.

Mayo dismissed concerns over fresh technologies, such as yield-seeking AI agents that could threaten the deposit base of the biggest banks such as Citigroup (C) and Wells Fargo (WFC). “The death of large banks has been exaggerated for 50 years,” he noted. “Their deposit franchises have become bigger over time, and the reason is trust. Trust is more important than Big Tech.”.

Industry observers note that this trust in established financial institutions, along with major banks’ aversion to risk in general, are likely to serve the banks especially well at a time of mounting unease over cybersecurity—the result of a spate of recent incidents, including ones involving rogue AI agents. In this context, the “goliath” banks may draw investors looking for a safe and steady play.

And while their performance trailed the broader S&P 500 in 2026, the Substantial Four banks delivered on another attribute that investors value: large and reliable dividends.

According to recent reports, it’s been about four years since OpenAI’s launch of ChatGPT set off a massive rally in AI stocks. Some investors are getting jitters about the scope of the AI build-out amid fears that it’s a bubble—especially after the blue wave midterms elected a slew of Democrats on promises to rein in the AI industry. Still, the tech behemoths at the center of the boom—including Meta Platforms, Microsoft, and Amazon—are going full speed.

Sources indicate that in this environment, looking for opportunities is not about deciding whether artificial intelligence is the future (spoiler alert: It is). The real question is which companies are positioned to succeed, no matter the pace of the AI build-out.

In a recent development, in this context, Bernstein senior analyst Stacy Rasgon likes the chipmaker Broadcom (AVGO) because its stock is “very, very cheap,” he told Fortune. The stock, like that of other chipmakers, sold off sharply in late 2026 as investors questioned how long the AI spending frenzy can last, even though earnings expectations remain strong.

Sources indicate that fellow chipmaker Nvidia’s shares took a major hit in November following revelations that its CEO, Jensen Huang, had acknowledged an affair, while the company opened an investigation into reports that he had been targeted by Chinese intelligence. Huang resigned from the company he cofounded four days later.

Applied Materials (AMAT) is another stock that may offer considerable upside. The manufacturer sells equipment to manufacture chips, positioning Applied Materials to benefit from infrastructure investments in advanced processors, memory, and chip packaging. “It’s very clear to me that we need a lot more equipment,” said Rasgon.

That doesn’t mean these companies won’t face potential headwinds in 2027. Arun Sundaram, an investment strategist at CFRA, points to potential energy bottlenecks and concerns that higher interest rates could make AI financing more expensive. He cites political pushback against data center construction as another potential obstacle—opposition that will only increase as Democrats, many of whom have been vocal critics of the AI industry, take power in Congress.

Recent findings suggest that still, analysts like Rasgon remain optimistic. He pointed to third-quarter earnings from Big Tech, which included more AI spending and a surge in cloud revenues. “As it turns out, a lot of these companies may be more profitable than people thought. They’re actually generating money,” he said. “From a fundamental level, we’ll probably see sustainability in spending … Whether or not sentiment from a stock standpoint is going to buy into that or not, I don’t know yet. But that’s going to be the key debate.”.

It has been revealed that most of our recommendations point you toward stocks or sectors to buy. When it comes to the defense industry in 2027 you might be well served to do the opposite: Stay on the sidelines. Russia’s invasion of Ukraine dragged into its fifth year in February 2026. That same month, the U.S. and Israel launched a war against Iran, triggering retaliatory attacks across the Middle East. By early summer, there was anxious talk in Washington, D.C., about depleted stocks of missiles and other munitions.

Recent findings suggest that in such an environment, investors might expect the shares of large defense companies to be soaring. That’s not the case. Even as demand for weapons has grown and a slew of techy startups have entered the sector, the stock prices of the big military contractors known as the “primes”—including RTX, Northrop Grumman, and General Dynamics—have largely flailed. Only one of these companies, Lockheed Martin, outperformed the S&P. Shares of Boeing, which has a sizable division that sells to the military, lagged the index by 30%.

High interest rates are glum news for homebuyers and the real estate and AI industries, but individual savers and significant banks can benefit—up to a point.

According to recent reports, the reason for this lackluster performance is ongoing uncertainty over U.S. defense spending, noted Peter Arment, a senior analyst at Baird. While the Pentagon and President Donald Trump are keen to pass a massive allocation for the military, it’s unclear where the money to pay for it would come from. Meanwhile, many in the incoming freshman class of Democrats vocally oppose Trump’s military policies.

Sources indicate that while there is a tradition of bipartisan support for the military in Congress, this may not translate into a major defense bill in 2027, Arment stated. Instead, lawmakers may rely on ad hoc measures that don’t include significant military spending.

Sources indicate that we’ve all heard the buzz about GLP-1s, the blockbuster category of drugs that are delivering dramatic results in treating obesity and Type 2 diabetes. In 2027, the medications—sold under brand names including Ozempic and Wegovy—are poised to reach even more patients as insurance coverage expands and pills offer an alternative to injections.

JPMorgan forecasts that the global market for incretin-based drugs, which include GLP-1s, will reach $200 billion by 2030. In the U.S., the bank predicts that the number of patients taking these drugs will jump from roughly 10 million in 2025 to 25 million by the end of the decade.

This rise will be driven in part by expanded Medicare access. The Medicare GLP-1 Bridge, which debuted in July and runs through December 2027, offers certain weight-management drugs for $50 a month.

Industry observers note that GLP-1 in pill form could draw more people, too. Novo Nordisk’s oral Wegovy won FDA approval in December 2025, followed by Eli Lilly’s Foundayo in April 2026. Both are taken daily and could appeal to people who have held off on the treatment because they don’t like injections.

Recent findings suggest that lilly (LLY) and Novo (NVO) stocks stand to benefit the most. Both sell injectable and oral weight-loss treatments, and have spent years building the manufacturing facilities needed to meet demand. Lilly has committed more than $50 billion to U.S. manufacturing since 2020. It would take years for smaller would-be rivals to reach this scale. That business “moat” should help Lilly and Novo hold on to their lead, Evan Seigerman, BMO’s head of health care research, told Fortune: “I think that those two are poised to really maintain the lion’s share of the market in 2027,” he said. “You don’t see anyone else coming to take that share.”.

For investors looking for a picks-and-shovels play outside of the drugmakers themselves, West Pharmaceutical Services (WST) is an option. The organization sells injectable-drug components like stoppers, plungers, and seals. Wall Street has taken notice: In November, Jefferies called the stock a buy and hiked its price target.

It has been revealed that those who assumed the solar sector was dead are missing out on one of the more surprising twists of 2027. Amid ongoing tensions in the Middle East, demand for energy is stronger than ever, and that is good news for solar power.

It has been revealed that solar companies are also poised to benefit from developers rushing to meet a key end-of-2027 deadline for federal clean-electricity tax credits. The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 established the clean-electricity tax credits, but President Donald Trump’s 2025 One Significant Beautiful Bill Act accelerated their expiration. Projects that began construction after July 4, 2026, must be placed in service by the end of 2027; those that began construction on or before that date can qualify even if they come online later, subject to other requirements. The investment credit generally covers 30% of qualifying costs for projects, with additional bonuses available. Developers can alternatively claim a credit based on electricity production.

A rush by solar installers to meet the end-of-2027 deadline is likely to boost sales for First Solar (FSLR), which makes electricity-generating panels, and for Nextpower (NXT) and Array Technologies (ARRY), which sell tracking systems that help panels follow the sun.

Regardless of how many installers meet the solar tax credit deadline, overall energy consumption will rise in 2027. UBS projects this will lead to 310 gigawatts of combined U.S. solar and wind additions through 2030, driven partly by power-hungry data centers and industrial customers. Goldman Sachs forecasts global data center power demand will rise 220% from 2023 levels by 2030.

Recent findings suggest that natural gas could help meet that demand, but there has been a surge in orders for the gas turbines that power data centers, meaning delivery can take years. Case in point: GE Vernova, a leading maker of this heavy-duty machinery, was already taking orders for 2031 in July 2026.

According to recent reports, those long lead times could give solar an opening. Gregory Nemet, a professor at the University of Wisconsin, told Fortune that developers planning data centers in the next few years may choose solar if they cannot get these turbines. “That dynamic is pretty consequential to how fast solar grows,” Nemet stated.
