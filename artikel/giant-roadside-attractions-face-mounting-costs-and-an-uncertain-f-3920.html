Karla Adam grew up in Trochu, Alberta, home to a 40-foot-tall golf tee.

In a recent development, tyra the Tyrannosaurus is very large, even by T. rex standards.

According to recent reports, at 86 feet tall, Tyra looms over Drumheller, Alberta, a town of 8,000 in the Canadian prairie that is known for fossil discoveries and bills itself as the dinosaur capital of the world. Tyra is about four times the size of a real T. rex, which would have barely come up to her knee.

Now, Tyra is facing an extinction event of her own. This one involves an expiring lease and mounting maintenance costs.

Recent findings suggest that she is not alone. Across North America and Australia, thousands of communities have used oversize roadside sculptures to put themselves on the map. Decades after their construction, these landmarks face a crossroads as novelty fades, costs escalate, and local groups are left holding a steep bill.

Tyra’s fate hangs in this balance.

Sources indicate that last year, the Drumheller and District Chamber of Commerce decided to close Tyra’s site when its land lease expires in 2029, citing unsustainable maintenance costs. The decision set off a scramble to determine her future.

Recent findings suggest that for a quarter-century, tourists have stepped out of their cars near the intersection of Gorgosaurus Street and Tyrannosaurus Drive to snap photographs of Tyra. The bravest visitors climb 106 steps to peer out from between her fiberglass teeth to views of the Canadian Badlands.
