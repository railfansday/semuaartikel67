Is Cottage Cheese Good for You?

Yes, cottage cheese is good for you! It's packed with protein and calcium, but the kind you buy matters. Here's how to choose the best cottage cheese for cooking, baking and snacking.

In a recent development, I have to admit: I can see why cottage cheese recipes go viral. TikTok trends have turned this humble dairy staple into everything from high-protein flatbread to better-for-you ice cream. Packed with protein and key vitamins and minerals, cottage cheese is an easy way to make everyday meals more satisfying.

Recent findings suggest that as a dietitian and recipe developer, I love an ingredient that’s not only nutritious but also versatile and affordable, and that can help me meet my daily protein goals. There are so many creative ways to use cottage cheese, and a whole newly introduced world of options opens up when you whip it smooth. Think: smoothies, creamy dips, pasta sauces, puddings and more!

Sources indicate that but as versatile as cottage cheese is, you can’t swap it into every recipe without a little know-how. And with so many milkfat options, the one you buy matters. I’ll share my favorite ways to use it, which varieties work best for different dishes and a few tricks to help you get delicious results every time.

First things first: Here’s why you might want to toss another tub of cottage cheese into your grocery cart.

It has been revealed that cottage cheese is one of my go-to high-protein foods. A half-cup serving packs 12 to 14 grams of protein, roughly the same amount you’d get from two large eggs.

Adding a scoop of cottage cheese to my plate is an easy way to make meals and snacks more satisfying without turning on the stove. I especially like it at breakfast, when it’s all too easy to end up with a meal that’s heavy on carbohydrates but light on protein. A scoop alongside toast and fruit, or blended into pancake batter, gives breakfast a little extra staying power without much effort.

You don’t have to be lifting heavy weights to care about muscle health. Our bodies need plenty of protein to maintain and repair muscle tissue, and that becomes especially important as we get older.

It has been revealed that I like to keep cottage cheese on hand for an easy post-workout snack because there’s nothing to prep. Pair it with fruit or whole-grain crackers, and you’ve got protein plus carbohydrates to help muscles refuel and recover after exercise.

The protein in cottage cheese does more than help keep you full and support your muscles—it also plays an crucial role in keeping your bones strong. Cottage cheese aprovides two minerals that work together to support bone health: phosphorus and calcium.

It has been revealed that it’s worth noting that cottage cheese contains less calcium than a serving of milk or Greek yogurt, but it can absolutely contribute to your daily intake. Just be sure to include other calcium-rich foods in your diet, too.

Emiko Franzen For Taste Of Home.

The type of cottage cheese you buy can make a major difference in how your recipes turn out. Like other dairy products, cottage cheese comes in different milkfat percentages, which affects its taste and texture.

I usually reach for 2% or 4% cottage cheese, depending on how I’m using it. Low-fat (2% cottage cheese) is perfect for whipping into dips, spreads and pasta sauces. It contains enough fat to blend smooth and creamy without feeling too heavy.

Full-fat, or 4% cottage cheese, gets my vote for baked goods, casseroles and lasagna fillings. The extra fat adds richness, which is especially helpful if you’re using it in place of sour cream or cream cheese.

Recent findings suggest that I usually skip fat-free cottage cheese for cooking. It tends to be watery with a slightly chalky taste, and the lack of fat can make creamy recipes less satisfying. That extra moisture can also affect the texture of baked dishes, so I prefer cottage cheese with a little more fat for the best results.

For all its nutritional perks, cottage cheese can also pack a lot of sodium. Some brands contain more than 400 milligrams per half-cup serving. Eat a full cup, and you could be getting more than 800 milligrams of sodium (about one-third of the recommended daily limit) from cottage cheese alone.

Too much sodium can raise blood pressure and increase your risk of heart disease, so it’s worth paying attention to how much you’re getting, especially if you eat cottage cheese regularly.

Recent findings suggest that if you’re watching your sodium intake, compare labels and choose a brand that’s lower in sodium. Good Culture is one of my favorite cottage cheese brands. With 380 milligrams of sodium per serving, it’s a little lower than other brands.

I also go easy on added salt when using cottage cheese with salty ingredients like Parmesan or ranch seasoning.
