12 common physical symptoms of anxiety (and how to ease them)

Anxiety doesn’t only impact your brain. Sometimes the first sign is your heart pounding for no clear reason, or a wave of nausea before a substantial conversation. You might feel dizzy, short of breath, or like there’s a knot in your stomach. You’re not in immediate danger, but your body is reacting as if you are.

This is what the physical symptoms of anxiety feel like. They can be alarming, especially if you don’t recognize them as anxiety right away. But your body responds this way for a reason, and understanding it can take some of the fear out of the experience.

Below we’ll look at the most common physical symptoms of anxiety, why they happen, and some ways to ease them.

According to recent reports, anxiety can affect your body before you've consciously registered that you're anxious at all. These are among the most common physical symptoms.

It has been revealed that 1. Racing or pounding heart (palpitations): A fast-beating or fluttering heart is one of the most recognized signs of anxiety. When your body senses a threat, it releases adrenaline, which speeds up your heart rate to pump blood to your muscles faster. This is useful when you actually need to run. Less so when you’re just checking your inbox.

2. Shortness of breath: Anxiety can make you feel like you can’t get a full breath, or like you’re slightly suffocating. Anxious breathing tends to be shallow and fast, which can lower carbon dioxide levels in the blood. This can feel scary, and anxiety is a common cause.

3. Chest tightness or pain: Muscle tension is a core part of the anxiety response and can be most noticeable in your chest, where you might feel tightness, pressure, or even mild pain. Seek emergency care if you feel sudden or severe chest pain, pain with shortness of breath or sweating, or pain spreading to the arm or jaw.

It has been revealed that 4. Dizziness or lightheadedness: Fast, shallow breathing can also leave you feeling faint or spaced out.

5. Tingling or numbness: Lower carbon dioxide levels in the blood can affect nerve signals and cause tingling or a pins-and-needles feeling in the hands, feet, or face. It can feel unsettling, but it’s usually short-lived. Numbness on one side of the body, sudden weakness, or trouble speaking needs urgent care.

6. Sweating: Sweating is part of the fight-flight-freeze-fawn response. Anxiety and stress can switch on your sweat glands even when you’re not hot, especially on your palms, underarms, and face.

Industry observers note that 7. Nausea or upset stomach: Your gut and brain are closely connected through the vagus nerve. When anxiety activates the stress response, digestion can slow or become disrupted. That can cause stomach upset, nausea, cramping, or an urgent need to use the bathroom.

8. Dry mouth: The stress response can slow saliva production, so many people notice a dry or sticky mouth when they’re anxious. It’s common and usually temporary, though it can make speaking feel harder than usual.

According to recent reports, 9. Muscle tension, aching, or trembling: Anxiety hormones prime your muscles to act promptly. Sustained tension without a chance to release can cause aching, stiffness, or subtle trembling, particularly in the hands, legs, or jaw.

10. Headaches: Tension headaches often appear with anxiety and stress. They’re typically felt as a band of pressure around your head or tension in the neck and shoulders.

Recent findings suggest that 11. Difficulty sleeping: Anxiety and sleep tend to make each other worse. Anxious thoughts and a body that’s physically on alert make it hard to fall or stay asleep. Poor sleep, in turn, can heighten anxiety the next day, which is how the cycle keeps going.

12. Fatigue: Anxiety takes energy because running the stress response tires the body, and poor sleep or constant tension can add to it. Ongoing anxiety can leave people feeling exhausted even without much physical effort.

It has been revealed that note: Some of the above symptoms can also point to a medical condition. If you have chest pain, trouble breathing, or symptoms that are newly introduced, severe, or don’t feel like your usual anxiety, it’s best to seek medical care.

Anxiety triggers the fight-flight-freeze-fawn response (also known as the stress response). When your brain senses a threat, it activates the sympathetic nervous system and releases hormones like adrenaline and cortisol. These hormones prepare your body to respond to danger. Your heart rate increases, your breathing quickens, your muscles tense, and non-essential systems like digestion temporarily slow down.

In a recent development, this response is useful when the threat is physical. The challenge is that your nervous system doesn’t always tell the difference between real danger and everyday stress. A tense conversation can trigger a milder version of fight-or-flight. The result is a body primed for action in situations that don’t call for it.

Industry observers note that for most people these symptoms rise and fall with the stressful moment. When anxiety is ongoing, the symptoms can stick around longer.

There’s no instant off switch for anxiety, but these approaches can help ease physical symptoms, both in the moment and over time.

In a recent development, controlled breathing can help calm the nervous system, and research suggests slow breathing helps many people feel calmer. Slow, gentle breathing, especially with a longer exhale, can shift your body toward the rest-and-digest (parasympathetic) system, which counteracts the stress response.

Sources indicate that try: Breathe in through your nose for four counts and out slowly for six. Repeat for a few minutes. You could also try box breathing, where you rhythmically hold your breath between inhales and exhales.

Recent findings suggest that progressive muscle relaxation involves intentionally tensing and releasing muscle groups throughout the body. It’s designed to help you relax by encouraging each muscle to unclench. It doesn’t take long, with typical sessions lasting just 10 to 20 minutes.

According to recent reports, try: Start with your feet. Tense the muscles for five seconds, then release completely. Work your way up through your calves, thighs, abdomen, hands, arms, shoulders, and face. Skip tensing any area that’s injured or painful.

Caffeine is a stimulant that can raise your heart rate and cause jitteriness, which may feel like anxiety or make it stronger.

Alcohol may feel calming at first, but it can disrupt your sleep and may make anxiety worse.

Cutting back on both is worth considering if you’re dealing with anxiety symptoms frequently.

According to recent reports, if physical symptoms of anxiety are frequent, severe, or getting in the way of daily life, talking with a doctor or mental health professional is a good next step. Effective treatments exist, including cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and medication when appropriate. You don’t have to manage this on your own.

Physical symptoms are a common part of anxiety. Anxiety activates the body’s stress response, which causes real changes like a faster heart rate, muscle tension, and altered breathing. Many people with anxiety notice at least some physical symptoms alongside the mental ones. If they’re frequent or getting in the way of daily life, it’s worth talking with a doctor or mental health professional.

It has been revealed that “Anxiety attack” isn’t a clinical term, but people use it to describe anxiety that builds until it feels overwhelming, usually in response to a stressor like a looming deadline or a tense situation. The physical symptoms can include a rapid heartbeat, shortness of breath, muscle tension, trembling, dry mouth, dizziness, and nausea. These sensations can be distressing, but they tend to ease as the stressful situation passes or your anxiety settles.

In a recent development, some anxiety symptoms feel strange because the stress response affects many parts of the body at once. For example, fast breathing can cause tingling in your hands. Or you might have a feeling of unreality or disconnection (called dissociation). Muscle tension might make it feel like your throat is closing. These varied symptoms can all have anxiety as the common cause. Of course, if they’re latest, intense, or don’t fit your usual anxiety symptoms, it’s best to check in with a doctor.

A worry loop is a cycle where anxiety feeds on itself. One anxious thought leads to another, and the more attention it gets, the stronger it grows.

For example, an anxious thought sets off a physical sensation, that sensation becomes the focus of fresh worry (“Why is my heart racing? What’s wrong with me?”), and the worry drives more symptoms.

Breaking the loop usually means addressing both the mind and the body. You can calm the body directly with breathing and grounding, or use cognitive approaches like CBT to interrupt the thought patterns that keep the cycle going.
