The artificial intelligence boom could lead to a recession, whether through a collapse in AI investment or widespread job losses as the technology advances, a hedge fund manager noted.

"I do worry that if we don't have an AI-led recession in a few years, we're going to have an unemployment-led recession," Sheru Chowdhry, founder and chief investment officer at DSC Meridian Capital, mentioned during a panel discussion moderated by CNBC's Joanna Ossinger at the iConnections Global Alts Asia conference in Singapore last week.

Sources indicate that chowdhry's warning highlights a risk that extends beyond a potential bursting of the AI bubble. Rapid advances in the technology could lead to widespread job losses, with implications for the housing market and consumer spending, he stated.

"If this continues to go at the pace it is, I think in a few years a lot of us will probably not have jobs," Chowdhry said, adding that investors would have to rethink their approach to sectors such as housing and discretionary spending.

Even so, the fund manager remains bullish on computing power and sees opportunities in data centers, despite concerns about AI valuations.

The current AI investment cycle reminds Chowdhry of the dot-com bubble and the telecommunications infrastructure buildout of the early 2000s.

"This cycle reminds me of the dot-com bubble and the telecom build we had going on in the early 2000s," he stated. "Every cycle is different, but it kind of rhymes in some ways.".

Chowdhry also questioned whether semiconductor companies could sustain their profit margins in the coming years.

However, he sees opportunities in data centers, pointing to access to power and grid connections as major bottlenecks in the AI buildout.

"If you can identify data centers, I feel like that's going to be the picks and shovels of this cycle," he remarked.

In particular, data centers that can secure permits and access to power within the next two years present a significant investment opportunity, he added.

Sources indicate that beyond AI infrastructure, Chowdhry also pointed to attractive yields in U.S. credit markets.

He cited yields of around 8.5% on U.S. high-yield bonds and 9.5% on leveraged loans, describing the current market environment as an opportunity to "get paid to wait.".

Investment portfolios may be more vulnerable to market volatility than they appear, according to Dan Gelernter, director of AI at R.G. Niederhoffer Capital Management, who spoke on the same panel.

Average monthly correlations across investable assets rose from around 5% between the 1960s and 1990s to roughly 11% to 12% by 2020, according to research cited by Gelernter.

His own analysis suggests that the figure has climbed further to around 15% to 16% in 2026.

"Most portfolios are more highly levered, less liquid, more highly correlated than they appear to be, and that problem will get worse the more markets move," Gelernter stated.

Within the AI trade, Joo Kian Ong, a partner at AP3X Capital, noted Samsung Electronics would be a good long position, with SK Hynix or Micron on the other side as potential shorts.
