Fossil teeth challenge a long-held idea about where early humans could live

Eight fossil teeth from two caves in southern China suggest that Homo erectus lived in rainforests and survived in the region longer than scientists thought.

Dense tropical rainforest was long regarded as a habitat that only our own species, Homo sapiens, had mastered.

It has been revealed that homo erectus, an extinct human relative, spread from Africa across Asia. It was thought to need grassland and open woodland.

Fossil teeth from two caves in southern China suggest that Homo erectus lived in a rainforest landscape.

They are also far younger than any other firmly dated fossil of the species on the Chinese mainland.

The study was led by Kira Westaway at Macquarie University in Sydney and Renaud Joannes-Boyau at Southern Cross University. Colleagues in China and Europe also took part.

Only a handful of teeth were found, and their dates span a broad window. By the team’s estimate, the species was present sometime between 324,000 and 184,000 years ago.

The two caves are known as Baxian and Naxian. Both are found in Guangxi, among steep limestone hills, roughly 9 miles (15 kilometers) from each other.

At the back of each cave sits a small chamber where fossils have been cemented into rock. More than 18,000 mammal fossils have been recovered from these chambers.

Porcupines may have brought many of the fossils into the caves.

Nine of those fossils were teeth from ancient humans.

Researchers assigned eight of the nine teeth to Homo erectus based on their shape, with ancient enamel proteins supporting the identification of four.

It has been revealed that researchers from the Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology in Beijing were part of the team. The institute also provided access to the excavated fossils.

Westaway’s team used five independent methods to date the teeth and the cave deposits around them.

Two of them work out when grains of sediment were last exposed to sunlight. The rest rely on uranium, which decays slowly in the teeth and in a mineral crust capping the fossils.

In total, 45 ages were obtained. Forty-three of them were fed into a statistical model.

The model gave a 95% probability that Homo erectus was there sometime between 324,000 and 184,000 years ago.

It has been revealed that that span is a measure of how uncertain the dates are. It doesn’t mean the species stayed there for all 140,000 years.

The latest teeth date to between 324,000 and 184,000 years ago, making them younger than the previous firmly dated finds in China, which were about 400,000 years old.

“This is a fascinating discovery because it confirms the presence of Homo erectus in a location we were not expecting and at a time we hadn’t considered,” Westaway said.

Recent findings suggest that elsewhere, the species hung on even longer. Fossils from the Indonesian island of Java show it there until roughly 117,000 to 108,000 years ago.

What an animal eats is written into its tooth enamel as chemistry. Carbon from trees and shrubs looks different there from carbon taken in from tropical grasses.

So by testing a tooth, scientists can tell whether its owner fed in forest or in open country.

No signature of tropical grasses was found in the teeth that were tested.

According to recent reports, many values were low enough to indicate a dense, closed canopy. A similar pattern has been recorded in forests of the western Amazon.

It has been revealed that the mix of species fits this picture. Orangutans, elephants, rhinoceroses and tapirs were found among the fossils. Most of the animals were forest dwellers.

Recent findings suggest that other chemical tests pointed to a humid climate. Conditions were wetter and cooler than in the region today.

Industry observers note that three Homo erectus teeth from Naxian were then tested in the same way. Their carbon values fell in the same forest range.

Those readings fell right where dense canopy gives way to more open forest.

Nicolas Bourgon, affiliated with the Max Planck Institute of Geoanthropology in Germany, also took part in the research.

“In this case, the animal teeth and the Homo erectus teeth are telling the same story,” said Bourgon.

In a recent development, strontium is locked into enamel as well. It’s picked up from local rock by water and plants, and animals take it in when they eat and drink. Each type of rock has its own strontium signature.

Industry observers note that teeth form in childhood, between the ages of 2 and 6. Their strontium therefore records where a child’s food and water came from.

In a recent development, strontium was measured in four Homo erectus teeth. One came from Baxian and three came from Naxian. Each tooth carried signatures from more than one kind of rock.

So each child either moved between regions, or else ate food that had been gathered from several places.

A few of those signatures were consistent with the limestone near the caves.

The most extreme ones best matched rock about 155 miles (250 kilometers) to the south. That area lies toward the Red River valley in northern Vietnam.

“This suggests these humans were far more mobile than previously recognized, moving across surprisingly large distances through a complex rainforest landscape,” Joannes-Boyau said.

Still, the strontium evidence was called limited by the authors, and their reading of it tentative.

Rainforest has played a part in the debate over how Homo erectus died out.

On Java, the last known members of the species lived in open woodland. Rainforest was expanding around them at the time.

It has been revealed that it has been proposed that the spreading forest contributed to the extinction there. In this view, the species could not adapt to it.

The teeth from Guangxi complicate that idea. Homo erectus was “not ecologically excluded from rainforest environments,” the authors wrote.

Scientists use the word hominin for humans and our extinct close relatives.

“This is the first clear, direct evidence we have for another hominin living within these habitats,” mentioned Patrick Roberts, also of the Max Planck Institute of Geoanthropology.

The date range also overlaps with fossils of Denisovans, another extinct human group. Their remains have been found in Laos, on the Tibetan Plateau, in northeastern China and off Taiwan.

The authors wrote that several kinds of humans may have lived across the region during the same period.

The case for rainforest life rests on very few human teeth. Carbon values were obtained from only three of them, and all three came from one cave.

It has been revealed that the teeth show that Homo erectus could survive among rainforest animals. They cannot show that the species did well there.

The authors also wrote that rainforest may not have been its ideal habitat.

Recent findings suggest that it remains unclear whether this ability was limited to late survivors in southern China. It may also have been shared by the species more widely.

To settle that question, the same enamel tests will be needed on fossils from other sites of a similar age.

The full study was published in the journal Nature.
