We Come to Paul Thomas Anderson’s Cameron Winter Live at Carnegie Hall to Feel

The Cambridge Dictionary recently declared that its selection for Word of the Year is human. Human as an adjective that denotes that something was not made by computers or A.I. (Last year’s word was parasocial.) The editor at Cambridge told the Times that they found phrases like “human governance,” “human written,” and “human need” come up often in their research.

According to recent reports, I think about this while I’m at the movie theater watching Paul Thomas Anderson’s latest film, Cameron Winter at Carnegie Hall, which documents the 23-year-old’s performance at the legendary venue. It is the film’s release date, and all of the showings are sold out, but since I’ve come alone, I am able to find the perfect seat. Winter’s face is large on the screen, and it’s almost expressionless except for his eyebrows, barely peeking out from underneath what can truly only be called a mop of hair, which curves this way and that way as the only proof of the emotional energy he is exerting. Otherwise, he seems to simply open his mouth and the words come out, devastating and romantic, because what else would they do? What else would he do but this? For an hour and a half, we all sit here enthralled by a single man and a piano. Then I think about how my most recent moviegoing experiences required staring at people talking, or thinking, or simply staring at a very close range. (I’d just seen You Can See Everything a few weeks before.) We come to the movies to see humans was my brain-rot thought, in the style of Nicole Kidman’s famous AMC commercial. (Brain rot was Oxford’s 2024 Word of the Year.).

I think back to when it all began. The day after Valentine’s Day last year, my friend Tran casually texted me, asking, “You get into this guy?” with a Spotify link to an album called Heavy Metal by someone called Cameron Winter. I couldn’t tell what it would sound like just by looking at the cover, which was a close-up of his face, criss-crossed by silver chains, but what I soon found were songs dominated by a deep baritone voice and a sweet, jangly guitar or a kind of hazily sensual piano. Who was this guy? Well, it turned out he was the lead singer of this band called Geese—not to be confused with Goose—which he’d started when he was a teen with his high school friends. He’d made this album (jokingly rumored to have been recorded in Guitar Centers, from which he was now banned) and released it without any fanfare that previous December, against the advice of people who knew him and thought the record was too strange or would somehow interfere with the planned Geese release in the new year. (That album, Getting Killed, was released in September 2025 and turned the band from little-known Brooklyn musicians into both “saviors of rock & roll” and “music label psy-ops,” topping off numerous best-of lists and garnering multiple Grammy nominations.) But none of that had happened yet.

As I sat in the movie theater, I wondered how many of us in the audience had also been at the performance. What were we looking for in a recording of something we had experienced firsthand? That night, Winter emerged on stage and sat at the piano, his back turned to us. We watched his head sink beneath his shoulders as if he was searching for something deeper inside the instrument, or maybe he was becoming one with it. We had no choice but to completely give ourselves to the music. But Anderson’s camera was nimble and dug itself in other places: a peephole on the stage that looked out to Winter in the foreground and the ocean of people behind him, indeed turning the whole affair into a kind of landscape, swooping in from the audience to Winter at the piano like a detective looking for the culprit of an unnamed crime, or training his lens on the oversize shadow cast by Winter over the audience sitting stage left. I think, “Oh, this is like a musical Rashomon.”.

During “Drinking Age,” as he sings, “Today, I met who I’m gonna be / from now on / and he’s a piece of shit,” the screen goes dark blue, with only his faint silhouette visible, the weight of his words carrying a brief silence so that you sit there in the emotion. Other times, we experience through Anderson’s POV, seeing the ushers open the backstage doors so that he can step onstage, deftly twirling the camera around Winter’s body. This time he is singing “$0.” It’s perhaps the best song on the album (or at least according to this writer), where he sings with wild abandon, “God is real / God is real / I’m not kidding, God is actually real I’m not kidding this time / I think God is actually for real …” Anderson trains his camera on one of the dramatic light fixtures at the concert hall, a round disc that resembles the sun, then he pans to the audience, then to Winter, small on stage, confessing his small miracle. Afterward, as if exhausted, he delicately plays a simple riff on the piano, while there’s a close-up of his face on screen with heavenly rays of light reflected on his face. I believed him when I first heard it on the album, and I believed him twice more as I heard him sing it live.

According to recent reports, winter stands up; the show is over. The lights come up, and the crowd rises to its feet. I can’t remember now if there was an encore, though I do remember we stood there applauding for a very long time, emotionally drained but joyful that we’d been witnesses. On the screen, the credits roll over images of the crowd, and the two teen girls sitting to my right have spotted themselves in the audience, elated. Winter comes back out for two more songs.

The movie ends. “Cameron Winter. Live at Carnegie Hall. Dead at 23,” flashes on the screen. Inside the movie theater, the people applaud. I’m still thinking of Winter singing of “com[ing] down to see / my private ceremonies / All on the floor of this room,” and I think, yes, we’re all here in ceremony—or are we in communion?—with one another. The human need for human connection, for human revelations.
