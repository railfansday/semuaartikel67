Will AI take your job? 3 principles for making yourself harder to replace

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The Navier-Stokes equations, first formulated in the early 19th century, are widely used today to help design airplanes, create computer graphics, and even predict the weather. Yet for almost two centuries, a central question about them remained unsolved. We knew the equations worked in practice, but no one had proven whether they always hold up mathematically without breaking down.That all changed in September when OpenAI claimed to have solved the Navier-Stokes problem, followed on October 6 by a torrent of proposed breakthroughs across nearly 400 math problems that had baffled researchers for generations. As Cornell University mathematician Steven Strogatz explained in an Atlantic essay, the announcements have left the field reeling. How should people think about their work when a machine can do it so much better? Strogatz is no ordinary mathematician. A pioneer in the field of nonlinear systems and network science, he is also an award-winning author and a sometime Latest York Times columnist. His livelihood is not in danger. Yet OpenAI has him asking, “What’s the point?” It’s a question we’re all going to have to answer. Here are three principles that will help you figure it out. Principle 1: The Infinite Monkey Theorem The Argentine writer Jorge Luis Borges had a fascination with a concept known as the infinite monkey theorem. The idea is that if you had an infinite number of monkeys pecking away at an infinite number of typewriters, they would randomly create the collected works of Tolstoy and every other masterwork ever written (or that could be written). Clearly, today we are living in our own version of the infinite monkey theorem, which is what Strogatz was getting at in his Atlantic piece. If you can have infinite agents attacking every aspect of an age-old problem, solving it over a period of hours, what is the value of a mathematician’s work? Is it even worth pursuing? Borges, in his story The Library of Babel, gave us—and mathematicians like Strogatz—some guidance about creating value in a world of infinite monkeys. First, there is the problem of curation. Yes, infinite monkeys could write War and Peace, but good luck finding it amongst all the flotsam and jetsam. We’re seeing the problem in real life today. While AI should theoretically be ramping up productivity, in reality, workers are drowning in workslop. The second problem is that of intent. War and Peace isn’t just meaningful for the words it contains, but because of what Leo Tolstoy intended when he wrote them. In much the same way, while it is true that artificial intelligence can run circles around human mathematicians, it is also true that humans chose the problems that OpenAI solved, and they chose them because humans found them meaningful and, possibly, useful. So one way that mathematicians—or anyone else—can find meaning and value in the age of AI is by acting as curators and as principal agents. Humans can choose the work that gets done according to what is meaningful to them and other humans. You could see the seeds of that idea in Strogatz’s piece. Even if all problems are solvable, we still need to choose which ones we want to solve. Principle 2: The economics of automation Another way to think about how you find meaning and create value in the age of AI is to analyze the economics of automation. One effective framework involves examining three constituent forces: the displacement effect, the productivity effect, and the reinstatement effect. Together, they can help us determine where threats and opportunities might lie. The displacement effect refers to jobs that are replaced by an automating technology—in this case, AI. The productivity effect refers to the increase in output per given unit of input due to the technological advance. The reinstatement effect refers to new jobs made possible by a technological advance. To understand how the three effects work together, it’s helpful to look at the life of Henry Ford. Growing up on a family farm, young Henry would once have been expected to remain in the fields. But as agricultural productivity increased, he had more opportunities beyond the farm. He eventually went to work for Thomas Edison and later created an entirely new automobile industry, reinstating millions of jobs both directly and indirectly. In the case of Henry Ford, the story was a happy one. The productivity and reinstatement effects dwarfed the displacement effect and everybody, for the most part, was better off. But the AI data is fuzzy. We are certainly seeing early signs of the displacement effect as people lose jobs, but so far we’ve seen few signs of a significant productivity or reinstatement effect. Still, understanding the economics of automation can help us find value and meaning. Clearly, with answers to tough problems so much more accessible, mathematicians—and other experts—can help the rest of us formulate good problems to apply to real-world challenges. Some value will likely shift from solving problems to finding good questions. 3. Jevons Paradox and the O-Ring model During the Industrial Revolution—which the AI age is often compared to—James Watt’s steam engine used coal much more efficiently than Thomas Newcomen’s version. So, many assumed that coal use would go down. But English economist William Stanley Jevons saw that just the opposite was happening. Coal use was actually increasing. Ever since, that phenomenon has been known as the Jevons paradox, the effect that occurs when technological improvements that increase the efficiency of a resource’s use lead to a rise, rather than a fall, in its use. If price elasticity is high enough, the decrease in costs encourages people to find more uses for that resource and demand increases. Another useful concept is the O-ring theory of economic development, which gets its name from the Challenger space shuttle disaster. The shuttle, for the most part, was in good working order, but a malfunction in an O-ring seal due to unusually cold weather led to a massive midair explosion. The point is that every job is made up of many interdependent tasks, and if even a single one fails, the entire thing doesn’t work. We’re seeing signs of both effects in the AI economy. While software developers can outsource many coding tasks to AI, they still need humans for others, such as communicating and collaborating with other experts on the team. Nevertheless, with code being produced so much more efficiently, job postings for software developers are on the rise. Strogatz may find something similar happening in mathematics. After all, there are many facets of his job that don’t involve developing proofs. He is a teacher, a writer, and an incredible translator of complex mathematical concepts for laypeople. Maybe more mathematicians can follow suit,  helping experts in other fields solve important problems with mathematical insights. The future of technology is always more human Technological revolutions are always fraught, with plenty of optimism, anxiety, and visions of doom to go around. A breakthrough proves to be transformational, attracting investment that overestimates the opportunity. Boom leads to bust. Jobs are displaced, then new ones appear and, at some point, we look back and it all seems as if it were preordained. Yet when you’re in the midst of disruption, there is little comfort. Yes, things tend to work out in the aggregate, but many are often left worse off. In fact, despite all of the incredible technological advances of the last half-century, a 2018 Pew Research study found that Americans’ real incomes have remained essentially unchanged, with many households far worse off. Today, there is no shortage of opinions about where AI is taking us. Tech entrepreneurs claim that we are on the brink of such a productivity boom that it will lead to a new age of abundance—if, of course, it doesn’t kill us. Economists are much more muted, seeing meager gains amid massive overinvestment. Yet for most of us, those macro-trends aren’t nearly as important as how AI will affect us—and our well-being—directly. The good news is that we have good tools to help us navigate our way through. The infinite monkey theorem highlights the importance of curation and intent. The economics of automation reveals the need for productivity growth to finance reinstatement. The Jevons paradox and the O-ring model encourage us to identify our place within increasingly complex ecosystems. AI can give us answers, but it can’t tell us what questions we want to ask. In the future, that’s where human value and meaning will increasingly lie.
