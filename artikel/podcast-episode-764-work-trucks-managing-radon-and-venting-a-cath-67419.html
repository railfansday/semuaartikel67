It has been revealed that I meant to send this story a bit ago when you were discussing those scary old Milwaukee right-angle drills. Mike really knocked it out of the park with his story about a person spinning under a truck. My story is on par, though I didn’t see it first hand.

Sources indicate that an electrician I worked with had a right-angle drill mishap that stands out as one of the greatest, mostly harmless construction stories I’ve heard. He was standing on an upside-down bucket reaching and drilling upward into the ceiling area with a right-angle drill. You see where this is going, right?

According to recent reports, the drill stuck on something, kicked back, and it twisted him off of the bucket. When the bucket tipped he was left hanging by the drill and holding onto the trigger. He spun, dangling in the air, until the cord wrapped around him enough to unplug itself. If I remember correctly, his partner had to help extract him from the situation.

I sold my right-angle drill to an electrician recently because I hadn’t used it in 10 years due to fear of injury. That was the scariest tool in my carpentry arsenal, not the spinning blade tools.

Are active radon mitigation systems creating radon mines? Does constant suction create defined pathways for more nearby radon to escape the ground? In my 1946 Pleasant Grove, Utah, house I have a partially completed radon mitigation system. It is right now just sucking air from the unencapsulated crawlspace. I’m actively monitoring radon level, and they stay below the 4 picocuries-per-liter threshold while the fan is on.

Recent findings suggest that I turned the radon fan off temporarily. When I finally noticed the radon levels creeping back up, they were much higher than ever before (including before installing the fan). That’s what made me wonder about this idea of creating an easier channel for the radon.

I’m renovating an entire house in Rochester, Newly introduced York, and have an insulation question about the ceiling of a mid-1980s addition.

Sources indicate that when I bought the house, the ceiling had Sheetrock with significant sags between the 24-in.-o.c. framing, including one much wider sag in a single bay. I removed the Sheetrock and found the ceiling cavities packed tightly with cellulose, which was weighing down very stretched-out poly sheeting. I also found a roughly 42-in. span with no framing (related to old skylights, I believe). I’ve since had all the cellulose removed and am starting fresh.

It has been revealed that the roof is a vented assembly with a ridge vent and several soffit vents on each side, but there were no rafter/insulation baffles. I’m wondering whether there was ever meaningful soffit-to-ridge airflow through the roof cavities.

The existing roof sheathing is also in mediocre condition. There aren’t active leaks, but I expect to replace the sheathing when I redo the roof down the road.
