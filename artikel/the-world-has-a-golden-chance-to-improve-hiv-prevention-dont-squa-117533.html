The world has a golden chance to improve HIV prevention: don’t squander it

Sources indicate that earlier this month, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved a generic version of the HIV drug lenacapavir for use as a preventive treatment. Twice-yearly injections of this game-changing medicine — originally developed by the organization Gilead Sciences in Foster City, California — have been shown to be more than 95% effective at preventing HIV acquisition1. At the same time, as Nature reports in a News feature, an HIV vaccine — that would ultimately be a more robust solution to preventing HIV — is closer than ever before.

It has been revealed that in 2024, Gilead signed licensing agreements with six pharmaceutical companies, allowing them to make and sell generic versions of lenacapavir in 120 low- and middle-income countries, subject to regulatory approval. The recent FDA approval brings access a step closer in these countries. The cost to consumers has been set at US$40 per year, fuelling hopes that the drug will help to bring the global HIV epidemic under control.

In a recent development, these welcome developments contrast with the situation of public funding for HIV research and treatment, which is reeling from deep and unconscionable cuts from the US government. Many of the researchers whom Nature spoke to for the News feature had been funded by a programme for preclinical HIV-vaccine research run by the US National Institutes of Health (NIH). But the agency ended the programme last year, forcing researchers to scale back their research and seek alternative funding. Whatever funding comes next is likely to be less stable and less predictable than the NIH funding. It’s imperative that the cuts be reversed — or new government funding sources found.

According to recent reports, the path to an HIV vaccine has been long and bumpy, with decades of high-profile and costly failures, but there is now light at the end of the tunnel. The latest strategy — known as germline targeting — is designed to trigger the production of rare, highly potent antibodies that can prevent infection with diverse HIV strains. The technique has shown encouraging results in animal models2–4 and in early trials in humans5. Researchers are more optimistic than they’ve been in years that an effective HIV vaccine could be on the horizon.

Industry observers note that but in the meantime, antiretroviral drugs such as lenacapavir look poised to have a major impact on infection prevention. In Africa alone, newly introduced infections could be cut by around one-third if just 5% of adults most likely to acquire HIV received the drug, and by half if it was given to one-fifth of adults, according to the Gates Foundation’s Institute for Disease Modelling in Seattle, Washington.

But lenacapavir alone is unlikely to bring the HIV epidemic fully under control. There are several reasons for this: first, it’s not always easy to determine who needs the drug most. Second, to be effective, the drug must be taken for most of someone’s life — or at least while they are sexually active. Third, even $40 a year is unaffordable for people on the very lowest incomes.

Until recently, the United States was the stand-out funder for antiretroviral drugs for people living with HIV in low-and middle-income countries. That stopped in January 2025, when the US administration suspended funding. Although it later restored a limited amount, the effects have been devastating. A survey published earlier this year found that some 1,700 facilities previously offering treatments to people living with HIV had closed as a result (see go.nature.com/4a7ftp4).

Sources indicate that such instability highlights the importance of vaccines. “The funding cuts that happened in the past couple of years have really made it even more obvious how fragile that system of reliance” on drug access is, claims Penny Moore, a virologist at South Africa’s National Institute for Communicable Disease in Johannesburg. And that fragility, as Rogier Sanders, an HIV-vaccine researcher at Amsterdam University Medical Centre, says, is why research into a vaccine remains a priority: “A vaccine makes you independent from access to drugs.”.

But the US cuts have not only curtailed the preclinical vaccine programme, they have also forced the postponement and scaling back of an HIV vaccine trial in South Africa. They have also affected supporting research, such as surveillance efforts tracking HIV evolution in African countries, claims Nairobi-based Vincent Muturi-Kioi, who is leading development of HIV vaccines at the International AIDS Vaccine Initiative in New York City.

Success stories such as the repurposing of lenacapavir as a preventive treatment and progress in vaccine development show the importance of investment in health. It is imperative that HIV vaccine research and deployment continues to receive support, at the same time as rolling out lenacapavir as widely as possible.

It could take more than a decade to translate the encouraging results with germline targeting into an effective HIV vaccine, and the current strategy might encounter more hurdles.

In a recent development, the world is closer than it has ever been to an effective HIV vaccine and has a drug capable of preventing infections. Now is the time for funders to ramp up investment, not cut it back.
