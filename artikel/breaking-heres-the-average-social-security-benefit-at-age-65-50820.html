By Kailey Hagen, CFP® – Nov 19, 2026 at 4:00PM EST.

It has been revealed that for many workers, age 65 represents the first major gateway to retirement because it's when you become eligible for Medicare. While your health coverage begins at this milestone, your Social Security benefit might not be at its full strength. As of December 2025 -- the most recent data available -- the average benefit for 65-year-old retirees is $1,607.27 per month, according to the Social Security Administration (SSA).

Industry observers note that this monthly average serves as a baseline for those eyeing the Medicare milestone. But if your full retirement age is 67, claiming at 65 means accepting a permanent 13.33% reduction in your monthly check. This reduction occurs because you're receiving payments for two additional years compared with those who wait until their full retirement age.

The average benefit varies significantly depending on work history and lifetime earnings. Among 65-year-olds, men receive an average of $1,772.00 per month. Women of the same age receive an average of $1,457.40, according to SSA data. This represents a monthly gap of $314.60 between the two groups.

According to recent reports, these figures represent the average for all retirees who are at the moment age 65, regardless of when they first claimed their benefits. It's a snapshot of current payments rather than a projection of what any individual worker will receive. Your personal benefit depends on your specific earnings history and the exact month you choose to file.

Medicare eligibility at 65 doesn't mean you've reached full retirement age for Social Security. For everyone born in 1960 or later, full retirement age is 67. If you claim at 65, the SSA calculates your benefit by taking your average monthly income over your 35 highest-earning years, adjusting earlier wages for inflation, running it through the benefit formula, and then applying a permanent reduction for claiming early.

Sources indicate that A 3.6% cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) will take effect in 2027 to help benefits keep pace with inflation. This adjustment adds about $58 per month to the $1,607.27 average for 65-year-olds. Because the SSA designed these adjustments to protect purchasing power, they don't necessarily increase your standard of living; they simply aim to cover rising costs.

In a recent development, it's also vital to frame Social Security as supplemental income. The program was designed to replace only about 40% of your pre-retirement income. Most retirees need a much higher share of their former earnings to maintain their lifestyle, so they generally rely on savings or other income sources to bridge the gap.

The claiming decision at 65 is often a trade-off between starting your benefits alongside Medicare or waiting for a higher monthly amount. If you live an average lifespan, you tend to receive roughly the same total regardless of when you claim. Comparing these averages to your own estimated benefit can help you decide if age 65 is the right time to file.

Industry observers note that every month that you delay your Social Security application increases your checks. From ages 65 to your FRA, your checks will grow by 5/9 of 1% per month, or about 6.67% per year.

In a recent development, once you reach your FRA, your checks grow by 2/3 of 1% per month, or 8.00% per year, until you qualify for your largest benefit at age 70. Waiting beyond this point will only cost you money.
