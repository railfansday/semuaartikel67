Latest Update: Jack Grealish recalls a golden age of mavericks – but football has moved on

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Everton forward has magical talent and popularity to burn, but has come up against hard reality of the modern game.

Thomas Tuchel has never called up the player he once claimed to love “everything about”. The reason for Jack Grealish’s omission from Tuchel’s first England squad in March 2025 was given as “he simply has no rhythm”.

Catch a falling star? Grealish was suspended by Everton for three matches following “a breach of club discipline”. Suggestions his loan from Manchester City might be cancelled were calmed by David Moyes. Last week, Grealish played in a behind-closed doors friendly against Derby before issuing an Instagram statement: “I want to apologise to the fans and everyone at the club for not being able to help the team the last few weeks.” Amid tabloid tales of karaoke nights and extended socialising, how close is the former boy wonder, now 31, to blowing it?

By many definitions, Grealish’s is an outstanding career. From teenage home town hero with Aston Villa, an emblem of their revival as a Premier League club, to record £100m signing and treble winner with City. All while maintaining a popular public persona, an everyman quality to accompany the looks and the lifestyle.

Even in the Republic of Ireland, the nation he forsook to play for England, Grealish is nothing like the pariah figure Declan Rice is having taken a similar decision. And yet Grealish is fast becoming a tale of charms unfulfilled, talent inelegantly wasted. The trophies he won at City were as a functioning cog, the “rest station” of Pep Guardiola’s machine, not the expressive soloist who won hearts for those rippling leg muscles. At Euro 2020, as Gareth Southgate’s England ground through the gears in the group stage, Wembley crowds bayed for Grealish.

English football is forever seeking its new Paul Gascoigne, the ball-carrying maverick breaking through heavily fortified enemy lines. Garrulous, fun-loving Grealish threw back to memories of football bad boys who made red-top headlines while remaining idols on the terraces.

The tradition was deep-set, from the 1950s heyday of Len Shackleton, the “Clown Prince” of the north-east, to the 1970s when an era of cloggers and roughhouse football was lifted by “The Mavericks” who inspired the late Rob Steen’s 1995 book of the same name. “We treasure them because they embraced risk, because they dared not to give a damn, because, most of all, they dared on our behalf,” wrote Steen.

Sources indicate that peter Osgood, Alan Hudson, Stan Bowles, Frank Worthington, Charlie George and Tony Currie were household names who enjoyed a 1990s revival in the era of Loaded and Fantasy Football League, when football and debauchery were freely coupled. In 2026 there is an entire cottage industry of podcasts and YouTube channels recalling golden days where the pub was as prevalent in a footballer’s life as the training pitch.

According to recent reports, in 2026, the elite modern footballer’s is a near-monastic existence. On Sunday, the Brighton head coach, Fabian Hürzeler, described the disciplines Pascal Gross follows to remain a high-functioning Premier League player: “So much work in the shadows, recovery, preparation.” The “refuelling habits”, to use the euphemism Graham Taylor attached to Gascoigne, are not workable for a required body-fat percentage between 8% and 12% or the muscle repair from a target of eight to 10 hours of proper sleep.

According to recent reports, technical ability is the barest minimum. Focused application is a necessity, particularly when player wages continue to climb. Jadon Sancho has winners’ medals in the Europa League, Conference League and played in a Champions League final but, at 26, has not found a club willing to take on a player released at the end of his Manchester United career.

Sources indicate that the paradigm player of this age, officially after his Ballon d’Or, is Harry Kane. Both he and Grealish made breakthroughs in the 2014-15 season; Kane as new king of White Hart Lane, Grealish as teenage star of Villa’s run to the FA Cup final. Grealish, spoken of with hushed tones in Birmingham and Irish youth football circles, was the greater talent. Kane’s rise came via a drive to continued improvement from a lower base level.

According to recent reports, as Kane received his individual prize at the London Palladium last month, Thierry Henry, co-compere, made an unscheduled intervention. “Harry Kane is a perfect example that work ethic is a talent, working hard is talent, not only pure skill … this guy had to suffer.” Henry was criticised for comments depicted as elitist, though his point drove at the pair’s diverging paths.

Industry observers note that it also recalled the philosophical battle of the 1970s when cavalier players such as Bowles and George, with five and one England cap respectively, were ignored by the roundhead England managers Alf Ramsey and Don Revie. Succeeding George Best, the exemplar of talent unfulfilled, in being British football’s biggest star was Kevin Keegan, whose playing style was all about hard work and energetic running, such that an innate, deep talent was masked.

In a recent development, best received plenty of second chances. His appearances for Hibernian in the 1979-80 season allowed him to receive a share of Easter Road gate receipts. That same season at Nottingham Forest, Brian Clough tried to get a spin out of both George and Bowles. All three ventures were soon curtailed.

It has been revealed that how many chances remain for Grealish? On joining Everton, he appeared renewed. Moyes stated after a match-winning performance at Wolves last August: “He probably needs a bit of love and attention.” In December at Bournemouth, Grealish returned the sentiment: “I love David Moyes to pieces.” More behaviour like that he was suspended for and that love will be dashed on the rocks. The maverick tradition Grealish was born to follow and its attendant behaviours belong in football’s past.
