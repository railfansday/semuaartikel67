This 9-Year-Old Owns Dozens of Vintage Toilets. He May Have Found His Calling in Plumbing—and Is Inspiring Others.

Like most kids his age, 9-year-old Damian Skaer loves YouTube. Unlike most kids his age, Damian also loves toilets, a passion he discovered on the social media platform.

Now, he’s a social media star himself, showing off his growing collection of vintage toilets to hundreds of thousands of followers across TikTok and Instagram: a pale yellow 1965 American Standard Cadet, a robin’s egg-blue 1963 Crane Oxford, and a 1972 Kohler Wellworth the color of an army tank. Standing on the 11-toilet, fully plumbed platform his dad built in the family’s Eastern Washington backyard, Damian points out their trapways, the fill valves, the siphon jets, the lid bolts, and, of course, the pièce de resistance—the flush.

Industry observers note that roger Skaer, Damian’s dad, says his son’s obsession began a few years ago. “He stumbled across these plumbers on YouTube who replace toilets, test them, fix them, and, man, he just loves watching videos of people working on toilets,” Roger says. “There’s been a lot of engineering behind toilet evolution over the last 100 years, and he’s fascinated by it.”.

Damian’s collection began when he found a couple of old toilets in his grandparents’ barn. Since then, he has amassed about 40, according to Roger. Each week, new models arrive from brands looking to collaborate with the 9-year-old.

According to recent reports, while the topic of young people and screens continues to cause serious consternation among adults, for some kids, they’re serving as an introduction to the skilled trades, occupations like carpentry, welding, and plumbing that have struggled to attract and retain upcoming talent. According to a 2026 National Association of House Builders survey, only 6 percent of young adults 18 to 25 planned to pursue a career in construction trades.

In a recent development, but a crop of young social media influencers are bucking that trend. Guiseppe D’Anna has been working on cars at his dad’s collision repair shop since he was six; today Guiseppe’s Garage has a quarter-million followers on YouTube, and the automotive wunderkind is on “a mission to help shape the next generation of gearheads.” British woodworker Gabriel River Clark has been creating DIY content since he was 12, crafting anything from dinnerware to toys to home decor. On TikTok, 10-year-old Jackson Laux, a.k.a. Just a Jackson Thing, delights his roughly million followers with his deep knowledge of vintage tractors and mowers.

This kind of early exposure is vital to promoting trade occupations, says Paul Goodrum, PhD, a professor in Construction Engineering and Management at the University of Colorado Boulder. While lots of kids used to learn these skills in high school shop or career tech classes, over the past few decades, policy makers and administrators have phased vocational educational courses out of high school curriculums, emphasizing core academic subjects and college readiness instead. The best vocational programs, according to Goodrum, tend to exist in alternative schools for students with behavioral challenges.

Recent findings suggest that “There is a bit of a disconnect between how we measure the quality of our schools and what the economy and the workforce needs in this country,” he says. “As an industry, we really need to be trying to more actively expose kids to these career paths not just in high school but middle school too.”.

But it’s a vicious cycle, Goodrum says: fewer trades workers mean fewer teachers to teach vocational classes, “and how do you convince someone to give up a really high-paying job to go teach high school?”.

Recent findings suggest that the shortage of skilled workers is creating headaches for more than just hiring managers. A reduced workforce is causing construction project costs to soar and go over schedule. It’s also turning worksites into danger zones, as understaffed crews are working longer hours at a brutal pace. In 2024, construction workers accounted for about one in five workplace deaths in the country.

Most workers who end up in trade occupations do so later in life, often as a second career. The average age of an apprentice in a construction program is 28, for instance, while plumbing and electrical apprentices are often pushing 40.

“For so long we nurtured this culture saying you’ve got to go to college and get this degree or that degree, and if you end up working in construction it was because you somehow failed,” states Roger Skaer, a trained carpenter. “I think it’s a product of a long-lived, misguided representation of what success should look like.”.

Recently, young people have started to question that college-or-bust narrative. Between 2020 and 2025, enrollment in two-year vocational or trade schools grew by almost 20 percent. And while the trades have their own difficulties—dangerous work conditions, wear and tear on the body—they offer something lots of other fields can’t right now: job security. According to the Fresh Hampshire–based nonprofit Bring Back the Trades, more than 1.4 million trade jobs will be vacant in 2030.

Industry observers note that roger is confident that eventually Damian will fill one of those positions. “Right now, he’s saying plumber, I’m thinking maybe a toilet designer,” he says, explaining that Damian’s been angling for a 3D printer so that he can build his own commodes. “I truly feel he's going to be in some kind of trade or hands-on craftwork.”.

And if toilets don’t pan out, Damian has a backup hobby: washer and dryer installation and repair.
