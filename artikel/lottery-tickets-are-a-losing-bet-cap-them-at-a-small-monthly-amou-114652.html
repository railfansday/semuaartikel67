Lottery Tickets Are a Losing Bet – Cap Them at a Small Monthly Amount

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Treat lottery tickets as entertainment you pay for, never as a blueprint to get ahead. On average, states pay out about 67 cents in prizes for every dollar of tickets sold, and the Powerball jackpot is a 1-in-292-million shot. Set a monthly limit near what a night out costs you, and stop once it’s spent.

In a recent development, A record jackpot can make a few extra tickets feel like a smart move. Each ticket’s odds stay the same, though, however big the prize grows.

In a recent development, those odds are 1 in 292.2 million for a $2 Powerball play and 1 in 290.5 million for a $5 Mega Millions play. Across all lottery games, states sold $104.7 billion in tickets in fiscal 2024 and paid out $70.2 billion in prizes, the Census Bureau reports. Players as a group lost about a third of what they spent. That loss is a fair price for a few days of daydreaming, but it rules tickets out as a way to build wealth.

Industry observers note that in a national survey for a National Bureau of Economic Research study, lottery spending fell only modestly as income rose. That means lower-income households spend a bigger share of what they earn on tickets than higher earners do. Pay for your tickets only with money left after bills and savings.

A $20-a-week habit costs $1,040 a year. Invest that $1,040 at the end of each year for 20 years instead, at an assumed 7% annual return, and you’d have about $42,600, more than double the $20,800 you put in. Keep the ticket budget small, and save or invest what you’d have spent beyond it.

If you enjoy playing, these steps keep the thrill and limit what it costs.

Count every ticket as money spent the moment you buy it, since about 24 of every 25 Powerball plays win nothing at all.
