Industry News: Why employees are ‘habit hugging’ at work

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that if you’re prone to hanging on to the way things always used to be done at work, you might be “habit hugging.” It’s where employees hold on to familiar routines even after latest tools and processes have been introduced. Habit hugging may be a sign of insecurity, or a form of self-preservation — finding stability and control in a working world that feels increasingly unfamiliar. A new report by online learning platform TalentLMS, based on a survey of 1,500 US employees who had received training on a new tool, technology, process, or way of working in the previous 12 months, found that 65% of respondents mentioned letting go of an old habit is harder than learning a new one. And only 41% consistently used a new way of working after they were trained on it. Meanwhile, 42% admitted they still used tools or processes their managers believed had been replaced. Multiple industries are evolving and being disrupted by AI. Habit hugging could be a response to the pressure employees feel to continually adapt as everything changes around them. As uncertainty grows both inside and outside the office, sticking to what you know may offer a little comfort. There’s a tension between employers demanding adaptability and employees being expected to maintain productivity throughout that change: The report found that 48% of respondents felt rushed to adopt new ways of working. Employers aren’t necessarily helping their workforce overcome their old habits when they expect them to adopt new systems without slowing down or making mistakes. That pressure may also make employees reluctant to admit they’re struggling. The report found that 45% of respondents had avoided questioning a new way of working because they didn’t want to appear resistant. Among those who continued to use, or fell back on, old ways of working, 22% said they feared making mistakes or looking slow. When workplaces demand this constant adaptation without creating the space for it, employees might prefer to stick to what they know. Sixty percent said their workplace prioritized results over the process of getting them, while just 41% said they felt they were given room to be less productive while learning something new. There may also be a question of trust. Employees who have seen workplace initiatives and technologies come and go may be reluctant to embrace the latest change, particularly if they aren’t convinced it will last. They may prefer to future-proof themselves by leaning on the skills and systems they already know, rather than investing time in learning a new tool that could be forgotten about in six months. That reluctance could be costing employers: The report found that 29% of employees said their company was investing in tools they’d stopped using. But habit hugging isn’t necessarily an outright rejection of change. The report found that 73% of respondents mixed old and new ways of working to get the job done, suggesting employees may be finding ways to adapt without abandoning familiar routines altogether. Rather than blindly embracing the latest systems, people may be finding their own ways of adapting to a future nobody is sure of.
