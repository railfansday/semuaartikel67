Industry News: Ireland’s Experiment in Paying Artists a Basic Income Is Working as Intended For Artists

Recent findings suggest that life was pretty rosy for the 2,000 Irish artists who landed a spot on Ireland’s Basic Income for the Arts (BIA) pilot scheme. For three years, from 2022 to 2025, €325 arrived in their bank accounts every week. The money was intended to provide a dependable income, reduce the need to take on unrelated jobs to cover living costs, and give artists more time to concentrate on making art. Judging by the recipients I spoke earlier this month at Dublin Gallery Weekend (DGW), the pilot was a success. And since the BIA was made permanent in February, thousands more artists are hoping to reap the rewards.“Basic Income for the Arts is a game changer, and now it is here to stay,” Ireland’s minister for culture, communications, and sport, Patrick O’Donovan, mentioned in February. Recent findings suggest that now in its fourth year, DGW was launched in 2023 by the city’s commercial galleries to raise the profile of its contemporary art scene. Organized by the Contemporary Art Gallery Association (CAGA), this year’s four-day program brought together galleries and cultural institutions for exhibitions, talks, screenings, and performances. A major addition was the inaugural Francis Bacon Awards for Irish Visual Arts, a €40,000 prize fund supported by Château La Coste. Painter Patrick Graham received the €25,000 Lifetime Achievement Award, while Belfast-born artist Cathy Wilkes took the €15,000 Mid-Career prize. Both will stage solo exhibitions at Château La Coste in Provence, France.

According to recent reports, while the artists I spoke to described the BIA as a major boost to their working lives, I found little evidence that it has fundamentally changed Dublin’s contemporary art scene. None of the dealers, curators, or museum directors I spoke to suggested that its impact had been felt on a wider scale across the city’s galleries or commercial market.

Applications for the BIA’s latest three-year round opened in May, with previous recipients eligible to reapply for one of the 2,000 available places. Around 10,000 artists applied, making competition fierce for a program to which the Irish government has committed €18 million.

Recent findings suggest that “Over the course of the scheme, my practice flourished,” Dublin-based artist Gearóid O’Dea, who was selected for the pilot, told ARTnews. The funding allowed him to curate an exhibition based on Samuel Beckett’s work that travelled to Berlin, and collaborate with four other artists through the Glue Factory collective. He remarked the financial support made it possible to bring artists together and exhibit internationally without constantly having to find other jobs to make ends meet.

In a recent development, cork-born Elinor O’Donovan, who is now studying for a master’s degree in Gothenburg, Sweden, also received the pilot payment. She told ARTnews that “it completely transformed my ability to work as an artist.”.

Before joining the scheme, O’Donovan worked part-time as a receptionist alongside her artistic practice. The weekly payment allowed her to give up the job and concentrate on making art full-time. “Knowing the money was coming for three years was a massive relief,” she said. “My psychological wellbeing improved because there was security.”.

Sources indicate that lauren, another former recipient who is now based in London, said the payment arrived at a pivotal moment in her career. Having just completed her undergraduate degree, she had secured a graduate residency at the Royal Hibernian Academy in Dublin, where she was given a studio and access to painting classes. The BIA helped her develop her practice and take up further opportunities, including residencies and exhibitions. She has since secured funding to continue her studies in London. “There’s kind of nothing I haven’t done in the last four or five years that hasn’t, in some way, been impacted by my experience with the basic income,” she told ARTnews.

Recent findings suggest that despite the BIA’s unprecedented support for artists, not everyone is waxing lyrical about it, including some who benefited from the original pilot. Tadhg Ó Cuirrín, a visual artist who received the payment and works with the artists’ trade union Praxis, believes the government has missed an opportunity to build on its success. More than 10,000 artists applied for the fresh round, yet the number of places remains unchanged at 2,000. Recipients will also be limited to three years of support in every six-year period.

“It feels less like a base income per se and more like a kind of a multi-annual grant chosen by lottery,” he told ARTnews.

It has been revealed that although the program is now permanent, individual artists are not guaranteed support beyond three years. Artists must meet the scheme’s eligibility criteria, after which places are allocated through a lottery, with selection also taking demographic representation into account. Ó Cuirrín argued that the three-years-on, three-years-off model reinstates the financial insecurity BIA was designed to address. Artists risk losing their income just as their practices are beginning to flourish.

O’Dea has reapplied for the new round but is still waiting to hear if he’s been selected. He noted the prospect of losing the regular income has brought financial anxiety back into his life.

The transition has also caused difficulties for artists who relied on other forms of state support. Michaële Cutaya, a spokesperson for Praxis, previously told The Art Newspaper that some former recipients have had to return to other work to cover their living costs. “If you have to then take on a different kind of job, it’s really disruptive,” she mentioned.

Noel Kelly, chief executive of Visual Artists Ireland, said he welcomes the permanent scheme, but pointed to practical problems. BIA payments can affect artists receiving means-tested benefits, including disability allowance, while some applicants’ immigration status may prevent them from registering as self-employed, a requirement for participation.

Industry observers note that kelly also cautioned against judging the program solely on its financial return. An independent evaluation estimated that every euro invested generated €1.39 in social and economic benefits, taking into account factors including improved wellbeing. Kelly stated that calculation misses much of what artists contribute to society.

It has been revealed that “I hate that argument,” he told ARTnews. “It allows us to participate one in the local community, two in the local economy, three in the local creative ecology.”.

Industry observers note that the impact of the BIA on Ireland’s commercial art market is harder to measure. John Daly, director of Hillsboro Fine Art and chair of the Contemporary Art Gallery Association, told ARTnews that the country’s galleries continue to operate with a relatively small pool of collectors. “Success actually is survival,” he remarked. Daly argued the Irish art scene needs to attract younger collectors and encourage greater corporate support. Despite the presence of major international technology companies like Google in Dublin, he said corporate collecting remains limited, with companies tending to support arts initiatives such as murals and workshops. “It should be a huge opportunity, given the presence of all these multinationals, but not yet.”.
