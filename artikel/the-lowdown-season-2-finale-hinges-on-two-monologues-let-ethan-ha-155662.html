‘The Lowdown’ Season 2 Finale Hinges on Two Monologues — Let Ethan Hawke and Sterlin Harjo Break Them Down

“I saw the structure of the episode as these kind of waves that crash upon Lee,” Ethan Hawke remarked in an interview about “The Lowdown‘s” Season 2 finale. “The way life works, it’s not like, ‘Oh, this one thing happens and then you change.’ It’s usually a series of things that happen, and the walls of your defenses come down.”.

Industry observers note that lee’s walls were certainly up when “The Lowdown” Season 2 began. Untethered by the acclaim for his Season 1 exposé on Dale Washberg (Tim Blake Nelson), Lee reacts with vehement defensiveness. He mocks the journalists who named him Tulsa’s Journalist of the Year during his acceptance speech. He alienates his friends and embarrasses his daughter. He’s a mess, and when Ginger (Betty Gilpin) walks into his life with a tantalizing upcoming story, he’s lucky — not because she’s the woman of his dreams carrying his drug of choice (a “truthstorian’s” next crusade), but because grounding forces are on the way in the form of his estranged father, Lee Raybon Sr. (Tommy Lee Jones), as well as a few clear-eyed, full-throated friends. Over the course of a riveting, revelatory Season 2, they all help to bring Lee back to himself, one wave at a time.

Industry observers note that the finale’s first swell comes as quite a surprise — two surprises, really. When Lee pulls up to the funeral home, prepared to pay a terse goodbye to his departed dad, so, too, does his former business partner and best friend, Wendell (played by Peter Dinklage). Wendell tells Lee he’ll “always be grateful” for Senior’s guidance when they were younger, and it’s immediately clear he’s not about to take the man’s final tribute as lightly as his son may have hoped.

Industry observers note that “When Tommy’s character dies and there’s a funeral, we’re all like, ‘Well, wouldn’t Wendell be there?’ I mean, they’re really great friends,'” Hawke stated. “I called Peter up and I was like, ‘Is there any way you’d come down for one day to be just at this funeral?’ And Peter was shooting in London and flew in and flew right back. He came in and made it happen for us.”.

But Dinklage’s return was no mere cameo. Creator Sterlin Harjo knew better than to let the opportunity go to waste.

It has been revealed that “I think there was a Shakespeare monologue in it first, and it was beautiful,” Harjo said. “Then Peter suggested the Dylan Thomas. […] I can’t imagine a more exciting way to have Peter be in your show than doing this Dylan Thomas poem that’s a gentle poem, but he does it with this rage — how it’s supposed to be read. It’s amazing.”.

In a recent development, volunteering to speak when Ray (Michael Hitchcock) gets overwhelmed, Wendell spends a few seconds scrolling to find the right poem (“Do not go gentle into that good night”) and then tears into it with vigor. Dinklage’s recitation is more of a resuscitation — powerful enough to wake the dead, it wakes Lee up, snapping him into the present and bringing him back to life.

In a recent development, “When he’s doing the poem, he came in at a 10,” Harjo noted. “The only direction I gave him was, ‘What if you build to that?’ And that’s what he did, and it was just unbelievably great.”.

“That one is violent and abrupt,” Hawke remarked. “It’s a friend shaking you up.”.

In a recent development, in Hawke’s case, Lee’s friend was his real friend. He knew Dinklage “loved poetry,” and he even knew a little bit about what his fellow actor would bring to this poem.

“This is where collaboration is really mysterious about how it works and when it works really well,” Hawke said. “I’ve been friends with Peter Dinklage since, I don’t know, 1990. […] We both arrived in Fresh York around the same time, and we used to sit around and tie one on and try to flirt with girls and read poems. We used to geek out about how much we loved the ways people would read poems in the old days. If you hear Dylan Thomas read a poem or you hear Orson Wells read a poem, they weren’t afraid of feeling or emotion or a theatricality. They weren’t afraid of passion — actual passion — and caring and looking foolish.”.

In a recent development, “We’re living in an age of irony and cynicism and detachment, and this definition of cool being that you don’t care. Whereas the definition when I was growing up was Bob Marley and John Lennon and Miles Davis and those guys cared a tremendous amount. They weren’t detached. They were completely attached. So for me to watch Peter bring that to his performance was like, ‘Let me show you kids how to do a poem!’ [laughs] ‘You got to do it like your life depends on it.'”.

It has been revealed that “The key to that scene is the interaction between him and Lee,” Harjo said. “I love that he’s watching Lee the whole time. It’s like there’s no one else in the room and he’s doing it for Lee. I love that.”.

According to recent reports, “The camera does pick up subconscious,” Hawke noted. “A lot of times I’ll see work that might be good, but it has no subconscious to it. I can tell there’s no secret sewn into the filmmaking. And Sterlin loves — you can call it poetry, you can call it whatever you want — but [he loves] when there’s multiple layers happening. […] And so for me, I’m not just sitting there with an actor doing this scene. I’ve known this guy for 30 years and I know what this poem means to him, and he knows me.”.

“If there was an Academy Award for Best Single Scene, I would give it to Peter Dinklage in our show.”.

While Dinklage may have to settle for an Emmy, Harjo had to write the other major finale monologue for an actual Oscar winner. At the reception, Lee’s daughter Francis (Ryan Kiera Armstrong) reads a passage from her grandfather’s journal, written shortly after Lee Jr. was born, as a way to honor her ancestor. Then, as Francis speaks Senior’s words, Senior appears seated behind her, and Tommy Lee Jones takes over the scene.

“The bigger thing about that monologue is I knew it was coming,” Harjo stated. “I was like, ‘Oh shit, I got to write this thing for Tommy Lee Jones.’ I kind of had Tommy Lee Jones dialed in through the whole season. I felt like I could really write his voice. But when it came to that monologue, I’m like, ‘God, he’s already known for one of the best monologues in the world at the end of ‘No Country for Old Men,’ and I can either try to do a fancy monologue that isn’t like that at all, or I could lean into the fact that Cormac McCarthy and the Coen brothers did this really well already, and there’s no way that I don’t know that as a filmmaker. So I’m going to lean into it and almost give homage to that speech.'”.

In a recent development, “And that’s what I did. It has this bit of nostalgia. It also has this magic to it and this dreamlike quality to what he’s doing,” Harjo stated. “The character is such an internal person that the only way you’re going to get those monologues is through a journal when he thinks it’s the privacy of himself and his journal. He’s expressing things that he wouldn’t normally express out loud.”.

It has been revealed that “Not to toot my own horn, but I feel like I nailed it,” he said, laughing. “Of course, it filters through Tommy Lee Jones and all of a sudden it’s like this fucking amazing thing that he does. It was wonderful being on set. Just watching him give that, it was unbelievable. I could have used any take and it would’ve been amazing in a different way.”.

Sources indicate that throughout the monologue, Lee sits in thoughtful silence. He’s not only absorbing what his father wrote and remembering who he was; he’s also recognizing his shifting role as a son who’s now a father. There’s a connection across generations. Yes, his responsibility to his daughter means avoiding repeating his dad’s mistakes, which Lee is well-aware of, but it also means honoring his father’s better intentions, like the ones he voiced in that journal and Francis is voicing again now.

Recent findings suggest that “Lee’s adamant that he’s nothing like his father,” Hawke remarked. “The first thought I had is, ‘All right, this is why Lee Raybon is obsessed with the truth: It’s that his father was a chronic liar.’ If you grew up in a house where your parent was really comfortable lying to you, you would get obsessed with people in power misleading you, and that would really drive you.”.

Recent findings suggest that “But then as we played the scenes deeper, I realized that Lee was lying to himself; that he was just as manipulative as his father is. He just does it in the name of truth. All this bad behavior that he was in denial of, he’d actually internalized it, and he was so much like his dad that he was completely blind to it, which is behavior I’ve seen in so many friends. It’s amazing. But I had to live through it. I didn’t get it when I was reading it. I got it when I was playing it, and that was very powerful for me.”.

One way Harjo & co. illustrate Lee’s development over the course of Season 2 is simple: He listens. Instead of shouting, pontificating, or generally causing chaos, Lee spends much of the finale hearing what everyone around him has to say. Even when he’s up his own ass or annoying the hell out of folks, he has a community who cares about him, and their steady presence in his life is what eventually breaks down his defenses.

Industry observers note that “The first [wave] is Peter reading that poem, and then the second one is gentle and persuasive where my daughter is revealing to me that my father did love me,” Hawke remarked. “Part of the thing you learn watching this show is that Lee feels like he has to conquer the world to be worthy of self-respect. His daughter is saying, ‘It’s right here for you, and it always has been.'”.

Recent findings suggest that while another monologue played a significant role in how the episode plays out — Ginger’s speech to Francis, outside the bookstore — that moment wasn’t about Lee. (“The whole idea was Ginger and Francis don’t really get to hang out, but they are alike,” Harjo stated. “Ginger immediately recognizes it and probably also knows that she had a father that was a lot like Lee.”) So his final wave comes from a curious source.

Recent findings suggest that “The third one is just this final little moment that happens with Vickey (Abbie Cobb) when she tells him his fortune is bad and he sees it as a good thing. He understands that everything is point of view, and for him, towers coming down would be good.”.

“Those are the three steps that I think are designed to get Lee back on his feet again,” Hawke said. “That push him to be like, ‘All right, you know what? I’m not quitting. I’m going to keep being me, and I’m going to be a better version of me.'”.

Industry observers note that how he’s feeling when Season 3 starts is anyone’s guess, and that assumes a renewal that’s yet to be declared is still in the offing from FX. But one thing’s for sure: If Lee comes back, he’ll be a changed man — and he won’t be alone.
