Meta’s teen restrictions have a gap: TikTok, Snapchat and YouTube leave teens room to scroll

Meta’s upcoming teen restrictions, set to take effect by late February, could meaningfully curb endless scrolling. A new study suggests they probably won’t.

Sources indicate that that’s because the restrictions, which are part of a settlement between Meta and dozens of attorneys general, including Washington’s, apply only to Instagram and Facebook. They leave out Snapchat, TikTok and Google-owned YouTube.

“The simple workaround right now is that you just switch platforms, and we already know that teens are on three to four platforms on average,” remarked Dr. Dimitri Christakis, a principal investigator at the Center for Child Health, Behavior and Development at Seattle Children’s.

Christakis led research published Wednesday showing Instagram and Facebook accounted for roughly 20% of total daily social media use for youth. So when the restrictions kick in, teens could just shift to other platforms.

The rules establish a two-hour daily limit combined across Instagram and Facebook, restrict access to content from midnight to 6 a.m., and mute most school-hour push notifications. Parents can adjust the protections, and direct messaging is exempt.

If the same limits were applied across the five most popular platforms, that could make a dent in teen usage, according to the research letter in the Journal of the American Medical Association.

According to recent reports, the study has no way of knowing how teens will respond to the restrictions or the impacts on their health. It simply estimates how much previously recorded activity would fall under the rules.

It has been revealed that the researchers disclosed ties to social media litigation. Christakis reported paid legal consulting work, including for Washington’s attorney general, and co-author Lauren Hale of Stony Brook University reported fees for serving as an expert witness. The third author is Yihong Zhao of Columbia University.

Despite the limited benefits of the coming restrictions, Christakis stated they’re a “step in the right direction.”.

“Let’s say it’s an incremental improvement. It’s clearly not going to be enough by itself because of what our study found,” he said. “But these are the right kinds of restrictions. We need to make them universal, and we need to enforce them.”.

Meta, not surprisingly, is in favor of having the restrictions apply industry wide.

When the settlement was unveiled in August the firm issued a statement saying that “ensuring teens have a safe and productive experience on our platforms is an absolute imperative.”.

Meta called on others to do the same: “For meaningful progress to happen, we urge TikTok and YouTube to join us and state attorneys general in adopting this fresh standard…”.

In a recent development, the agreement resolves claims by 48 states, Washington, D.C., and U.S. territories that Meta designed addictive platforms and failed to protect young users, among other allegations. Meta did not admit wrongdoing.

In addition to the safety changes, Meta agreed to pay participating governments up to $17.1 billion over a decade, according to Washington’s attorney general. Washington will receive at least $237 million and could receive nearly $339 million.

The higher settlement is unlocked only if TikTok and YouTube adopt similar protections and make payments of their own, according to Meta. If that happens, additional restrictions kick in, curbing access on all the platforms from 10 p.m. to 7 a.m.

In a recent development, those additional hours are significant. The researchers found nearly as much use between 10 p.m. and midnight as during the entire midnight-to-6-a.m. window covered by the initial limits.

According to recent reports, whether the other companies will follow remains unclear. All five social media platforms are named in additional legal challenges alleging harm to youth, Reuters reported, including suits filed by individuals, school districts and municipalities.

It has been revealed that in September, TikTok reached its first settlement in a state-led case, agreeing to teen usage restrictions in Alabama and a payment of at least $100 million, according to Reuters. YouTube CEO Neal Mohan, meanwhile, dismissed joining Meta’s agreement and emphasized his manufacturer’s own safety efforts, CNN reported.

In a recent development, christakis said both time spent on social media and the platforms’ features can exacerbate problems involving sleep, mental health, body image and attention. He hopes the growing body of research translates into action.

Sources indicate that “I’m not a legislator, and I’m not an advocate. I’m a scientist that’s really trying to make sure that we apply science in the best interests of our children’s health,” he said. “And in this space, in particular, we haven’t been doing that.”.
