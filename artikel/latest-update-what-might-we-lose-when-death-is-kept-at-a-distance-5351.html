It has been revealed that death is everywhere, yet for much of my life I have encountered it only in glimpses. As a scholar with the Centre for Death and Society at the University of Bath, I regularly encounter different perspectives on death and dying. My own experiences, though, have felt brief and distant.

Industry observers note that in my 20s, I visited my grandma in hospital. She had suffered a stroke that would prove fatal. I remember gasping quietly as I entered the room. Something about her contorted face, laboured breathing, and pained expression shocked me.

Sources indicate that I felt relieved when I was told visiting hours were over. I left the ward feeling sick to my stomach.

At funerals over the years, I watched a succession of coffins disappear behind plush curtains: Grandma, Grandpa, Nana, Uncle Chris, Aunty Lil, Aunty Rachael, Aunty Mary, and Uncle Mick.

Looking back, these were glimpses of death and dying: processes that felt detached, as though they somehow existed outside my life.

Sources indicate that it was much the same with Dad. I got the call from a hospital consultant around 11 p.m.

“I’m sorry, but your dad is now unconscious,” he told me.

In a recent development, “I’d suggest coming to visit him as soon as you can. There’s nothing more we can do but keep him comfortable.”.

In a recent development, this was it. Dad was dying. I drove through the night.

It has been revealed that the curtain was drawn around his bed when the nurse guided me to his cubicle. I was terrified of what I’d see. Again, I wanted to run away when I first saw him lying there, occasionally flinching, his face slightly contorted, struggling for breath, no longer fully alive.

Recent findings suggest that I asked for a little time alone with him, and the nurse told me she thought he could still hear me. “Leslie! Leslie…” she said loudly, gently shaking his shoulder as though she was trying to wake him from a deep sleep. “There's someone here to see you, Leslie. That's nice, isn't it?”.

I had things I wanted to tell Dad. I wanted him to know that I loved him, and that wherever he was going next, I hoped he would find peace.

I also felt a need to touch him. At first, I touched his arm, like a child prodding something mysterious they have found in a rock pool. As my reticence evaporated, I placed the palm of my hand gently on his skin, closed my eyes, and spoke.

Sources indicate that “I love you, Dad. And Em, and Mum, and Joe—they told me to tell you they love you too.”.

He died five days later. At his funeral, I watched his coffin disappear behind a pair of curtains.

Recently, I examined an excellent Ph.D. thesis on home death care in the United Kingdom. It explored the experiences of a small group of people who chose to care for a loved one’s body at home before the funeral.

According to recent reports, for various personal reasons, they chose to care for a loved one’s body at home after death—sometimes after the person had died elsewhere. They became, in the words used in the research, “custodians of the dead.”.

I read their stories with interest.

The practical commitment could be considerable. Depending on the circumstances, caring for the body could involve washing or dressing it, repositioning it, preparing a room, and deciding who could visit. If the body remained at home for several days, arrangements also had to be made to keep it cool. Families described living alongside a body that cooled, stiffened, and gradually changed.

In a recent development, the experience also gave people time and space to confront things they might otherwise not have been able to. They began to make sense of a loved one’s transition from living person to dead body, taking a closer look at death itself.

Keeping the deceased at home for an extended period also allowed people to say goodbye at a slower, more organic pace. One woman described talking to her mother as she cleaned the living room, commenting on the birds, the sky, and the trees, just as she had when her mother was alive.

Industry observers note that home death care will not suit every person or family. The thesis focused on people who chose it, and we also need to hear from those who found the experience difficult or regretted it.

As I listened, I thought about Dad and the short time I spent with him as he lay dying in hospital. My experience sits within a broader shift.

The Lancet Commission on the Value of Death describes how, over generations, dying has moved from family and community settings toward health systems, where it is often treated primarily as a medical or physiological event. In England in 2023, hospital was the most common place of death: 42.8 percent of people died there. Another 28.4 percent died at home and 21 percent in care homes.

The separation can continue after death. Research on home death care in England describes how the body is commonly moved to a mortuary or funeral home, where its care passes to professionals. I began to wonder what this separation from the body might mean for the people closest to someone who has died.

Industry observers note that what might it have been like if I’d had the chance to be with Dad in the way the people in the thesis had been with their loved ones? Might I have moved through my initial shock and experienced something beyond it? Would our goodbye have felt different? And might I have begun to relate to death and dying in a way I can’t quite fathom now, when the physical experience of death feels so far removed from my life?
