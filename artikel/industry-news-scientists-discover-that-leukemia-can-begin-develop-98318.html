Industry News: Scientists discover that leukemia can begin developing 50 years before diagnosis

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A deadly form of blood cancer may begin taking shape more than 50 years before a person is diagnosed, according to new research revealing the surprisingly long and predictable path to leukemia.

In a recent development, scientists have reconstructed how acute myeloid leukemia (AML) develops over a lifetime, discovering that in two-thirds of the cases studied, the first genetic mutation that helped drive the cancer appeared before age 20. These early changes could remain largely unnoticed for decades before eventually contributing to the disease.

The findings suggest that doctors may one day be able to identify people at risk of developing leukemia years or even decades before symptoms appear. They also raise the possibility of preventing the cancer by slowing the growth of abnormal blood cells long before the disease takes hold.

Blood Samples Reveal Leukemia's Hidden History.

Published in Nature, the study was conducted by researchers at the University of Cambridge and University College London (UCL). By examining blood samples collected over many years, including some obtained as long as 15 years before diagnosis, the team pieced together a detailed history of how leukemia develops.

Industry observers note that the scientists treated these stored samples as a kind of genetic fossil record. Using highly sensitive DNA sequencing and mathematical models, they worked backward to estimate when cancer-related mutations first appeared, how rapidly the affected cells multiplied, and how they competed with other blood cells as the disease approached.

According to recent reports, AML is an aggressive blood cancer that affects approximately 2,700 people in the UK annually. Its outlook is often poor, with only about one in six patients surviving at least five years after diagnosis.

Scientists have long known that many otherwise healthy older adults carry blood cells with mutations associated with leukemia. This condition, called clonal hematopoiesis, does not necessarily lead to cancer. In fact, most people with these mutations never develop AML. Until now, researchers have struggled to understand why some individuals progress to leukemia while others do not, partly because they lacked a detailed record of the genetic changes occurring in the years before diagnosis.

According to recent reports, to investigate, the team turned to samples from the UK Collaborative Trial of Ovarian Cancer Screening (UKCTOCS). That project involved approximately 50,000 women who provided blood samples annually for as long as 11 years.

Recent findings suggest that for the latest analysis, researchers examined samples from 47 women who eventually developed AML, with an average age at diagnosis of 72. They compared these findings with samples from 46 similar women who remained free of blood cancer.

Cancer Mutations Can Remain Hidden for Decades.

Recent findings suggest that the results revealed an extraordinary timeline. In two-thirds of the women who developed AML, the earliest mutation contributing to the disease was estimated to have occurred before their 20th birthday.

According to recent reports, at first, the altered blood cells multiplied extremely slowly, sometimes taking several years just to double in number. But as additional cancer-driving mutations accumulated, their growth accelerated dramatically.

According to recent reports, each fresh mutation typically increased the rate of growth by roughly two to three times. By the time cells had acquired four mutations capable of driving cancer, their populations could double within weeks instead of years.

Perhaps even more surprising was how consistently these populations behaved.

According to recent reports, researchers found that groups of genetically related blood cells, known as clones, generally expanded at predictable rates once they became established. Their growth tended to slow only when they faced limited space or competition from another clone that was multiplying more rapidly.

This consistency could have vital medical implications. By measuring the growth of a particular clone using just a few blood samples, scientists may be able to estimate how large that population will become many years into the future, potentially even decades later.

A Hidden Warning Sign of Leukemia.

The researchers also identified a striking pattern that appeared in some patients well before leukemia was diagnosed.

In at least one-third of AML cases, a single population of mutated blood cells had grown so extensively that it dominated the blood years before diagnosis. Scientists call this process a "somatic sweep.".

None of the women in the comparison group who remained free of blood cancer showed the same pattern.

The researchers then examined data from the UK Biobank and confirmed that people showing evidence of these sweeps faced a substantially greater risk of developing AML.

This finding suggests that somatic sweeps could serve as an important early warning sign. However, conventional genetic testing can miss them because the mutations involved are sometimes incorrectly classified as inherited genetic variations.

Professor Jamie Blundell, who led the research at the University of Cambridge's Early Cancer Institute, explained how the team reconstructed the disease's history.

Industry observers note that "Serial blood samples are a fossil record of the blood's evolution. They let us go back in time and reconstruct when each mutation arose and how fast it grew. What surprised us most is how predictable the growth of these clones is. Once a clone is established, its growth rate today tells you where it will be years, even decades, from now.".

Industry observers note that he added: "Because the growth is exponential, even modestly slowing a high-risk clone over the decade or more before diagnosis could substantially reduce the chance it becomes leukemia. This opens up strategies we can exploit to prevent these clones ever becoming leukemia.".

Could Leukemia Be Prevented Before It Develops?

The discovery could change how doctors approach a disease that often appears to develop with little warning.

Recent findings suggest that dr. Caroline Watson, the study's first author and a researcher at the Early Cancer Institute, emphasized the contrast between the sudden impact of a leukemia diagnosis and the decades of biological changes that may precede it.

According to recent reports, "As a hematologist, I've seen how suddenly AML can change someone's life. This study indicates that the changes leading to AML can build over decades, with the affected cells growing in surprisingly predictable ways.".

It has been revealed that watson is also an Honorary Consultant Haematologist who treats patients at the Cambridge Clonal Haematopoiesis Clinic at Addenbrooke's Hospital. The clinic is the first in Europe dedicated to leukemia prevention and already monitors people whose blood cell mutations place them at increased risk of developing the disease.

It has been revealed that she added: "At our clinic, we already assess and monitor people who carry blood-cell mutations that increase their risk of leukemia. Today's findings give us real hope of improving how we identify which of them are at greatest risk and point to a window in which prevention could one day be possible.".

Years of Blood Samples Unlock Fresh Cancer Clues.

A major reason the researchers could reconstruct leukemia's development was the availability of blood samples collected repeatedly from the same individuals over many years.

Professor Usha Menon of the UCL Institute of Clinical Trials and Methodology, who led the original UKCTOCS project, highlighted the unexpected scientific value of those samples.

"While UKCTOCS was designed to assess ovarian cancer screening, a key outcome was the establishment of a unique bioresource of serial serum samples. This resource has provided unprecedented opportunities to study how cancers develop over time and to discover and validate biomarkers for earlier detection. We owe an enormous debt of gratitude to the participants whose willingness to contribute their samples and data over many years made this possible. Their generosity is the foundation of this achievement.".

Industry observers note that the women who developed AML received their diagnoses between ages 53 and 83. Because the researchers did not have blood samples taken at the time of diagnosis, they could not directly examine the final stage of the disease's development in those patients.

Sources indicate that the findings also showed that not every case followed the same extended timeline of detectable changes. In approximately one in five cases, growing populations of mutated blood cells became detectable only one to two years before diagnosis. This suggests that some patients may have a much shorter opportunity for early intervention.

A Latest Path Toward Preventing Leukemia.

Despite these limitations, the results offer an encouraging possibility: leukemia risk might be reduced by intervening long before the disease develops.

According to the researchers' mathematical models, even a modest reduction in the growth of high-risk blood cell clones could have a substantial effect over time. Because these cells multiply exponentially, slowing their expansion across the ten or more years between a second cancer-driving mutation and diagnosis could significantly reduce the likelihood that they eventually progress to leukemia.

This raises the prospect of preventive treatments designed to restrain dangerous cell populations rather than waiting until they have developed into cancer.

The next challenge is determining whether such an approach can be used safely and effectively in people identified as having an elevated risk of AML. Although the research provides a promising framework for earlier detection and prevention, clinical studies will be needed to establish whether slowing these abnormal cells actually prevents leukemia in humans.
