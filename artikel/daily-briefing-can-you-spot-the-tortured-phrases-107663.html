Industry observers note that today, as of 10:16 GMT, NASA’s Voyager 1 spacecraft has journeyed one light-day, or 25.9 billion kilometres, from Earth — farther than any human-made object has gone before. At this distance, signals from Earth’s surface travelling at light speed will take a full 24 hours to reach the craft. The trailblazing probe is expected to lose communications with Earth in 2029, when its plutonium power finally dies. But that time might not signal the end of Voyager 1’s contributions — the craft carries a golden record engraved with sights and sounds from Earth, an interspecies message for any aliens who might encounter it as it continues its journey.

Sources indicate that A device that stimulates the brain with flickering light and rhythmic clicking sounds has failed to slow memory loss in people with Alzheimer’s disease. The Spectris AD headset is designed to induce brain activity called gamma waves, which have been linked to increased attention and memory. The results of a long-anticipated clinical trial highlighted that participants who used the device did not show robust improvements in their cognitive function after a year of treatment compared with those assigned to a sham treatment. The results were reported at the Clinical Trials on Alzheimer's Disease meeting on 16 November.

Recent findings suggest that the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) has announced that the United States has lost its measles elimination status amid an outbreak that’s caused more than 5,700 infections and 10 deaths this year. The US had eliminated measles in 2000 after an intensive vaccination campaign. But declining vaccination rates in the past few years have left pockets of the population vulnerable to infection. “We’ve taken an enormous step back in public health,” says virologist Paul Offit. Measles has also been re-established in Mexico and Bolivia, PAHO announced.

Source: International Vaccine Access Center, Johns Hopkins University.

Consumer tests that promise to reveal the full range of microbes living in your gut are taking off — but their results can vary widely. “This is an industry that’s running wild,” says genomicist Jacques Ravel, who co-authored a study that tested several providers. With regard to testing methods, he says, “there’s no standard”. But it’s hard to set those standards, writes journalist Amber Dance, who sent off her own stool to investigate the field. Part of the problem is that “there is no known ‘ground truth’ for a natural microbial sample”, she says.

It has been revealed that the proportion of reviewers at Nature who are early-career researchers. Increasing it would benefit all researchers and science as a whole, argues a Nature editorial. (Nature | 6 min read).

Amid the tedium of investigating potentially fraudulent papers, “there is some light relief, thanks to some hilarious phrases used by authors seeking to avoid detection by plagiarism checkers”, writes research-integrity sleuth Dorothy Bishop. Bishop and Nature’s careers team have compiled a quiz to test your ability to match ‘tortured phrases’ — those that replace common words with synonyms in an attempt to skirt past plagiarism-detection software — with their original text.

From the Bering Strait to Doggerland, much of human history occurred in places that are now underwater, covered by the sea-level rise that occurs after ice ages. Geographer Jerome Dobson has dubbed this drowned land ‘Aquaterra’. It’s the site of many discoveries — such as Blinkerwall in the Baltic Sea, one of Europe’s oldest and largest Stone Age structures — and possibly many more to come. It also offers an interesting thought experiment: if we continue to drive global warming, what might future archaeologists make of our inundated world?
