A major NFT marketplace is shutting down—5 years after the boom, there's a lesson for investors

Five years ago, NFTs seemed to be everywhere.

Although the digital collectibles, known as nonfungible tokens, had existed since 2014, they exploded in popularity in 2021 alongside the cryptocurrency boom, becoming status symbols for celebrities and wealthy collectors.

Weekly NFT trading volume across major blockchains surged from about $4.4 million in late 2020 to a peak of $3.2 billion in August 2021, according to CryptoSlam data published by The Block.

Sources indicate that today, much of that trading activity has disappeared. Weekly NFT trading volume has fallen more than 99% since August 2021, and several major NFT marketplaces have closed. On Thursday, Blur, once the largest marketplace for Ethereum-based NFTs, shut down its website.

At the height of the boom, Twitter co-founder Jack Dorsey sold an NFT of his first tweet for $2.9 million, while a digital artwork by an artist known as Beeple fetched $69.3 million at Christie's. Paris Hilton and Jimmy Fallon showed off their Bored Ape NFTs on "The Tonight Show," and even McDonald's released NFTs celebrating the return of its McRib sandwich.

Recent findings suggest that back then, some buyers believed NFTs represented the future of digital ownership, while others bet they could resell them at higher prices. But as enthusiasm faded, so did the demand that had driven the digital tokens' values higher.

The NFT boom and bust shows how promptly speculative investments can unravel when demand dries up. For investors, it's a cautionary tale about spotting the warning signs before buying into the next craze, financial experts tell CNBC Make It.

Part of the initial appeal of NFTs was the promise of a fresh way for artists and other creators to make money from their work. Using blockchain technology, NFTs allowed creators to sell digital collectibles with verifiable ownership records and potentially earn royalties on subsequent sales through smart contracts.

More broadly, NFTs were touted as part of the future of digital ownership, with the potential to transform how people bought, sold and traded digital assets.

But establishing ownership of a digital collectible was different from creating lasting demand for it, claims Evan Mills, a financial advisor at wealth management firm Scholar Advising.

"NFTs tried to manufacture that demand," he says. "Blockchain could establish who owns the token, but it couldn't guarantee that anyone would really value the ownership at the end of the day.".

In practice, many of the NFTs that attracted attention were relatively simple digital images, such as cartoon animals or promotional artwork featuring beer cans.

Traditional collectibles, such as paintings, antiques and baseball cards, have established histories and communities of collectors that can sustain demand, Mills says. NFTs, by comparison, became enormously popular in a relatively short period.

In a recent development, unlike many traditional collectibles, however, NFTs could be created in virtually unlimited quantities. While individual collections might contain a limited number of tokens, there was little stopping creators from launching new series. At the height of the boom in 2022, roughly 500 new NFT collections were being created every day, according to blockchain analytics firm IntoTheBlock.

The rapid rise in NFT prices helped fuel expectations that buyers would continue paying more for them. But as enthusiasm faded, so did the pool of potential buyers.

And unlike stocks or bonds, most NFTs didn't generate income, meaning buyers hoping to turn a profit generally depended on someone else being willing to pay more for them, claims Martin Mulyadi, the YHB Endowed Chair in Accounting at Shenandoah University.

"Most NFTs paid nothing, so the only way to make money was finding a next buyer at a higher price," he indicates. "When the buyers stopped coming, there was nothing holding up the price.".

For everyday investors, the NFT bust is a reminder that an asset's popularity doesn't necessarily make it a worthwhile investment.

Recent findings suggest that one common justification for buying risky investments like collectibles is that they make up only a small portion of an otherwise diversified portfolio. Financial advisors often recommend limiting speculative holdings to money an investor can afford to lose. But keeping an investment small doesn't necessarily make it worthwhile, Mulyadi states.

"While keeping a bet small limits the damage, it doesn't turn a weak asset into a good one," he says.

Mills argues that investors can also confuse speculation with diversification.

According to recent reports, "Diversification is not just owning more things," he states. "It's owning things that have different economic characteristics that change the risk and return of your overall portfolio.".

In a recent development, for collectibles, that means looking beyond an item's scarcity or popularity to whether there's an established market of buyers likely to value it over time. Even then, future demand can be difficult to predict.

There's also a difference between buying a collectible because you actually like it and buying it because you think someone else will pay more for it. For many NFT speculators, the appeal was likely less about owning what was essentially a JPEG than the prospect of reselling it for a profit.

Before buying a speculative asset, Mills recommends asking two questions: What is the economic reason it should generate a return, and what role does it play in the overall portfolio? If an investor can't answer those questions, he claims, the investment may not belong in the portfolio at all.

"A bad investment doesn't become a good investment just because you only own a little bit of it," Mills notes.
