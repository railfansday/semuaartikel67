Like Jess McClain, I Took Time Off From Running—and Came Back Stronger. These Signs Mean It’s Time to Step Away.

🌐 Source: Runnersworld • 📅 24 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 8 min read (1684 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways
  • They say distance makes the heart grow fonder, and that’s true for me with running.
  • Around this time last year, I went from lifting two to three days a week while training for my first marathon to stopping workouts completely and struggling to get off my couch.
  • Professional distance runner and New York City Marathon runner-up Jess McClain previously told Runner’s World she felt similarly about six years ago.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

They say distance makes the heart grow fonder, and that’s true for me with running. But by “distance,” I don’t mean bumping up my mileage or running farther. I mean taking time and space away from my sport during seasons when I feel burned out, lost, or simply exhausted.

Around this time last year, I went from lifting two to three days a week while training for my first marathon to stopping workouts completely and struggling to get off my couch. The second I crossed the finish line, all I wanted to do was let my running shoes collect dust, avoid my local trail, and avoid stepping foot in the gym for a while.

Professional distance runner and New York City Marathon runner-up Jess McClain previously told Runner’s World she felt similarly about six years ago. At that time, she consistently stopped hitting her paces in her workouts and decided to step back from running—a break that lasted about two years.

In a recent development, in my case, I wasn’t as decisive. For months, I resisted the idea that I needed to take a break.

I’d heard that a month is generally how long you need to recover from a marathon, so I told myself that’s when I would jump into winter base training. But every time I tried to get back out there, I got bored and I struggled to recover from my runs. I waited until summer for the urge to work out again, but it never really came back.

It has been revealed that for a while, I felt like getting burned out was my fault, that I must have pushed too hard in training. Looking back now—and with help from an expert—I understand that my break was exactly what I needed to get back to my sport in a sustainable way.

It has been revealed that my break from running lasted about nine months, and I came back feeling like the runner I had always been before. Plus, I raced one of my fastest half marathons as my comeback.

Below I offer the telltale signs it’s time for a break from running, what a “break” really means, and the benefits you can gain from stepping away from the sport for a while—even if it seems impossible to do so at first.

The farther away I got from my training cycle, the more I realized how much I overtrained. I’d been strength training for 90 minutes three times a week with heavy weights while balancing 30-plus-mile weeks and 20-mile long runs. As I gained more perspective after my race ended, I noticed signs that I was in desperate need of a break.

Industry observers note that “Pay attention to trends and patterns [throughout your training],” Erin Ayala, PhD, a sports psychologist, certified mental performance consultant, and founder of Skadi Sport Psychology, tells Runner’s World. “We all have bad days where the numbers aren’t coming together or a run feels harder than it normally does at a given pace. But if it continues to occur, then it can definitely be a sign to take a break,” she says.

Ayala says there are three main factors that often lead to athletic burnout. Here’s what each can look like, and how I experienced them.

You might need to take a step back if you find yourself hitting training plateaus despite putting in your best efforts.

I picked a training plan that included paces far too fast for my fitness level at the time, so every interval workout I did felt difficult, and my paces never really improved. The longer the intervals got, the farther I was from my desired split. But I kept checking off the weekly workouts anyway.

This lack of personal achievement and growth can halt your motivation levels. “When you’re not able to progress and you lose sight of [personal achievements] you’re just going through the motions, that’s a really big red flag,” Ayala indicates.

After the first month or so, marathon training started to feel more like a chore and I felt really tired and disconnected during my workouts. I figured it was probably normal because I had never run a marathon before, but it turns out that’s not how you should expect to feel daily in your training. “If the workouts are just a grind and you’re having to peel yourself off of the couch in order to get them in, and that’s a sustained pattern, then that’s another massive red flag,” Ayala says.

Sources indicate that A change in my relationship with running and my “why” meant grappling with my identity as a runner, which Ayala says is the third factor that can contribute to athletic burnout. You might ask yourself questions like, “Why do I even do this anymore?” and “What’s the point?” She says that if those questions linger for more than a week or so, then that’s a sign you might need a break.

Sources indicate that by the time I got to the start line, I questioned why I was even there, and all I could do was get through it. “There’s a very real sense of grief and loss, and that’s really scary,” when taking a step back from running, Ayala explains. “Often we hang on quite desperately to our athletic identity when it’s also okay to move on and to do something different.”.

Once I knew I needed a break from running, I didn’t quite know how to hit pause.

Then Ayala pointed out that perfectionism can affect your relationship with your sport. And simply hearing that helped me realize why I needed to step away.

There are two types of perfectionists, Ayala explains. The first type is the high achiever, who wants to be the best and perform at the top as much as possible. The second type of perfectionist takes a more “maladaptive form.” These individuals are highly motivated by others’ perceptions and expectations, she says. They might fear failure and generally don’t want to disappoint people.

Industry observers note that that second type of perfectionism resonated with me because I realized I was listening to a nagging voice in my head that expected me to give absolutely everything I had to my training. And if I didn’t, I would face that critical voice’s judgment and let it fill me with disappointment and shame.

Recent findings suggest that “Breaks can be helpful because they essentially force you to turn that perfectionistic tendency off. They’re a good way to keep yourself balanced,” Ayala adds.

Once I knew this and decided to take a break, I figured out how to fill my time.

According to recent reports, those first few months after marathon training, I was so excited to not think about running at all. Instead, I looked forward to filling my free time by rebooting my old Webkinz account (an online virtual-pet game popular in the mid-2000s), decorating my latest apartment, and watching a lot of TV.

It has been revealed that “First, just give yourself time. Then, you can create other bite-sized goals like daily movement, getting outside, spending time with friends, or exploring different hobbies,” Ayala suggests.

When I passed the two-month mark of my break, I took up pottery classes. While that hobby didn’t last, it gave me the space I needed from running.

Sources indicate that I knew, deep down, that rest was probably what I needed. But when I took time off, I felt too anxious and ashamed to even enjoy it: I kept thinking about how much time I was wasting and how out-of-shape I felt. “The problem [with athletic burnout] isn’t that you just need to motivate yourself harder. You need to rest in order to get that intrinsic motivation back,” Ayala says.

Ayala explains that if we lose our intrinsic motivation, we often rely on feelings of shame and discipline to keep up with our routine, which ultimately isn’t sustainable.

When I was honest with myself, I found my upcoming “why” for running—my intrinsic motivator—and that was because of how strong, creative, and capable it makes me feel.

It has been revealed that one piece of advice from Ayala especially resonates with me: Accept where you’re at and normalize taking a break. “I would argue that getting burned out is a very normal and expected part of being an athlete,” she states. “If you are an athlete long enough, you are going to get burned out at some point and that’s okay. It doesn’t mean that you’re doing something wrong.”.

Once I accepted that I was dealing with a longer-term case of burnout and some depression, I began to see light at the end of the tunnel. I set bite-sized goals, per Ayala’s suggestion, and began by committing to running just five minutes a day for a month. With that, running clicked for me again.

Looking back on my time away from running, I don’t view it with shame or anxiety anymore. I see it as both a period of my life when I learned about myself as an athlete and about how my brain works. It was also a time of physiological and mental recovery from so much training stress.

It has been revealed that by stepping away, I unlocked a newly introduced life experience that can help inform me if—and probably when—I experience burnout again. That perspective shift alone lifts so much weight off my shoulders.

In a recent development, plus, I’ve come back to the sport now feeling refreshed. I ran one of my fastest half marathons and feel ready to tackle another training plan. I have the drive to set and achieve goals again. And that’s an irreplaceable benefit to taking some time away.
