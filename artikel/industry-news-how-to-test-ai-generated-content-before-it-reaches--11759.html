Sources indicate that most teams now ship something an AI helped write: help-center articles, product descriptions, release notes, even code comments. The draft appears in seconds, and that speed is the trap. Nobody tests it the way they would test a new feature, so mistakes slip through that a quick review would have caught.

This guide is about the other side of AI testing. Instead of using AI to test your software, you test what AI hands you. Here is a simple routine that fits into a normal publishing or release process.

Industry observers note that AI writing sounds confident whether or not it is right. A model can invent a statistic (researchers call this hallucination and have documented it across many text-generation systems), quote a feature your product does not have, or describe a setting that was renamed two releases ago. The sentence reads smoothly, so the error hides in plain sight.

Industry observers note that traditional QA does not cover this. A unit test tells you the page loads. It claims nothing about whether the claim on the page is true. That gap is where most AI-related incidents begin.

Industry observers note that 1. Define what "good" means before you generate anything. Write down the audience, the claims you are willing to make and the sources you trust. A short brief makes the output easier to judge, because you are comparing it with something concrete.

2. Check every factual claim. Highlight each number, name and statement of fact, then verify it against a primary source. Google's own guidance on generative AI content tells publishers to fact-check AI output, including titles, descriptions and alt text, before publishing. If you cannot confirm a claim in a minute or two, cut it or rewrite it as something you can support.

3. Test the instructions. For tutorials and how-to content, follow the steps exactly as written on a clean machine or account. Commands that fail and menus that no longer exist show up immediately this way.

According to recent reports, 4. Review the voice. Replace vague phrases with specifics from your own experience: a real customer question, a real error message, a real number from your analytics. This is also what separates useful content from filler.

5. Keep a human owner. Every piece should have a named person who approved it. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework makes the same point at organization level: people stay accountable for how AI is used and evaluated. If something goes wrong later, you know who to ask and where to fix it.

Some teams also run drafts through an AI detector. That can be useful as a prompt for a closer look, for example when freelancers or vendors deliver work and you want to know how much of it was machine-generated. A high score is a reason to read more carefully. It is not proof of anything, and it should never decide an outcome on its own, because detectors make mistakes in both directions. A Stanford study found that GPT detectors are biased against non-native English writers, and short texts are easy to misjudge too. If you want a quick second opinion on a draft, a tool like Tester AI can give you a starting point for that conversation.

According to recent reports, review works best when it is not optional. A few small habits make it stick.

Treating the first draft as finished. It is a starting point. Plan time for review the same way you plan time for writing.

Sources indicate that reviewing for grammar only. Clean sentences are not evidence of correct content. Check meaning first and polish second.

Industry observers note that letting the tool grade its own work. Asking the same model to confirm its answer rarely catches real errors. Use a source or a person.

In a recent development, skipping legal and brand review. Generated text can echo existing material or make promises your organization cannot keep. Anything customer-facing deserves a second pair of eyes.

AI can save hours of drafting, but speed only helps if the result is trustworthy. Treat generated content like code from a new contributor: useful, often good, and always reviewed before it goes live. A short routine of defining quality, verifying facts, testing instructions and keeping a human owner will catch most problems before your consumers do.

According to recent reports, do I need to test AI-generated content if a human edits it? Yes. Editing for style does not confirm that the facts are correct. Verify claims separately from polishing the wording.

Sources indicate that how long should a review take? For a typical article, expect roughly the time it takes to read it twice plus a few minutes per factual claim. Reviews are faster when the brief and sources are prepared in advance.

According to recent reports, can an AI detector prove that text was written by a machine? No. Detectors estimate likelihood, and they can be wrong. Use the result as a signal for human review, never as the final decision.

In a recent development, what should I check first? Start with numbers, names, dates and anything a customer could act on, such as prices, steps or legal statements. Those carry the highest risk if they are wrong.
