More Managers Require AI at Work, but Employees See Its Role Differently

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that managers are more likely than they were a year ago to require AI at work. The harder question is whether they understand how employees actually use it. In Slingshot’s 2026 Digital Work Trends Report, 68% of surveyed managers said they require employees to use AI, up from 59% in the previous edition. Yet most employee respondents said they would use AI by choice, and far fewer thought they would struggle without it than managers expected.

Sources indicate that slingshot, a work management platform from Infragistics, published the first installment on November 19. Market research firm Dynata collected the responses in the fourth quarter of 2026 from 300 employees and 200 managers in the U.S., covering four age groups and all 50 states. The report was commissioned by Slingshot. It describes what people in the sample mentioned about AI, not how much work they completed or whether their decisions improved.

Sources indicate that about half of the managers surveyed (51%) expected employees to struggle or work more slowly without AI. Among employee respondents, 26% expressed that concern about their own work. Those are different questions put to different groups, not a comparison between managers and their own direct reports. Still, the contrast is useful: leaders can see how often a tool appears in a workflow without knowing what workers could do without it.

The survey also suggests that a mandate is not the main reason many respondents use AI. Two-thirds of surveyed employees said they use it without a organization requirement, and another 23% said it was required but that they would choose to use it anyway. At the same time, 46% of Gen Z employees in the survey said they would struggle or slow down without it. The published summary does not give a headcount for that subgroup, so the percentage is a signal to investigate rather than a precise estimate for younger workers nationwide.

Research and summarization ranked first among employee uses, at 57% compared with 48% in the prior report. Drafting written content was cited by 45%, while 41% identified drafting emails, reports or other written material as work they most often keep manual. The results are not mutually exclusive. An employee can use AI to pull together background information for a report and still write the report without it.

It has been revealed that A separate Gallup survey of 21,648 U.S. employees offers a useful point of comparison. Among frequent AI consumers, 52% remarked they spent less time gathering information after adopting AI. But across the broader Gallup sample, 32% used AI at least a few times a week. The studies asked different questions of different groups, so their numbers are not directly comparable. Together, they suggest that gathering information is a productive place to study how AI changes work, while showing why adoption depends on who is asked and what counts as use.

Sources indicate that slingshot says 42% of surveyed managers reported that their organizations brought in AI tools to improve decision-making, up from 20% in 2025. That measures the reason for adopting the tools, not whether decisions turned out better. An AI summary can save time at the start of a task and create more work at the end if reviewers have to chase down missing references or correct confident errors.

Employers can track that trade-off directly. For a recurring research task, compare the time from assignment to verified output, including review and rework, rather than counting prompts or measuring how promptly the first answer appears. For decisions based on an AI-generated summary, check whether a colleague can trace the recommendation to the underlying evidence. As use becomes more routine, those measures will tell leaders more than a mandate or an adoption rate alone.
