Carnegie Mellon’s Buggy Is the 100-Year-Old Race Series You Never Knew About, But Won’t Be Able to Stop Watching

Industry observers note that ever heard of Buggy? With a capital B? Referring to a specific type of racing? Neither had we, until recently—but where there’s racing, count Road & Track intrigued. Buggy, so you know, is a unique form of racing only taking place at Carnegie Mellon University (CMU), and it’s been happening for more than 100 years. Similar to Formula SAE, students engineer, build, and drive the vehicles that compete in the odd spectacle. And yes, in case you were questioning how the buggy is driven, someone is actually inside that tube of carbon fiber.

To understand how this sport works from the car design to the track, Road & Track spoke with Caroline Kiesnowski, the head mechanic and driver for a team. Currently a junior in mechanical engineering at CMU, she broke down what it takes to compete in Buggy.

“Basically, the easiest way to describe it is you put a person in a tube, and you use it as a baton in a relay race,” Kiesnowski notes. “You get pushed up two hills, you do a free roll where the driver is completely in control—they're steering and everything—and then you get pushed back up three more hills to a finish line.”.

It’s incredibly competitive, with lots of teams vying for the win. Just like professional motorsports, each team designs a new buggy every year using experience from years past and new digital tools to refine the vehicle. The priorities are fairly self explanatory: you want the smallest, lightest buggy possible for being pushed up the hill, then for the downhill sections, designing a buggy with the smallest coefficient of drag and wheels with minimal rolling resistance is paramount. Speeds top out around 40 miles per hour, so these little vehicles are positively cruising at their fastest pace—especially considering their pilots are a foot above the pavement.

The students who end up piloting them are highly recruited, Kiesnowski tells me. Each team wants the smallest possible buggy, which means you need a small person to design the buggy’s body around. If you watch how a driver load into their respective buggy, you’ll quickly understand how tight the squeeze is and how much the design is centered around the driver.

It has been revealed that while most of the course is straight or mild curves, there’s one rather challenging turn called The Chute. It’s the source of many crashes and spins, and is something students need to design their buggy around every year. Two layouts are widely used: one is the standard trike that positions two wheels in the rear and one in the front; while the other is the reverse trike, which, logically, puts two wheels up front and one in the rear.

They each have their pros and cons, but in general, the standard trike is thought to be a bit dodgier in The Chute. With only one front wheel controlling steering and turning, it’s a simple design, but ergonomics and visibility for the driver can suffer. Meanwhile, the reverse trikes most often use Ackermann-style steering, which increases complexity, but Kiesnowski finds it to be a better design for steering and braking.

“The steering question is interesting because of the nature of buggy,” Kiesnowski stated. “It's in a sense, proprietary information; it's historically been super-secret about people talking about their teams. Some teams even have blacked out windshields, like reflective windshields, so you can't see their steering.”.

Recent findings suggest that another challenge for the track is that remarked track is actually just a road, and it’s mighty bumpy. Kiesnowski recalls that teams in the past even designed suspension to give the trike a better ride, but it’s a rare design choice that adds weight to a structure you’re trying to keep as light as possible. And yes, there is passing, especially in the downhill roll section, so you could end up doing some proper wheel-to-wheel racing. As you’d expect, though, safety is the number one goal in this whole competition, so drivers have helmets, and there are regulations teams have to follow in building their vehicles to ensure the sport is as safe as possible.

In a recent development, as with any good motorsports event, I asked Kiesnowski about teams trying to bend the rules and regulations for an advantage. She declined to offer details, but still said there have indeed been instances of teams looking for an advantage beyond the book. (After all, the grand prize of pride and bragging rights over your peers is a mighty tempting trophy in college.).

In a recent development, the competition takes place every April at Carnegie Mellon’s campus in Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania. And while the engines are essentially members of the team pushing, it’s still a form of racing we can get excited about.
