Breaking: U.S. Chamber Backs Capital Gains Tax Exclusion Bill Aimed To Reduce Taxes on Profits From Home Sales

The U.S. Chamber of Commerce has confirmed support for reducing taxes on the profits homeowners make on the sale of their home, saying it could stimulate more home sales.

It has been revealed that raising the capital gains tax exclusion—something hundreds of members of Congress and a number of real estate groups have pushed for this year—is one key lever that Washington, DC hasn't yet pulled. It could have major implications for the housing market, the Chamber mentioned.

In a recent development, "Capital gains taxes tend to distort housing choices by discouraging individuals from selling their homes because of changing family or health circumstances," the group noted. "And the taxation of capital gains in excess of exemption caps creates inequities between homeowners with different job circumstances and those living in different parts of the country.".

It has been revealed that the most visible effort in Congress comes from the More Homes on the Market Act, sponsored by Rep. Jimmy Panetta (R-CA), which would double the current exclusion. Realtor.com® has reached out to his office for comment.

According to recent reports, both the Senate and the House have versions of the bill, which are almost identical. The Chamber stated that bill offers a "reasonable, cost-effective solution" to spur more home sales.

It has been revealed that the two versions have picked up 186 sponsors and cosponsors in Congress, including large numbers of Democrats and Republicans. Earlier this year, Panetta acknowledged the push faces resistance. A congressional analysis from last year estimated such a change could deprive the government of $46.4 billion in tax revenue. Even a more targeted approach, like raising the exclusion only for people over 55, could still cost $4.9 billion.

The National Association of Realtors® also champions the bill to change the tax scheme, because it could allow more people to sell their homes without incurring a punishing tax bill. Kevin Brown, president of NAR, lobbied for the law as he spoke to a panel in Congress in June.

Congress set the current capital gains scheme in 1997. Sellers can exclude up to $250,000 in profit if single, or $500,000 if married and filing jointly, when they sell a primary home, with few strings attached.

But the law didn't include adjustments for inflation. As home prices have appreciated in the years since, homeowners see a mounting impact. The longer the value of their home appreciates, the larger the cut the federal government can take as a capital gains tax when they do sell.

Recent findings suggest that oftentimes, these might be older Americans whose lifestyle might support downsizing. But they're "locked in" to their homes thanks to high mortgage rates.

That causes large tax bills, especially for those who have stayed in their homes the longest. Or, those who live in areas where home prices have grown the fastest. NAR has estimated that nearly 13 million homeowners would face a tax penalty if they sold their homes today. Brown argued that raising the exclusion limits would help free up housing inventory at a time when the country is facing a major supply crunch.

"Capital gains indexing has huge bipartisan support," Colin Allen, executive director of the American Property Owners Alliance told Realtor.com. He brought up the possibility that it might appear in the next Congress in the form of a reintroduced bill or as a part of a reconciliation package.

"It would certainly unlock a number of homes immediately and free up their owners to do something else once they retire, and help families looking to make that next move," Allen noted.
