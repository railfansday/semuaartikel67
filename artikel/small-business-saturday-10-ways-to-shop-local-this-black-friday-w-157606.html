Small Business Saturday: 10 ways to shop local this Black Friday weekend

These 10 ways to shop local on Small Business Saturday, from gift cards and independent bookstores to reviews and makers' markets, explain how each one helps a neighborhood business.

In a recent development, where you buy a holiday gift decides how much of the money stays with the business that sold it. A sale on a significant online marketplace comes with a commission the seller pays. A card payment comes with a processing fee the merchant absorbs. A gift card bought in November puts cash in a shop's register before the recipient ever walks through the door. None of that shows up on a receipt, but all of it affects what an independent owner actually keeps.

Small Business Saturday sits right in the middle of that math. It falls on the Saturday after Thanksgiving, wedged between Black Friday and Cyber Monday. American Express $AXP started it in 2010 to carve out one day for independent businesses in a weekend that otherwise belongs to substantial-box chains and online deals. Independent shops, restaurants and service businesses now proposal sales and events around it.

In a recent development, for shoppers, the useful question is a practical one. Showing up matters, but some ways of spending leave more money with a local business than others. And several of the most helpful things you can do are free. A detailed review can help a shop turn up in map and search results. A word to a friend can bring in a customer the shop never had to pay to reach.

In a recent development, the 10 ideas below cover both kinds of support. Some involve buying: gift cards, books from an independent bookstore, orders placed through a shop's own website, food from local kitchens, classes given as gifts and finds from farmers markets and makers' markets. Others are about how you pay, what you post online and who you tell. One looks past the weekend altogether, since independent businesses depend on sales that keep coming through December and into the fresh year.

Each one works on its own. None takes a big budget, and none guarantees a particular payoff for the business. Together, they're a handful of practical ways to point your holiday spending toward the shops, bookstores, bakeries and studios in your own neighborhood.

A gift card from an independent shop puts money in the owner's hands at the moment of sale. The merchandise stays on the shelf until the card is redeemed, and the recipient gets to choose what they want.

In a recent development, that timing matters for a small retailer. Holiday inventory is usually ordered and paid for well before December. Cash that comes in on Small Business Saturday helps cover those costs, even though the goods it pays for may leave the store long after the sale.

In a recent development, gift cards also bring upcoming people through the door. A recipient who has never visited the shop now has a reason to walk in, browse and learn what it carries. Some recipients spend beyond the card's value. Others come back later on their own.

Most independent shops sell cards at the counter. Many also sell them through their own websites, often as digital cards delivered by email. A digital card suits a recipient who lives in another city or a gift that has to arrive on short notice. A physical card can go inside a holiday card or be wrapped with a small item from the same shop.

Read the terms before buying. In the U.S., the federal Credit CARD Act of 2009 bars most retail gift cards from expiring within five years. That clock runs from the purchase date or the last time money was loaded onto the card. State laws can add further protections. Ask whether the card works at every location if the business has more than one. Ask too whether it covers sale items, services or online orders.

A small business can close or change hands. Keeping the receipt helps if a card is lost or disputed. Registering a digital card, where the shop offers that option, does the same.

Industry observers note that the choice of shop can reflect the recipient. A card for a neighborhood hardware store, a toy shop or a record store points to their interests. It still leaves the final pick to them.

In a recent development, an independent bookstore can get almost any book in print, not only the titles on its shelves. Booksellers routinely take special orders for titles a customer requests. Many also ship books to a customer's home or straight to a gift recipient.

Special orders run through the same channels a store uses to restock. Bookstores buy from publishers and from book wholesalers. Either route can usually supply a title that is still in print. Giving the bookseller the ISBN, the 13-digit number printed near the barcode, removes confusion between editions, translations and formats.

A special order usually takes longer than buying a book off the shelf. That makes holiday timing something to initiative around. Publishers' warehouses and shipping carriers handle heavy volume in late November and December. Popular titles can also sell out before a reprint arrives. Placing gift orders during Black Friday weekend leaves room for those delays.

Preorders follow the same logic for books not yet published. A shop can reserve a forthcoming title for a customer. It can then set the copy aside or ship it when the stock comes in.

Staff recommendations are one advantage of shopping in person. Booksellers spend their working days handling upcoming releases and older backlist titles. Describing the recipient helps. A favorite author, a recent book they enjoyed or a subject they follow gives a bookseller enough to suggest titles a search algorithm might not surface. Many stores mark staff picks with handwritten shelf cards.

Check a shop's event calendar in December. Independent bookstores host author readings, signings, children's story hours and book club meetings. Some sell signed copies after those events. A signed book can make a more personal gift than a standard copy.

It has been revealed that secondhand books are another option. Some independent shops sell used books alongside new ones. Dedicated used bookstores can carry out-of-print titles that a new-book shop has no way to order.

According to recent reports, ask about holiday services before checking out. Some shops offer gift wrapping. Others hold orders for in-store pickup or mail books to a recipient in another city.

Ordering from a local shop's own website, or calling the store, keeps the sale off large online marketplaces. Those platforms charge sellers a commission on each sale. A direct order leaves more of the purchase price with the business that made the sale.

According to recent reports, many independent retailers list products on major marketplaces to reach shoppers who would never find them otherwise. That reach comes at a cost. Marketplace sellers typically pay a cut of every order. Some platforms add charges for advertising, storage or fulfillment. The exact terms vary by platform and by product category.

A shop's own site works differently. The merchant still pays for web hosting and an online store platform. No marketplace takes a share of each order, though. Shops that don't sell online at all can often take an order by phone or email instead.

Finding the direct route is usually straightforward. A shop's web address often appears on its receipts, shopping bags, business cards and social media profiles. Searching the store's name along with its city can bring up the official site alongside any marketplace listings. When a product appears in both places, buying it on the shop's own site is the direct sale.

Industry observers note that A shop's own site may also carry stock that never reaches a marketplace. One-off pieces, small batches and items made by nearby producers sometimes appear only there.

Sources indicate that check for local pickup before checkout. Many small retailers let customers order online and collect in store. That spares the buyer a shipping charge and spares the shop from preparing a parcel. It also brings the buyer through the door, where other items on the shelves are in view.

Industry observers note that shipping from a small shop can cost more or take longer than shipping from a large retailer with its own logistics network. Ordering early in the holiday season leaves room for that. Some shops post holiday shipping cutoff dates on their sites.

Direct orders also change who holds the customer relationship. Marketplaces often limit how sellers can contact buyers. When a shopper buys from the store's site, the shop can follow up on the order and answer questions itself. Returns and exchanges then go through the shop rather than a platform. Reading the store's own return policy before paying avoids confusion later.

Industry observers note that following an independent shop's stated payment preference is one of the simplest ways to help it on Small Business Saturday. A shop that asks for cash or debit is often trying to manage what card acceptance costs the business.

Every card sale carries processing fees. The merchant pays them, not the shopper. Several parties share those fees, including the bank that issued the card, the card network and the company that processes the payment. Depending on the processor, the charges come out of each deposit or arrive as a periodic bill.

Industry observers note that card money also takes time to arrive. Card sales settle through the processor and land in a merchant's bank account after a delay. Cash is in the register the moment the sale ends. For a small retailer paying for holiday inventory or covering extra staff in a busy month, that timing can matter.

Preference is the operative word. Cash carries its own costs for a business. Someone has to count it, store it securely and take it to the bank. Some owners would rather skip that work and run a fully card-based or cashless counter. Paying cash at a shop that does not want it does not help the owner.

It has been revealed that the clearest guide is the shop itself. Look for a sign at the register or a note on the shop's website. Some small businesses post a cash discount. Others add a surcharge for credit cards. Rules on surcharging vary by state and by card network. Card network rules generally bar surcharges on debit cards, even where credit surcharges are allowed.

Cashless stores are not allowed everywhere. Massachusetts, Upcoming Jersey, New York City, Philadelphia and San Francisco are among the places that generally require retail stores to accept cash. Those rules are meant to protect shoppers who do not have bank accounts or cards.

Recent findings suggest that A short question at checkout works when no sign is posted. Asking which payment method the shop prefers takes a few seconds. It costs the shopper nothing extra, and it leaves the decision with the owner.

Industry observers note that planning ahead makes this easier. Withdrawing cash before a trip to a downtown shopping district helps when several smaller vendors are on the list. Some pop-up sellers work mostly in cash, and a shopper without it may have to walk away from a purchase.

A specific, honest online review helps a local business appear when nearby shoppers search, and it costs the reviewer nothing. A review that names what was bought and how the visit went gives other customers far more to go on than a star rating alone.

In a recent development, search engines and map apps treat reviews as one of several signals when deciding which nearby businesses to show. A shop with recent, detailed reviews gives those systems more information to work with. It also gives a stranger scrolling through results a concrete reason to walk in.

It has been revealed that the most useful reviews read like a short report. Name the product or service, such as a hand-thrown mug, a house-made hot sauce or a bike tune-up. Mention whether the item was in stock or ordered in, and how long the wait was. Describe what the staff did, whether that meant tracking down a size, wrapping a gift or suggesting an alternative. Those details answer the practical questions future customers tend to ask.

Timing shapes a review's value as well. A review posted soon after a Small Business Saturday visit captures details while they are fresh. A post that week adds a recent entry during a season when many shoppers are deciding where to spend. Photos of the storefront, the shelves or the purchase can add context that text cannot.

In a recent development, honesty keeps a review useful. A balanced account that notes a long line or limited parking alongside good service gives readers a fuller picture than unqualified praise. Many review platforms bar reviews written in exchange for discounts or free products. A review should reflect a real purchase or visit. Owners can often reply publicly, and a constructive note gives them something concrete to act on.

Many independent shops work with limited advertising budgets. For a shop like that, its search listing and the reviews attached to it can be one of the first things a new customer sees. Each customer who writes a few clear sentences adds directly to that public record.

Recent findings suggest that A short review takes only a few minutes. It can be written on a phone before leaving the parking lot or later that evening. It stays attached to the business listing long after the holiday weekend ends.

Farmers markets and holiday makers' markets let shoppers buy straight from the people who grow, cook or craft the goods on the table. With no wholesaler or retail middleman involved, the vendor sets the price and collects the sale directly, minus any stall fee the market charges.

Recent findings suggest that that direct link is the main difference from a store shelf. A farmer selling apples, winter squash or eggs at a market booth is often the same person who raised them. A potter, woodworker or soapmaker at a craft fair usually made the pieces on display and can explain how.

Holiday makers' markets tend to appear in late November and December. They are often held in school gyms, church halls, community centers, breweries and public squares. Many run for a single weekend or one evening. The window to shop them can be short.

Sources indicate that outdoor farmers markets also shift with the season. In colder parts of the U.S., some move indoors for winter and cut back to fewer market days. Others close until spring. A market's own website or social media page is usually the most reliable place to confirm dates, hours and location before going.

Shelf-stable goods make practical gifts from these markets. Honey, jams, maple syrup and dried herbs travel well and keep for weeks or longer. Candles, soap and ceramics hold up in shipping and storage. Fresh produce and baked goods are better suited to a host gift or a holiday meal.

Sources indicate that face-to-face buying also allows questions that a product listing rarely answers. Shoppers can ask what wood a cutting board is made of, how a glazed mug should be cleaned or whether a soap contains fragrance. Farmers can explain how a crop was grown and how to store it.

It has been revealed that many makers take commissions and custom orders, such as a personalized ornament or a piece in a specific color. These requests need lead time. Asking in late November or early December rather than the week before a holiday gives the maker room to finish the work.

It has been revealed that vendors at seasonal markets often have no permanent storefront. Picking up a business card or noting a maker's name makes it possible to find them again. Many also sell between markets by email, by phone or at other recurring events.

A local class or service works as a holiday gift that sends money to a nearby business owner instead of a distant warehouse. A pottery class, a haircut or a pass for a neighborhood fitness studio all fit this approach.

Service businesses sell time and skill rather than stocked inventory. That puts studios, salons and independent instructors outside the doorbuster discounts that dominate Black Friday weekend promotions. A gift of their time gives them a holiday sale that doesn't depend on a shelf of products.

According to recent reports, most of these businesses sell gifts in one of a few forms. A studio may offer a single-session certificate, a multi-class pack or a set membership period. Salons and barbershops often sell certificates for a named service, such as a cut or a color appointment. Some instructors sell seats in a specific workshop on a set date. A question at the front desk will show which formats a business offers.

Booking terms matter more for experiences than for physical goods. Classes fill up, and instructors keep limited schedules. Some sessions run only in certain months. Before buying, check whether the gift is tied to a date and how far ahead the recipient needs to book. Ask what happens if they need to reschedule. Expiration rules vary by business and by location. Confirm them at the time of purchase.

In a recent development, A gift that fits the recipient's interests is easier for them to use. A beginner wheel-throwing class suits someone who likes working with their hands. A cooking class can suit someone who hosts often. A massage, a tailoring appointment or a bike tune-up can suit someone who rarely books those services for themselves.

In a recent development, many of these businesses work in small groups or one-on-one sessions. That format gives the recipient direct time with an instructor, stylist or technician. It also introduces the recipient to a business they may return to on their own.

A gift for two is another option. Paired classes, such as a couples' cooking night or a two-person workshop, let the giver attend alongside the recipient. Some studios also sell private group sessions for families or friends. Those sessions can double as a shared holiday outing.

The same idea works for a recipient who lives in another city. Many studios and salons take gift orders by phone or online. A giver can buy a session at an independent business near the recipient's home rather than near their own.

Ordering pies, platters and party trays from a neighborhood bakery or restaurant moves holiday food spending to local kitchens. Order early, order directly and plan the pickup. Many small kitchens can only produce so much in a single week.

Catering follows a similar timeline. Restaurants that sell trays of appetizers, roasted meats or side dishes usually ask for a headcount and a pickup date in advance. Some ask for a deposit to hold the order. Calling with a rough guest count makes it easier for the kitchen to suggest portions and quantities.

According to recent reports, dietary questions are easier to sort out with a local kitchen than with a packaged product. Customers can ask what goes into a dish and how it is prepared. They can also ask whether it contains nuts, dairy or gluten. Some small bakeries make egg-free or dairy-free versions on request. Shared equipment can still mean cross-contact. Asking the kitchen directly is the reliable way to know.

Industry observers note that how an order is placed also matters. Calling the restaurant, ordering at the counter or using its own online ordering page keeps the sale off third-party delivery apps. A direct order also lets staff confirm details or flag a sold-out item before the event.

Pickup is the final step. Holiday pickup days tend to be crowded, and many shops assign time slots to keep lines moving. Arriving within the assigned window helps the kitchen hand off orders while the food is fresh. Asking for reheating instructions for hot dishes saves guesswork at home.

Sources indicate that the same approach works for more than one dinner. Office parties, Upcoming Year's gatherings and winter potlucks all call for platters, desserts and bread. Restaurants that do not advertise catering may still take a large takeout order if a customer asks a few days ahead.

Small Business Saturday lasts a single day, but local shops need customers for the rest of the holiday season and well into the newly introduced year. Spreading purchases across December and January gives independent businesses steadier sales than one busy weekend can.

In a recent development, the Saturday event puts independent retailers in the spotlight for one weekend. The weeks that follow still bring gift buying, holiday hosting and year-end errands.

It has been revealed that last-minute gifts are easier to find at a store close to home. National carriers publish holiday shipping cutoffs, and orders placed after those dates may not arrive before Christmas. A shop down the street already has its stock on the shelf. A shopper can walk in on Dec. 23 and leave with a gift in hand.

Industry observers note that local staff can also help with a late gift. They know what is in stock and can suggest an alternative when a planned item has sold out. Some independent shops also wrap gifts at the counter. That saves time in the final days before a holiday.

Sources indicate that january brings its own reasons to buy close to home. Exchanges and returns are simpler when the store is nearby and the owner can handle them in person. Post-holiday clearance sales help local shops move seasonal inventory. Cold-weather needs such as boots, coats, snow shovels and ice melt send people to hardware and clothing stores in many parts of the U.S.

The quieter stretch after the holidays matters for owners too. Foot traffic often drops once the gift season ends. Rent, payroll and utility bills keep arriving each month regardless. A purchase during a slow week adds revenue when fewer customers are coming through the door.

One version of this approach is a routine. Picking up coffee beans, a birthday card or a replacement hardware part from an independent shop turns occasional support into regular business. Those everyday errands happen in every month, not only in late November.

One practical rule is to check a local store before ordering something online. That habit carries the purpose of Small Business Saturday into the rest of the year.

Telling friends about a local shop, or sharing its posts online, points new customers toward an independent business at no cost to you. The recommendation comes from someone the listener already knows, which gives it weight with them.

In a recent development, independent shops rarely have the marketing budgets of national chains. At many of them, the owner or a single employee runs the shop's social media accounts alongside other work. Each share, tag or mention carries the shop's name to people outside its usual audience.

Recent findings suggest that specific recommendations give friends something to act on. Saying a shop "is great" offers little detail. Naming the hand-poured candles, the cheese counter or the employee who tracked down a hard-to-find gift tells a friend why to go. It also tells them what to ask for.

Social media makes this kind of referral easy to repeat. Posting a photo of a holiday purchase and tagging the shop's account lets followers click straight through to its profile. Resharing the shop's own posts spreads the details an owner most wants customers to see. Those posts often cover newly introduced stock, extended holiday hours or a Small Business Saturday event.

In a recent development, adding a location tag to a post connects it to the shop's address. That helps followers who live nearby figure out where to find it.

Private messages and group chats count, too. A friend stuck on a gift for a relative can get a quick text with a link to a shop that fits. That one message can send a customer the shop might not have reached otherwise.

Email newsletters offer another direct line. Signing up means hearing about sales, restocks and events straight from the shop. The news arrives without depending on what a social platform chooses to display. Forwarding a newsletter to a friend passes that information along.

Sources indicate that neighborhood groups and community forums are another outlet. Someone may ask for gift ideas or a place to find a particular item. Naming a local shop in reply puts it in front of a person already planning to buy.

In a recent development, some owners prefer that customers not photograph certain items, displays or staff. Asking before posting from inside a shop respects that preference.
