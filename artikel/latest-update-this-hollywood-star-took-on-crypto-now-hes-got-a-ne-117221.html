📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that ben McKenzie promised his wife that he was through with being an activist; that he’d go back to the acting gigs that had made him famous. At the end of his documentary taking on the cryptocurrency industry, Everyone Is Lying to You for Money,” the former star of The O.C. and Gotham turned to his spouse and fellow actor Morena Baccarin and swore he was done.

Turns out, that scene might need an edit. McKenzie is launching a new nonprofit, The Public Trust and Transparency Project, that not only continues his battle with the crypto business but widens it to fight prediction markets like Polymarket and Kalshi.

McKenzie tells WIRED he’s worried about “the gamification of the economy and the notion that we're turning everything into gambling—both investments, in the form of cryptocurrencies, and “prediction markets,” which look an awful lot like online casinos.”.

“The more gambling gets into our culture, the more people feel like it's somehow a good idea to view these things as legitimate investment strategies,” he states. The prediction markets reject the comparison (although 80 percent of Kalshi’s trades came from sports in September, according to Bloomberg). McKenzie rejects the rejection: “I mean, if your investment strategy is, ‘I'm going to gamble on the Spurs-Knicks,’ like, come on! So there's a real, real problem here.”.

According to recent reports, john Reed Stark, who spent 18 years in the Securities and Exchange Commission’s enforcement division, will serve as an adviser to the Project. American Kleptocracy author Casey Michel and Tonantzin Carmona, who runs economic policy for the left-of-center Century Foundation, will sit on the board.

It has been revealed that the group is coming together at a time when regulators and law enforcement officials at every level are looking to rein in prediction markets. Federal regulators have debuted latest probes into both major prediction markets. The GOP-led House Oversight Committee recently released its investigation into US troops and government insiders trading on insider knowledge on Polymarket and Kalshi. The Senate just passed a bill prohibiting prediction markets for military operations and assassinations. Executives at the markets know they’re in for a rough few months; Kalshi even launched an ad campaign, starring Martha Stewart, reminding its users not to trade on confidential information.

But insider trading is only one concern among many. There’s also the problem you find in old-school gambling houses too: The vast majority of people who use the markets lose their money. A recent Wall Street Journal report demonstrates that .01 percent of accounts take in two-thirds of all of the profits on Polymarket. On Kalshi, unprofitable accounts outpace profitable ones by almost three to one.

Recent findings suggest that then there’s the corruption angle, which McKenzie says is going to be front-and-center for the Project, especially with it featuring as a top concern in the recent midterm elections. Donald Trump Jr. was, until recently, a paid adviser to Kalshi. Trump Jr.’s venture firm, 1789 Capital, made its latest and biggest in a series of investments in Polymarket over the summer; months earlier, the Trump Justice Department sued the state of Minnesota when it tried to ban the prediction markets in the state. (At a recent conference, Polymarket CEO Shayne Coplan called Trump Jr.’s involvement “heavily overblown. We have all types of investors.”).

Sources indicate that prediction markets have been around for hundreds of years, as a forthcoming book on the subject notes. American academics have been turning to them as teaching tools since the late ’80s. Their advocates note that they can be an effective way to hedge against risk, and a form of trading on sports that’s far more consumer-friendly than the online gambling houses. “I accept that there may be some utility,” McKenzie notes. “I'm not saying these things should not exist. I'm just saying they need to be properly regulated.”.

McKenzie’s plan will be to push Congress in that direction, sort of the inverse of how the former actor lobbied publicly and privately against the “Clarity Act,” which would’ve brought cryptocurrencies into the financial mainstream. (The bill was rejected in September.).

That pressure is only bound to increase when Democrats take control of Congress next year. “In the bold latest world starting [on the] first Monday in January,” Representative Frank Lucas, an Oklahoma Republican, told Politico, “nobody’s untouchable anymore.”.
