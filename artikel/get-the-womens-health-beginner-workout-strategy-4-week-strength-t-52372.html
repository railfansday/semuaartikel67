Industry observers note that it’s not just you—everyone really is starting to work out lately. My feed is full of people hitting the weights for the first time ever or setting big goals like getting their first pull-up. And let me just say: It’s awesome!

So, if that’s you, let me officially say welcome to the party. When you’re starting to work out, it can be hard to sift through all the options and find a program that’s actually accessible for a true beginner (or, someone restarting after quite some time). That is, until now.

Recent findings suggest that the Women’s Health+ 4-Week Beginner Workout Plan takes the guesswork out of starting your fitness journey. This comprehensive program guides you through strength and cardio workouts that only take between 20 and 45 minutes. And, of course, it answers every burning question you could have about starting a fitness routine.

No matter your age or experience, starting an exercise routine has many benefits, says Kristen McParland, CPT, your coach for the next four weeks. Building muscle increases total-body strength; your bones, ligaments, and tendons get healthier; and even your blood pressure and immunity may improve. So lace up those sneakers and we’ll see you at the gym now, too.

Download the WH+ 4-Week Beginner Workout Plan.

Over the next four weeks, you’ll follow a specific blueprint each day, focusing either on strength training, optional cardio, active recovery, or rest (more on these, below). Each strength workout includes a warm-up and a cooldown (which you can use on cardio days, too). More info on each below.

Simply follow the instructions of each full-body strength training workout in the Women's Health+ Beginner Workout Blueprint. You can find detailed how-to instructions for each exercise in the Exercise Guide section of the PDF.

Equipment Needed: Dumbbells, kettlebells, a step or box, and a slam ball. Don’t have access to a kettlebell or slam ball? Don't worry—you can still do those exercises with a dumbbell.

Cardio is important for everyone, regardless of fitness level or routine. That’s because when you’re lifting heavy, your heart needs to be able to take on that stress. Training cardio—through running, cycling, or brisk walking—will help improve your heart strength.

It has been revealed that if you’re a beginner when it comes to cardio, simply start at your own pace and build. Complete 20 to 30 minutes of running, biking, cycling, rowing, or using the elliptical or stair climber (whatever you enjoy). No matter which form of cardio you choose, start with interval training: exert yourself for 30 seconds, then go at a light pace for 1 to 2 minutes, alternating the pattern for a total of 20 minutes. The goal is to “get your heart rate up, but then pull it back and recover,” McParland claims. Eventually, you’ll be able to decrease the rest time and maintain that higher-intensity movement for 15 to 20 minutes without a break.

Industry observers note that your activity on this day is up to you. The goal is to do some kind of low-intensity movement lasting 20 minutes. Some ideas: outdoor walk or hike, bike ride, incline treadmill walk, yoga, or Pilates class.

Take this day to relax so your muscles have time to repair. Still, if your body is craving some type of activity for recovery, you could stretch, go for a walk, or even try a treatment like a massage, sauna, or cold-plunge session.

Here’s how things will progress each week.

In a recent development, complete the five programmed workouts when it’s convenient for you. Focus on your form and take your two rest days when you need to recover.

It has been revealed that if you completed all the workouts in Week 1, repeat them during Week 2, adjusting one variable in your routine, McParland claims: the number of reps or sets, the weight, the speed of the moves (slower is harder). If you missed a workout, don’t stress. Refocus on completing all of the Week 1 workouts before making any of the changes above.

You’ve officially done two full weeks of this workout blueprint—nice job! During this third week, repeat the workouts from Week 2 in exactly the same way or adjust one variable again (reps or sets, weight, range of motion, speed, and rest periods). If you feel you’re crushing it and are ready to level up, try the third full-body strength workout.

Still going strong? Thought so! Hit all six workouts and adjust one variable per workout for an extra challenge (reps or sets, weight, range of motion, speed, rest time). You can also ramp up your cardio time (to 30 to 40 minutes) or speed, shorten rest intervals (for interval training), or raise your step goal by 10 percent (e.g., if your daily step goal was 5,000 a day, make it 5,500).

When you’re just beginning to work out, do a minimum of two full-body strength training days each week, McParland recommends. During both of those days, you should hit at least one exercise from each category of the foundational movement patterns. This will give you enough of a challenge to stimulate muscle growth, she says.

During the first two weeks of this blueprint, you will have two strength training days per week (each session will run 36 to 43 minutes), two optional cardio/step days per week (each session will 20 to 30 minutes), one day of active rest (a stint of low-intensity movement lasting at least 20 minutes), and two recovery days, for a total of five workouts per week.

After those two weeks, see how your body feels and if your body is craving a third day of full-body training. If so, progress to the next phase of this plan. That weekly schedule consists of three strength training days per week (each session will run 36 to 43 minutes), two optional cardio/step days per week (each session will run 20 to 30 minutes), one active rest day (lasting at least 20 minutes), and one recovery day, for a total of six workouts per week. If your body isn’t ready for three strength-training days yet, wait another two weeks. Once you’ve completed the full four weeks of this plan, consider adding in the third day.

What kind of weights should I choose?

Recent findings suggest that start with medium weights that will challenge you for 10 to 12 reps, states Sariya. Essentially, you’ll want to feel you’ve reached 70 to 80 percent of your rate of perceived exertion (i.e., your fatigue level is at a 7 or 8 out of 10) by the time you’re doing your final reps in a set. When you feel you can do more than 12 reps, it’s time to increase your weight, but keep the same number of reps. This way, you’re always working toward your goal of building strength.

As for how much to increase by: a good rule of thumb is 2.5 to 5 pounds for an upper-body exercise and 5 to 10 pounds for a lower-body move.

What if I need to rest in the middle of a set?

Resting during a workout is key—if you don’t take enough time to recover between exercises, you won’t have enough energy in your body for the next one. With this strategy, you’ll rest between moves in a superset or tri-set for 20 to 30 seconds, or however long it takes to transition between exercises. Between supersets and tri-sets, you’ll rest for one minute.

Industry observers note that if you get fatigued in the middle of a set, push through if you feel safe doing so, Sariya says. However, if you feel dizzy or fear you might drop a weight on yourself, put it down and take time to recover. When you’re feeling better, you can start again. If you’re in any sort of pain, stop the workout entirely.

What kind of cardio should I do?

In a recent development, cardio is a great supplement to strength training, McParland says. Plus, it has a slew of benefits, such as better endurance in strength training, better blood pressure regulation, and higher VO2 max, she says.

Sources indicate that if you’re a beginner when it comes to cardio, simply start at your own pace and build. Pick any form of cardio you enjoy, whether that’s brisk walking, running, cycling, or swimming, and sprinkle it in two or three days a week for 20 to 30 minutes, McParland recommends.

What should I do on recovery days?

According to recent reports, on an active rest day, you’ll still exercise a bit through low-intensity movement, like a leisurely bike ride or hike. On a recovery day, you’ll do just that: recover. After all, you need to give your muscles time to rebuild and repair, Sariya indicates. But to maximize the recovery aspect, you can also opt to stretch, go for a gentle walk, or even get a self-care body treatment like a massage.

Once you’ve got the WH+ Beginner Workout Blueprint under your belt, you’re ready to take things to another level. One way to do that is by simply restarting the program—and completing the moves with heavier weights this time. (This is called progressive overload, and it’s another foundational concept in strength training. The idea is that over time, you will keep increasing the stimulus to your muscles to continuously challenge them and make them stronger.).

Recent findings suggest that picking the right weight is an art as much as it is a science, but for a general rule of thumb, for upper-body moves, increase your weights by 2.5 to 5 pounds and see how that feels. For lower-body ones, try adding 5 to 10. You should feel like you’re ending each set two to three reps away from failure (where your form starts to break down). You can tell you’re close if your range of motion starts to shorten or your reps are slowing down. That’s how you know you’re successfully challenging yourself. And, if you don’t happen to have a lot of different-sized weights at your disposal, you can always just increase the number of reps you do instead.
