Sources indicate that from the 4-judge unsigned majority opinion in Patterson v. Meta Platforms, Inc., decided today by N.Y.'s high court:.

On May 14, 2022, a man motivated by heinous white supremacist ideologies he allegedly adopted due to content he viewed on social media carried out a mass shooting at a Buffalo grocery store, killing 10 and injuring three others. Plaintiffs, victims of the shooting and their family members, commenced these actions against the shooter's parents, firearms manufacturers, and social media companies, seeking compensation for their injuries.

According to recent reports, these appeals concern motions to dismiss plaintiffs' claims against several social media companies whose platforms the shooter allegedly used before committing this unspeakable act of violence. Specifically, plaintiffs contend that their complaints state causes of action for products liability based on defective features of these social media defendants' platforms and their failure to warn thereof….

[P]laintiffs failed to adequately plead that features of [the] social media defendants' products proximately caused their injuries. Generally, a defendant's acts qualify as a proximate cause when they are "a substantial cause of the events which produced the injury." This inquiry examines.

"the foreseeability of the event resulting in injury; the passage of time between the originally negligent act and the intervening act; the spatial gap, if any, between the original act and the intervening act; whether the original act of negligence was a completed occurrence or was ongoing at the time of the intervening act; whether and, if so, what other forces combined to bring about the harm; as well as public policy considerations regarding the scope of liability.".

Industry observers note that foreseeability is often the most significant factor. Indeed, if an unforeseeable intervening criminal act of a third party causes the plaintiff's injury, the causal connection is severed. Thus, proximate causation will not be found when the intervening act "is extraordinary under the circumstances, not foreseeable in the normal course of events, or independent of or far removed from the defendant's conduct." "Although foreseeability and proximate cause are generally questions for the factfinder, there are instances in which proximate cause can be determined as a matter of law because 'only one conclusion may be drawn from the established facts'" such as when "the original negligent act merely 'furnished the occasion for'—but did not cause—'an unrelated act to cause injuries not ordinarily anticipated.'".

Sources indicate that here, plaintiffs failed to adequately allege that the remaining social media defendants' product defects proximately caused the shooter to carry out the mass shooting that resulted in plaintiffs' injuries. The complaints, even accepting the factual allegations as true, fail to establish that the shooter's actions were a natural and foreseeable consequence of these defendants' decisions about how to design and operate their platforms.

In a recent development, instead, under the circumstances, the shooter's actions were so extraordinary as to sever the causal chain as a matter of law. {Our conclusion is limited to the unusual facts of this case, where the platforms' assertedly addictive features were alleged to have harmed the shooter rather than the plaintiffs directly.} We therefore need not address the scope of section 230 of the Communications Decency Act or how it would apply to the claims against these defendants.

Judge Halligan joined the majority, but also wrote a concurring opinion:.

Sources indicate that I join the majority because I agree that the plaintiffs have not adequately pleaded proximate causation. I write separately to underscore that, for me, this conclusion rests squarely on the highly unusual facts presented here, and to offer some additional thoughts regarding the scope of section 230.

As an initial matter, I agree with my dissenting colleagues that the plaintiffs alleged that several of the defendants tortiously designed addictive products. The complaints make a number of general claims about social media and youth: that defendants' platforms are addictive; that compulsive use of social media, particularly by young people, causes emotional disturbance and "impulsive decision-making and risk-taking behavior"; and that such use can also yield other deleterious consequences, including "sleep disturbances, mental health problems and suicidal ideation, and violent, hateful acts." The complaints further assert that the social media defendants, knowing of the addictive quality of their products and the heightened susceptibility of young owners, not only failed to implement features for limiting use or warning parents, but also took deliberate advantage of that vulnerability by utilizing "strategic timing and clustering of notifications to lure back users" and by implementing "barriers to the deactivation and deletion of accounts." ….

According to recent reports, I find the claims before us quite distinct from the cases cited by the dissent that have allowed products liability claims premised on the addictiveness of social media platforms to proceed. I have little doubt that a plaintiff could allege a sufficient connection between the design of a platform and adverse consequences for the mental health of young users. In those cases, the harms that form the basis for the claims—for example, the alleged inducement of "compulsive use among young users to the detriment of their health and well-being"—flow directly from the platforms' allegedly defective features, leaving little question "that the defective design was a substantial factor in causing plaintiff's injury.".

In a recent development, but the plaintiffs here are not bringing a claim on behalf of owners. Instead, they are seeking recompense for the victims of a crime committed by a social media user. In other words, the allegedly addictive properties of the platforms were not alleged to have harmed the plaintiffs here directly, but rather to have harmed the shooter, who in turn engaged in extensive research and planning before murdering ten people and wounding others…. I do not see the connection between the two as sufficiently articulated to allow the case to proceed.

Sources indicate that three aspects of these claims inform my conclusion. First, although intervening criminal acts do not necessarily sever the causal chain, the shooter's conduct was apparently painstakingly planned and extraordinarily violent.

Second, the prominence of content in many of plaintiffs' allegations gives me real pause. Even if we were to interpret section 230 as not immunizing defendants against claims for actions that rest, at least in part, on content, I am not aware of other instances where a court has held that provocative content proximately caused someone to commit a violent crime, and I am hesitant to take that step here. {I understand the plaintiffs to disclaim on appeal any effort to base liability on defendants' roles as publishers of content. But their complaints can be read to allege that the platforms are defective at least in part because of the racist and extremist content they make available, and the plaintiffs acknowledge that content is a necessary link in the causal chain leading to their harms.}.

Sources indicate that third, the defendants' platforms have billions of worldwide people. To the extent that proximate cause could rest on a correlation between social media use and a specific type of harmful act committed by any one of those users, the potential liability would sweep broadly, to say the least. If all such conduct were "a natural and foreseeable consequence" of a platform's design choices, the concept of proximate cause would have little meaning….

As for section 230, …. "we [have] read section 230 to bar 'lawsuits seeking to hold a service provider liable for its exercise of a publisher's traditional editorial functions.'" … Our endorsement of a "traditional editorial functions" test for section 230 immunity, however, does little to answer whether the conduct of modern social media platforms can be labelled such a function.

It has been revealed that as to claims that a defendant designed its platform features to encourage or facilitate addiction, irrespective of the particular content promoted by the platform, I agree with the dissent that they are not barred by section 230. That is because such conduct does not constitute a traditional editorial function, even under a broad understanding of that term.

As to claims that challenge a social media platform's use of content-recommendation algorithms, the dissent appears to take the view that content-recommendation algorithms which are designed to be addictive are not covered by section 230 immunity, since they increase engagement in a way that could not have been envisioned at the time that provision was enacted in 1996. I have no quarrel with how we framed the scope of section 230 in Shiamili: the key question is whether a platform engaged in a traditional editorial function, akin to a decision "to publish, withdraw, postpone or alter content." But whether platforms' use of content-recommendation algorithms can be noted to resemble traditional decisions to publish, withdraw, postpone, or alter content is, for me, a more difficult question….

According to recent reports, [O]verly broad interpretations of section 230 in the face of a rapidly changing technological landscape are unmoored from Congress's purpose in enacting that provision—to protect minors from being exposed to pornography online…. Given that caution, I would not be inclined to extend section 230's reach to all content-recommendation algorithms. I am particularly skeptical that use of individualized content-recommendation algorithms—that is, algorithms that "assess[ ] a user's prior posts, friends, or viewing habits to recommend upcoming content and connections"—constitutes a traditional publisher function. After all, "[t]raditional publication has never included selecting the news, opinion pieces, or classified ads to send to each individual reader based on guesses as to their preferences and interests." Instead, such functions may be "more analogous to the actions of a direct marketer, matchmaker, or recruiter than to those of a publisher.".

In my view, a closer question is presented as to engagement-maximizing algorithms that, unlike the algorithms described above, do not take into account any individual user's conduct but instead seek to "promote[ ] third-party content" based on "cumulative user engagement." On one hand, algorithms that do not respond to individual user conduct may be thought to resemble a publisher's traditional aim of presenting content that customers, on the whole, want to see. On the other hand, one might conclude that "generic" content-based algorithms bear no more resemblance to traditional publication than their "'advanced'—individualized—counterparts" because they still rely on an assessment of user viewing habits in a manner akin to "a direct marketer" rather than a traditional publisher. My dissenting colleagues offer yet another variation on this point; they assert that engagement-maximizing algorithms differ from an editor's choice of where to place a news story because the algorithms are "designed to prey on neurological frailties and alter brain chemistry.".

According to recent reports, whether section 230 protects any of these algorithms is, for me, a difficult question, and I do not think the record before us sheds much light on how different types of content-recommendation algorithms actually operate on the social media defendants' individual platforms—information that might affect my view. We need not decide those questions today because, whatever the scope of the "traditional editorial functions" protected by section 230, the plaintiffs have failed to sufficiently allege that the defendants' platforms' features caused their grave injuries.

Judge Rivera, joined by Chief Judge Wilson and Judge Troutman, dissented in relevant part; an excerpt from the very long opinion:.

The complaints detail the shooter's transformation from a seemingly ordinary teenager into a virulent racist and violent white supremacist…. [In his manifesto, t]he shooter also wrote that he "was not born racist nor [did he] gr[o]w up to be racist." Rather, he was exposed to and indoctrinated in his ideology of racial violence after using various social media platforms, starting with Instagram, YouTube, and Snapchat in his early teen years, before he transitioned to more niche platforms like Reddit, Discord, Twitch and 4chan. The shooter claimed he quickly "became transfixed with and addicted to [the] online world." He wrote in his diary:.

"I'm addict[ed] to consoooooming. It's 2 in the morning. w[hat] t[he] f[*ck] … I'll admit my mental health has seriously degraded these past few years, especially in the last few months. I should be sleeping right now. I'm just browsing online:/" (emphasis added).

Sources indicate that by his own account, he sacrificed "years of [his] life" to being online, often "stay[ing] up late" "browsing Reddit or watching YouTube" "every day." He stated that his compulsive social media use "seriously degraded" his mental health and isolated him from his peers. Rather than "go[ing] to friend's houses" or other social gatherings, "[e]very day after school [he] would just go home and play games and watch YouTube, mostly by [him]self." Ultimately, as he descended further into isolation, he believed he had no other choice but to commit this barbarous act.

Plaintiffs allege that the shooter's experience of compulsive social media engagement is widespread because the products' addictive features are fundamental to the social media defendants' business model. According to plaintiffs, defendants' business models seek to maximize user engagement through products "designed to be addictive." Plaintiffs assert that the operation of these products on the shooter, and their adverse impact on his mind caused by their addictive features, are links in the proximate causal chain underlying plaintiffs' tort liability causes of action against the social media defendants….

The Appellate Division erroneously concluded that defendants are immune from plaintiffs' lawsuit under Section 230. As I discuss, Section 230 does not shield defendants from plaintiffs' products liability and failure to warn allegations grounded on allegedly addictive products, as those claims have nothing to do with defendants' role as a third-party content publisher or as the creator of protected speech….

Industry observers note that to the extent plaintiffs' claims against the social media defendants are based upon defendants' publication of third-party content, including racist, white supremacist, and violent content, defendants are immune from suit pursuant to Section 230. However, not all claims asserted in the complaints are based upon defendants' publication of third-party content. Rather, as I explain, the complaints also stated causes of action based on defendants' design of allegedly defective products and failure to warn….

Plaintiffs assert that these defendants are civilly liable for their role in addicting the shooter to social media and consequent deterioration of his mental health. Plaintiffs' complaints set forth the entire chain of causation that allegedly led to the mass shooting. By designing highly addictive algorithms known to cause severe mental harm to teenagers—like the shooter—the social media defendants created a direct link to the resulting damages, particularly given their failure to warn users and parents of these risks…. [T]here is no Section 230 immunity that attaches to the architecture of defendants' social media products—particularly their purposefully addictive algorithms….

In a recent development, defendants' alternative argument that the complaints were properly dismissed on First Amendment grounds is unpersuasive. At this pre-answer stage of the litigation, it is unclear how precisely the content-recommendation algorithms function, meaning it is not possible to determine, as a matter of law, on a motion to dismiss that the algorithms themselves are protected speech. That determination requires factual findings dependent on future discovery. On a motion to dismiss we only decide whether the plaintiffs' claim is cognizable and not whether it will eventually succeed based on the facts established by the record….

And here's a short excerpt from the dissent's long discussion of why it rejects the majority's proximate cause theory:.

The majority's conclusion that plaintiffs "fail to establish that the shooter's actions were a natural and foreseeable consequence" of the social media defendants' platforms because "the shooter's actions were so extraordinary as to sever the causal chain as a matter of law" is unsupported by our well-established tort liability doctrines. First, we have made clear that there may be more than one link in a causal chain and there may be more than one tortfeasor liable, jointly or individually, for a plaintiff's injuries. "[T]he mere fact that other persons share some responsibility for [the] plaintiff's harm does not absolve [a] defendant from liability." Thus, the fact that the third-party content creators may bear responsibility for the harm does not mean that defendants bear none.

Recent findings suggest that second, a defect need only be a "substantial cause"—not the sole cause—"of the events which produced the injury." Substantial means more than slight or trivial. Plaintiffs' allegations that defendants' product adversely affected the shooter's mental health and behavior, making him a compulsive social media user, with the attendant negative impact on his mind and body, isolated from others, rendering him more susceptible to commit this horrific violent act, easily satisfies the "substantial cause" standard.

Third, not all intervening acts break the causal chain. An intervening act only breaks the causal nexus if it is extraordinary under the circumstances, unforeseeable in the normal course of events, or independent of and far removed from the defendant's conduct. That determination depends on whether the intervening act and its consequences were the foreseeable result of a defendant's tortious conduct. Even the "intentional" or "criminal" intervening acts of a third party can be reasonably foreseeable….

[W]hile here, the harm arose from exposure to graphic violence and white supremacist propaganda, plaintiffs allege that the algorithm is content-agnostic. According to plaintiffs, defendants' products and platforms rely on these complex algorithms to keep consumers engaged, which negatively impacts young people's mental health, and, in turn, increases the tendency to violent behavior, all the while driving revenue for these defendants. Thus, the majority's assertion that the shooter's actions were not foreseeable is nothing more than a factual conclusion, but that determination cannot be made by the Court on a motion to dismiss….
