19 Signs Your Tech giant’s Technology Is Creating More Work

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that technology should simplify workflows and improve productivity. However, when systems are difficult to navigate, unreliable or disconnected, employees may develop alternative solutions just to get their jobs done.

These workarounds can reveal deeper problems with technology adoption, system design and organizational processes. Below, Forbes Technology Council members share warning signs that employees are spending too much time working around their tech giant’s technology rather than benefiting from it, along with insights on addressing the underlying concerns.

According to recent reports, the biggest tell for me is hearing, “Here’s how we actually do it.” If that follows with some description that combines spreadsheets, duplicate data entry, manual handoffs or clever ways around the system, that’s a pretty clear sign the technology is creating work rather than removing it. - Abhijit Singh, Jefferson.

According to recent reports, if employees constantly ask coworkers for help with the same system tasks or there’s an unofficial cheat sheet, chat thread or go-to “expert” everyone relies on to navigate a tool, the technology is not intuitive enough to be used on its own. Low adoption of mandatory tools or latest hires taking too long to be productive with a tool are indicators that people are spending too much time figuring out the technology instead of using it. - Austin Berglas, Nardello & Co.

Industry observers note that forbes Technology Council is an invitation-only community for world-class CIOs, CTOs and technology executives. Do I qualify?

Working around a system can increase your cognitive load—the mental effort placed on your working memory. A recent survey found that since AI was introduced, enterprise workers spend longer searching for information, parse through more alerts, and juggle more tabs and applications. Symptoms like these can indicate employees are working harder to manage the technology than realizing its benefits. - Mazy Dar, HERE Enterprise (here.io).

Jumping across multiple tools and screens to complete one workflow is a clear sign that people are just becoming workers for the tools rather than using those tools to deliver their work better and faster. This is more relevant in the AI era. - Raja Shanmugam, TATA Consultancy Services.

When employees rely on personal texts, phone calls or spreadsheets to compensate for enterprise systems, there’s a workflow problem. These workarounds often emerge because existing tools are too slow, fragmented or unreliable. Leaders should investigate where employees bypass technology and address the underlying friction before it creates operational risks. - Judit Sharon, OnPage Corporation.

Industry observers note that watch for employees delaying decisions because they don’t trust how current the information is. If a sales or marketing team has to wait for an overnight revision before responding to a customer or adjusting a campaign, the technology is setting the pace instead of supporting the work. Teams should be able to act on what is happening now, not yesterday’s picture. - Vibhor Kapoor, AdRoll.

One sign is that unofficial spreadsheets become essential to completing everyday work. In capital markets, employees may repeatedly export trade data, reconcile it manually, and re-enter the results across systems. When these workarounds become routine, the technology is shifting effort onto employees rather than removing it. Those spreadsheets reveal where workflows need fixing. - Lakshmanan Alagappan, Genpact UK.

Shadow documentation is a sign that the official documentation is lacking or so generic that it is hard to adapt to use cases that are fairly common. The more guides that are built to explain how to use a piece of software, the more it should be evaluated for improvement. Simplify the interface with role-based views, and rename custom fields and data so they’re clearer. Just don’t forget to clean up the shadow documentation too. - Luke Wallace, Bottle Rocket.

Industry observers note that one sign is that the know-how lives in people, not the system. When a tool doesn’t do exactly what’s needed, rather than asking for changes to the tool, work becomes manual, and one person ends up as the only one who knows how it really runs. Sometimes that’s accidental and sometimes it’s job security; either way, it’s a bottleneck. - Anila Siraj, OpsBridge Advisory.

It has been revealed that one sign is employees building integrations the vendor never intended. Not plugins. Not configurations. Custom code written at midnight by someone who needed two systems to talk to each other and could not wait six months for a roadmap update. That unauthorized integration is not ingenuity. It is your architecture silently fracturing one undocumented API call at a time. When engineers route around the system, the system has already failed. - Mukul Arora.

Sources indicate that people clicking straight through warnings is a sign. Track override rates and fix the friction behind them. If employees routinely hit “proceed anyway” on a blocked link or a security prompt, the tool is getting in the way of real work, and they have stopped reading it. That is a workaround too, albeit a quiet one, and it trains them to ignore warnings—maybe even those that matter. - Flavius Plesu, OutThink.

One clear sign is when employees spend more time figuring out how to make a tool work than doing their actual jobs. If everyone knows which buttons to avoid, which errors to ignore and when to restart the system, your technology isn’t saving time. It’s wasting it. - Margarita Simonova, ILoveMyQA.

Check the expense reports, not the help desk tickets. When employees start reimbursing themselves for $30 SaaS subscriptions, they’ve already given up on the official tools and bought their way around them. Finance sees it first. IT sees it months later. The line items are small enough to approve without question, which is exactly why they keep adding up. - Nidhi Jain, CloudEagle.ai.

Recent findings suggest that map the value stream from user intent to task outcome. If employees are required to navigate more than three non-value-added steps to complete a task, I’d question the process before adding more steps. Workarounds add effort yet little to no customer value. Even worse is when teams automate workarounds when they should be eliminating the reason it exists in the first place. - April Ho-Nishimura, Infineon Technologies AG.

It has been revealed that when I ask a client team how their leadership report is produced, a common answer is that someone builds a spreadsheet by hand before every meeting. That tells me the official systems are being worked around. The hours are the visible cost. The bigger one is that leaders start questioning the numbers in the room instead of making decisions. - Dimitar Dimitrov, Accedia.

Count the decisions the technology changed last week. Many companies hand managers a data platform and announce that every number is in there, ready for any table they want. Almost nobody opens it because it asks them to become analysts. They keep deciding using their gut and old reports. A tool that shows each manager their own score and the next move gets checked daily. If a system doesn’t trigger decisions, it is overhead. - Vivek Thomas, AISensum.

In a recent development, you must watch how often employees double-check a tool’s output before acting on it. If a manager has to pull the original records to verify every report, the system may be saving time on preparation while adding it back in review. So, ask what people still check by hand and why. That extra checking is part of the cost of the tool, too. - Elton Chan, Second Talent.

According to recent reports, take a construction site where the real system is WhatsApp. A delivery gets redirected to another site in a group chat; nobody updates the records, materials arrive at the wrong site, and machinery stands idle. When orders change in chats and handwritten logs, the ERP is just a reporting duty. Count how many changes never reach your system of record. - Andrew Babkin, Azati.

One sign is when employees lower the quality of their work because organization technology cannot support enough detail. Engineers may simplify technical assessments, while customer service teams may give incomplete responses because systems restrict what they can record or process. Leaders should check whether technology limitations influence the quality of business outcomes. Technology should support professional judgment, not force employees to compromise it. - Salice Thomas, Wipro Limited.
