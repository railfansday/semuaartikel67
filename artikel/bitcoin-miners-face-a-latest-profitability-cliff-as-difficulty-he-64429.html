Bitcoin miners face a latest profitability cliff as difficulty heads for an 8.5% jump

Industry observers note that bitcoin miners’ expected dollar revenue for the same computing power fell about 6% from Nov. 16 to Nov. 20 in two readings of Luxor’s revenue index.

A difficulty cut had lifted expected Bitcoin earnings earlier in the month, but with the next adjustment now tracking higher, less efficient machines could soon need cheaper electricity to cover their power bills.

Simple Mining’s Nov. 20 reading of Luxor’s hash price index put gross mining revenue at $28.58 per petahash per second (PH/s) per day, with Luxor’s Nov. 16 roundup listing $30.45.

To estimate a machine’s gross daily revenue, multiply the hash price by the machine’s computing power in PH/s. One PH/s equals 1,000 terahashes per second.

Sources indicate that the slide also appeared in CloudMineCrypto’s daily series, which estimates revenue from Bitcoin blocks. In its partial Nov. 20 reading, dollar hash price had fallen to about $28.90 from $30.30 per PH/s per day on Nov. 16, while the coin rate held near 0.00047 BTC.

The paired readings explain why a miner can earn roughly the same amount of Bitcoin for a day of work and still have fewer dollars to pay an electricity bill.

Bitcoin’s Nov. 14 adjustment lowered difficulty by 9.13%, from 148.36 trillion to 134.82 trillion. Holding the reward per block steady, the same amount of work could then earn about 10% more Bitcoin. Luxor’s Nov. 16 roundup found that the BTC hash price had risen from 0.00042763 a week earlier to 0.00046839 BTC per PH/s per day.

Over the same period, the dollar hash price fell from $31.95 to $30.45.

It has been revealed that the expected coin payout depends on the block subsidy, transaction fees, and how hard it is to find a block. The dollar payout also depends on Bitcoin’s price, since fees accounted for only a small fraction of block rewards in both data sets.

Sources indicate that luxor reported a weekly average of 0.0229 BTC in fees per block on Nov. 16, and the partial Nov. 20 data imply about 0.022 BTC in fees on top of the 3.125 BTC subsidy.

Sources indicate that as of Nov. 20, MineOrNot counted 1,008 blocks, halfway to the next adjustment. Blocks were arriving about every nine minutes and 14 seconds, compared with Bitcoin’s ten-minute target.

If that pace persists, MineOrNot estimated difficulty would rise 8.47% to about 146.24 trillion around Nov. 27.

If Bitcoin’s price, the subsidy, and average fees per block stay fixed, an 8.47% rise in difficulty would lower the $28.58 benchmark to about $26.35 per PH/s per day. A 10% increase would put it near $25.98 and bring difficulty almost back to its pre-cut level.

For a machine using 30 joules per terahash, the observed benchmark translates to a power-only break-even rate of 3.97 cents per kilowatt-hour.

According to recent reports, with an 8.47% increase in difficulty, the same machine’s ceiling would fall to 3.66 cents. A machine using 13.5 joules per terahash can absorb higher electricity rates, with its ceiling falling from 8.82 cents to 8.13 cents under the same assumptions.

Gross mining revenue can cover a lower electricity rate if difficulty rises 8.47%, even with Bitcoin’s price and block rewards held fixed.

At a hypothetical 3.8¢/kWh, a 30 J/TH rig would stop covering its power bill if difficulty rises 8.47%. The 13.5 J/TH rig would still cover electricity.

In a recent development, use the November 20 benchmark of $28.58 per PH/s per day. Model the retarget by dividing by 1.0847. At 1 PH/s, the 30 and 13.5 J/TH rigs draw 30 and 13.5 kW. Divide daily gross revenue by 24 times power in kW, multiply by 100, and round to 0.01 cent per kWh.

Electricity-only model. Excludes pool fees, hosting, hardware and other costs.

At a hypothetical electricity rate of 3.8 cents per kWh, the less efficient machine would just cover its power bill at the Nov. 20 benchmark. At that same rate, it would fall short after an 8.47% increase in difficulty. The more efficient machine would cover electricity at that rate in both cases.

The calculation divides gross daily revenue by daily electricity use. A miner at the ceiling has already used all its expected revenue on electricity. Pool fees and any separate hosting charges can lower the rate it can afford, and the operator’s contract and downtime will shape its actual costs.
