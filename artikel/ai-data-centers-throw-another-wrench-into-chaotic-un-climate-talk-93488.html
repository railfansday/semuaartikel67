The clean energy revolution hinges on electrifying the entire global economy just as power-hungry data centers are surging. It’s a challenge delegates from all over the world had to grapple with during the United Nations climate conference, called COP31, that came to a chaotic and unsatisfying close in Antalya, Turkey, on Sunday.

Recent findings suggest that each year, attendees try to make good on the commitment to stop climate change as part of the 2015 Paris climate accord. Preventing extreme weather from growing more terrifying, coastal communities from flooding, and droughts from wrecking food and water systems requires slashing greenhouse gas emissions. But in order to power homes or cars with solar and wind instead of oil and gas, they need to go electric.

“Every climate minister in the world is thinking about this”.

Recent findings suggest that to that end, one of the most anticipated outcomes of this year’s negotiations was a deal to raise electricity’s share of global energy consumption to 35 percent by 2035, compared to about 20 percent today. That becomes harder to do, however, when data centers are causing electricity demand to skyrocket. That made the rush of new AI data centers a climate glitch alongside talks on how to transition from fossil fuels to renewable energy.

According to recent reports, “Every climate minister in the world is thinking about this, and it will inevitably come up in the discussions. It’s part of the broader energy transformation,” Chris Bowen, Australia’s minister for climate change and energy and the president of COP31 climate negotiations, told Politico in an interview at the start of the COP31 summit.

In a recent development, despite becoming the longest COP yet, with debates lasting two days longer than scheduled, official negotiations fell apart spectacularly. Delegates ultimately failed to garner enough support to pass the “pact” they’d been bargaining on for two weeks. The EU, UK, and several smaller nations most vulnerable to climate change rejected the deal that they saw as too weak. The pact omitted the term “fossil fuels” despite many nations calling for stronger commitments to phase out oil, coal, and gas. And the US, the epicenter of the data center buildout and the biggest climate polluter historically, was a no-show after the pro-fossil-fuel Trump administration pulled the country out of the Paris accord (twice).

With official negotiations falling apart, and countries only making voluntary commitments on AI and electrification, some environmental and humanitarian groups called this year “the worst COP” ever.

To meet the Paris goals, nations will need to cut their greenhouse gas emissions roughly in half by 2030 before reaching net zero by the middle of the century. Many tech companies made similar commitments in recent years, including Google, Microsoft, and Meta. But thanks to the AI boom, those goals have fallen by the wayside.

In a recent development, meta is “re-evaluating” its timeline to reach net zero emissions as it focuses on AI, the tech giant noted in its annual sustainability report on Thursday. Meta reported a 45 percent year-over-year increase in climate pollution. Google’s and Microsoft’s carbon footprints are also growing despite climate commitments the companies have made. Data centers’ electricity consumption is forecast to double by 2030, according to a forecast by the International Energy Agency.

In a recent development, “That is a huge climate risk: rising power demand from data centres is not yet sustainable, and an AI boom cannot be allowed to become a fossil fuel boom,” Bruce Douglas, CEO of the Global Renewables Alliance, tells The Verge in an email. Data centers were “a key topic” at COP31, according to Douglas, whose organization cohosted a panel on the challenges during the event.

In the US, the country with the most data centers, power sector emissions could increase 42 percent by 2030 as energy companies build more fossil fuel infrastructure to meet data center electricity demand, according to a recent analysis by the Sierra Club. Already, hyperscale data centers in the US create more climate pollution than the country of Denmark, a separate study by Harvard researchers recently found.

It has been revealed that to stop data centers from creating more pollution, they would need to be powered by additional new sources of renewable energy, Douglas notes. He adds that data centers are currently competing with everyone else for grid connections, finance, and electricity generation capacity — and against arguably more essential tasks like heating and air conditioning, as well as electrifying transportation.

According to recent reports, many tech companies match their electricity use with renewable energy purchases. But unless they’re helping to build latest sources of green energy to meet their added electricity demand, they might just end up hogging limited renewable energy capacity. And if more fossil fuels make up the difference, then greenhouse gas emissions continue to rise overall.

“Should newly introduced green electrons be used for air-conditioning, electrification or data centres?”.

According to recent reports, “In many cases, datacentres are outbidding other consumers to get speed to power,” Douglas says. “Should new green electrons be used for air-conditioning, electrification or data centres?”.

According to recent reports, there was some progress. Around 80 nations signed onto a voluntary pledge to increase electricity’s share of energy consumption to 35 percent over the next decade, according to COP31 president Murat Kurum. The list of countries that includes, however, hasn’t been published yet, Carbon Brief reports.

Industry observers note that another voluntary pledge to keep AI from derailing climate action by calling on data centers to run on less polluting energy also failed to garner much support. Douglas notes that by using the term “low-emission energy,” the pledge still leaves the door open for natural gas (which produces less carbon pollution than coal when burned but is a significant source of methane pollution) to keep powering data centers.

Sources indicate that “The Antalya A.I. pledge is a step in the right direction to address an vital piece of the impacts, but needs teeth in order to translate into stronger protections for people and the planet,” the nonprofit Natural Resources Defense Council mentioned in a statement when the pledge was first revealed. “The speed, scale and scope of this challenge is bigger than perhaps anything before it, and the guardrails must be commensurate.”.
