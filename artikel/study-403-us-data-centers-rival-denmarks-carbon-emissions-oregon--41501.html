Study: 403 U.S. data centers rival Denmark’s carbon emissions; Oregon ranked No. 2 in power use

According to recent reports, A Harvard-led study estimates that electricity use at 403 U.S. hyperscale data centers was equal to roughly 25.7 million metric tons of carbon dioxide emissions — a volume roughly comparable to Denmark’s carbon footprint.

Industry observers note that the research examined some of the nation’s largest and most powerful data center facilities operating between May 2024 and April 2025. Together, those facilities accounted for an estimated 1.8% of total U.S. electricity consumption.

“What we’re seeing is already striking, but it’s only a snapshot of a rapidly expanding industry,” Gianluca Guidi, the study’s lead author and a postdoctoral fellow in the Department of Biostatistics at the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health, noted in a statement.

In a recent development, guidi noted that the data center boom has continued since the study period ended. “That means the electricity use and carbon footprint we’re reporting could already be substantially larger now in 2026.”.

The research, published in the journal Nature Sustainability and explored in an associated website, comes amid growing pushback and concerns about the facilities’ impacts on electric bills, water consumption, noise and carbon emissions.

Sources indicate that in the Pacific Northwest, government officials are enacting moratoriums and exploring legislation to regulate data center development. Oregon Gov. Tina Kotek in October directed a state agency to pause processing and issuing permits for latest and expanding data centers until July 2027. In Washington, state Rep. Beth Doglio, D-Olympia, will propose data center rules in the legislative session that starts in January.

Hyperscale sites handle massive computing workloads and data storage, supporting operations including large language model training and AI deployment.

The researchers found that Oregon ranked second in electricity used by hyperscale data centers in the study, consuming about half as much as those in Virginia. Given Oregon’s access to clean hydropower, the state ranked sixth in carbon dioxide emissions attributed to the facilities’ electricity use. The publication highlighted data from the top 10 states for energy use, which does not include Washington.

Nationwide, roughly 54% of the electricity attributed to the facilities came from fossil fuels, primarily natural gas and coal. The estimates were based on regional power generation mixes, not individual operators’ clean energy purchases.

The study did not break out energy use by data center operator. Amazon, Microsoft and Google have the largest data center footprints worldwide and account for 57% of hyperscale data center capacity, according to Synergy Research Group.

All three have aggressive carbon reduction targets. While their emissions have all increased in recent years, the companies say they remain committed to their goals. Microsoft and Amazon point to their support for newly introduced clean power projects and investments in low-carbon building materials for the facilities as positive steps towards reductions.

“We continue to really be focused around carbon negativity by 2030,” Melanie Nakagawa, Microsoft’s chief sustainability officer, told GeekWire in July.

Kara Hurst, Amazon’s chief sustainability officer, acknowledged that it will be difficult for the company to reach its target of net-zero emissions by 2040.

“So that is a real challenge,” Hurst said in September, “and we’re here to meet the moment. I’m not saying that it’s going to be easy, and I’m not saying it’s going to be linear. But we’re going to put everything we have behind that.”.

At the same time, both tech giants are pursuing projects involving fossil fuel energy in Texas. Microsoft in June signed a 20-year deal to purchase power from a natural gas facility slated to come online in 2028. Amazon has agreed to become the anchor tenant of a massive planned data center campus with proposed natural gas and nuclear power generation, according to the Financial Times.

“Assessing the Carbon Emissions and Energy Consumption of US Hyperscale Data Centers” published in Nature Sustainability and researchers from the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health, University of Pisa, IMT School of Advanced Studies, Aurora Energy Research, Environmental Systems Research Institute, Baxtel and UCLA Fielding School of Public Health.
