November 13: Spot Mirach’s Ghost.

In a recent development, following inferior conjunction early this month, Mercury has reappeared in the morning sky. Now magnitude 0.3, you can find the small planet some 5° above the eastern horizon an hour before sunrise. It lies to the lower left of brighter Venus (magnitude –4.8), which sits alongside Virgo’s luminary Spica, about 12° to Mercury’s west.

This morning, Mercury is less than 1° south of 4th-magnitude Kappa (κ) Virginis, which should be visible until the sky brightens enough to wash out the star. You can capture both in the same field of view in either binoculars or a telescope. Mercury now appears as a 32%-lit disk that is 8” across. Compare that with Venus, which features a narrower phase just 13% lit but stretches 51” by comparison. The brighter planet lies just 1.4° northwest of Spica, the brightest star in Virgo.

Recent findings suggest that both Spica and Kappa can act as guideposts to help you spot Venus’ and Mercury’s motion, respectively, over the next few days. Mercury is moving east and will pull away from Kappa to the lower left. Venus, which just recently reached a stationary point, is also moving east, but will swing slightly northeast (to the upper left) of Spica for a few days before tracking more directly east.

According to recent reports, mars passes 1.2° north of Jupiter at 10 P.M. EST — we’ll focus on this pair in the early-morning sky tomorrow.

According to recent reports, jupiter and Mars are approaching opposition in February next year. They are now rising late in the evening and dominate the early-morning sky. By 3 A.M. local time on the 15th, the two planets are 40° high in the east. Mars sits just to the upper left (north) of Jupiter, having passed 1.2° due north of the gas giant late last night, shortly before rising. Both planets sit to the upper right (west) of Regulus, the brightest star in Leo the Lion.

In addition to spotting the pair of planets together in a telescope, you’ll want to zoom in on Jupiter in particular, as its Galilean moons are showing off this morning. Around 3 A.M. EST, Callisto stands alone west of the planet, with Io (closest) and Ganymede to the east. Twenty-two minutes later (at 3:22 A.M. EST), Io’s shadow appears at the eastern limb and begins to transit. Just over an hour later, with Io closing in on the same limb, Europa pops into view as it reappears from an occultation at 4:27 A.M. EST. It is just south of Io, which passes due north of Europa at 4:34 A.M. EST. Io then begins a transit two minutes later, slowly following its shadow. Io’s shadow reaches the western limb and disappears at 5:38 A.M. EST, while the moon takes a little over an hour to reach the same point, ending its transit at 5:52 A.M. CST (now daylight in the Eastern time zone).
