Russia allows Bitcoin trading as sanctioned Moscow Exchange prepares December launch

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that from Nov. 22, Russian exchanges can begin trading Bitcoin once the central bank approves the rules, but customers still can't use it to pay for anything.

It has been revealed that moscow Exchange plans a Dec. 1 pilot through a US-sanctioned clearinghouse, with ordinary investors limited to 300,000 rubles in annual purchases per intermediary.

Sources indicate that the central bank's retail purchase rules took effect Nov. 17, limiting ordinary investors to Bitcoin, Ethereum and the dollar-linked stablecoin USDT. A separate ordinance governing exchange operations allows venues to begin trading from Nov. 22, provided the regulator has approved their trading rules.

Moscow Exchange aims to launch on Dec. 1, starting with pilot transactions as participating firms connect. The newly introduced market will let investors buy crypto through familiar financial intermediaries while prohibiting its use in everyday domestic payments.

The allowance covers purchases through a single intermediary during a calendar year.

If you buy 100,000 rubles' worth of Bitcoin and another 100,000 rubles' worth of Ethereum (about $1,200) through the same broker, you've used two-thirds of the allowance, regardless of what the coins are worth a week later.

Purchases can also go through asset managers and registered crypto exchange offices, including deals arranged outside an exchange's order book. The limit is measured in rubles spent on purchases, rather than the market value of the investor's holdings.

Qualified investors can buy other coins without the same purchase cap, although they must pass a test and review the risks before trading, just as ordinary investors do.

The central bank chose Bitcoin, Ethereum, and USDT for the retail list partly because they're heavily traded and have at least five years of price history on foreign exchanges.

Exchanges must register the companies trading there, track orders and completed transactions, calculate market prices, and report activity to the central bank.

It has been revealed that russia's fresh crypto law also provides for digital depositories that record asset ownership rights. They are part of the exchange and custody framework the new market will need once trading begins.

Recent findings suggest that owning Bitcoin through that system still won't let someone spend it at a Russian shop. Domestic merchants remain barred from accepting cryptocurrency for goods and services.

Sources indicate that exporters and importers can use it under a separate provision for cross-border settlement. A broker can help a customer exchange rubles for Bitcoin, and an exporter can receive Bitcoin for a shipment abroad, but a restaurant in Moscow still has to charge for lunch in something else.

According to recent reports, moscow Exchange already has a market for investors who want exposure to Bitcoin's price. By mid-September, it reported more than 600 billion rubles in crypto-futures turnover since its first contracts began trading in the summer of 2025.

Those cash-settled contracts pay gains or losses in rubles as crypto prices move, without delivering the underlying assets. The December pilot would trade the assets themselves, so the futures figure tells us little about how much Bitcoin investors will want to buy and hold through the fresh system.

The exchange's technical specification describes same-day and next-day settlement, while the National Clearing Centre's published market plan lists five trading pairs: Bitcoin, Ethereum, and USDT against rubles, plus Bitcoin and USDT against USDX, a specialized settlement currency used by the clearinghouse.

The exchange isn't planning direct BTC/USDT trading for the initial launch, although it may consider crypto-to-crypto pairs in 2027.

Members will be able to place anonymous orders or negotiate deals with known counterparties, and the clearinghouse expects to process settlements roughly every 30 to 60 minutes.

Short selling will initially be prohibited on the exchange, although brokers may offer margin trading under the applicable rules.

One large deal arranged in advance could lift the day's turnover without creating the steady stream of buyers and sellers that an ordinary investor needs.

It has been revealed that the clearinghouse's FAQ also explains how the cryptocurrency will move once a trade is completed. Settlement will take place through accounts maintained at a separate digital depository outside the Moscow Exchange group, without a corresponding blockchain transaction for every trade.

When one participant buys Bitcoin from another, the depository can revision its records of who holds the rights to those assets while the underlying coins stay in custody. That allows the exchange to process trades without sending Bitcoin between public wallets every time an order is matched.

The arrangement makes the depository's identity and the rules for moving assets into and out of its custody particularly crucial. Moscow Exchange has described the proposed account structure, but the published materials reviewed don't identify a separate depository or confirm whether customers can withdraw their holdings directly to personal wallets.

The market is initially intended for existing qualified trading and clearing participants, with brokers responsible for determining which customers can use their services.

Moscow Exchange says it will use the National Clearing Centre to clear the trades, relying on infrastructure already familiar to its stock-market participants.

Sources indicate that the US Treasury sanctioned both Moscow Exchange and the National Clearing Centre in June 2024. That adds a complication for the planned USDT market, which the exchange intends to introduce initially through a pilot with restricted trading volumes.

According to recent reports, the technical specification identifies USDT as issued on Ethereum, meaning a company outside Russia controls the underlying token. Tether has a policy for freezing wallets associated with people or entities on US sanctions lists, although that policy alone doesn't establish what will happen to any particular wallet used by Moscow Exchange or its clearing and custody partners.

Settling individual trades through depository accounts reduces the need to move tokens on a public blockchain, but the underlying USDT still has to be held somewhere.

The exchange hasn't identified the wallets that will hold those tokens, explained who will supply them, or described how Tether's sanctions controls would apply to the custody arrangement.

No freeze connected to the proposed pilot has been verified, and the available documents don't establish whether Tether has reviewed the arrangement.

The first trades in December may be deals clients already make elsewhere, brought onto Moscow Exchange's books.

If the pilot proceeds, repeat trading and the ability to buy or sell near the quoted price will tell us whether a usable market is taking shape.

For USDT, the exchange and its partners will also have to show they can hold the underlying tokens and complete settlement through the proposed depository, with the issuer's sanctions policy still unresolved.
