The oil painting, which was estimated to carry a value of $180m (Â£136m) prior to the sale, was put up for auction as part of a set called the Blaquier-Arrieta Collection.

Sotheby's declared in the hours before the auction that it had resolved a dispute between the Blaquier-Arrieta family and the heirs of the Fischer family - the prior German owners of the work - regarding the sale.

Representatives acting for heirs of the Fischer family had claimed the painting may have been sold under duress during the rise of the Nazis.

A lawyer for the family's heirs reportedly wrote to Sotheby's on their behalf, telling the auction house that the painting was sold in Amsterdam before World War Two because of financial losses the Fischer family's publishing firm suffered under the Nazis. The BBC has contacted the lawyer for comment.

"In recognition of the painting's complex Nazi-era history and the suffering endured by the Fischer family during the Holocaust, the two families have reached a mutually satisfactory resolution and the work will be sold pursuant to their agreement," Sotheby's said on Wednesday.

Sotheby's website details the provenance of the oil painting and how it has researched the artwork changing hands since 1890.

It notes Samuel Fischer - a famous German publisher - acquired it in 1909 before it was passed down in his family after his death in 1934.

The painting was deposited with Paul Cassirer in Amsterdam in 1932 or 1933 before it went to Henriette and Marcel Kapferer in Paris in 1936, Sotheby's noted.

Four other owners then had the painting.

The Blaquier family acquired it in 1975 and owned it for more than half a century before the latest sale, the auction house stated.
