Christina Henry on translating the unexpected horrors of motherhood into fiction

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

We all have a picture in our head of a mother—what we think a mother should be or shouldn’t be, what ideal motherhood looks like. Society creates expectations for mothers, and mothers who color outside those lines are often censured by those around them.

But being a mother, or a stepmother, or a mother figure, is hard. If you carry a child you might think that pregnancy will be nine months of glowing, radiant bliss (that’s what all those parenting influencers say on TikTok, don’t they?) Instead, you’re coping with swollen feet and sciatica, and the constant worry that you might do something that will accidentally harm your growing baby.

In a recent development, once your child is born you might think, Great, it’s going to be nothing but sunshine and roses from here on out. But parenting isn’t just drippy ice cream cones on the beach and smiling while riding carousels. There are so many things you can’t anticipate—unexpected illnesses, trouble at school, headstrong kids who simply want their own way.

Industry observers note that the truth of the matter that having a child and raising a child are often some of the most anxiety-riddled years of a person’s life. Even when nothing’s going wrong, a mother can often feel like something just might go wrong. The worry they are feeling is somehow a hedge against bad things happening—parental anxiety as a shield against actual harm.

According to recent reports, fictional mothers, of course, get the worst of it. It’s bad enough that real-world mothers have to cope with spiking fevers and school permission forms and judgmental in-laws. But what if you desperately want a child but it turns out your baby was also, say, the son of Satan? Or what if you love your children with all your heart but you keep hearing things, seeing things that you aren’t sure are actually there? What if the people around you aren’t to be trusted? What if you feel like you can’t trust yourself?

In a recent development, everyone knows about Rosemary, right? Poor Rosemary Woodhouse, whose gaslighting jerk of a husband, Guy, trades her womb for a successful career.

In Ira Levin’s classic novel of motherhood horror, Rosemary’s Baby, Rosemary is a cheery newlywed who wants to have a child. Guy is a struggling actor who keeps just missing his big break and wants to wait a little longer to start a family. A bit of timely luck allows them to sign a lease in an older building called the Bramford, a place that’s notoriously difficult to get into. Rosemary is thrilled that, at least, she will have the decoration of a fresh place to keep her busy, but she still wishes for a baby.

In a recent development, an older couple, Roman and Minnie Castavet, live in the next apartment. Rosemary finds them nosy and annoying, but for some reason Guy likes them and starts to visit regularly. Then, a miracle happens. The actor who cast in a role that Guy almost got is suddenly struck blind (a miracle for Guy, of course, not so much for the other actor) and Guy lands a role that can make his career. Soon after this unexpectedly fortuitous occurrence, he decides that he does, in fact, want a baby.

Shortly after, Rosemary has a terrible dream—a dream about a beast who rapes her while several people look on. And then, the second miracle happens. Rosemary is pregnant. Except her pregnancy doesn’t feel right, and everyone around her keeps telling her it’s fine.

Industry observers note that rosemary’s journey is so harrowing because no matter what she says or does or even knows with absolute certainty, no one will believe her. If you’ve never read this one it’s time to give it a try.

Kit Burgoyne’s The Captive is a clever play on the I’ve-been-impregnated-by-Satan-against-my-will story, or in this case, by an entity alternately referred to as “The Long-Before” or “Him.” An underground revolutionary group that call themselves the Nail decide to kidnap Adeline Woolsaw, the wealthy heiress of a vast network of connected businesses, in hopes of exposing the Woolsaws and their corruption to the public. The only time Adeline ever seems to leave the vast estate where she lives is to go to the doctor’s office, so the group decides to grab her as she leaves her appointment.

Unfortunately for the Nail, Adeline is pregnant. Even more unfortunately, Adeline is going into labor in the van. Just to add to their confusion, Adeline most definitely does not want to return home to her parents. It seems that her parents worship this evil entity and arranged to have Adeline carry His child in order to establish total world domination.

This pitch-black story skewers the cultists, the radical revolutionaries and the oligarchs that run everything equally and mashes it all up into a super-fun (and frequently gory) thriller.

It has been revealed that helen Phillip’s The Need is probably one of my favorite novels of the last ten years. I’ve never read another book that so accurately embodies both the intense love of a mother for her children and also the profound, dragging monotony of the daily tasks that make up her day.

Molly is a mom of two and a paleobotanist, alone with the kids while her partner travels. She is exhausted, exhausted in that bone-deep way parents often are, feeling like she wants to stay on top of everything but she simply cannot.

In a recent development, when she hears footsteps in her home she thinks she’s imagining them. She’s anxious about a potential home invasion. She can’t trust herself. She’s just sleep-deprived. But then she comes face to face with an intruder wearing a mask, and the reality of Molly’s world tilts precariously on its side.

The less you know about this one, the better. It unfolds like a strange and fascinating puzzle box, revealing layers as it goes.

Industry observers note that neena Viel’s debut Listen to Your Sister isn’t about a mother, per se. But it is about Calla Williams, a twenty-five-year-old woman just trying to keep it together, who reluctantly agrees to become the guardian to her sixteen-year-old brother Jamie. Jamie, like most 16-year-olds, is not interested in his sister’s authority, or any kind of authority, really. Their brother Dre agreed to help Calla with Jamie, but he has his own struggles and isn’t exactly present.

It has been revealed that calla keeps trying, keeps sacrificing, keeps muddling along trying to do her best like every mother figure. Ever since her brothers were young she’s had visions of their dying in horrible ways, and her nightmares stoke her anxiety over their well-being. But her resentment is growing. No matter how much she loves her brothers, they don’t seem to love her enough to change.

One day Jamie’s behavior goes a little too far and the siblings are forced to flee their homes to hide out at a cabin in the woods. It’s there that Calla’s patience finally snaps, and her brothers discover that the nightmares Calla has had all their lives might just be real.

Sources indicate that neena Viel’s writing is so intense, so profoundly visual, that this book has been indelibly imprinted on my brain. I’m still thinking about it months later.

In my own upcoming novel Pinfeathers, stay-at-home mom Nora Pacetti is tired and frustrated that her husband, Adam, isn’t keeping up his end of the parenting bargain. But those problems seem trivial once Nora discovers a strange object sticking out of her baby’s arm.

Sources indicate that nora thinks it must be a splinter. But when she examines it closely the object seems to have come through her daughter’s skin, not pierced it from the outside. Then Nora’s mother Giulia looks at it and renders her verdict—it’s a pinfeather, a baby feather.

According to recent reports, nora doesn’t know what to believe, and then she finds another one. Does her daughter have a strange disease, or is she actually turning into a bird?

Christina Henry is the author of The Mermaid, Lost Boy, Alice, Red Queen, and the national bestselling Black Wings series featuring Agent of Death Madeline Black and her popcorn-loving gargoyle, Beezle.
