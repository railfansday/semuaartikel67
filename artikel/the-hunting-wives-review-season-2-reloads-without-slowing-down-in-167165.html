Amid all the sex and murder in “The Hunting Wives” Season 2 — and there is a lot of sex and murder — the riskiest scene suspends itself between the two. Sophie (Brittany Snow), an East Coast liberal out of place in the conservative Texas town of Maple Brook, and Margo (Malin Åkerman), the mysterious local matriarch who can wrap just about anyone around her finely manicured fingertips, are confronted with a fresh dilemma. For spoilers sake, I won’t get into specifics, but the concern is urgent and impacts them both, without being serious enough to warrant what turns out to be their first-thought fix:.

In a recent development, in the moment, their lethal instinct seems reasonable. Margo already shot a pregnant teenager in the woods, and Sophie committed vehicular homicide against Margo’s brother (which invited suspicions about Sophie’s past, considering its eerie similarities to a deadly “accident” back in Massachusetts). Their friends, neighbors, and even a youth pastor met their maker, too — just in the first season! So what’s one more body to bury? Go on! Kill ’em! Get yours! But before their bloodlust gets the best of them, their better angels make one last plea. Margo hesitates. Sophie follows suit. The gears start turning as they stare into each other’s wide, worried eyes: Are they really prepared to take another life? Are people that disposable to them? What does their initial response say about how they’ve changed? What does it mean about who they’ve become?

No need to spoil what happens next. The pause makes all the difference. “The Hunting Wives” has always been self-aware, but Margo and Sophie’s recognition of slaughter as their default response to trouble is a critical moment in a second season that reloads without slowing down. Creator Rebecca Cutler knows her Netflix soap is careening past the point where its central couple can reasonably return to lives lived between Margarita Mondays and Sunday church. Killing again, so swiftly and carelessly, puts them on the precipice of becoming Bonnie and Clyde Bonnie — known fugitives who are only out for each other. Taking a beat lends the series a critical perspective — a vantage point from the top of the roller coaster, right before the big drop, where you can appreciate for a few brief, fleeting seconds the insanity to come.

Then you’re flying. You (and Margo and Sophie) are convinced it’s better to just move forward. Heck, this crisis isn’t going to fix itself!

It has been revealed that margo and Sophie’s breakneck pace to break necks is mostly a feature in Netflix’s surprise hit. The high-key camp comedy and low-key conservative satire moves quickly, sure, but where its characters are often flustered and panicked, it’s decisive in a way that exudes confidence. Progress is fast but assured. The drama is elevated but mindful. Characters don’t ask for permission or forgiveness; they just go after what they want, what they need, or what they feel they have to have, creating a propulsive viewing experience that zips by in a (muzzle) flash.

It has been revealed that it’s a welcome change from prestige dramas that regularly feel like they’re stalling for time. While other demonstrates tout firearms around for so long it’s like they’ve never heard of Chekhov, the hunting wives pull the trigger like they’re expecting money to come out — or lovers. Before you can think, “Wait, are they gay, too?” the person in question is already bending their same-sex partner over a desk. Even Season 2’s in-media-res opening lasts only a few seconds — like it’s flipping the bird to streaming TV’s favorite plot device — giving just the briefest glimpse of cop cars racing up to a giant cabin where Margo is (supposedly) being held hostage and Sophie is (supposedly) with her, holding a gun.

The opening tease only gets funnier as you find out how much unfolds between the start of the season and the flash-forward, especially considering its implication — that Sophie would threaten Margo’s life — makes sense from the start. Sophie begins Season 2 trying to repair her marriage to Graham (Evan Jonigkeit) and distance herself from Margo. The former goal sees her going to counseling sessions (where she comically compares her family to a box she wants to be trapped in), running errands, and playing the dutiful housewife.

In a recent development, the latter goal is harder and, arguably, more crucial. Margo doesn’t know her brother is dead. He’s not answering her calls or texts, but that’s hardly unprecedented behavior, so Margo ignores it — until the leader of a motorcycle gang (Dale Dickey!) demonstrates up, claiming Kyle owes her, and if he doesn’t pay, she’ll take what she wants from Margo.

It has been revealed that there’s never a good time for angry bikers to roar up your driveway demanding money, but it’s a particularly bad time for Margo. She’s in the doghouse with Jed (Dermot Mulroney) over the whole “a high schooler got me pregnant and then I had an abortion” matter, but a) that means she’s living in the pool house, not the poor house, and b) he’s really only mad because it’s a potential threat to his run for governor, which is gaining steam. Margo tries calling and texting Sophie, but she’s not answering. She wants to believe she doesn’t need Margo, love Margo, that she maybe even hates Margo. But a few cleverly laced connections make it clear she can’t.

Sources indicate that outside our core couple, Season 2 introduces plenty of plotlines that could pull them together and/or tear them apart. Callie (Jaime Ray Newman) doesn’t see why welcoming a camera crew to film her friends for a reality show (“Married Justice”) could lead to problems, even when the flirty producer warns participants that they should assume they’re “always on camera.” Her husband and co-star, Sheriff Jonny (Branton Box), is just as foolishly carefree, despite Deputy Wanda (Karen Rodriguez) investigating another dead girl in the woods — aka giving Callie a reason to ask questions about the last dead girl in Maple Brook, aka the aforementioned pregnant high schooler Margo killed.

Speaking of people Margo would prefer to forget, her former boy-toy Brad (George Ferrier), is having a hard time at Baylor. He can’t get over the recent murder of his mother, and his lonely boy coping mechanisms (playing acoustic guitar) soon attract the wrong kind of attention: a predatory music mogul named Chase (John Stamos) who promises to make Brad forget all his worries by turning him into a star (right after he loads him up with booze and drugs).

In a recent development, how Cutler shifts the puzzle pieces around to exert pressure proves just as engaging as before. There are plenty of big, loud laughs to go along with a steady supply of sly jokes and juicy melodrama. Anyone tuning in for the soap should be satisfied, just as anyone eager for some edgy satire should find it.

Sources indicate that so long as they’re as open as these characters. “The Hunting Wives” may don MAGA drag to tell its story, but it’s a savage critique of conservative values. There’s Brad’s holier-than-thou father, Reverend Clint (Jason Davis), whose over-reliance on Old Testament discipline masks a hands-off parenting style that feeds his desperate son to the wolves. There’s Jed’s hypocritical right-wing politician, who’s more than OK with a little murder if it helps his campaign (knowing his constituents wouldn’t hold it against him) but can’t abide by his wife’s emotional outbursts in the pursuit of personal independence.

Then there’s the fun fact that everyone is gay (or bi or curious). So much of the show’s tension stems from secret trysts that only need to stay secret because of Maple Brook’s pervasive intolerance. That goes double for Sophie and Margo, whose continued pursuit of their repressed desires manifests in bigger and bigger risks. They get a small taste of what their lives could be like if they could just follow their hearts, sans consequence, forever, and then someone comes along to remind them why they can’t. It so often feels like there’s no real hope for the life they want that they fight like hell for whatever sliver they think they can sustain.

That fight gives the series its heart and its haste, but not everything fares better at a high velocity. By season’s end, Margo and Sophie’s individual interiority is rooted in too many questions and not enough answers, while their relationship feels more symbolic than genuine. Perhaps that’s the point. Perhaps the couple isn’t fated to be together as much as they’re fated to go further alone. Perhaps their relationship is more of a means to an end; a reminder of how far off track they got when they were isolated, and how hard it is to get back on track when the world is working to keep them apart.

According to recent reports, either way, it’s good drama. Either way, they’re going to discover new sides of themselves. Either way, the coaster is already racing ahead. Maybe it jumps the tracks, maybe it doesn’t. Maybe after all that, they really should just keep killing.
