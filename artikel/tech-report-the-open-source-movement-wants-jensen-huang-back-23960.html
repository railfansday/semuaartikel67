📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that on Friday, a petition to reinstate Jensen Huang as CEO of Nvidia began circulating on social media, and by Monday it listed some notable signatories, including Elon Musk and several influential Nvidia researchers. But comb through the more than 115,000 names and you’ll notice a significant faction of Huang’s supporters—higher-ups from Hugging Face, a hub for sharing AI models, and venture capital firms Andreessen Horowitz and Sequoia Capital—come from a particular slice of the tech world.

According to recent reports, these names have a vested interest in the development of open-source AI, models that, unlike ChatGPT or Claude, can be downloaded and run independent of large tech companies. Supporters of such models say that concentrating AI in the hands of a few powerful companies will leave everyone else worse off (a concern that wouldn’t sound out of place among the AI industry's critics on the left, if so many open-source advocates weren’t also venture capitalists who stand to profit when startups can build on cheaper AI without paying Big Tech).

Recent findings suggest that huang had become a sort of ringleader for those who defend open source to policymakers against concerns that such models come with no guardrails and cede AI leadership to China, where the most powerful and popular open-source models are created. They now want their most influential voice returned to his post.

Industry observers note that but it’s not just Huang’s extramarital affairs that threaten his (and Nvidia’s) credibility as an advocate for open-source AI. Latest research into China’s startups using Nvidia’s chips, and a new rule from the US Department of Commerce announced Monday, could deal a blow to both the movement’s reputation and its technical progress.

According to recent reports, on Thursday, the day Huang resigned, ex-OpenAI researcher Will DePue published findings online suggesting that China’s AI companies had far more access to Nvidia’s advanced Blackwell chips than previously estimated. China is banned from accessing Nvidia’s most cutting-edge chips to help protect America’s AI lead.

It’s unclear how they would have gained access to the chips. DePue claims the results were verified by senior researchers at OpenAI and Anthropic, but an independent analysis has not confirmed the results. (DePue did not respond to a request for an interview. Nvidia did not immediately respond to a request for comment.).

The findings do not establish that Nvidia helped Chinese companies obtain the chips, and the broader problem of chip smuggling was already known to extend well beyond Nvidia’s control.

According to recent reports, but in suggesting that Nvidia may have profited from the rise of Chinese AI startups, whose models are increasingly being used by American companies instead of domestic alternatives, the findings do leave Huang in an awkward position. He has championed open-source AI as a way to strengthen American technological leadership. “Without open source, startups can’t thrive, university researchers can’t do research, you can’t teach AI, scientists can’t use AI,” he mentioned last December during a talk about helping the US win the AI race against China.

Sources indicate that it also makes the idea of reinstating Huang even more of a long shot. US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent even said last week that Nvidia should not consider someone from inside the company to replace him. The upcoming findings lower Huang’s chances further.

What happens next? Nvidia revealed in September it would acquire Hugging Face for nearly $13 billion. Its support for open source also serves a straightforward financial interest: The more businesses that build their own models instead of forking over money to Anthropic and OpenAI, the more chips Nvidia can sell.

It has been revealed that but a new rule from the US Department of Commerce could mean the models popular on Hugging Face will soon stop getting smarter. The rule aims to prevent Chinese companies from training models on Nvidia chips that they can’t own directly, but that they can access remotely in data centers in countries like Malaysia—a workaround that has helped China’s open-source models get so good.

According to recent reports, the open-source world rallied around Huang because of his influence on policymakers. That was vital, since the rise of China’s open-source models has splintered some in Trump’s orbit. If Huang is out, who is next?
