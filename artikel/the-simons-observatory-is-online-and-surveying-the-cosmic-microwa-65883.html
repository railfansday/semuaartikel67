It has been revealed that the Simons Observatory in the Atacama Desert has begun its mission to survey the Cosmic Microwave Background. Its goal is to detect undeniable evidence of the Big Bang and cosmic inflation. Can this unique survey succeed where others have failed?

In a recent development, we live in an age of large astronomical surveys, enabled by powerful computers and data handling methods. While powerful telescopes still perform detailed observations of individual targets, and always will, wide surveys have a power all their own. The ESA's Gaia mission, for example, has completed its survey of billions of stars and generated a massive amount of foundational data that researchers will draw on for decades to come.

There are automated surveys for transients like supernovae and asteroids, radio-sky surveys to spot objects obscured by thick dust, and massive surveys just getting started like the Vera Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) and the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope's three core surveys.

It has been revealed that but another Simons Observatory's survey hasn't garnered the same attention that some of the other have. The Simons Observatory (SO) consists of four telescopes working together: three small 0.5 meter aperture 'scopes in its Small Aperture Telescope (SAT) and one large 6-meter one in its Large Aperture Telescope (LAT).

The unusual configuration gives the SO the best chance to observe tiny details in the Universe's first observable light, the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB).

It has been revealed that the stuff of the Universe was too opaque for light to travel through it at first. Photons were effectively scattered by the hot, dense plasma that permeated the Universe. Eventually conditions changed. The Universe expanded, the plasma cooled, and photons could travel. This was the recombination epoch and it occurred about 380,000 years after the Massive Bang.

Note that the name is historical and reflected our earlier understanding of the Universe. It wasn't actually a recombination, it was the first time that electrons and protons combined to form neutral hydrogen, and that's what made the cosmos transparent. Its name should probably be changed to the combination epoch.

But regardless of its name, the cosmic microwave background is the relic radiation from the Universe's first light, and it holds clues to the Universe, especially to the Big Bang theory of cosmology.

In a recent development, other efforts have been made to map the CMB. NASA's Wilkinson Microwave Anisotropy Probe (WMAP) and the ESA's Planck satellite both made the effort from orbit. Right now, the South Pole Telescope (SPT) and related facilities are doing the same.

The SPT made an important discovery. It detected for the first time what's called the B-mode polarization of the CMB. The B-mode is important because it was created by primordial gravitational waves generated during the epoch of inflation, the first moments of the Universe's inflation. So finding the B-mode was important evidence for the Significant Bang theory.

According to recent reports, but there was a problem. Gravitational lensing can also create B-mode polarization, and it has nothing to do with the Big Bang or inflation. It's difficult to tell which source created the B-mode polarization the SPT detected. So its initial detection was put in doubt.

This is where the Simons Observatory comes into the picture. It's been calibrating its instruments for two years and is now getting down to business.

Sources indicate that the observatory's four telescopes collectively have tens of thousands of microwave detectors, almost 98,000 of them. That's 16 times more than one of its predecessors, the Atacama Cosmology Telescope. They're not only mapping the CMB the same way that WMAP and the Planck satellite did. They're also searching for the tiny fluctuations in the CMB, the B-mode polarizations that can confirm the Major Bang and cosmic inflation. If detected, the SO can determine if these are true artifacts of primordial gravitational waves, or artifacts of the polluting gravitational lensing that confounded the SPT.

The SO isn't isolated and alone in its work. All of these CMB surveys and observatories will work together in the end.
