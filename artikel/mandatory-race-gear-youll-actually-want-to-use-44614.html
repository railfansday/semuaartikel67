Cade Michael spent years as an unsponsored elite trail runner honing in his mandatory race kit with items that he both genuinely enjoys using and can be found affordably. He did this by taking advantage of sales, buying second-hand, and borrowing from friends. Here he shares his tips for building out your own race kit, including his favorite items in his pack.

Mandatory trail running race equipment is a lot like American health insurance: We all hate it. We’re all tempted to skimp on it. And we all inevitably need it when we’re least prepared. Required gear (or most of it, anyway) doesn’t have to be a waste of money or space. After spending yeas as an unsponsored runner curating my mandatory race gear, here’s my guide to getting gear that you’ll actually want to use more than once every 365 days, including my favorite items in my pack.

Recent findings suggest that UTMB, among other races, often has extensive gear requirements and—whether you like to admit it or not—it’s usually for good reason. Weather and conditions can change rapidly in the mountains, and when you’re pushing your body to the extreme for hours (if not days) on end, as a general rule of thumb, it’s better to have it and not need it than to need it and not have it—particularly for race organizers’ liability insurance.

For example, while it may feel ridiculous for a 100K on the notoriously hot Western States 100 course in Auburn, California to make you carry a rain jacket and emergency blanket, the 2025 Canyons 100K  (on the same trails) saw snow at the start and highs around 40 degrees Fahrenheit. Packing three layers, two pairs of pants, and multiple pairs of gloves might seem like overkill for a day spent backcountry skiing in February, much less a running race in late August, but at the 2025 UTMB, ultrarunning’s most iconic race turned into a game of surviving the winter storm-like conditions.

I know all too well the allure of going light. But much like Admiral Ackbar’s epiphany at the Battle of Endor, it’s (usually) a trap. At the 2026 Twisted Fork Trail Festival in Park City, Utah, weather forecasts changed drastically in the 48 hours leading into race day, projecting relentless freezing rain. I chucked on a pair of gloves and arm sleeves and figured I’d warm up plenty as the race went on. As soon as we hit the first descent I lost feeling in my hands, feet, legs, cheeks, and plenty of other places I won’t call by name. My heart rate plummeted and I struggled for the entire remainder of the day. Eventual women’s champion, Rachel Drake, on the other hand, donned a warm base layer, long tights, gloves, and vest loaded with extras. Drake is an MD/PhD, mom, and professional runner for Nike ACG. Be more like Rachel, and less like me.

But you don’t have to hate the gear you’re going to buy one way or another to participate in these events.

Quality, packable gear can not only serve to save your race should conditions take a turn through the night in the Alps, but it can also save your run or, heaven forbid, life, if you’re spending any time out on the trails. While you could probably get away with using children’s XXS sized Walmart rain pants, your niece’s tiny pinky swim goggles, and Amazon’s finest two dollar headlamp, you can also use the mandatory equipment list of your next ultra as an excuse to get quality gear that won’t sit at the bottom of your junk drawer until next summer.

Here are my tips and tricks for getting good, lasting gear that’s a little easier on your bank account than the race entry fees and international travel.

It has been revealed that required kit, in most cases, isn’t going to be the product you wear holes through everyday. One is built to be versatile, lightweight, and packable while the other is made for a specific purpose and durability. Some pieces of equipment, like your rain coat for UTMB, are ones you want to stand up in the most brutal of conditions. Others, like a secondary headlamp, are areas you might be able to save some weight and cash.

It has been revealed that lightweight, packable gear is usually updated every year just like the rest of a brand’s product line and often goes on sale during off-season months or discount days (ahem, Black Friday or REI member days). A lot of years, the only enhancement is the colorway, so just get the highlighter yellow and own it!

Better yet, buy gear second hand. Many of the products I’ll list below I grabbed from resale sites like Poshmark or Mercari for a fraction of the retail price.

According to recent reports, it may seem obvious, but always get your size. It might seem worth it to save a few grams and get a women’s XXS when you’re in the Chamonix Arc’teryx store panic-buying a rain jacket 24 hours before the gun goes off, but I promise you it’s not. Get the extra ounce and future you will be proud of the responsible decision to get something you’ll actually put on your body.

According to recent reports, if you’re questioning whether a piece of gear will satisfy the requirements set by race organizers for your next ultra, err on the side of caution. Get the marginal gains wherever you can within reason, but I promise you trimming two centimeters off your bandaging or emergency blanket will not make Col du Grand Ferret any easier or quicker.

People in your run club and or training partners have probably raced an ultra and used this stuff before. If it’s not something you will need time and time again, it’s OK to borrow it; chances are, they won’t need it in the next week or two anyway. The more you share, the more you save.

It has been revealed that get familiar with your equipment. Try it in training, just like you would a pair of shoes or a nutrition product. Being able to mindlessly locate and use your gear will pay dividends when you have no mindfulness left late into an ultra. Brand-spanking-newly introduced isn’t always best.

It has been revealed that if you’re between two rain jackets, just get the one with the extra zipper. The best gear is the gear you use. Ruth Croft and Tom Evans won a historically cold and wet UTMB in 2026. When they bundled up through the night, I’m certain they were rocking the clothes they trusted to get the job done, not the ones that came in a few grams under. You’re racing an ultra marathon through the mountains, not a 5K parkrun.

Take a few extra minutes to read a few pros and cons before you pull the trigger.

According to recent reports, A lot of thought and stress can go into mandatory equipment, but don’t let it take away from the race experience. At my first UTMB race, UTMB Whistler in Canada, the cold-weather gear policy was implemented two days before the race due to a snowstorm. I’d just come from racing in California and Spain in September, so I didn’t have a suitcase stocked with skimpy mandatory equipment. Luckily for me, I couldn’t afford to grab a bunch of last-second gear and just used what I had with me. Retrospectively, my pack was a little heavier and bulkier than it could have been had I been properly prepared, but I didn’t think about the weight once during the race. But boy, was I glad to have warm gloves and a beanie that fit.

Here’s a list of some of my favorite mandatory equipment I’ve amassed over a couple of years of racing in Chamonix as an unsponsored athlete.

Sources indicate that this one is totally optional and, frankly, probably only applicable for a shorter ultra like MCC or OCC during UTMB week. The mini phone is a great tool to have if you’re only out for a handful of hours. So long as you ensure you get a SIM card that has roaming and data enabled for whatever country or region you will be running through, it can call, text, Whatsapp, and even Strava. UTMB requires you to have a smart phone capable of downloading and using the LiveTrail app, and this Google Play Store enabled pocket rocket is the smallest you can get while satisfying the requirements. It also takes pretty cool retro camcorder-style videos. For the longer races like CCC and UTMB, however, it’s probably your safest bet to just bring along your standard, daily driver smart phone. You have to keep your phone on at all times throughout the race, and the number of charging bricks you’d have to haul to keep this thing running through the night outsizes any weight savings it might provide.

Typically, flasks are flasks. Your running vest will usually come with a couple that fit. That being mentioned, I usually hate running vests. One of the primary reasons is the way traditional flasks sit in the sleeves, bouncing around and uncomfortably rubbing against my collarbone. My pro tip here is to check out flasks with smaller lids like these Hydrapak pocket flasks. Theoretically, they’re made to fit flush with your running belt, but I’ve found they are less bulky and front heavy in a running vest, too. (As long as they fit in the sleeves.).

In a recent development, A sure bet to be on the required kit list for almost any ultra is a waterproof rain jacket. UTMB’s specifications are rather persnickety, with required minimum waterproof rating, breathability score, sealed seams, and an integrated hood. The On Ultra Jacket lives up to demands while remaining paper thin, uber light, and incredibly packable. It can end up feeling a little too trash-baggy and unbreathable to be your workhorse running jacket, but nevertheless it’s a great piece to be able to throw in your pack anytime you head up into the unpredictable weather of the alpine.

For headlamps, I always keep a couple of options hanging around for the varying amount of daylight in different races. For OCC, for example, I opt for the Petzl E+Lite. It’s so small and light it’s an almost imperceptible item to carry and it uses nickel-sized 2432 batteries, but, as you might expect, that means it is useless for anything more than an hour or so in a pinch.

In a recent development, for longer races where you will inevitably be running through hours of darkness, if not the entire night, this is not somewhere to skimp on weight. A popular option in this category is the Petzl Iko Core, which features an incredible 500 lumen bulb, outstanding shine time, and a replaceable battery that sits at the back of the crown for stability. If you’re running UTMB, don’t forget to pick up the mandatory red light filter for fragile wildlife areas.

In a recent development, the crown jewel of mandatory equipment is the running vest. It’s what will hold all of your other mandatory equipment. It’s what will be in direct contact with your body and can make or break the convenience and comfortability of your racing experience. But, most importantly, it’s what will show up in all your post-race photos and make you look either super nerdy or really cool—by which I mean slightly less nerdy. In all seriousness, choosing your hydration vest is critical—and critically personal. Salomon and Nathan offer popular options that have dominated the category dating back to the cradle of civilization. (Hammurabi allegedly kept his chisel in an ADVskin 5L.) But the market is flooding with alternatives as the sport continues to grow, so there is no shortage of options. The most important things to consider are fit and layout. For my preferences, which is for my vest to feel as least like a vest as possible, the Tantrums Crest 6 and Kiprun Pro Team 5L have been excellent. The pocket layouts have tons of nooks and crannies that scratch my obnoxious need for hyperorganization better than keeping foods from touching on my Thanksgiving plate. They have minimal bounce, soft fabric across the shoulder straps, and offer a rear tunnel pocket for an extra flask. Both the Tantrums and Kiprun also come in light colors, a touch that seems a blatant choice for any piece of athletic equipment but was, until recently, a white whale on the hydration vest market.

A good cap is indispensable and obviously extends beyond race day. It’s not that deep, just get one that feels and looks good. Ciele is a sure fire way to fit in at the coolest run club in town.

Waterproof pants can be a tricky one. They’re probably the thing you’ll use the least in everyday training (save for the tinfoil-esque emergency blanket), and they’re one of the most expensive pieces of kit on the list. Getting a pair that satisfies UTMB’s specifications and doesn’t necessitate a 25-liter pack to fit is going to be steep. On makes the Ultra Pant compliment to the aforementioned jacket, but I use these Rab Phantom Pants. The two are kind of “six-one, half-dozen another,” so just grab whichever you can find on a last-season sale.
