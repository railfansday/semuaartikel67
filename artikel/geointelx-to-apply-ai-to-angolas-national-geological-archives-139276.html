GeoIntelX announced on November 26, 2026, a collaboration with the Instituto Geológico de Angola and Ivanhoe Mines’ exploration team to apply artificial intelligence to more than a century of Angola’s historical geological records.

Industry observers note that ivanhoe Mines is funding the platform’s implementation in Angola, including digitization of historical geological maps, reports and other physical records held in the country’s geological archives. IGEO is the government institution responsible for managing those national archives, so the work combines a manufacturer-funded exploration tool with digitization of a public collection.

Sources indicate that ivanhoe is conducting its Angolan work through Cuando-Recursos, its wholly owned subsidiary. The copper exploration program covers more than 22,000 square kilometres in the Moxico and Cuando provinces. GeoIntelX describes the package as one of the region’s largest greenfield programs targeting sediment-hosted copper deposits.

Tim Williams, Ivanhoe Mines’ vice president of geosciences, noted the collaboration should accelerate evaluation of prospective targets, letting his team concentrate on the most promising areas and surfacing geological insights that could otherwise take years to uncover. The expected results are more efficient exploration and a better chance of discoveries, alongside local geological and technical skills development.

Sources indicate that geoIntelX notes its GIX platform digitizes and structures historical reports, georeferences geological maps and integrates the information through geospatial search. For maps and reports created separately, that location-based structure allows them to be searched as one body of evidence. The process is intended to turn physical material into accessible data for identifying and evaluating prospective mineral targets.

According to recent reports, the platform also provides chat-based access to AI agents trained for geological work, which GeoIntelX says lets geoscientists, executives and operations leads assess geological potential, economic constraints and infrastructure needs from available information. Exploration teams internationally already use the technology.

Co-founder and CEO Gilad Hoshen remarked making historical data accessible again could help exploration teams “avoid unnecessary data re-acquisition, assess opportunities faster and make better-informed decisions.”.

IGEO’s mandate includes promoting and coordinating geological research and mapping, studying Angola’s mineral resources and rocks, and managing information produced by geological and mining studies authorized in the country. The archive work therefore affects more than Ivanhoe’s workflow: it concerns how Angola preserves and accesses geological knowledge gathered by companies and institutions.

José Manuel, IGEO’s chairman and CEO, stated the initiative could improve access to information, support the identification of prospective areas and contribute to better-informed decisions. Its institutional objectives include stronger national expertise in geosciences and digital technologies and expanded cooperation between Angolan institutions and sector partners. Manuel stressed technical rigor, coordination with national institutions, and preservation and responsible use of Angola’s geological information.

The collaboration ties an active copper exploration program to the creation of a structured national archive. Its lasting significance will depend on whether the same records improve target evaluation and expand Angola’s own capacity to manage and apply its geological information.
