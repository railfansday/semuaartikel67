RUNNING SOMETIMES GETS a bad rap in the weight room, as if a few laps around the block will somehow undo all those hard-earned gains. Turns out, the two activities aren’t the rivals they’re made out to be. Adding miles to your strength routine can build endurance to back up all that muscle. A review study found that combining aerobic exercise with resistance training produced gains in muscle size and maximal strength similar to lifting alone. The benefits work in reverse, too: Smart strength work can also help make you a more efficient runner.

Recent findings suggest that the result? A body that can move serious weight, cover some ground, and handle whatever else you throw at it. Of course, getting the most out of running and lifting takes some strategy, from how you schedule your workouts to how you manage recovery. Another important factor is foot health, which can affect both your massive lifts and running splits.

Here’s how to balance running and lifting in your routine, strengthen the feet that support both, and choose shoes for each workout.

YOUR TRAINING SCHEDULE should reflect your priorities. Focused on building strength? Keep your toughest gym sessions front and center and work easy-paced runs around them. Training for a race? Schedule your key runs first. “If your goal is strength, you can’t show up tired,” says Courtney Conley, DC, a chiropractic physician specializing in foot mechanics who recommends separating demanding running and lifting workouts when possible to allow for recovery. Either way, she suggests hitting the weights two to three times a week without going all-out every session.

But if you’re running and strength training, consider also building your explosive power. “You have to train the spring, train that reactive quickness,” Conley indicates. This is where plyometrics like jumps and hops train you to generate force rapidly, absorb impact, and rebound. And, this comes in handy whether you’re sprinting, changing direction on the court, or charging uphill. It will also help your big lifts in the gym, particularly squats, deadlifts, and any type of Olympic lift because it develops things like neuromuscular coordination—allowing you to implement full-body movement patterns and stabilize heavy loads faster; and the rate of force development—how fast you can turn your strength on.

Recent findings suggest that just make sure you train this way early in your workout, not at the end. “You don’t want to do plyometric training when you’re fatigued,” Conley cautions. Doing so can compromise your form, making those explosive moves less effective and potentially increasing your risk of injury.

Industry observers note that THE BODYPART THAT makes all of this training possible is your feet. Their small muscles help you maintain balance, support your arches, and control movement under load. During a squat, for example, those muscles help provide a stable base as you drive through your legs and hips. “Deadlifting is a foot action,” Conley explains. “You’ve got to feel your mid foot and press it into the ground to stabilize your body.”.

It has been revealed that running demands just as much. Every stride is essentially a single-leg hop, with your feet and ankles absorbing impact, storing energy, and releasing it to propel you forward. In her practice, Conley sometimes sees clients with weak feet or Achilles concerns. “When they go to hop, they sink into the ground, and there’s just no spring out of that system.” It’s one more reason those muscles below your ankles deserve just as much attention as your quads and glutes.

And while you could do specific mobility exercises for your feet, those small muscles get quite a workout when performing single-leg moves that help build strength, balance, and stability.

Your plush running shoes may be great on the road, but they’re no match for the weight room. “When you’re in the gym and you want to lift heavy things, you cannot be wearing your 45 millimeter stack height cushion shoe that has a toe spring on it,” Conley claims. “You can’t feel the ground.” For strength training, look for a firm, relatively flat sole that keeps you grounded when the weights get heavy.

According to recent reports, one thing to consider is looking for a shoe that allows for balanced weight distribution across the foot. Another is a wide toe-box that allows for your toes to splay. When your massive toe has the space to remain straight, it can help keep you stable.
