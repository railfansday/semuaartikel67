Whitehall talks up supplier diversification after Redmond's 18-hour outage.

As many as 99 percent of Britain's civil servants rely on Microsoft to communicate, a government minister has admitted, days after Redmond demonstrated the perils of putting quite so many eggs in one basket.

Ian Murray, minister for creative industries, media and online safety, laid bare the extent of Whitehall's Microsoft habit in the House of Commons on Monday, where MPs were also told that the public sector shelled out £1.9 billion on Microsoft licenses in 2024-25 alone.

"We know that 98% to 99% of civil servants use Microsoft as their platform for communications, which is incredibly high," Murray told MPs.

The admission followed last week's 18-hour Microsoft 365 outage, which left businesses unable to carry out transactions, public sector workers struggling to do their jobs, and even MPs unable to access their usual means of communication.

Rebecca Paul, shadow technology secretary, mentioned at least 47 NHS trusts experienced problems, with some services having to fall back on pen and paper. The Conservative MP, who cited the £1.9 billion licensing bill, pressed Murray on whether the government would seek contractual remedies from Microsoft.

In a recent development, murray said the government was unaware of any delays to NHS treatment or instances in which patients had been put at risk. Emergency departments remained open, and 999 services continued to operate. Only two NHS trusts activated incident response units, the minister said.

The outage, which affected 74 countries, has since been blamed on a software update Microsoft rolled out in July. A surge in Teams traffic hit infrastructure already short of server capacity, exposing the months-old bug. More than 80 percent of the servers in the affected gateway stopped responding, leaving Redmond's engineers scrambling to fix their previous fix.

Much of Monday's discussion centered on how the UK had allowed itself to become so dependent on a handful of American technology companies in the first place.

Dame Chi Onwurah, chair of Parliament's Science and Technology Committee, pointed out that the government had been unable to tell her committee how much it spends on IT and which suppliers it uses. She also challenged ministers over their reluctance to embrace open source software, something both France and Germany have pursued to loosen Major Tech's grip on their public sectors.

“Germany has 500,000 civil servants using open source products and services, but the government told my Committee that though open source could address vendor lock-in, they would not include open source targets because of a lack of what was called ‘commercial accountability’,” she remarked.

Paul, meanwhile, reminded Murray that just last month the government had rejected the committee's recommendation to introduce supplier diversification targets.

Murray conceded that the government's extensive dealings with Microsoft probably result in "vendor lock-in," but defended the arrangement, saying it gives Whitehall access to the latest technology at scale.

The admission comes less than a week after research from the Institute for Public Policy Research (IPPR) revealed just how little of the British public sector's IT budget ends up in British hands.

Of the £19.2 billion spent with the public sector's 150 biggest technology suppliers in 2024-25, just 15 percent went to businesses headquartered and owned in the UK, with a predominantly British workforce and which weren't reselling overseas technology. American companies took home almost twice as much as suppliers from all other foreign countries combined.

According to recent reports, murray said the government intends to use its considerable purchasing power to give British businesses a leg up. Its national cloud infrastructure program will see departments procure cloud services collectively rather than individually, with the intention of giving more suppliers a crack at lucrative government contracts.

Whitehall has also set up a technology risk group and a central IT operations function to scrutinize outages and work out what needs fixing across the public sector. Ministers intend to press Microsoft on how it prepares to prevent a repeat of last week's cock-up and whether it can get services back online more swiftly next time.

In a recent development, the government, however, offered no timetable for weaning civil servants off Microsoft's software, nor did Murray commit to targets for adopting open-source alternatives. Redmond's days as Whitehall's IT supplier of choice hardly look numbered. ®.
