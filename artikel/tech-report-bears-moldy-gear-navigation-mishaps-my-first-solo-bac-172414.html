Tech Report: Bears, Moldy Gear & Navigation Mishaps: My First Solo Backpacking Adventure on the Florida Trail

Follow Colleen Wilson and Lane Early.

There’s something empowering about stepping into the wilderness alone, knowing that whatever happens, you’re the one who has to figure it out.

Recent findings suggest that for my first-ever solo backpacking trip, I set out to hike a 60 mile section of the Florida Trail through the Ocala National Forest and Juniper Wilderness Area. I’ve spent thousands of miles backpacking alongside Lane, often leaning on his experience and confidence in the backcountry. This time, I wanted to put my own skills to the test. I wanted to see what it felt like to navigate the challenges, make the decisions, and handle whatever the trail threw at me without someone else to lean on.

What I didn’t anticipate was just how much the trail would throw at me in four short days. What started as a simple, well-planned backpacking trip, quickly turned into some of the most challenging days I’ve ever experienced on the trail and with so many things going wrong.

Between a mold-infested sleeping bag, curious black bears (including a face-to-face encounter), countless tick and chigger bites, sleepless cold nights, unexpected detours due to navigational mistakes, and a few genuinely beautiful stretches of wilderness, this trip turned into an adventure I won’t soon forget.

Recent findings suggest that more importantly, it built a confidence in myself that I hadn’t fully tapped into before. I discovered that I really enjoy the solitude, the independence, and the sense of empowerment that comes with figuring things out on my own.

Sources indicate that my dad dropped me off at Rodman Dam around 1:30 p.m., after we first cached some water at the County Road 316 crossing. I headed north through the tall longleaf pine forests, with sections of lightly overgrown trail scattered throughout the afternoon.

Industry observers note that I encountered only two northbound hikers during the first few miles, and they would turn out to be the only hikers I’d see over the entire 60-mile stretch. Within the first hour, I also came across some fresh bear scat, a friendly little reminder that I was no longer walking through the Florida I was accustomed to growing up here in the Sunshine State.

I made it about seven miles before finding a spot off-trail to set up camp around 5:30 p.m. I could hear ATVs riding throughout the area, and rather than push on toward the campground, I decided to call it a day and settle in so I could enjoy some reading in the tent before dinner.

That’s when things started getting interesting.

After setting up camp, I discovered that my sleeping bag had developed mold all over it since the last time I’d used it. Awesome.

So, I laid the bag outside on the ground beside my tent to start drying before I would pack it away in my backpack and hope it wouldn’t get cold enough that I would actually need it.

According to recent reports, after prepping dinner in my tent, which consisted of cold soaking some ramen in a talenti ice cream container, I heard sniffing around my tent. I turned around and found myself practically eye-to-eye with a black bear investigating my moldy sleeping bag.

According to recent reports, my first reaction was to yell, “Get out of here bear!” Making my voice deeper and louder with each attempt at repeating this chant. It jumped back, startled by my yelling.

It has been revealed that I grabbed my bear spray, got out of the tent, and yelled until it eventually wandered off. It kept stopping to look back at me curiously before disappearing out of sight. Not exactly the wildlife encounter I’d been hoping for on my first solo night.

I packed up my food and ate dinner a good distance away from camp, making sure to keep everything bear-proofed.

Industry observers note that naturally, I was now convinced the bear would be returning for a second visit. Like they do in every story you hear about when a person is attacked by a bear and decides to sleep with their food even after a bear sniffs their campsite the first time.

Recent findings suggest that then I discovered it wasn’t just my sleeping bag. My sleeping bag liner and pillow were all covered in mold, too. This was going to be a long first night.

At this point, I wasn’t going to be using any of this gear and all I had to rely on was my sleeping pad and a makeshift pillow made from a stuff sack and anything I could find in my pack to give it some volume. I could only hope that all the clothing layers in my pack would be enough to get me through the night as I layered them on, one-by-one.

And because apparently everything comes in threes, I found a tiny tick on my leg while changing clothes for bed.

I crawled into my tent, listened to coyotes howling in the distance, and tried to settle in with a book. Their howls seemed to get closer as the evening went on. Each sound startled me awake, thinking the bear was returning to take me out.

According to recent reports, at this point I was questioning even sticking out this backpacking trip and considering calling it quits by calling for a pickup. It was going to be a tougher few days than I’d anticipated. But, as they say, “Never quit on a bad day.” I’d try to make it through the night and assess things tomorrow.

It has been revealed that there were three main reasons. First, my sleeping setup was covered in mold, and although I initially refused to use it, I eventually got cold enough to give in. I ended up wearing practically every layer I had in my pack, including three pairs of socks, before finally pulling the moldy sleeping bag over my feet.

Second, every unfamiliar noise had me convinced a bear was circling my tent.

And third, the ATV riders and country boys in trucks seemed to be going strong until around midnight.

Needless to say, I was ready to get moving at sunrise.

It has been revealed that the morning brought some beautiful stretches of trail, although there were plenty of overgrown and thorny sections to navigate. At one point, I heard what I believe was another bear charging onto the trail behind me. I made my way swiftly toward the busy traffic of CR 316, where I felt a little more comfortable.

I accidentally walked about 0.4 miles past Store 88 where I planned to charge my phone which I needed for navigation and had to backtrack. While there, I met a 70-year-old British guy named Calvin Kline, who was working on fixing up the old place alongside his elderly dog. We chatted for about 45 minutes about life experiences and places we have traveled that we both had in common. One of those unexpected trail encounters that makes long-distance hiking so interesting.

According to recent reports, after lunch, I pushed on for another five or so miles until reaching the Salt Springs blue-blaze junction. There was a bench where I’d planned to take my dinner break, but instead I discovered something much more key: a campground another 6.5 miles ahead that had bear boxes.

I desperately needed a bear box.

It was already a little after 5 p.m., but I knew I needed to make it there. I pushed through the remaining miles and arrived shortly after 8 p.m. and hiking by headlamp.

I set up camp, secured my food in the bear box, and finally felt like I could relax a little. Dinner was cold-soaked ramen that I’d started preparing 6.4 miles earlier.

Recent findings suggest that after dinner I tried to access the food in the bear box and the doors were jammed. Great. Now I would really need to pull the plug and call for a pickup in the morning. No food means no fuel to keep hiking, unless I could find someone camping in the area that could help me get the doors unjammed. Eventually I broke into the box and was relieved that I didn’t have to quit, again.

There were other campers around, a secure place to store my food, and a much better chance of getting some sleep.

After the previous night, it felt like a luxury to not have to sleep with my food in this heavily bear dense area.

I woke up at sunrise and headed out through a beautiful prairie section before entering one of my favorite stretches of the entire trip: the Juniper Wilderness Area.

Sources indicate that the trail through the forest was absolutely gorgeous. It was one of those sections that makes you forget about all the little inconveniences of backpacking and reminds you exactly why you spend so much time out here.

Industry observers note that eventually, I made my way to Juniper Springs Recreation Area, where I had planned a seven-mile kayak float. The current was ripping pretty good and with all the down trees it made for quite the obstacle course. At one point I was pinned in between a down log and some overhanging brush and ended up snapping my sunglasses into two pieces.

A few miles into the float I met Bryan, who works for the local kayaking tech giant. We ended up floating about 3.5 miles together, chatting and swapping stories about Florida and it’s wild backcountry along the way. It was a nice change of pace from the solitude of the trail.

According to recent reports, I camped at the recreation area that evening for $50, charged my electronics, and met a couple from Ohio who work for the North Country Trail. We spent a while chatting and they were eager to ask questions about my experience on The Florida Trail so far as they were day hiking sections of it during their visit to the state.

It was a much-needed reset after two eventful days in the woods.

I woke up at sunrise with the goal of hiking the remaining miles to Alexander Springs, where my parents were scheduled to pick me up.

I believe I had around 18 miles left.

According to recent reports, the morning started off well, but somewhere around lunchtime, I managed to make a pretty significant navigation mistake. I sat down on a log to eat, got back on trail, and somehow ended up hiking in the wrong direction, back toward a road I’d already passed.

Recent findings suggest that by the time I realized what I’d done, I’d walked 1.7 miles in the wrong direction. That meant an additional 3.4 miles of bonus hiking for the day.

Sources indicate that exactly what I needed. Plus, the trail had was quite overgrown and I was covered in spiky stickers.

I picked up the pace to make sure I’d still reach my parents on time. Along the way, I continued seeing plenty of bear scat, including some that looked particularly fresh.

Then I came upon a very large black bear directly on the trail.

Recent findings suggest that I startled it, and it took off running with such incredible speed and force that, for a split second, I was terrified it was charging straight toward me. Thankfully, it veered into the woods and disappeared.

I spent the next mile or so periodically shouting, “HEY BEAR!” with my bear spray close at hand.

For the remainder of the hike, I found myself constantly scanning every turn in the trail, anticipating another bear around the next corner.

Recent findings suggest that it was an interesting balancing act. I needed to speed hike to make my scheduled pickup time, but I also didn’t want to move so swiftly as to startle another bear.

Eventually, I made it to the forest road and found a log to sit on while waiting for my parents.

I was exhausted, but incredibly proud of myself.

I had navigated the trail, dealt with unexpected challenges, made mistakes and figured them out, and pushed through situations that would have been much easier with someone else there to lean on.

According to recent reports, and honestly? I already felt ready to keep going.

But those miles would have to wait. I couldn’t stand the thought of spending another night in my moldy sleeping bag, especially without a bear canister or bear box to store my food.

Four days was enough for this adventure.

One of the things I appreciated most about this trip was how it changed my perspective on Florida.

According to recent reports, for a long time, I associated Florida with bustling cities, traffic, and endless stretches of concrete. I spent time commuting an hour to work in Miami, and my idea of escaping the city was taking a scenic backroad on a weekend drive to my home in Fort Lauderdale.

I never really appreciated just how much wilderness existed beyond the interstates connecting Florida’s major cities.

In a recent development, after walking more than 500 miles through Florida’s wilderness over the last several years, I’ve come to see the state in an entirely different light. There are enormous stretches of forests, springs, prairies, rivers, and protected wilderness that feel worlds away from the metropolitan areas most people associate with Florida.

There’s still a wild Florida out there. You just have to get off the interstate to find it.

More than anything, this trip gave me a new appreciation for what I’m capable of on my own.

I’ve spent so much of my backpacking experience alongside Lane, and while I’ve learned an incredible amount from him, I think there’s something important about stepping away from the comfort of having someone more experienced beside you.

In a recent development, you have to trust your own judgment. You have to make your own decisions. When something goes wrong, there’s nobody to immediately turn to for reassurance or a solution.

I loved the solitude. I loved making my own decisions, moving at my own pace, and having the freedom to experience the wilderness on my terms.

And perhaps most importantly, I came away with a confidence that I hadn’t realized I was missing.

Sources indicate that I don’t need to wait for someone else to be available to go backpacking. I don’t need someone else’s experience to validate my own abilities.

And I have a feeling this won’t be my last solo adventure.
