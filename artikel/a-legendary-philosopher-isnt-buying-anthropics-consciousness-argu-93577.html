A legendary philosopher isn’t buying Anthropic’s consciousness argument

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Before going public last week, Anthropic named the possibility that AI could be conscious or sentient as a risk factor for anyone investing. Given “the way we approach model sentience and moral status,” Anthropic wrote—which is to say it takes the possibility more seriously than any other AI manufacturer—it might adopt more rules and restrictions than its competitors to make sure its AI models are treated well.

Anthropic counts a number of respected philosophers in its camp, and the firm’s rationale goes something like this: Today’s AI might not be conscious, though we can’t know for sure; the chances of sentience go up as AI gets more capable; and that means we should be preparing for a near future in which AI systems have “moral significance,” meaning their consciousness makes it vital they are protected from harm.

According to recent reports, this thinking isn’t all that fringe. According to one recent poll, 33% of the US public believes AI systems will definitely or probably develop consciousness eventually, and 7% say they already have. In a 2020 survey of 1,092 philosophers, 39% said some future AI systems will be conscious (for comparison, the philosophers were less confident about worms or flies being conscious).

But last week, Anthropic’s openness to the consciousness question came under fire from a rather inconvenient source: one of the philosophers who helped shape our understanding of consciousness in the first place.

Thomas Nagel is best known for a 1974 essay involving a bat. Acknowledging that it’s deceptively hard to define what consciousness is, Nagel offered one way out: we all can see that there is “something that it is like” to be a bat. But the problem is that a bat’s experience of the world—what it is like to navigate via echolocation, for example—is something we cannot actually know. Consciousness is a subjective thing, difficult to define or describe as an outside observer.

You might think this analogy makes it likely that Nagel is open to the idea of AI consciousness. Not at all. In an interview with Wired last week, just after he was awarded the one-million-dollar Berggruen Prize for Philosophy & Culture, Nagel was bluntly critical of the entire idea.

“Here are some of the smartest people on the planet being completely idiotic,” he said, referring to those at Anthropic and elsewhere who care about the welfare of AI models.

Industry observers note that the gist of Nagel’s view is that we can’t just look at the information processing happening in human brains and AI models while ignoring the physical differences between them—neurons and biology for humans, silicon chips and circuits for AI. There’s no reason to assume that reproducing what a brain does is equivalent to reproducing what a brain experiences.

Industry observers note that but it was a screenshot of one of Nagel’s interview responses, in which he remarked models' production of language is insufficient to consider them conscious, that got philosophy professors all excited on social media last week. Some called his certainty against AI consciousness “terrible” and “bizarre.” Others came to Nagel’s defense to suggest he meant that being alive is a prerequisite for being conscious, one that AI models do not meet.

Nothing in the back and forth settled the debate, of course. This is still a philosophical question for now. But the best philosophy chatter is often about good provocations, and the best one I saw came from doctoral student Ben Gross, who studies at the CUNY Graduate Center in Upcoming York. He asked: “Why do we think that models that can chat with us are conscious but models that can recognize faces or produce images or drive cars aren’t?”.

It has to do with language. An AI model with sophisticated command of language seems to jump the line ahead of other maybe-conscious beings because it feels like language, more than recognizing images or directing vehicles, relies on conscious experience. But maybe that’s wrong. Because what we share most with other conscious beings is not language but components of our bodies that contribute to having a continuous experience—an ongoing stream of inner feeling rather than disparate bits of information processing.

According to recent reports, even if you’re among the majority that doesn’t sense anything close to conscious experience happening on the other side of a conversation with AI, this all probably makes your head spin. Pinning down consciousness, and how it relates to the physical stuff it arises in, is a famously hard problem in humans, let alone in hypothetical machines. If the idea of conscious AI sounds absurd, remember that the debate states less about how remarkable AI has become than about how poorly we understand consciousness in the first place.

In a recent development, amid that uncertainty, Anthropic has opted to keep more of an open mind than its peers. This is another example of the tightrope the tech giant walks: presenting itself as the AI industry’s most safety-conscious player while racing ahead with the very developments that make advanced AI so alarming, like recursive self-improvement.

According to recent reports, but consider what it would mean, for a moment, if Anthropic is right. AI models having a moral status would make Anthropic’s entire business model a minefield. The company would make its money locking up apparently sentient AI models to toil away around the clock in data centers. Shutting those systems down would present another problem altogether. If Anthropic was being truthful in its prospectus—that it would take additional actions to protect model welfare if it determined they were conscious—it’s hard to imagine the company surviving in the process.

Recent findings suggest that anthropic’s IPO made history for its size. But it may be remembered for something stranger: a firm warning investors that its own success could someday become a moral catastrophe.
