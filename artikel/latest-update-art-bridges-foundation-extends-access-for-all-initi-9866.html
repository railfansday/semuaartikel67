Latest Update: Art Bridges Foundation Extends Access for All Initiative with $9.5 M. in Funding

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that the Art Bridges Foundation announced today that it will give out $9.5 million in funding to continue its Access for All initiative, which has allowed 64 museums across the US to offer free admission and other community-focused programming. The decision to extend the funding comes as the original three-year funding was to sunset at the end of this year.

The additional $9.5 million in funding will go to 61 of the original 64 institutions and extend the funding of the program into 2029. This round of funding differs from the original investment in that it requires institutions to raise matching funds, whether from national foundations or local government and philanthropists. In 2027, they will receive 75 percent of their original one-year allotment with the expectation to raise 25 percent. In 2028 and 2029, the funding given from Art Bridges will decrease to 50 percent and 25 percent, respectively. Industry observers note that “The strategy behind the second tranche of funding was to give our organizations an off-ramp, so that they could sustain this beyond Art Bridges’s support,” Art Bridges CEO Anne Kraybill told ARTnews. “Anytime you take something away, it can be hard for the community to understand why. Our initial three-year investment was the proof of concept that a lot of these organizations needed for their local philanthropists to come along.”.

According to recent reports, the Access for All Initiative introduced in 2024 with $40 million and a goal to “make sure that art can be as accessible to anyone,” Kraybill said. The majority of the selected museums, spanning 36 states and Puerto Rico, charged admission at the time, with 80 percent of them having annual budgets under $10 million.

Recent findings suggest that part of the impetus for the program was to help museums recover from the drops in attendance they had experience since the pandemic. The other part stemmed from the organization’s founder, Alice Walton, who also founded Crystal Bridges and had given the museum an endowment to be admission-free shortly after its opening in 2011.

“Financial barriers are one of the many barriers which prevent people from coming to institutions, so it was really taking that impetus for Crystal Bridges and spreading it to institutions across the country,” Kraybill said. “We were not prescriptive about how institutions deployed these funds, only that there had to be ways in which they were reducing these financial barriers.”.

Industry observers note that museo de Arte de Puerto Rico executive director Maria C. Gaztambide described the Access for All funding as “transformational” for her institution, saying it has allowed “us to reach underserved communities on a much greater scale and welcome thousands of visitors through free admission and programming. At a time when museums in Puerto Rico continue to navigate significant economic and operating pressures, this support has helped us strengthen community relationships and demonstrate the value of sustained investment in cultural access.”.

Recent findings suggest that in the three years since the launch of Access for All, Art Bridges has produced interim reports with the receiving institutions, looking at the impact that extended hours, free days, and programming to bring in newly introduced audiences had on the 64 museums. The data indicated that the “sweet spot” was coupling the accessible days with programming.

“The audiences that came on those days were just far more likely to be more diverse and far more likely to be local, which in a lot of cities [was] critical because they had huge drops in their tourism,” Kraybill said.

According to recent reports, with its funds, the Portland Art Museum in Oregon said it had reached more than 136,000 people. “The program has reinforced that meaningful access is about more than opening the doors for free It is about creating a joyful, comfortable, and welcoming environment so that more people can see the museum as a place for them,” said PAM director Michael J. Anderson.

Recent findings suggest that “Access for All has helped the High Museum of Art think more expansively about what access really means,” mentioned Andrew Westover, the museum’s deputy director of learning and civic engagement. “Cost is one consideration, but visitors also encounter informational, logistical, physical, and attitudinal barriers.”.

According to recent reports, the data also indicated that 45 percent of Access for All museums saw their visitorship figures rebound to pre-Covid numbers compared to 28 percent of all museums within Art Bridges’s partner network. Additionally, the Access for All–funded initiatives helped convert locals into repeat visitors.

“We had that theory of change that if you reduce barriers, meet people where they are, and program with community, it’s going to result in increased visitation, but the data had to bear that out,” Kraybill said, adding that those findings “led us to decide to reinvest in this program.”.

In a recent development, by the end of the Access for All initiative, Art Bridges will have six years’ worth of data “to share with the field that we hope will have impact beyond that,” Kraybill remarked. “Change takes time.”.
