You May Be Thawing Your Turkey Wrong—Here’s How To Avoid Spreading Salmonella

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that thanksgiving is a marathon, not a sprint. And the most involved, time-consuming dish is undoubtedly the turkey. The centerpiece of your holiday table needs hours in the oven and at least a day of brining, but don't forget the time it takes to defrost beforehand, too.

It has been revealed that frozen turkeys are generally the more accessible and affordable option. But they do require a little extra TLC and planning. So if you buy a frozen bird this year, you'll need to know how to work with it.

Recent findings suggest that making sure it tastes good is one thing, but ensuring that it's safe to eat is something else entirely. Like chicken, turkey can spread Salmonella and other harmful bacteria. You should exercise caution at every stage of the process, including thawing, to avoid making your guests sick. Here’s what you should know.

Sources indicate that the USDA says it's also safe to defrost turkey in cold water, but in our opinion, it's not worth the hassle. The best bet is to thaw it in the refrigerator, where the bird can gradually defrost at the safe temperature with less risk of bacterial growth. What you should never do: thaw it on the counter, in the garage, or with warm water.

It has been revealed that these environments are less controlled and can expose the turkey to the temperature danger zone (between 40°F and 140°F), where germs grow rapidly. Even if the center of the turkey is still frozen, the thawed exterior can become a hotbed for Salmonella by the time you're ready to roast.

According to recent reports, roasted turkey drippings make great gravy. But raw turkey juice dripping all over your fridge? Not so much. Even shrink-wrapped birds can leak, so you should always store your turkey on a baking sheet or roasting pan while it thaws.

Sources indicate that food safety experts also recommend storing raw meat on the lowest shelf. That way, any leaks are less likely to drip onto other ingredients in your fridge.

We know you're probably keeping a lot of other ingredients in your fridge, so it can be hard to set aside extra room for the turkey. That stated, it's a step worth doing. Giving your turkey plenty of personal space minimizes the risk of raw meat or its juices coming into contact with other ingredients.

According to recent reports, in the event that your turkey does contaminate another ingredient, here's what to know: Foods that won’t be cooked, like salad greens, should be tossed if they’ve come into contact with raw turkey juices. Cooking contaminated foods thoroughly can kill harmful bacteria, but you’ll also need to clean any surfaces the juices touched.

Industry observers note that turkeys are massive, so they take a long time to thaw all the way through. The experts at Butterball say you should allocate a day for every four pounds of turkey if you're keeping it in the fridge. That means if you're working with a 16-pound bird, set aside four days just for defrosting. Once it's fully thawed, Butterball says your turkey will stay safe in the fridge for up to four days.

And on top of that, brining can make your turkey even more flavorful. If your frozen bird didn’t come pre-seasoned, consider setting aside another day or two for that step. (Want some guidance? Check out our brining guide.).

In a recent development, it’s extremely vital to keep things clean while you’re handling a turkey. One of the most common ways Salmonella spreads in the kitchen is through cross-contamination caused by poor hygiene.

Industry observers note that so wash those hands (including under your fingernails!) with soap and water for at least 20 seconds. Make sure you also thoroughly clean any utensils and surfaces that come into contact with raw turkey or its juices using hot, soapy water.

And above all, don’t stress! Americans prepare millions of turkeys every Thanksgiving, so you're not alone in your turkey-roasting journey. Follow the tips above to ensure yours turns out safe to eat and delicious.
