Football comes together across Spain to honour Jesús de Miguel: ‘We’ve seen how fragile life is’

Across La Liga and beyond this weekend, footballers took to the field knowing it could have been them.

In the final few minutes before stepping into the arena once more, heading out for his 199th Osasuna match on Saturday evening, the man who has scored more first division goals than anyone in their history stopped his teammates for a moment and told them what it meant to wear the shirt. “This is very key,” Ante Budimir began as the dressing room, their sanctuary, fell silent around him. “We don’t have this shirt today for the sake of it. He was not chosen, and nor are we. It could have happened to anyone, anyone. Let us go knowing that we’re grateful to be on the pitch: playing, watching, warming up, sitting on the bench, whatever it may be. Boys, we were not chosen: not us, not him. Let us go, gratefully. Without thought. We have a grand day before us.”.

With that, Osasuna’s players turned left out of the room, where they were joined in the tunnel by footballers from Athletic Club, rivals from the north, and together they went out past the little shrine of Saint Fermin on to the field. They carried white lilies and all wore the same white shirts with a No 9 on the back, a black ribbon and the name of Jesús de Miguel, the Tenerife striker who died after a collision on the pitch five days earlier at the age of 30. At almost exactly the same time, 2,000km away to the west, Tenerife’s squad were walking out at the Heliodoro Rodríguez López in Santa Cruz. They did not have anyone alongside them, wore black suits not white shirts and did not have a game to play either, not this week.

Sources indicate that two days earlier Tenerife had been in Navahermosa, La Mancha, the tiny village their teammate’s family are from, carrying the coffin of the friend their captain called “our Demigol”. They were supposed to be in Granada on Saturday; instead, they were back on the island, in their home ground, to say goodbye and pay homage. Publicly, this time. Lilies had been laid on the pitch there too, a Tenerife shirt with them, and a stage built at one end. “We’ve seen how fragile life is,” the captain, Enric Gallego, remarked, offering words he said could never be enough, carrying the burden for all of them. “We think of the journeys, the wins, the losses, and hold on to those moments that seemed normal, but that now … a few days ago we shared that with you. To talk of you in the past tense is unfair. None of us are prepared to live what we’re living through.”.

Recent findings suggest that no one ever could be. Across Spain this weekend, footballers took to the field knowing it could have been them. That, Budimir had told his teammates, was something they must think about but not think about too. The players’ union in which De Miguel had a role, a footballer there for others, had asked for the competition to stop. Respect demanded that it did, they said, requesting a judge intervene when the league did not hear them. But it had gone on. The only matches postponed were those involving Tenerife; Andorra, whose goalkeeper Jesús Owono had crashed into De Miguel; and Castellón, who he had taken to the second division, delivering them to segunda for the first time in 16 years and himself for the first time at all, aged 27.

On the eve of the game that opened this weekend’s fixtures, the Sevilla head coach, Luis García, noted he thought they should wait. It was too soon, too raw, too theirs. His midfielder Giorgi Kochorashvili had missed training the day before: a former Castellón player, Kochorashvili had asked permission to go to the funeral, leaving early and returning late, his mind elsewhere. Of course he could, García had replied, “we are humans above all else”. And it was not just him. García had worked with Owono – “let’s not make two victims, we have had enough; he is in hell, for sure”, he had pleaded – and now he said: “If there’s a play that’s similar … what do I say to my goalkeeper?

It has been revealed that “We need time,” García had stated. “We need to reflect.”.

Industry observers note that instead, they played. From Seville on Friday night to Madrid on Sunday, via stadiums everywhere. It was, some suggested, the best way to pay homage to De Miguel but no one truly knew. “We can’t ask him what he would have wanted,” José Mourinho said before Madrid went to Valencia and won 3-0, Tenerife’s flag flying at half-mast at Mestalla. He might not have wanted anything at all. “You never wanted any attention,” Gallego said, but this weekend he was on all their minds.

Sources indicate that “Forever in our memories,” the league’s line read. At every game in the first and second divisions, the ritual was repeated. Teams came out together wearing the same white shirts bearing his name. Flowers were laid, a little uncomfortably, on the spot inside the centre circle where he had fallen and there was a minute’s silence, players and officials gathered around the wreath. As they stood before Cádiz-Oviedo, tears ran down the cheeks of the Tenerife-based referee Marta Huerta de Aza. On stadium screens, an image of De Miguel appeared. On television screens, a QR code did: a link to a crowdfunding campaign for the young family he left, set up by the former Tenerife player Fran Sol.

According to recent reports, the games began and then, in the ninth minute or as near as the referee could manage, they paused again: the teams stopped, looking up to the screen, where a video of his best moments played out. It was not always an easy watch, and some preferred not to.

Recent findings suggest that at Sevilla, where fans saw Antonio Puerta collapse on the pitch and pass away, applause still offered in the 16th minute every game, Kochorashvili stood by the bench, eyes lost somewhere his mind was not. At Espanyol, where in the 21st minute they applaud Dani Jarque, the captain who died during a pre-season tour and to whom Andrés Iniesta dedicated the winning goal at the 2010 World Cup final, Álex Calatrava and Álvaro González, teammates at Castellón and Tenerife respectively, looked about to break. In the ninth minute at the Metropolitano, the place was still rocking, the timing out of sync: Giuliano Simeone had just scored, so the referee Javier Alberola Rojas allowed a couple of minutes to pass, choosing his moment, Diego Simeone helping him impose silence on the stands when he finally stopped it. Then they started again, expected to carry on regardless.

In Pamplona, Alejandro Catena, who scored the late equaliser in a 1-1 draw with Athletic, raised an Osasuna shirt with De Miguel’s name and number on the back. Theirs was the game that felt most out of place, attention drawn from where it should have been, where it was. It would not be true to say that it did not matter, the Osasuna head coach, Jagoba Arrasate, admitting he had been carried along with it like everyone else, the calmness he thought they had found in the months out of the game soon abandoned again.

As they played, grateful for the grand day that they had before them, all the days they did, the tribute continued in Tenerife. The Heliodoro had filled early, access by every gate except eight and nine, engulfed by flowers and candles. De Miguel’s shirt lay on the turf where he had, lilies to one side, a spotlight on it. Another wreath occupied the bench. A giant shirt stretched across one end where the stage was, a lectern awaiting the speakers. A cross presided over it. Staff and players wore No 9 badges on their lapels. Supporters came in Tenerife shirts. And in the yellow shirts of rivals Las Palmas, whose president and captain Kirian, who had recovered after cancer, came. “This is the Canaries I want,” said Tenerife’s majority shareholder, Rayco García.

Recent findings suggest that in the seats set up on the pitch, Tenerife’s players and De Miguel’s family sat, their eyes heavy. The archbishop, Eloy Alberto Santiago led a mass. The club’s president and majority shareholder spoke. He promised they would never allow María, De Miguel’s widow, to be alone. He also announced they would adopt Carla, his 14-month-old daughter, supporting her economically until she is 18. Gallego addressed her, football as family meaning something. “I hope one day you can look back and see who your dad was, be proud of him, not just for the footballer he was but the person: for his humility, his goodness,” he said.

Sniffing, his voice breaking, the captain paid homage to his teammate, his strike partner, his friend. The kind of person, he said, who did not seem to be there, and definitely did not demand that people notice he was, but always was. A man, he noted, so disinclined to put anyone out that when his car broke down he walked home from training rather than bother anyone for a lift. That, he insisted, might not seem like much, but it said much. “Today we don’t want to talk only of the player; we want to talk about you, Jesús, about the person that was behind the shirt,” he had begun. “Above any game, any team, what really remains is the person and the impact you leave. And you were a very special person.”.

Gallego gave way to the archbishop, orations led by Tenerife staff, and as the act came to a close, difficult days ahead, the spotlight taken elsewhere, the drums began and the fans belted out a familiar tune, once more with more meaningful than ever before:.

double quotation markWe have to sing with even more strength if you’re losing Even those who left us sing, from heaven This will never stop We will sing to eternity.”.

Sources indicate that then Tenerife’s players departed. They have training on Monday and next week they play.
