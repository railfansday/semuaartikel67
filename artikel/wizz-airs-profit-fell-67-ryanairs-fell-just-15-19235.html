Wizz Air’s Profit Fell 67%. Ryanair’s Fell Just 15%

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a recent development, wizz Air reported its first half results on Thursday for the six months ending September 30, and as expected, it didn’t go well. Net profit fell 66.6% to €108.1MM (about $120MM) on revenue that rose 13.8% to €3.8bn. Operating profit fell 39.8% to €264.5MM. Passengers rose 26.2% to 46MM and the load factor held at 92.5%, so Wizz had no trouble filling planes. But without a doubt, fuel was the biggest drag on Wizz Air’s performance.

Recent findings suggest that looking across the continent to the market leader, Ryanair reported the same six-month window on November 2, 2026. Profit after tax fell 15% to €2.15bn (about $2.4bn) from €2.54bn, on revenue up 3% to €10.08bn and traffic up 6% to 125MM passengers. Average fares fell 4% and ancillary revenue rose 6% to €3.08bn. Ryanair also revealed another €800MM share buyback starting in February.

In a recent development, both airlines fly the same kind of customer around the same continent, and both took the same fuel shock from the Iran war. One kept 85% of its profit. The other kept a third.

Recent findings suggest that ryanair hedged about 80% of its fiscal 2027 fuel at roughly $67/barrel. The unhedged 20% roughly doubled in price, which pushed Ryanair’s unit costs up 5%. That hurt, but the hedge was highly effective (and reminds me of Southwest’s great fuel leap forward.

Wizz’s fuel cost per seat kilometer rose 14.5% in the first half of the year. It now has 82% of its second half fuel hedged with a cap of $860 per metric tonne, and 41% of next year’s needs capped at $1,009 a tonne. By my rough conversion, $860 a tonne is a little over $100 a barrel, which is a long way above where Ryanair locked in. CEO József Váradi said the hedge “is doing what it was designed to do.” It is, though it was designed later and higher than where Ryan Air secured supply.

Sources indicate that the timing of when this financial pain arrived is key as well. Wizz’s Q2 (by itself) was profitable and growing, with net profit up 7.4% to €306MM even as fuel costs rose 28.7%. Some of the damage came in the first quarter, when Middle East conflict disrupted its flying in the region. Wizz has since postponed restarting flights to Abu Dhabi and Dubai because airspace restrictions require longer, costlier routings.

According to recent reports, an astounding 22 Wizz aircraft were parked as of September 30th for Pratt & Whitney GTF engine inspections, down from 35 a year earlier. Wizz expects 15-20 still grounded at the end of its fiscal year and none by the end of 2027. Because Wizz chose Airbus A320s for its common fleet, of its 276 aircraft, that’s a decent amount of equipment it’s paying for that cannot fly is carrying a cost Ryanair simply does not have, because Ryanair flies Boeing 737s with CFM engines. Boeing has had its own delivery, certification, and safety concerns but for this particular matter Airbus A32os are at a significant disadvantage.

Sources indicate that wizz now expects unit costs excluding fuel to rise by a mid single-digit percentage this fiscal year, blaming “increased transitional costs,” after a flat first half. For Q3, it guides capacity up about 12% with the load factor down low single digits. That suggests an adjusted economy that is absorbing these elevated costs for an extended duration – especially at the lower end of the market. Growing that fast while your costs rise and your fleet is partly parked is a challenge, and it is the same growth-first instinct that has defined Wizz since Indigo Partners (also the former owner of Frontier and Spirit) backed it.

Frontier just hired Michael Delehant from Wizz Air as its upcoming chief operating officer, which I covered last weekend. Frontier lost $104 million in its third quarter. Importing the operating playbook from the airline that just lost two-thirds of its profit is an interesting choice, though to be fair to Delehant, he ran operations and commercial at Wizz, not its hedge book or its engine contracts.

The fare picture also matters for anyone flying within Europe next year. Ryanair’s fares fell this summer, but Michael O’Leary has already warned that fares will rise when the cheap hedges roll off, as Matthew reported in October. Ryanair has only 25% of fiscal 2028 fuel hedged, at about $95 a barrel. Wizz is guiding unit revenue up only low single digits this winter. When the two largest low-cost carriers in Europe are both short on cheap fuel at the same time, expect the rock-bottom intra-Europe fare to get harder to find. But it speaks more to the wider ULCC market which follows Europe’s lead. Indicators on the continent don’t always pair perfectly with those of other global markets including the United States, but it could show the way forward if Frontier hits upcoming headwinds.

There is also an outside chance that wars in Ukraine and Iran will conclude and hedging won’t be a factor in the way that is now.

The war(s) and the subsequent fuel spikes hit every European airline. What separated Ryanair from Wizz Air this summer was a hedge placed early and low, a fleet that was not sitting on the ground waiting for engines, and a willingness to grow slower when costs rose. Wizz still fills 92.5% of its seats and its second quarter was profitable. However, it serves as a reminder that in the low-cost business, the airline with the cheapest seat wins only when it also has the cheapest cost, and right now that is still Ryanair.
