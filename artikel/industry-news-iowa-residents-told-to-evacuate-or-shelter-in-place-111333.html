Industry News: Iowa Residents Told To Evacuate or Shelter in Place as U.S. Army Prepares To Detonate Nearly 10,000 Pounds of a Lead Explosive

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The U.S. Army intends to detonate 9,700 pounds of a lead-based explosive in Southeast Iowa on Wednesday, despite concerns from environmental groups.

The detonation is scheduled to go ahead at 10 a.m. on Wednesday at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant (IAAAP) in Middletown, IA. This comes after a lawsuit was filed by the Iowa Environmental Council and Sierra Club Iowa Chapter asking the federal appeals court to halt the plan.

Two rapid, controlled detonations were initially scheduled to take place last Friday, but the plans were pushed to Wednesday due to a combination of public concern and weather optimization.

Set to be detonated are 68 drums of degraded lead azide, adding up to roughly 9,700 pounds, which have become sensitive and unstable. It is the primary explosive used to initiate munitions.

A controlled detonation is now the only viable option, according to the Army’s Q&A page about the event.

As lead azide degrades, it crystalizes and becomes sensitive to temperature changes, shock, and friction, making it unsafe to move. There are chemical neutralization procedures, but the drums are too unstable for the physical manipulation this option requires, according to the Army.

U.S. Environmental Protection Agency Region 7 Director Jim Macy wrote a letter to Iowa Gov. Kim Reynolds, ensuring her that the EPA was working alongside the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services and the Department of Defense to ensure the controlled detonation was “conducted in the safest manner possible.”.

“All agencies involved are prepared to take the most extreme mitigation measures available to ensure there is no risk to the public and any disruptions are minimized,” Macy wrote in the letter, according to the Iowa Capital Dispatch.

In a recent development, an updated public safety advisory shared by the Army on Tuesday shared the agency’s plans to make the detonation as safe as possible. The lead azide will be detonated in earth-covered magazines, which are built for the purposes of holding ammunition and explosives, and are reinforced.

Additional physical barriers made of sand, a “water wall,” gel blocks, and aqueous foam barriers will be in place to contain particle dispersion, absorb thermal energy, and reduce airborne particulate dispersal. These measures, as well as drone surveillance of lead levels, are in place to track and minimize deposition outside the plant.

The Army has acknowledged that lead particles will impact communities outside the plant. It has defined an evacuation zone as a "5-mile cone extending southwest of IAAAP.".

Those in the evacuation zone will be provided short-term hotel lodging, food, water, and transportation as needed.

It has also laid out recommendations for those outside the evacuation zone, such as sheltering in place, closing all windows and doors, and setting heating to recirculate and avoid pulling in air from outside.

Still, environmental groups are concerned about the lead plume, which they say could spread 100 miles, depending on weather conditions.

"The EPA itself admits that there is no safe level of lead exposure for humans," said Michael Schmidt, general counsel at the Iowa Environmental Council, in a press release shared with Realtor.com®.

"When our federal government makes decisions that result in a potentially toxic plume of lead dust that could cover much of southeastern Iowa, Iowans deserve to know that every available alternative was considered before choosing this method of disposal.".

The Iowa Environmental Council and Sierra Club, Iowa Chapter, were only made aware of the unprecedented detonation plan early last week, initially scheduled for Friday. In response, the groups filed a lawsuit on Thursday challenging the permit that the EPA issued the Army.

“Based on the information that was made public at the time, which was very little information, there were serious concerns about the effectiveness of the public notification and the information being put out to protect the public,” Thien Chau, a senior attorney at Earth Justice, tells Realtor.com.

“There were serious concerns about the mitigations measures in place to reduce the impact as well as to properly do remediation and cleanup and address any harm that people may face from the impact of the detonation.”.

Many local lawmakers were not made aware of the controlled detonation either, and were only informed about the looks and risk once the Iowa Environmental Council and Sierra club told them, despite the widespread impact on surrounding communities, according to Chau.

“That’s why we filed the lawsuit: to make sure that all the information and resources and capabilities that could be provided were provided to the people potentially impacted by this,” states Chau.

In a recent development, “Since that filing, a lot of things have happened. The government has put out a lot more information, and while we expect a detonation to take place this Wednesday, we are glad to see that because there was additional time, there are now additional mitigation measures and resources have been provided for potentially impacted people.”.

According to recent reports, the Department of Justice responded in opposition to the lawsuit. Both the DOJ and Army maintain that a controlled detonation is the safest option and further delay, especially with cold weather approaching, could lead to a natural, uncontrolled detonation, which would be more difficult to mitigate.

“The Army has delayed detonation by three days now,” the DOJ’s brief remarked. “But every day of delay increases the risk of an uncontrolled explosion that will cause far greater public health and environmental harm than the planned, controlled detonation initiated under the protective terms of the Permit.”.
