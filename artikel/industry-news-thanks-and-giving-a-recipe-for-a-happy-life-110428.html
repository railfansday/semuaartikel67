Thanksgiving has always been my favorite holiday, a sentiment I frequently hear from others.

What makes Thanksgiving so beloved? Its simplicity, I think.

Unlike other holidays, there are no gifts to buy or exchange, no decorations to hang, or costumes to wear. Instead, we gather, connect, give thanks, and eat. Across religious beliefs and family traditions, this day offers an opportunity to sit around a table, prepare and share a meal, and appreciate what is present in our lives.

In a recent development, of course, Thanksgiving holds different meanings and histories for different people. It can also bring grief, loneliness, and family tension. Yet, I wonder whether the enduring appeal reveals something crucial about our psychological needs and the path to a well-lived life.

Industry observers note that the word thanksgiving dates back to the 1530s, when it referred to the act of expressing gratitude. By the 1600s, it became associated with public celebrations of thanks, and later became the American holiday we know today.

Sources indicate that beyond the delicious meal, I believe Thanksgiving offers a recipe for living. The word itself implies action. Thanks is how we appreciate life, and giving is how we participate in it. By giving, we move our gratitude into service. Gratitude changes what we notice, while giving changes what we do with what we notice. Together, they create a powerful practice of recognizing and reciprocating the goodness in our lives.

I think of Thanks+Giving as a prescription in perception, a conscious practice for approaching life with greater grace, generosity, and connection.

Sources indicate that in my clinical work and my own life, I see how expectations contribute to emotional suffering. Often, we become attached to how people should behave or feel, how circumstances should unfold, or how life should look. Gratitude offers another perspective. It encourages curiosity and appreciation, even when things are not as expected.

Research increasingly supports the psychological benefits of gratitude. An international study with over 10,000 participants found that even brief gratitude practices produced modest improvements in positive mood and well-being (Coles et al., 2026). In another recent study with 1,240 participants, researchers found that expressing gratitude in supportive relationships improved both emotional well-being and relationship quality (Nelson-Coffey et al., 2026). Maybe the power of gratitude is how little it takes to experience the benefits.

My daily gratitude practice is a running list of three things I appreciate. Today, I added trees, my friend Josie, and apples. I do not add explanations or further descriptions. Naming something as ordinary as a tree leads me to notice it again and appreciate its qualities, without asking it to do or be more than it is. Children often find wonder in these ordinary gifts, something we can miss amid the responsibilities and distractions of adulthood.

Noticing our gratitude does not necessarily erase grief, disappointment, or longing. We can think of gratitude as expanding our emotional experience, not denying what hurts. For example, we can miss someone while appreciating the moments we shared. We can be worried about tomorrow while finding something beautiful in today. Gratitude can co-exist with other emotions, and direct our momentary focus toward something positive.

I like to remind myself to look for the awe in all, rather than waiting for something extraordinary to call awesome.

If gratitude allows us to recognize goodness, giving suggests we share it. Giving is both an expression and an extension of gratitude.

It has been revealed that another 2026 study examined whether “grateful” people were more likely to engage in generous acts. Drawing on large international samples and field research, the researchers found that people with a disposition toward gratitude were more likely to engage in personally costly actions benefiting others (Lee et al., 2026). Interestingly, temporarily inducing gratitude did not produce the same effect. This suggests that an enduring grateful orientation is more closely associated with generosity than a fleeting moment of appreciation.

Industry observers note that we usually think of generosity in terms of money, donations, or charitable work. But giving can also mean attention, compassion, encouragement, time, and presence of mind for both others and ourselves.

Again, simplicity might hold the secret. I remember my daughter, as a young child, finding a tiny flower, and immediately gifting it to me, my husband, or her brother. She found something that inspired awe and without hesitation, she passed it on.

That spontaneous gesture captures the beautiful reciprocity of giving. Without expecting anything in return, we can experience true connection and the satisfaction of contributing to another person’s joy. The recipient receives a gift, and the giver experiences the pleasure of sharing something meaningful.

Industry observers note that this giving can also include ourselves. Resting when we are tired, accepting help, and treating ourselves with kindness and care, are forms of generosity that sustain our capacity to give to others.

Beyond our annual Thanksgiving feast, what if we made time for thanks and giving every day? Imagine pausing throughout the day to ask ourselves two simple questions:.

What can I name that I am grateful for in this moment?

What can I consciously give to myself or others in this moment?

It has been revealed that the answers and actions do not need to be grand. The recipe is remarkably simple: Notice something good. Give something good. Repeat.

It has been revealed that when we make Thanks+Giving a daily practice, we may discover that the joys of living emerge where gratitude, connection, and service meet. It may just be that the secret sauce to a happy life is found in the name of a favorite holiday.
