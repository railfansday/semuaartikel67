A few months back, I wrote a piece here about the viral conspiracy that Earth would lose gravity for 7 seconds on August 12th. I discussed how, despite not actually believing that the event would occur—because of a slew of reasons—the open-minded part of me (albeit still skeptical) nevertheless suggested developing a quick “just in case” protocol for the unlikely event. I received some positive feedback on how I exemplified the importance of cautious thinking throughout the piece and how, in context, we can engage two notable dispositions towards thinking (Dwyer et al., 2016): open-mindedness and skepticism. On the other hand, though, some implied a level of foolishness in my thinking.

The latter cohort, I believe, missed my point, and so I aim to make it clearer in this follow-up. In that previous piece, I stated on multiple occasions that I didn’t actually believe the event would occur. In fact, I highlighted why it was nearly impossible.

According to recent reports, but I used this event prediction as an example to make the point that cautious thinkers are typically happy to make minor mistakes in efforts to avoid risk, even if the risk is extremely low. That is, through cost-benefit analysis (consistent with many existing cost-benefit models we commonly see in psychology; e.g. Lave and March 1975; Thibaut and Kelley 1959; Uehara 1990), good thinkers may often choose a 99 percent likely mistake made out of caution, if the mistake yields minimal negative impact (e.g., looking foolish in front of one’s spouse). This will be selected over doing nothing potentially “foolish” (e.g., in light of being 99 percent “sure”), despite the disastrous impact that 1 percent chance would have because they failed to act (e.g., if the event occurred, death would be a near certainty if we were outdoors).

Simply, good thinkers don’t care about labels of wrong or right; they care about the outcomes of their decisions. It’s cautious thinking… and critical thinkers are cautious thinkers.

In a recent development, it’s much like hope for the best, initiative for the worst. It’s why we develop contingency plans; it’s why we buy insurance. It’s largely why I don’t go outside in a thunderstorm, despite the odds against getting hit by lightning. It’s why I buy a lotto ticket once every few weeks, when the thought occurs to me on the day of a draw (i.e., I will not notice the loss of a couple of euros [thankfully], whereas winning the lotto would be life-changing). You never know.

With that, context (as well as moderation) is key. It might be foolish to buy too much insurance, to spend €200 weekly on the lotto, or to waste your time hiding in a cellar for three hours during your run-of-the-mill thunderstorm. Again, caution is considered in terms of cost (be it time, money, or status) relative to benefit or risk avoidance.

From an epistemological perspective, an established truth is that we cannot see into the future (as of yet, anyway [there’s some doozy of a meta-joke in there]). We do not know what’s going to happen. Objectively, we can place likelihoods on certain events happening, but that’s it. It’s our human interpretation that labels these probabilities as impossible, unlikely, possible, likely, highly probable, definite, etc. We might say definite, but that’s not exactly a guarantee. Consider the late Daniel Kahneman’s work on weather and stock forecasting (see also Kahneman, 2011). If we lived in a world of certainty (which we don’t), you’d never hear the phrase, “Well, that was unexpected.”.

In moments of excitement and uncertainty, good thinkers are able to promptly evaluate probabilities, not just in terms of odds, but in terms of interpretation of outcomes (e.g., consider naturalistic decision-making [Klein, 2008]). The ability to do this quickly is a result of previous consideration and reflective judgment on priorities and values—things for which good thinkers would be concerned and cautious. They apply these considerations efficiently, if and when they need to. For example, despite less than a half-percent chance of your school burning down during your tenure in that school, how many fire drills were you a part of as a student? A lot, right? But you knew what to do in case it was for real.

Recent findings suggest that bizarrely, what’s your initiative for a zombie apocalypse? Stop for a moment and think about it... Did your answer come to you fairly swiftly? Even in the extreme unlikelihood of such a thing happening, it’s likely you thought about it or something similar before, at least once.

In a recent development, there’s nothing wrong with considering such things. Planning in such a way is a good cognitive exercise. It’s also good for creativity!

Cautious people often think in similar ways. Such cautious thinking might get accused of being “overthinking.” But that’s not necessarily a bad thing, unless it becomes a common or costly focus. Indeed, catastrophizing can be problematic. Moderation is important. But so too is caution, especially when it comes to ensuring good outcome-focused thinking. So, don’t sweat making small mistakes in life, especially if it’s done out of calculated caution. There’s nothing foolish about it.
