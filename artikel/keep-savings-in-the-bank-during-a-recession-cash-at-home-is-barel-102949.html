Keep Savings in the Bank During a Recession – Cash at Home Is Barely Insured

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, leave your savings in the bank when a recession looms, and keep your balance at each bank under the $250,000 insurance limit. Insured deposits have been paid in full through every downturn, while cash at home can be stolen or burned and gets only $200 of coverage under a typical homeowners policy.

Industry observers note that bank failures do rise in a recession, which is why pulling your savings out can feel like the careful move. It actually takes money out of a federally insured account and leaves it where almost nothing protects it.

The FDIC, the federal agency that insures bank deposits, says no depositor has lost insured money since it was founded in 1933. That record held through the last massive wave, when 465 banks failed from 2008 through 2012, by the FDIC’s count. Federally insured credit unions have similar coverage through the National Credit Union Administration, and none of their members has lost a cent of insured savings either.

Sources indicate that cash at home has no such backing. A burglar or a house fire can take all of it, and it earns nothing while inflation shrinks what it buys. The FDIC’s disaster guide suggests depositing large sums at an insured bank and holding a small amount of cash for food and supplies, since ATMs and credit cards may not work in a disaster.

Say a family withdraws $20,000 in a panic and hides it in a closet. If a fire or a break-in takes it, a typical homeowners policy covers only $200 of cash, according to the nonprofit Consumers’ Checkbook, so at least $19,800 is gone. Kept in an insured savings account paying 3.5% a year, the same $20,000 would be covered in full and earn $700 in its first year.

According to recent reports, hold your emergency fund, sized to your bare-bones bills, in an insured high-yield savings account. FDIC coverage is $250,000 per depositor, per bank, for each ownership category, meaning how you own the account, such as alone or jointly.

Total your balances at each bank before you move a dollar, and send only the amount over the limit to an account at a second insured bank.
