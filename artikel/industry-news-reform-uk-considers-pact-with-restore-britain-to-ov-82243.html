📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Thursday’s byelection in Castle Bromwich will be latest test for Nigel Farage’s party as rival eats into support.

Voters go to the polls in the village of Castle Bromwich in the West Midlands on Thursday in a little-noticed election that is the latest test for Reform at a time when it is particularly vulnerable.

Yet, while it is the Conservatives who are edging ahead in recent polls, the decisive difference could well be the performance of Restore Britain – the hard-right party set up by Rupert Lowe after the MP fell out with Nigel Farage.

Reform has lost no less than five elections since September, on one occasion by as little as two votes, after Restore attracted voters who might otherwise have voted for Farage’s party.

In a recent development, the possibility of offering an electoral pact to Restore is now under discussion among some figures at Reform’s Millbank HQ, the Guardian understands. There is also fervent internal opposition from some who balk at the ethno-nationalism that has found a home in Restore.

“Just take a look at some of the people who have attached themselves to Restore, as well as what some of their people have remarked about Jews and Muslims in their comments,” said one.

At the same time, and even after an aborted attempt to bury the hatchet between Lowe and Farage, others in Reform are leaving the door open to a reconciliation with Restore at a time when the party needs to find a way to overcome its stalling support.

“There’s an argument that we should be going after Restore and we’ll benefit from that, but there is also one where we work with them … and, to be honest, I don’t know the answer yet,” mentioned one.

One senior Reform source stated the idea of a “non-aggression pact” with Restore was in the air and there was a “deal to be done” but was more sceptical about a deeper tie up or merger.

At the same time, other senior Reform figures who are close to Farage are pushing for him to go much further towards a detente with Rupert Lowe, with efforts under way to push them back together.

Among Reform insiders, there is an acknowledgment that Farage is at least as exercised and frustrated about Restore eating into the party’s vote as he is about the investigations into his own financial affairs. One Reform politician said they envisaged Lowe ultimately being readmitted, but that the price would be the exit of Zia Yusuf, who had accused Lowe of assaulting him amid the furore that led to the MP’s suspension from the party.

However, that was before Yusuf this week appeared to praise Lowe in a conversation with the Spectator’s editor, Michael Gove. “Rupert is a force of nature,” said Yusuf, who went on to describe him as a “formidable person” in comments that drew a strikingly warm response from Lowe himself, who tweeted that he appreciated Yusuf’s acknowledgment of Restore’s progress.

“I suspect on policy and trust in politics, Yusuf and I have more in common than the former Tory ministers in Reform’s leadership,” he added.

Last week, there was a public display of Reform working with Restore when the former Tory MPs Andrew Rosindell and Suella Braverman signed an early day motion by Lowe opposing a Liberal Democrat bid to pave the way for census questions to include more ethnicity options to allow people to define themselves as Black English or Asian English.

Even within Restore, and away from the public rhetoric, there is curiosity about the openness of Reform to some form of pact as the speculation about an early general election persists.

“Our offer remains on the table and we are open to negotiating that offer,” remarked a Restore source, who cited events in the summer when it looked like a reconciliation between Farage and Lowe was in the offing.

“It is Farage and others who have outright rejected it, but the ball is in their court.”.

On that occasion, what seemed like tentative moves towards peace broke down amid a fresh row between the two leaders, with Lowe accusing Farage of lying after the latter had said he had reached out but had not heard back.

What remains clear now though is that Restore’s consistent nudging of 5% in serious national polls has taken some in its own ranks and even in Reform – whose leader predicted that its rival “would be lucky to get 1% anywhere” – by surprise. A Restore source said of Reform: “It feels like they’re having a bit of an identity crisis and falling below 20% has clearly given them pause for thought.”.

More stark has been the proliferation of Restore branches across the UK – sometimes numbering just a handful of people but in others much larger – and the steady dripfeed of defections from Reform.

Last week, those who joined Lowe at a lavish central London dinner for members of Restore’s “Cromwell Club” for those donating £2,500 a year included Holly Vallance, the estranged wife of Reform’s honorary treasurer Nick Candy, who had frequently been cited as a potential candidate for Farage’s party.

Also there was Charlton Edwards, who was Reform’s treasurer until recently and a figure at the very heart of the courting of donors by Farage’s party.

Aside from the beef between Farage and Lowe – arguably the biggest alpha males on the right – the biggest stumbling block to a pact is the antisemitism within Restore, said one source in Reform, which has made a concerted effort to court the British Jewish community.

In a recent development, “Restore emerged because Rupert was out for revenge and it’s hard to ignore. But there are also things that have been mentioned by people around Restore about Jews, Muslims, that we find deeply uncomfortable,” they said, citing the explicit antisemitism directed towards Restore’s Molly Kingsley online when Lowe unveiled her as his party’s “accountability” spokesperson.

Recent findings suggest that figures such as Charlie Downes, a Restore election candidate who is often seen by Lowe’s side, have said that only white Christians can truly be British. Another Restore member who was pictured alongside Lowe at last week’s Cromwell Club dinner, the former GB News presenter Calvin Robinson, has been condemned in recent days for comments in which he talked of the need to “drive out the Jews” from the US.

However, extreme language can also be found in Reform. Last week, the Welsh Senedd member Joe Martin became the first frontline Reform politician to use the term “remigration” – one that originated on the far right and is frequently used by Restore.

Others in Reform, meanwhile, are impressed by the social media strategy of Restore, which has on occasion been backed by Elon Musk.
