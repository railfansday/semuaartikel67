Aging reflects shared humanity; we are all in this together. Yet perceived purpose in life is a factor that we can improve. Research explores how to forge a positive path forward at any age.

Yiqing Yang and Kelin Li (2026) explored pathways to perceiving aging in older adults.[i] They acknowledge that self-perceptions of aging (SPA), defined as personal experiences, beliefs, and evaluations of the aging process, predict behavioral health outcomes as people grow older. Encompassing both positive and negative assessments, SPAs include perceptions of purpose, growth, and resilience, as well as concerns about physical, mental, or social decline. Yang and Li note that people experience aging differently, some seeing a decline in energy or physical ability while others enjoy improved emotional regulation. Not surprisingly, they recognize SPA as a robust predictor of physical and mental health, cognitive functioning, health-related behavior, and longevity.

Recent findings suggest that studying a racially diverse sample of adults aged 65 and older, Yang and Li identified some positive and negative predictors of SPA. One of the most significant was purpose in life, which consistently predicted higher positive SPA and lower negative SPA, making it an essential psychological resource over time. They note that purpose in life may prompt both meaning and direction, supporting an adaptive interpretation of aging. Accordingly, purpose can be viewed as an accumulated asset that may help older adults see aging as continuous growth as opposed to inevitable decline. This reframing can also protect against what Yang and Li refer to as internalized age stereotypes through maintaining a forward-looking orientation, counteracting age-related loss or perceived constraints.

Industry observers note that yang and Li note that their results also emphasize the concept of SPA as multidimensional. Positive SPA was linked with psychological strengths such as life satisfaction, purpose, and self-esteem, as well as a robust social life and religious affiliation. They interpret these findings as suggesting that maintaining a favorable view of aging is developed through psychological well-being, meaningful social engagement, and a sense of belonging. They contrast these positive outcomes with negative SPA, which was linked with negative outcomes such as having a limited social network, perceived constraints, more health problems, and higher religiosity, indicating that negative perceptions of aging may be explained less by the lack of resources than by perceptions of restrictions, loss, and declining autonomy.

Yang and Li advise caution when interpreting their findings regarding religious involvement. They observe that religious affiliation produces social connection and identity benefits that bolster positive views about aging, although a higher degree of religiosity may suggest different coping styles and responses to stress or health challenges that are linked to negative SPA. They therefore acknowledge the importance of assessing multiple dimensions of individual religious involvement, as opposed to viewing spirituality as a single construct. This advice is consistent with the reality that different religions have different belief systems, including views of the afterlife, which will effect older people differently than their younger counterparts.

In a recent development, celebrating purpose in life can promote adaptive attitudes about aging and mitigate negative self-perception. It is never too late to be thankful for your circumstances, including faith, family, and friends, as well as your ability to continue to grow emotionally and spiritually. By taking stock of your blessings, which include not only worldly wealth but wisdom, you can refresh and renew a sense of purpose in life, as well as health, happiness, and hope for the future.
