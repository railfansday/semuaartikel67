Around 265,000 customers potentially affected as London transport operator grapples with another security breach.

Transport for London (TfL) has copped to another cybersecurity breach, this time involving the account details of around 265,000 Oyster photocard customers.

The capital's transport operator told The Register it first became aware something was amiss on November 16, when Oyster photocard customers started receiving emails asking them to create accounts they already had. The emails came from TfL's own systems, the transport body confirmed, but were generated as a result of the compromise.

Sources indicate that "We immediately took the TfL photocard website down while this was being investigated," remarked Shashi Verma, TfL's director of technology strategy and revenue. "The protection of personal information is a priority for us, and I apologize to our customers affected by this incident.".

Sources indicate that tfL reckons its speedy response spared a considerable number of customers from having their details compromised. The transport operator notes it has since contained the incident and contacted those potentially affected, though it is rather more forthcoming about what the hackers didn't get their hands on than what they did.

TfL notes no passwords, bank or payment card details, or customer journey information were compromised. It also believes the names and dates of birth of children with Zip Oyster cards were unaffected.

Among those whose accounts may have been compromised are holders of Zip Oyster cards – issued to children as young as five – as well as students, apprentices, care leavers, veterans, and Londoners aged 60 and over.

Industry observers note that despite the assurances about passwords, TfL indicates it will ask all photocard customers to change theirs, along with their security questions and answers. Those in the habit of recycling passwords across multiple websites are being advised to change them elsewhere, too.

TfL claims it has yet to find any evidence that the compromised data has been used and has notified the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO). The transport operator hasn't disclosed how the perpetrators got into its systems.

Recent findings suggest that existing photocards will still let holders through the ticket barriers, though anyone needing to renew one or check their journey history will have to contact TfL's customer services team while the website is out of action. There's no word yet on when it'll be back.

"We hope to restore the affected website services as promptly as possible," Verma said.

TfL should be well versed in dealing with data breaches by now. In March, the transport operator told The Register it had notified more than 7 million customers following its September 2024 cyberattack. The hackers had accessed systems holding customers' names, email addresses, and home addresses, while around 5,000 customers were contacted as a priority because their bank account details may also have been compromised.

Sources indicate that two teenagers were subsequently charged in connection with the attack, which authorities linked to the notorious Scattered Spider cybercrime crew. The ICO looked into that incident but opted not to take enforcement action against TfL, having deemed its response proportionate. There's nothing at present to suggest the two incidents are related.

According to recent reports, londoners can at least rest assured that their Oyster photocards still work. Whether the information they handed over to obtain them is in someone else's hands is another matter. ®.
