To Write ‘The Brightness,’ Chad Harbach Planned a Friends Trip to Block Island

This is Write Here, a column where authors and literary luminaries tell us about the travels they took to find inspiration and create their latest books.

In this installment, author and n+1 editor Chad Harbach describes his love for Block Island, Rhode Island, and a 2016 trip that informed a keystone part of his latest novel The Brightness. In the book, a bildungsroman that tackles topics like campus politics, the discourse around sexual assault, and the anxiety of being in one’s twenties, Block Island serves as the dramatic setting for a long, eventful wedding night that alters the lives of Harbach’s characters—particularly that of Pella Affenlicht, who originally appeared in Harbach’s 2011 debut The Art of Fielding. Below, Harbach recalls gathering his own friends for a summer holiday on Block Island as an excuse to refamiliarize himself with the destination as he began to write it in fiction—and the local peafowl that nearly made it into the book.

“In the early stages of writing The Brightness, one of the first things I decided was that the book’s centerpiece, the substantial wedding of the character Owen, would be located on Block Island, Rhode Island. I wanted the scene—which is now this 150-page stretch of the story, when all sorts of crazy things happen over a single, almost Shakespearean night—to be on the island’s eastern shore where houses are positioned at the top of these stone cliffs. I pictured an outdoor dance floor overlooking the beach and the ocean with the moon in the distance. Essentially I wanted to write the destination wedding I desperately wanted to attend. But as I was working on it, I realized I needed to go back there to check if my memories of the place were accurate, to have it fresh in my mind.

According to recent reports, my then-partner and I organized a trip to Block Island with a large group of friends in the summer of 2016. We rented a big house and folks came from places like New York, Philadelphia, California, and Amsterdam. It felt like a wedding in that sense—everyone gathered for this joyous celebration in a beautiful place. We didn’t do all the terrible stuff to one another that’s maybe required for the juicy plot of a novel, but we did spend eight days cooking together, playing the piano, singing songs, drinking a lot, and staying up very late.

In a recent development, but we couldn’t sleep in very late because, well, there were these peahens. There was a sort of farm with animals, maybe a petting zoo, near our house, and their peahens liked to come and roost in the trees on our property. Every morning at 5 a.m., after we had been up until 2 or 3 a.m., the peahens would just start. I don’t know if you’ve ever heard the squawking of peahens, but suffice to say it was awful. It’s funny: When I started doing publicity for The Brightness, an announcement mentioned that part of it is set on Block Island. I got texts from friends who were on that trip, saying, congrats, we’re so excited, the fucking peahens better be in there. I initially did have this whole bit about the peahens, but there was so much going on in the plot that I cut them—not without really trying to make it work first.

Other than the loud birds, it was a great trip. The place struck me as just as magical as it had when I first went at 19 years old, when I was in college. I had a friend whose grandparents had a house there. I’ve visited several times since, and always loved it. But this was the only time I’ve ever gone somewhere for book research, and I was able to lock in some granular details like what the downtown looks like, the shape of the buildings, the sight of the marshlands, and what it’s like to bike out to the airport.

I was also excited to find that the feel of it was exactly as I remembered. Block Island has this rugged, natural beauty—it’s a rocky, tree-filled place surrounded by ocean, where everyone just rides bikes around, and part of it is a protected nature preserve. It’s sort of the forgotten sibling of Nantucket or Martha’s Vineyard. Some people don’t even know that Block Island exists, which keeps it from changing too much, even though it was settled by Europeans in the mid-1600s. The cemetery there has the tombstones of families that date back centuries, long before the United States became a country. I think some of those same families still live on the island now. So there’s this sense of continuity and timelessness.

Recent findings suggest that even before you reach Block Island, there’s just something about climbing on to the ferry and heading out into the open water, this freedom and openness that’s very inspiring to me. I grew up in Wisconsin, on Lake Michigan, which isn’t an ocean but looks like one, and I’ve always loved that feeling of vastness and mystery that comes with a massive body of water—that literal depth. It reminds me that we as humans actually haven’t mastered everything, and there are many things in this world that are not easy for us to touch.

All of this made Block Island a helpful setting in The Brightness. What happens on this wedding night propels the characters toward life paths that are very different from what they thought they were going to have, and having the story be imbued with the island’s sense of openness, possibility, and what might be feels fitting. As a writer, that can be part of the work: tapping into that feeling or gestalt of a place. It can be very easy to do at times, and difficult at others. And, you know, sometimes it’s just hard to write. So it was a great help writing about somewhere I love visiting literally and creatively. Instead of resisting sitting down at my desk, I’d be like, I’m ready to go to Block Island. It’s this setting I could picture well, enjoy being in, and keep traveling to—even if it was just in my imagination.” —As told to Matt Ortile.
