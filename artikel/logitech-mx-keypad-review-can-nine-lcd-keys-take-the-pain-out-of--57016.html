Logitech MX Keypad review: Can nine LCD keys take the pain out of Photoshop?

Keys: 9 customisable LCD keys, 15 pages.

Connection: Wired USB-C, 1.5m USB-C to USB-C.

Dimensions: 91.7 x 77.9 x 25.5mm.

I’m always drawn to gadgets that promise to save time or make things easier and more accessible, whether it's the best drawing tablets, shortcut keyboards, or odd little controllers parked next to my mouse: if it claims to shave seconds off a repetitive job, I want to try it. Photoshop is my worst offender, because half the battle is remembering where Adobe has hidden things this time with each latest patch.

The Logitech MX Keypad is a desktop control pad with nine customisable LCD keys, each showing an icon for whatever you’ve assigned to it. Two physical buttons flip between pages, so you get more shortcuts without another huge peripheral crowding your desk. It's in the Logitech MX line, gadgets aimed as much at creatives as at everyday folk looking to make life easier.

Industry observers note that at $99.99 / £89.99, it’s not an impulse buy but something you should consider, and after years of drilling Ctrl, Alt and Shift combos into my left hand, I wasn’t sure I needed one. I also use a drawing tablet and stylus for art, so I have just enough buttons on the pen, or I can access more on my tablet's virtual keyboard. But knowing a shortcut and having a button for the thing you hit countless times an hour, whether it's in Photoshop, calling up Google files or browser pages, is a different matter. That's where the MX keypad aims to be a decent all-rounder for everyday work and creative projects alike.

According to recent reports, nine square keys sit in a three-by-three grid, and because each has its own screen, the icons change based on what you’ve assigned. That beats a traditional macro pad with fixed labels: you can see what a button does instead of memorising obscure combos.

Industry observers note that it works with the best digital art software and creative apps, including Adobe Photoshop, Illustrator, Lightroom, Premiere Pro, and Figma. Importantly, it’s context-aware, so you can select your app in Logitech Options+ and it loads presets for that software’s most common tools; from there, you can edit or set up your own actions.

Nine keys run out fast, so using up to 15 'pages' of nine preset buttons (135 total assigned actions) earns it keep as a feature. Overkill, yes, but some will want all that power at a button press. The Options+ app lets you build separate layouts for different jobs, one for Photoshop, another for Lightroom and so on. Rather than living with Adobe’s shortcuts, you build the layout around how you work, and it's as easy as going into the Options+ app and assigning keyboard shortcuts to the buttons, including combinations of buttons, and it supports different saved shortcut collections, so you can also tailor favourites to different apps.

At roughly 92 x 78 x 25mm, it sits beside a mouse and keyboard without taking over, and it's easy to hold, which matters if your desk already hosts a pen tablet or display. A kickstand and a 1.5m USB-C cable come in the box.

Sources indicate that it’s not perfect. The keys lack the click of good mechanical keys, but they do have an old-fashioned soft-push feel, much like an old 90s dial-up digital phone, but that also means you need a little force to push one, and the stand lacks weight or 'stick', so it drifts as you press. The solution? Hold it by the top and depress with my thumb. I do like how each button has its own little digital decal icon showing its function, and you can buy sticky pads to keep it in place.

It’s also wired only, which is a little surprising at this price, but it's not the end of the world, and the cable is long enough for most setups.

It has been revealed that for me, the MX Keypad makes the most sense with Photoshop (I've also downloaded and set it up for Clip Studio Paint, CorelDRAW, Google, and Slack), and even fast keyboard customers end up wishing something were easier to reach. Favourite brushes, colour swapping and latest layers all suit the MX Keypad, and you can split them across pages for selections, masking, painting, and layer management. I’m a light Photoshop user, but I found that adding shortcuts for Lasso selections, favourite brushes, and layer options sped up how I use the software.

When I’m drawing, I don’t want to look down at the keyboard or lift my hand off the tablet, so a few key actions right beside my drawing area help. For the most part, I find the custom buttons on a stylus work well alongside touch controls on a tablet, but if you are more professionally minded and need all the options all the time, a shortcut keypad is essential. While XPPen, Huion, and Xencelabs all offer good remotes, the MX Keypad is a step above, with more buttons, more configuration options, and easy-to-understand icons and button labels.

If you’re already quick on a keyboard, reaching across to press a button may not be any faster. The gain is in the visual reminders and having rarely used commands grouped on pages somewhere easy to find. With only nine keys, you’ll need to plan your pages carefully, but for serious users, once organised, you'll be able to cycle to function collections at speed.

According to recent reports, of course, not all apps are supported; for example, Rebelle and ArtRage aren’t here, but you can add the apps manually and set up profiles and shortcuts based on keyboard commands; it just takes more time than Logitech’s preset app library. Using presets is as simple as clicking a drop-down menu, but adding and editing a latest app setup and customising each key can take around 30 minutes per 'page'.

According to recent reports, because profiles are set per app, the buttons change colour and icon designs as you switch software. I found this to be the MX Keypad's strength, because it becomes one device that can follow you around your creative workflow, so if you’re someone who works between software, from Blender to Photoshop, for example, the MX Keypad can follow along.

It has been revealed that options+, the same app used by the MX Master 4 Special Edition mouse, can configure everything, but it takes some time to get used to if you’re editing a upcoming app that isn’t supported out of the box. If you use the most popular, most common software, it’s easy; if you use niche apps, it can feel far from plug-and-play, since you have to manually enter each keyboard command.

For artists and designers who bounce between the biggest and most common apps, it’s a compact way to build your own control pad, and LCD keys beat cryptic labels on a standard macro pad or half-remembered keyboard shortcuts.

At $99.99 / £89.99, the Logitech MX Keypad is a fair price for a quick key remote. By comparison, the Xencelabs Quick Keys cost a similar $99.99 / £95.90 and also have LCD icons, but only words and no images, while the decent XPPen Remote is $49.99 / £49.99 but more basic than both Xencelabs and the Logitech. Both are wireless remotes, unlike the MX Keypad, but as a shortcut remote, the MX Keypad is the best of the three because Options+ offers an easy setup with established software and a huge number of buttons and pages.

Industry observers note that but the MX Keypad has more going for it than those remotes: more custom buttons, more context-aware presets, and the option to use ‘pages’, which packs a staggering number of functions onto one face. The big advantage over a traditional drawing tablet remote is two-fold: you get little icons that explain each function, plus the depth of Options+ with multiple pre-loaded shortcuts for the best graphic design software and art apps out of the box.

Do beginners and hobbyists like me need this much choice? I mostly use two to three apps, for example, and three or four functions, and I don't need speed, but if you're a pro with a library of art, design, 3D, and video editing software to pair with and move across in one project, it would be a good gadget. For pros and tinkerers, it’s essential; for everyone else, it may be overkill.
