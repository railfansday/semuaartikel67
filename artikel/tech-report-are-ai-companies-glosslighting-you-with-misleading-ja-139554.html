Beware: you may have been glosslit by the AI industry. “Glosslighting” combines “glossary” and “gaslighting.” It’s for situations where industries develop lingo that intentionally misleads consumers in order to shape public perception—something that researchers at Simon Fraser University think the artificial intelligence industry is doing in spades. Every industry has its own specialized vocab, and there’s nothing inherently wrong with insider lingo. But with an industry like AI, where development is hotly debated and potential controversy lies around every corner, word choice takes on extra weight. What is glosslighting? For the study, researchers at the university in British Columbia analyzed 30 of the AI industry’s biggest buzzwords, breaking down the ways that common language around the technology can control public opinion. The study’s authors emphasize that while AI terms have been looked at in isolation, they’re more interested in AI’s “interlocking vocabulary” and the way it protects AI companies from taking accountability for the dark sides of their technology. “Behind the language of seamless intelligence are workers who classify traumatic content for low wages, communities whose water is consumed by server farms, and individuals whose lives are determined by probabilistic decision tools sometimes named, reassuringly, as ‘solutions,’” the authors write. How AI jargon causes harm The study’s authors group the terms by the way they cause harm, the truth about AI they obscure, and the questions they deflect. Those groupings include the following: “Hallucination,” “copilot,” “robot rights,” and “agentic AI” humanize the technology, diverting attention from the people and companies making the decisions around them. “The cloud,” “data mining,” “scaling,” and “human-in-the-loop” hide the environmental and social costs of AI development. “Superintelligence,” “frontier AI,” “AGI,” “existential risk,” “smart city,” “AI superpower,” and “race to AI” shift focus to the future of AI, limiting attention on present-day concerns. “Democratizing AI,” “open AI,” “responsible AI,” “ethical AI,” “collaborative AI,” and “human-centric AI” let AI companies frame themselves as trustworthy. “Bias,” “ex-ante risk regulation,” “deep learning,” and “data subjects” paint some problems as technological or too complex for laypeople to understand, shutting down debates about their usage before they begin The study’s authors go on to recommend alternatives for many of these terms that simplify communication, demystify confusing terms, or go in depth to provide greater accuracy to descriptions of AI’s impact. One author, Nicholas Vincent, an assistant professor of computing science, told Tech Xplore that “issues where there’s real complexity mean there’s a responsibility to do more to help people understand.” “Everyone exposed to AI deserves accurate descriptions of what it is and how it’s used,” Vincent noted. “As consumers get a little bit pickier, there’s going to be more demand for specifics. That is, ‘If you want me to buy your expensive AI product, you better have a pretty clear sales pitch for what it actually is.’”.
