Recent findings suggest that golden Board is a binary message designed to teach chess to an unknown recipient without a shared language, culture, or prior knowledge of the game. It begins by teaching the format needed to read its own contents.

The full project code and documentation are available on GitHub.

Recent findings suggest that this guide follows that process: recover numbers from the bits, use them to reconstruct pictures and interactions, then learn how those pictures describe chess. One complete game connects the layers, from its physical bits to its final displayed position.

Before we dive in, I'd like to clarify a few things about the project.

Sources indicate that in a nutshell, Golden Board is a sequence of 4,194,304 bits, or 512 KiB of ones and zeros. That number suggests arranging them into a 2,048 × 2,048 square.

Recent findings suggest that the first 64 rows contain 32 drawings, each 64 × 64 cells. Together they form the bootstrap: it teaches how to read the rest of the message. It's chess-agnostic and can be used to decode other structured messages.

The payload below contains visual chess lessons, exercises with feedback, complete games, and the information needed to find, check and display them.

I took inspiration from messages intended for unknown intelligences, such as the Voyager Golden Record, Lincos, and others.

But instead of sharing scientific knowledge or works of art, Golden Board teaches chess.

First, a small set of rules opens up an enormous world of possibilities, ideas and beauty.

Second, a game can share more than knowledge: it offers a glimpse of the human mind.

I ran trials using LLMs as stand-ins for unknown recipients, testing decoding and understanding with raw bits, decoded content, and varying levels of assistance.

In a recent development, correctly recovering the bytes and understanding the lessons are separate tests. These models already know chess, so correct chess answers alone cannot establish that the message taught them. The trials helped identify ambiguities and improve the design; they do not establish that an unknown intelligence would understand it. Read the trial account.

No set of 64 games is complete.

I chose games for their chess: brilliant attacks, surprising sacrifices, clever defenses, quiet strategy, and beautiful endings. The PGN anthology contains 64 games, while the binary message omits names, dates, and events.

There is a special joy in sharing something you love.

What could we share with a civilization that knows nothing about us? They might already know our science or lack the context for our art, but chess can be taught from scratch, revealing centuries of human imagination, mistakes, and brilliance.

Most likely, no one will ever receive this message. But even a small chance that someone might discover a position with a move like Bf5 makes the whole thing worth trying.
