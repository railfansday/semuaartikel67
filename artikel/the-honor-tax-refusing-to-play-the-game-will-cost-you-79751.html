The Honor Tax: Refusing To Play The Game Will Cost You

With Anthropic finally listed on the Nasdaq under the ticker ANT, it’s a good time to talk about honor, money, opportunity, and happiness.

Recent findings suggest that I put in an indication for 1,300 shares at $83 and got 60 shares. That’s 4.6% of my indication, or about $5,000 out of $107,900. I entered my order the morning the books opened, hoping that would signal I was a serious buyer.

But naively, I indicated a true order. I wanted up to $107,900 of stock to reach my goal of roughly $500,000 in Anthropic exposure through traditional VC, public VC, and direct ownership.

The embarrassing part is that I should have known better. During my 13 years at Goldman Sachs and Credit Suisse, I worked on over 100 IPOs. I went on roadshows, introduced management to my institutional clients, and read demand back to the syndicate desk. Everybody padded their orders. A fund that wanted 500,000 shares would put in for 2 million because it knew the syndicate desk would cut it back.

It has been revealed that the problem with being honorable is that you lose when everybody else is inflating their order to get their true allocation. I thought being a Fidelity customer for over 25 years, with my kids’ 529 plans, custodial accounts, Roth IRAs, a SEP IRA, and Solo 401(k)s, would count for something. It clearly didn’t. To them, I’m just a number, like most people who work are.

The allocation process reminded me that if you want to compete in this hyper-competitive world, you have to work the system to your advantage. If you don’t, you will likely lose to those who do.

To protest my insultingly low allocation, I decided to take the day off. But that’s what I do every day anyway.

Here are some real-life examples where playing fair cost money, time, and happiness.

Recent findings suggest that when my wife and I applied to preschools for our son, we decided not to ask our network to write letters of recommendation to the school heads. Despite having friends who were significant donors and board members, I wanted to see if we could get in on our own merits as a high school tennis coach and his editor wife.

We could not. Six preschools rejected us after happily taking our $75 to $100 application fees. We only got into our neighborhood preschool through serendipity. We kept running into one of their teachers on paternity leave in Golden Gate Park.

If we had leaned on our connections, we would have saved hundreds in application fees, dozens of hours, and a lot of stress.

In a recent development, the families who got in weren’t less honorable. They were more effective. Our honor tax came to six rejection letters and several hundred dollars in application fees.

I first thought about starting Financial Samurai in 2006, the year I graduated from Cal’s MBA program. But I didn’t because I felt I needed at least 10 years of finance experience before I had the credibility to write publicly.

Sources indicate that so I waited until the world was falling apart in 2009, 10 years after graduating from William & Mary. The three-year delay cost me revenue, likely earlier book deals, a bigger reputation, and probably pushed back my exit from finance by at least a year. I wrote more about this in Don’t Let Honor And Pride Keep You On Hard Mode Forever.

Recent findings suggest that the internet has shown me time and again that you do NOT need expertise in what you’re talking about or selling. A not-rich 26-year-old can write a book on how to get rich. Someone whose marriage is falling apart and doesn't have kids can host a podcast for couples with kids. Someone with a $100,000 portfolio can become an investing guru on social media.

Recent findings suggest that plenty of people overlook your experience or credentials. So you might as well put yourself out there and start.

After 13 years of grade school and four years of college, you might have too much pride to accept money from your parents. Making it on your own feels like the honorable thing to do. It’s also good for your self-esteem.

Sources indicate that so you sleep on a friend’s couch and lose weight because you can barely afford groceries. Meanwhile, your college friends rent nice two-bedrooms in the city and go out every weekend because their parents are paying. Some even buy their first homes and have their girlfriends move in, courtesy of the Bank of Mom and Dad.

Sources indicate that you, on the other hand, turn down an amazing job offer in Newly introduced York City because it’s way too expensive. Instead, you stay in Des Moines, Iowa, take a $60,000 job with benefits, and feel like you’ve made it.

In a recent development, at your 10-year college reunion, you’ve finally saved $100,000 and are ready to settle down. But the girl you liked moved to NYC to be with your college buddy, who is now a director at a hedge fund making $1 million a year.

Sources indicate that he’s worth $7 million and no longer needs his parents’ help. So he buys a $6 million, three-bedroom condo on the Upper West Side, marries your dream girl, and starts a family.

Industry observers note that his parents’ help wasn’t a handout. It was a launch pad. Your pride was an anchor. More parents would rather help their adult children while still alive than after they are gone. Accept help with grace.

Sources indicate that in 2012, I wanted out of banking. The classic move would have been to give two weeks’ notice, thank everyone, and walk away. That’s what most people do, leaving their deferred compensation and any chance of severance on the table.

Instead, I negotiated. I offered to train my junior colleague to take over my business, which gave management a smooth transition. In exchange, I kept five years of deferred compensation and walked away with a six-figure severance check. That money gave me a multi-year runway to grow Financial Samurai and remain free.

Had I just quit, my honor tax would have been five years of deferred compensation plus a six-figure severance check. That’s an expensive way to feel good about yourself, when companies treat you as a number and promptly move on once you’re gone.

I break down the exact process in How To Engineer Your Layoff if you're looking to leave with a severance package as well.

You’d have to be naive to think a wealthy parent who virtue signals about how amazing public schools are, but sends their own kids to private school, would ever give up their kid’s college spot for yours.

Recent findings suggest that the world is extremely cutthroat. Yes, you will have good friends and good people who try to help you. But in most competitive situations, you’re up against people who care about themselves and their families first.

In my IPO example, my competitors were the thousands of other retail investors who wanted shares. If I had inflated my order by 10X, I might have gotten about $50,000 of stock instead of $5,000. With a 15% first-day pop, that’s a $7,500 gain instead of $750.

It has been revealed that so whatever credentials or goodwill you have, don’t assume they mean much. At every competitive juncture, you must speak up and market yourself. People will forget who you are and what you’ve done for them if you don’t remind them.

Working the system doesn’t mean lying, cheating, or stepping on people. It means understanding how the game is actually scored so you stop paying an honor tax you don’t owe. Here’s how.

1) Inventory your unfair advantages. Write down your network, capital, credentials, platform, free time, and family support. Most people underestimate what they have because they’ve never made the list.

2) Learn how the game is really scored. IPO books get padded. Admissions offices respond to relationships and donations. Many jobs get filled through referrals before they’re ever posted. Find an insider and ask how decisions actually get made. The official rules are often only half the story.

3) Make the ask early. A referral, an introduction, or a note from a board member costs your friend five minutes. Getting rejected six times costs you far more. Most people are happy to help. They just need to be asked.

Sources indicate that 4) Negotiate everything with leverage. A competing job offer, a severance package, a retention offer from your cable tech giant, a lower rate from your lender. If you don’t ask, the answer is always no.

Industry observers note that 5) Accept help with gratitude, then pay it forward. Taking help from parents, mentors, or friends isn’t weakness. It’s compounding. The honorable part is what you do with the head start.

6) Remind people of your value. Send the upgrade email. Check in with the fund manager. Tell your longtime broker you’d like a bigger allocation next time. Silence gets read as indifference.

Industry observers note that 7) Know your line and your risk. Padding an IPO order is common, but there’s a catch. If demand comes in weaker than expected, you could get filled on everything you asked for. Never inflate an order you can’t afford to pay for. Game the system, but don’t break the law or your word.

Be honorable with your family, your friends, and anyone who trusts you. Keep your word. Pay your debts.

Industry observers note that but when you’re competing for a scarce resource against strangers, following an honor code nobody else follows isn’t noble. It’s expensive. And the cost compounds over decades in the form of lost money, lost time, and lost happiness.

Industry observers note that if you’re a parent, it’s your responsibility to fight to give your children every chance to survive and thrive. Your kids won’t care that you were too proud to make a phone call. They’ll care whether they got the opportunity.

It has been revealed that as I wrap up this post, I have a confession. Maybe honor wasn’t the only reason I placed a true order.

Recent findings suggest that between my traditional venture funds and public venture funds, I already own a meaningful amount of Anthropic. So deep down, I probably felt my exposure was significant enough. If I had really needed those shares, I suspect I would have padded my order like everybody else.

In other words, maybe I just didn’t care that much if my order got cut by 95%. I’m at a stage in life where I should have enough. Missing out on a $7,500 first-day pop won’t change how I live, where my kids go to school, or what time I wake up tomorrow.

Recent findings suggest that which makes me wonder whether our actions show our real financial position better than our words do. The person who fights hardest for every share, every school spot, and every introduction is often the person who feels they don’t have enough yet. They say they are financially independent, yet stay for one more year at a job they hate. The person who shrugs may simply be full.

So before you judge yourself for being too honorable, or someone else for working the system, ask what each of you actually needs.

Sometimes the honor tax is a choice. Sometimes it’s just a sign you can afford to pay it.

Have you ever lost out because you played by the rules while others didn’t? Did you pad your order in the Anthropic IPO, and how much did you get relative to your true demand? Where do you draw the line between leveraging your advantages and gaming the system unfairly?

In a recent development, would you accept financial help from your parents to get ahead, or is making it on your own still worth the price? And if you have kids, how hard are you willing to work your network to give them a better shot?

Do you think your willingness to play hardball shrinks once you feel you have enough?

Most people don’t fall behind financially because they’re lazy. They fall behind because they play by rules nobody else follows. They accept the first offer, wait their turn, and hope someone notices.
