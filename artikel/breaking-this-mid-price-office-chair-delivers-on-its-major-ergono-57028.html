Recent findings suggest that the Sihoo Doro C300 Pro V2 is a worthy upgrade to its predecessor, cleverly interlinking back and lumbar systems so you're completely supported as you move around in the chair. It's still not the most attractive of chairs, and assembly could be more straightforward, but once it's up and running, you'll find levels of ergonomics that actually belie its $499.99 / £459.99 price.

Chairs seem to come in pairs. I've been getting a lot of chairs in for review recently, with the remarkably similar Secretlab Titan Evo and the DXRacer Martian Pro showing up around the same time. Now, having just reviewed the Boulies EP600, the Sihoo Doro C300 Pro V2 has taken my focus – and they have so much in common, it’s like they were separated at birth.

The design of the Sihoo Doro C300 Pro V2 is almost a mirror image of the Boulies EP600: Both feature curvy three-piece back panels with responsive lumbar supports, pull-out footrests, and they’re roughly the same price. But where the Boulies EP600 was feature-packed and poorly executed, the Sihoo Doro C300 Pro V2 nails the execution, and it’s definitely one of the best office chairs for back pain. It’s also a marked improvement on its predecessor, the Sihoo Doro C300 Pro.

Industry observers note that like most of Sihoo’s chairs, the Doro C300 Pro makes use of mesh throughout. There’s nothing wrong with that: mesh makes chairs lighter and easier to pack, but it lacks the sink-in sumptuousness of cushioned chairs, including its own budget-friendly Sihoo M18.

According to recent reports, however, where the Sihoo Doro C300 Pro gets everything absolutely right is in its engineering. While it can feel a little clunky and firm to start with, once it’s dialled-in it reaches astonishing levels of comfort with the versatility to move around and change positions.

This begins with the lumbar support, which naturally moves and follows your lower back as you lean backwards and forwards. Unlike the aggressive system in the Boulies EP600, it flows with you and never becomes intrusive.

Sources indicate that sihoo also includes a sacral support system here, which cradles the heart-shaped sacrum bone at the base of your spine with a gentle pressure I’ve never felt before. It’s one of the best lumbar systems I’ve used, and it’s adjustable so you can hone it just as you want it.

In a recent development, the lumbar support moves alongside the rest of the chair as you shift around; lean back and the armrests follow the backrest and the seat cushion moves forwards. It's always got your back (literally), and it delivers on the promise that the automated Hbada X7 failed to deliver, and it does so without motors or AI.

According to recent reports, endless adjustments don’t end there: the armrests can also be tweaked and rotated to optimise your comfort levels, apparently through eight dimensions. This sounds like it might break the very fabric of reality, but if I’m going to implode with an alternative-dimension version of myself, at least I can do so with comfortable elbows.

One neat feature here is that the rear portion of the armrests flips up and locks into place, giving you a little more comfort when you’re reading, sketching, or diving back into another 16-hour GTA VI binge, and the (optional) mesh leg rest also helps you relax into a state of utter relaxation as you cause chaos in Vice City.

In a recent development, sihoo reckons the chair will suit anyone up to 6’ 3”, and it fits my 6’ 4” height perfectly, with the headrest in just the right place. Sihoo has also upgraded the weight accommodation to a maximum of 136 kg, so this is a chair that can be used by pretty much anybody and everybody.

Beren’s take on the original C300 Pro was that it was one ugly motherfu…rniture. The Sihoo Doro C300 Pro V2 doesn’t break the mould in terms of looks, and it’s almost indistinguishable from other ultra-adjustable ergonomic office chairs: it’s the Boulies EP600, it’s the Hinomi H2 Pro.

These chairs tend to be triumphs of function over form, although I’m not sure anyone outside of Gold's Gym will have shoulders wide enough to justify the upper width of the backrest. Where they differentiate themselves is in the view from the back.

The Sihoo Doro C300 Pro V2 looks reasonably attractive: the headrest’s large “eyes” and its nose-like supports actually recall Gonzo a little bit, while the armrests, in grey, have a friendly cetacean look about them. It’s still not the best-looking chair, but these touches add a playfulness that reduces its dour, generic design.

Choosing the grey colourway Sihoo sent us also helps lighten the heaviness of the design and stops it looking like a predatory black blob in front of your desk.

It has been revealed that take note: this is a herculean, 27kg chair that comes in two boxes, and not something you’ll want to be moving vertically once assembled. Within one of those boxes you’ll also find my pet peeve with Sihoo chairs: having to put the base together and insert those 10 screws. I’ve done it countless times and it never gets easier or more fun. It makes me think about my own mortality.

Recent findings suggest that sihoo’s assembly process is nowhere near as straightforward as, say, Branch’s. There is a fair amount of cross-referencing to be done between the manual, the screw blister-pack, and the chair itself, to the extent that I thought an escape room-style door would open somewhere when I’d completed it. The armrests connect via washers that seem a little too big for their holes, and they take a bit of brute force to get them in place.

Industry observers note that sihoo has kept the number of steps involved to the absolute minimum, and when it’s done it feels remarkably sturdy. But the niggling annoyances in its construction do add up, and it feels like there are plenty of opportunities for Sihoo to simplify things. They can start with that wretched base.

In a recent development, office chair pricing is so complicated and fluid that it can be hard to find a coherent and consistent price, but apparently the Sihoo Doro C300 Pro V2 carries an RRP of $499.99 / £409.99, with the footrest costing an extra $40 / £50, but these prices are often discounted. The Sihoo Doro C300 Pro V1 was a frankly absurd RRP of $699 when Beren reviewed it; thankfully, Sihoo seems to have made its pricing more sensible since then.

In a recent development, is it worth it? I’d say a definite yes; despite my grumbles about the construction process, this is a comfortable and innovative chair that gets a lot of things right. If you’re torn between the Sihoo Doro Pro C300 V2 and the Boulies EP600 ($489.99 / £429.99) I’d definitely recommend the Sihoo option.

If your budget stretches a little more, then the Hinomi H2 Pro ($ / £759) is a worthy contender, with similar adjustability and a neat folding feature — although it lacks the Sihoo Doro C300 Pro V2’s versatile armrests. And, of course, there’s the ever-dependable Sihoo M18 (under $ / £200), if you want to spend less but still need a decent level of lumbar support.

Sources indicate that sihoo’s upgrades really do make a significant difference with the Doro C300 Pro V2. The way it all works together is a marvel of engineering, and it adds up to one of the best back support systems I’ve ever used. The fact that it achieves it without batteries and motors is impressive in itself. It’s a chair that cradles your back from the moment you sit in it, but that can also be tweaked in just the right ways, without ever becoming overwhelming or overcomplicated.

The biggest downsides here are its chunky looks and its fiddly assembly process, but both of these will evaporate the moment you sit on it. It’s the perfect chair for people who like to move around a lot, and who like to kick back for entertainment in the same chair they work in.
