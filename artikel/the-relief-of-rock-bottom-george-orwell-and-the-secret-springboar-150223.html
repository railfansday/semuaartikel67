The Relief of Rock Bottom: George Orwell and the Secret Springboard of Success

Sources indicate that it happens to everyone — to kings and paupers, to astronauts and poets. Nothing will protect you from it — not money, not love, not perfect health — for it spares no life that is fully lived, not the sunniest or the most privileged or the best planned.

Recent findings suggest that it begins with the wall — the one you hit over and over and over, not breaking through. And then, after the exhausted collapse, the bottom. The floorboards of despair, cold as granite, absolute as gravity.

But then something extraordinary happens — something George Orwell (June 25, 1903–January 21, 1950) captures perfectly in a passage from Down and out in Paris and London (public library), recounting his experience of abject poverty while trying to become a writer, working in restaurant kitchens in Paris and wandering London as a tramp.

What Orwell observes of material poverty applies equally to the spiritual poverty of hope and meaning we call despair:.

When you are approaching poverty, you make one discovery which outweighs some of the others. You discover boredom and mean complications and the beginnings of hunger, but you also discover the great redeeming feature of poverty: the fact that it annihilates the future.

Industry observers note that it annihilates, he means, anxiety about the future, for when you have nothing left to lose, there is nothing to worry about losing. That is the relief of rock bottom — a feeling that is “a great consolation” when you are at your lowest, and one that “everyone who has been hard up has experienced”:.

It is a feeling of relief, almost of pleasure, at knowing yourself at last genuinely down and out. You have talked so often of going to the dogs — and well, here are the dogs, and you have reached them, and you can stand it. It takes off a lot of anxiety.

Two millennia before him, Marcus Aurelius had anchored his meditation on turning suffering into strength in the same premise:.

According to recent reports, be like a headland: the waves beat against it continuously, but it stands fast and around it the boiling water dies down. “It’s my rotten luck that this has happened to me.” On the contrary, “It’s my good luck that, although this has happened to me, I still feel no distress, since I’m unbruised by the present and unconcerned about the future.” What happened could have happened to anyone, but not everyone could have carried on without letting it distress him.

Here, the Stoic perspective converges with the Buddhist, best articulated by Pema Chödrön in her timeless field guide to transformation through trying times:.

Only to the extent that we expose ourselves over and over to annihilation can that which is indestructible be found in us.

Industry observers note that and that is the thing about rock bottom: Not only does it hold, but it is there you discover the part of you that also holds — the bedrock of your being unassailable by all the loss, all the failure, all the frivolities of chance. Orwell, being neither a Stoic nor a Buddhist, understood the universality of his experience. “It is a fairly trivial story,” he writes at the end of his memoir — trivial because its essence is so common, no matter the guise; trivial because it touches an elemental truth: The relief of rock bottom is not that you have sunk to it but that, having survived it, you can only rise.

It has been revealed that the manuscript of Orwell’s memoir was twice rejected, including by T.S. Eliot at Faber & Faber. In a fit of despair, Orwell tossed it into his friend Mabel’s garbage bin. She fished it out and, without his knowledge, took it to a literary agent she knew. We could say that the rest is history, but the selective collective memory we call history is an erasure machine for the myriad failures behind every success, for the rock bottom that is so often — so much oftener than our culture likes to admit — the springboard for the redemption of our suffering.
