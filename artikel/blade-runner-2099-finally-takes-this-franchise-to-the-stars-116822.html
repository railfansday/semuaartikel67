Blade Runner 2099 finally takes this franchise to the stars

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Blade Runner 2099 walks lovingly in the footsteps of Ridley Scott and Denis Villeneuve's preceding Blade Runner films, echoing their memorable cyberpunk settings and noir detective stories. However, there's one area where the TV show completely outpaces both films.

It has been revealed that like Blade Runner and Blade Runner 2049, Blade Runner 2099 takes place primarily in Los Angeles, rarely leaving the city outskirts. Its world is one of bustling streets, crowded apartment buildings, and the massive pyramids that tower over the Los Angeles skyline. Yet episode 2 opens with a different landscape entirely: a fiery red terrain bursting with rocky crags and rushing lava flows.

In a recent development, this is Namaka Moon, a real-life satellite of the dwarf planet Haumea. It's also the first time an on-screen Blade Runner project has been to space. (Notably, comic Blade Runner 2019: Off World has already blasted off to space.).

It has been revealed that space travel is a fact of life in the world of Blade Runner, as well as in its source material, Philip K. Dick's novel Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep? As part of larger space colonization attempts, humans are moving to off-world settlements. Who is preparing these settlements for human occupation? Replicants (or androids in the novel), whose enhanced strength and durability make them the ideal slave labor force for braving inhospitable outer space environments.

According to recent reports, while off-world colonies seem prime for sci-fi exploration, they have just been a background world-building detail in the Blade Runner franchise. Billboards frequently tease the promise of a new off-world home, while extraterrestrial landmarks like Orion take center stage in Roy Batty's (Rutger Hauer) iconic "tears in rain" monologue. Neither the Blade Runner films nor the animated series Blade Runner: Black Lotus have ever left Earth.

In a recent development, that all changes with Blade Runner 2099's Namaka Moon opening. The sequence focuses not on what life in an off-world colony looks like, but rather the labor that goes into establishing one in the first place.

In the episode, replicant Olwen (Michelle Yeoh) clambers across the moon's rocky surface, establishing a series of beacons that shoot beams of light into Namaka's cloudy atmosphere. Once she leaves the moon, the beacons detonate in a series of spectacular explosions, presumably the first step in Namaka's terraforming process. As she tells companion replicant Marek (Johnny Harris), Namaka will be ready for human habitation in 20 years' time thanks to her work. Fittingly, the episode is titled "Birther of Worlds.".

Industry observers note that the Namaka sequence does a spectacular job of ushering Blade Runner into its own Space Age. But it isn't just a trip to space for space's sake. The entire episode opening is rooted in the replicant experience: the loneliness of traversing a perilous moon alone. The awe of watching a world come to life thanks to your handiwork. The hollowness of realizing you will never live in that world, nor be recognized for that work. It's key to understanding Olwen's character, as well as understanding the role of replicants in general. You could almost imagine Roy performing a similar task back in his day.

Sources indicate that plus, since Blade Runner 2099 has yet to show what an actual off-world colony might look like, it still leaves the door open for future Blade Runner projects to flesh out that aspect of the world onscreen. Clearly, the world is far, far vaster than just Los Angeles. Maybe Namaka is just the beginning.
