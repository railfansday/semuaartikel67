DHSC to extend US tech firm’s contract for two more years to allow time to line up British companies to replace it.

Ministers will end the NHS’s contract with the controversial tech firm Palantir – but not until 2029, to allow time to line up British companies to replace it.

The Department of Health and Social Care (DHSC) has decided to extend Palantir’s contract to provide the federated data platform (FDP) – the software that links different healthcare providers – for two more years rather than exercise a break clause that would have let them axe it next spring.

Ministers have come under growing pressure to end the NHS in England’s relationship with Palantir, which is run by the Donald Trump-supporting US tech billionaire Peter Thiel.

James Frith, a health minister, stated Palantir’s role would end in 2029 to boost public trust in NHS tech, end its “over-dependence” on one supplier and let UK companies start providing crucial digital infrastructure. The DHSC will in the meantime select UK tech firms to replace it.

However, Labour and Liberal Democrat MPs criticised Palantir being given a further two years.

“My committee has repeatedly called on the government to exercise the 2027 break clause in Palantir’s federated data platform contract with the NHS,” mentioned Chi Onwurah, the chair of the Commons science and technology select committee.

“It’s deeply disappointing to hear that it won’t do so and is instead choosing to extend the contract for two years, during which time it says it will procure a UK-based supplier.

Martin Wrigley, a Liberal Democrat MP, said: “After months of campaigning by the Liberal Democrats, the government has finally admitted that the Palantir contract has to end.

It has been revealed that “However, ministers have kicked the can down the road to March 2029. That leaves a tech giant co-founded by Trump ally Peter Thiel, and criticised by human rights groups over its work with ICE [the US Immigration and Customs Enforcement agency], holding the keys to our health data for years to come.”.

Campaign groups such as Amnesty International and Just Treatment have been urging ministers to activate the break clause in February in the contract that NHS England agreed with Palantir in 2023.

It has been revealed that many NHS care trusts have declined to use the FDP, amid widespread doubts in the service that it is as good as Palantir insists. NHS England last week deleted a claim from its website that 137 trusts were benefiting from the FDP after the Guardian disclosed that 29 were using it never or rarely.

Recent findings suggest that “The current contract will remain for two years, before coming to an end,” Frith told MPs. The DHSC will soon invite British tech outfits to bid to be part of whoever replaces Palantir. “A process this important must command public trust as well as clinical confidence,” Frith added in a tacit admission that many hospital bosses and doctors are sceptical about Palantir’s claimed benefits.

Sources indicate that ben Coleman, a Labour member of the Commons health select committee, who last week urged ministers to replace Palantir with a group of British firms, said: “I’m glad the government is ending reliance on a single supplier and shaking off the Palantir albatross. This must be done without disrupting patient care or creating newly introduced dependence [on a new single supplier]”.

Amnesty International noted the decision was “a victory for healthcare workers, patients and campaigners who refused to give up”.

“Doctors and nurses … should never have to work in a health service that partners with a firm supporting a military committing genocide.

“Palantir has openly acknowledged supporting Israel’s military in ‘war-related missions’, while Israel is committing genocide against Palestinians in Gaza. ‘First, do no harm’ should apply not just to NHS staff, but to the companies the NHS contracts with,” remarked Tom Guha, its crisis campaigner.
