Tech Report: What do cadaver dogs smell when they find a buried body?

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a pilot study, three cadaver dogs and an electronic nose searched the same grave to see how the dogs’ alerts lined up with the device’s readings.

When a cadaver dog lies down over a patch of ground, a search team treats it as the word of an expert. The expert can’t explain its reasoning, though.

Handlers train the dogs on bone and fluid from decomposing bodies, but the exact chemicals a dog responds to are still unknown.

Over two months, an Australian research team tested three police dogs and a handheld electronic nose at the same grave.

The dogs located the grave consistently after researchers probed the soil, and the electronic nose picked up stronger signals after probing.

The study rests on a single buried donor and three dogs, so the authors call it a pilot.

Amber Brown, now at the University of North Florida, conducted the research while completing her doctorate at the University of Technology Sydney.

Maiken Ueland, who directs the forensic research site where the body was buried, supervised it, with co-authors at the University of Windsor in Canada and in China.

“We don’t know exactly what they are smelling – whether it’s a single compound or a combination of compounds,” Ueland said.

“The electronic nose can provide greater certainty during training by showing whether decomposition odor is present in an area, including when a dog does not alert.”.

Industry observers note that the body belonged to a 79-year-old man who donated it to research. Five days after his death in March 2020, staff buried him about 2 feet (60 centimeters) down in a 12-acre (4.9-hectare) forest plot in western Sydney.

Three empty graves were dug the same day so the dogs couldn’t simply react to disturbed soil.

Sources indicate that the grave then sat untouched for 98 days, long enough to match the standard definition of a long-term missing person. Searches ran on six days between July and September.

That is a Sydney winter, with daily averages between 48°F and 57°F (9°C and 14°C), when buried bodies give off less odor.

In a recent development, on five of the six search days, the team pushed a probe into the grave at 14 points to let trapped gas escape. Soil holds the chemicals of decay the way a sponge holds water, and a probe hole opens a path out.

It has been revealed that the dogs came from the New South Wales Police Force canine unit. Two were English springer spaniels, aged three and seven and a half, and one was a four-year-old Labrador retriever.

The youngest had about a year of experience and the oldest about six.

Recent findings suggest that none had worked a buried body before. Each dog was released off leash about 500 feet (150 meters) from the grave, from a different direction each visit, and alerted by lying down or barking.

The handlers didn’t know where the grave was on the first visit, but the head trainer did, and watched from a distance to rule on each alert.

The electronic nose, called NOS.E, carries five gas sensors tuned to the chemical families that buried bodies release, such as alcohols and compounds containing nitrogen or sulfur.

Before each dog ran, a researcher walked it over the grave with its intake about 6 inches (15 centimeters) above the ground. An empty control grave 16 feet (5 meters) away was sampled the same way.

After probing, every dog tested alerted over the donor’s upper torso, where the device registered its strongest readings.

Over the whole trial, the sensors for nitrogen and sulfur compounds and for alcohols responded most.

Industry observers note that the two younger dogs each gave a false alert during searches before probing. The oldest dog never gave a false alert.

Once the grave was probed, no dog missed it and none alerted in the wrong place.

Recent findings suggest that air samples sent to a lab confirmed the same chemical families were present on every search day. They surged after heavy rain and a warm spell around days 126 and 147.

With so few alerts to compare, the statistics could not confirm a link between sensor strength and correct alerts, though correct alerts tended to come with higher readings.

Brown stated the device could give trainers another way to check whether a dog’s alert coincides with a chemical signal consistent with decomposition.

“For younger dogs in particular, the technology could help trainers determine whether a dog is genuinely responding to decomposition odor or being influenced by another cue,” she mentioned.

In a recent development, the device is not meant to replace the dogs. A dog covers ground rapidly and smells things no sensor array can, while the device uses sensors that respond to broad groups of chemicals.

On the last day the probe punctured the remains, and every sensor fired at once on gases a normal search would never release.

One donor, buried at one depth in one season, is the whole dataset.

The dogs may have learned where the grave was over six visits, even though they started from different spots each time.

And the device has not been tested against other soils, deeper burials, or mixed smells, so it is not ready for a real search.

Whether a dog alerts to a single compound, a mix, or a concentration is still the open question.

Ueland’s team plans a larger study with several donors and more dogs over repeated visits. The hard part, she noted, will be keeping the dogs and handlers from learning the sites.

Until that study runs, the pairing has been tried on one grave, and there the sensors and the dogs agreed.

The full study was published in the journal PLOS One.
