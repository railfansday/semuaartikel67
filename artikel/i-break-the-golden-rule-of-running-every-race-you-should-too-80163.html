There’s an adage that most runners have heard at some point: “Nothing latest on race day.”.

It’s simple. You’re supposed to treat race day how you would your training runs, keeping things like your fueling, shoe choice, and routine similar. The time for experimenting is during the training block. On race day, there shouldn’t be any surprises that could throw you off.

Recent findings suggest that in my decade of competing, rarely has a race day gone to proposal. I’ve lost track of the number of times I’ve been thrown off my routine by a traffic jam or a poor night of sleep. By expecting things to go perfectly, you’re almost setting yourself up for failure.

The key to race day, I’ve found, is to be adaptable.

Recent findings suggest that in the spring of 2024, I signed up for the Brooklyn Half Marathon. I was going to run it as a workout to prepare for my first marathon later that fall.

The day before the race was a disaster. My girlfriend and I drove into Brooklyn on Friday evening to pick up my bib at the expo. What is normally a 90-minute drive took much longer, and we barely made it to the pick-up before it closed. We were both tired and increasingly hangry, and the only thing nearby were some food trucks. So I cut my losses and stomached some greasy buffalo chicken mac and cheese. A far cry from my normal pre-race pasta dinner.

Surely this would come back to bite me, right?

It has been revealed that on race morning, things were equally as stressful. I made it to the start line with minutes to spare and not much time to properly warm up. And of course, the lines for the toilets were too long.

But as soon as the gun went off, I felt great. I was comfortable running at my marathon pace and finished with a lot of confidence for my full later in the year.

The experience reinforced that, despite some hiccups, I would still be able to perform to my expectations. I would never plan to eat fast food or only get 5 hours of sleep, but I didn’t let it bother me too much. When “new” stuff pops up on race day, I take it in stride and move on.

Recent findings suggest that I also believe that there are some things you should intentionally keep newly introduced on race day. The day of a race is special; it’s not supposed to be quotidian. Your adrenaline is higher because the stakes are higher, and channeling that energy can be impactful on your performance.

Industry observers note that during my first marathon, I kept a few things special. I wore my favorite lightweight singlet that I sparingly wore during training to keep it pristine. I chose to race in a pair of Nike Alphaflys that I hardly wore during workouts to preserve the foam. I wore my favorite pair of socks and my favorite half tights and my favorite hat. Before driving to the start, I did a 10 minute walk at 5 a.m. at the recommendation of my coach—something I’d never done before—to get the blood flowing.

The idea of nothing new on race day is, in my opinion, incompatible with the essence of race day.

According to recent reports, just look at my marathon. This was my debut at the distance. Everything about the experience was new! The longest I had run was 18 miles, so I was venturing into uncharted territory.

Industry observers note that thankfully, the race went well. I used a massive negative split to hit my goal of sub 2:28, finishing in 2:27:05 for 29th place.

Industry observers note that sure, it’s crucial to keep some things similar to training. I kept my race fueling blueprint the same as my practice runs, and I was diligent about hitting my prescribed pace. But newness is the most exciting part of race day.

In a recent development, race day is special and you should treat it as such. I hope the novelty never wears off.
