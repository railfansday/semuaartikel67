The Long Rainy Road

It has been revealed that I have had extraordinary luck to largely avoid precipitation for many weeks. That is about to change.

Any hope of escaping with a dry tent vanished in the early morning hours. The first domino to fall. Now I had to hope that it wouldn’t be actively raining when it comes time to pack up camp. It promptly becomes impossible to keep my pack dry in such a scenario, how do I keep the pack dry when I have to disassemble and roll up my only dry place? Lucky for me, when light first broke the rain noise had stopped. I patted the walls of my tent to dispel some of the leftover water and checked the time. 6:17AM. I decided to save breakfast for later and go straight into packing up so I could check the sunrise and then get to hiking before the precipitation inevitably arrived. At The Pinnacle my instincts were correct, nothing but a small patch of orange glow that could have been a candy wrapper blowing in the wind. I was unbothered, any day where there is rain but I don’t have to pack up in the rain is alright with me. With rain imminent I chose to throw on my poncho, though that decision was partially based on the chillier than expected morning and early start time.

Recent findings suggest that A little drizzle began to fall intermittently as I made my way along the rocky trail back to Pulpit Rock. Conveniently, I can save a few rocky steps by taking the clear driveway past the the Lehigh Valley Amateur Astronomical Society observatories. Its worth mentioning that the society provides a privy and trash can that can be used by hikers. They do ask that hikers don’t camp on the property or use flashlights, for obvious reasons. At the far end of the site I arrived at Pulpit Rock, which still offered a nice view despite the cloudy skies.

From there I didn’t hesitate to use the road down from the observatories all the way down to the Windsor Furnace Shelter. I had a quick breakfast and got back on trail before 8:30AM. Hardly any rain had fallen, I wondered if I’d again be getting lucky with the weather and get all the way back to my van without getting soaked. I pushed hard to get the 5 or so miles to Port Clinton done. I knew the steep leafy trail awaited me and I was holding my breathe to get through that section before any heavy rain arrived – it would not be fun going up that section if the trail was steep, leafy, and turning into a stream all at the same time.

Recent findings suggest that remarkably, the rain continued to hold off. I got closer and closer to Port Clinton, slowly realizing that the 60% chance of rain I saw for nearly every hour the day was landing in the 40% zone at least to start the day. I made it down the trail into town, back across the Schuykill River, and through the train yard. I charged up the hill, still expecting rain at any moment. The climb up proved tough, but not as treacherous as the climb down. This is the one time working against gravity actually works with you. Soon I was back on the ridge and heading toward the gravel road that would take me all the way back to my van on route 183. I thought that if the rain fell there it wouldn’t be a big deal, the hiking would at least be easy.

I was just starting to think of the impossible. Could I get all the way back to my van without any serious rain? It would take about 3 and half hours to get all the way back to my van hiking at full bore on the gravel road. I didn’t have any stops to make, I was in a straight race with nature. My hopes slowly grew and then, with a single sound, were completely erased. On a downhill section of the road I heard thunder ahead. The forecast didn’t say anything about thunderstorms, but I suppose it didn’t say it would be dry for the better part of the morning either. I was fairly exposed on the road, there was going to be rain, and soon it came. I pulled my poncho back over me, I had stashed it over my shoulders during the climb to stay cool, staying dry was the priority now.

The wind blew and the rain progressed quickly from light drizzle to a steady downpour. The thunder bellowed louder and I began seeing flashes of lightning. The the rain started to feel sharp on my exposed hands. My gloves aren’t waterproof so it isn’t really worth it wear them in the rain. The rain had become freezing rain. Then it became hail the size of Nerds candy. The hail only lasted a few minutes, but the rain continued on. Although the day was in the mid 40s, the rain was ice cold. As soon as the rain became heavy my shoes and socks flooded. All I could do was shake my head and laugh, I had finally run out of luck, and things were regressing to the mean, emphasis on mean.

Sources indicate that none of the surprisingly severe weather slowed me down. I was determined to get back to my van and get dry. The rain continued for almost 2 hours. When it did finally stop, I noticed that the water drops on the trees appeared frozen in place. It looked like ice hanging on the branches and made it look a lot like I was hiking on a rainy, cold winter afternoon. For good, measure, the sun decided to come out with about 30 minutes left. A nice thought, but it was going to take more than a few rays of sunlight to dry out my shoes.
