AI may not be a bubble. But the way we’re financing it might be

🌐 Source: Fastcompany • 📅 26 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 24 min read (4845 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

  • According to recent reports, jensen Huang is back at Nvidia.
  • Investors may be relieved to have Huang back.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Jensen Huang is back at Nvidia. On November 23, the semiconductor giant reinstated its cofounder as president and CEO, less than two weeks after his abrupt resignation threw the firm into a leadership crisis. Investors may be relieved to have Huang back. But the events of the past two weeks have shown just how much Nvidia—and the wider AI industry—still depends on one man. The board said it had consulted senior U.S. government officials before reinstating Huang and concluded that his return posed no national security risk. Its investigation remains open, however, and Nvidia has confirmed additional measures to address national security and compliance concerns. Huang’s departure also raised questions about Nvidia’s maturity as a corporate institution. In an analysis published shortly after Huang’s resignation, Andy Thurai, founder and principal analyst at The Field CTO, pointed to companies like Apple and IBM that spend years preparing their next generation of leaders. “That separates a mature corporate entity from a founder-led juggernaut,” he wrote. Huang is back, but Nvidia still needs to prepare for a future without him. Meanwhile, another major artificial intelligence company was making history on Wall Street. Anthropic, the maker of Claude, went public on November 19, pricing its shares at $83 in one of the largest initial public offerings in history. At that price, every one of an estimated 96% of the company’s employees held equity worth more than $1 million, according to data from Hill.com reported by CNN. The stock opened at $111.50 before closing its first trading day at $97.17. Investors who bought at the IPO price made money, but those who bought when trading opened were already sitting on losses. Both developments come at a pivotal moment for the AI industry. Billions of dollars continue to pour into the sector, but how much money are the companies receiving those investments actually making? And how long will investors be willing to wait? Judah Taub, managing partner at Hetz Ventures, thinks investors need to separate their excitement about AI from their assessment of the businesses behind it. “AI isn’t a bubble. But the way we’re financing AI might be,” he tells Fast Company. “The internet wasn’t a bubble, but many internet companies were. AI will be exactly the same.” AI could transform entire industries and create enormous economic value. But there’s no guarantee that the companies spending billions to make that transformation possible will benefit most from it. Much of that value could instead go to businesses that find the most profitable ways to use the technology. THE MONEY BEHIND THE AI BOOM Anthropic’s IPO illustrates the extraordinary sums flowing into the AI industry. The company closed the offering on November 20, raising approximately $93.5 billion, including proceeds from shares sold by existing shareholders. Of that, $91.6 billion went to Anthropic before fees and expenses. The company also secured a separate $26 billion investment from Nvidia, Microsoft, Google, AMD, Broadcom, Micron, and an affiliate of TSMC, the Taiwan-based chipmaker. Notably, several of those investors also supply the computing power and infrastructure Anthropic needs to operate. AI developers need vast amounts of computing power, while chipmakers and cloud providers need customers willing to pay for it. Both sides benefit. But if a supplier gives its customer the money to buy its products, how do investors know how much of that demand would have existed otherwise? Such circular financing deals linking AI developers, chipmakers, and infrastructure providers have become increasingly common among the industry’s biggest players. “Nvidia has been selling the picks and shovels of the AI gold rush,” Taub indicates. “But when the shovel manufacturer starts financing the gold miners, investors should ask whether they’re measuring demand for shovels or demand for financing.” Isabelle O’Keeffe, a London-based deep tech investor, worries that the infrastructure buildout could outpace demand for AI products. “The circular financing works as long as real customer demand is pulling it through,” she says. “The risk is when the infrastructure gets built faster than end customers are paying for it.” That could leave cloud providers servicing debt on underused data centers and model developers struggling to meet their spending commitments. Anthropic’s IPO filings detail approximately $900.9 billion in compute and related commitments extending over many years. Though some arrangements are conditional and the money isn’t due all at once, the figure illustrates how much future demand the company is betting on. In a recent analysis for Fast Company, Mark Sullivan showed how Anthropic is pursuing more efficient computing techniques and developing infrastructure that could reduce its dependence on outside cloud providers. The company also needs to attract more customers, expand beyond its strength in coding, and find profitable ways to serve users as its models become more sophisticated—all of which could become increasingly difficult if competitors offer similar capabilities at lower prices. Complicating matters is that some of Anthropic’s competitors also control much of the infrastructure it needs to grow. “The hyperscalers sit on both ends of Anthropic’s supply chain: They control the route to market, and they ration the physical compute required to serve it,” Thurai wrote in his analysis of the company’s IPO filing. “The revenue growth is real, but so are the costs,” O’Keeffe says. She isn’t particularly alarmed by Anthropic’s first day on Wall Street, interpreting the stock’s retreat from its opening price as a sign that investors are becoming more discerning. “I’d read it as selectivity, not rejection,” she says. For other AI companies considering an IPO, O’Keeffe believes the lesson is to price their stock sensibly, demonstrate improving margins, and avoid relying on overly optimistic revenue projections to justify their valuations. Now that Anthropic is public, investors will gain regular insight into the financial performance of one of the world’s leading AI developers. Other AI companies looking to raise money may find their prospects increasingly tied to Anthropic’s performance. THE BILL IS COMING DUE Investors aren’t the only ones scrutinizing AI spending. So are the businesses buying the technology. Kevin Kiley, CEO of the enterprise AI management company Airia, says businesses are changing how they evaluate the technology. A year or two ago, executives faced pressure from boards and investors to adopt AI more rapidly. Some companies celebrated employee usage figures without assessing whether the tools actually improved their work. Many bought AI tools before figuring out what they needed them for or how much money they could save. “The bills are coming in, and executives are asking more pointed questions,” Kiley says. “Are we improving productivity? Are we reducing operating costs and risk? How is this investment contributing to revenue and the bottom line? The focus is shifting from input to output.” Businesses are becoming more selective about the applications they fund, and executives want to understand which departments are consuming AI resources and what they’re getting in return. The difficulty is that measuring AI returns remains a challenge. Employee usage can increase even as savings or productivity gains remain elusive. Kiley says some companies are spending more on AI even as models become cheaper to use. As organizations expand their deployments, AI agents take on more work, call additional services, and process more information. What looks like a simple request to an employee could involve dozens of separate model calls, each potentially incurring a cost. Some of that expense could be avoided. Kiley notes that organizations frequently use powerful, expensive models for routine tasks that smaller systems could handle. Airia’s internal data suggests that 89% of common enterprise AI tasks don’t require the capabilities of frontier models, though that estimate hasn’t been independently verified. The company has also encountered agents that process more information than necessary, repeatedly call external tools, or get stuck performing the same actions. For an enterprise with thousands of employees using AI daily, even small amounts of unnecessary computing can quickly become expensive. Airia says its testing has produced cost reductions exceeding 50% through better model selection and workflow design, without sacrificing quality. Actual savings will vary by workload. But identifying those opportunities requires companies to understand where their AI spending goes—something Kiley believes many organizations still struggle with. “Making a model 20% smarter doesn’t necessarily make an employee 20% more productive,” he tells Fast Company. This could become a problem for companies like Anthropic and OpenAI. If smaller, cheaper models can perform a growing share of everyday business tasks, how much more will customers be willing to pay for each new generation of frontier models? Companies that help businesses deploy, manage, and integrate AI could capture more of the economic value. Airia operates in that market, so Kiley has a commercial interest in that outcome. He also expects demand for AI governance, security, and cost management to grow. “I expect a more selective but healthier market, where organizations focus their resources on AI applications delivering measurable returns and scale back those that aren’t.” WHO GETS THE VALUE? Some of the biggest winners of the AI boom may turn out to be companies that have no intention of selling AI software. Michael Heltzen, CEO of the biotechnology company eXoZymes, sees opportunities in pharmaceuticals, materials, energy, and manufacturing, where AI could help develop products that are difficult or expensive to produce through conventional methods. “There’s a fundamental difference between generating financial activity and excitement around future potential, including hype-driven sales, and creating genuine, lasting economic value for shareholders,” he says. “The AI industry is doing plenty of the former. Now it needs to prove the latter.” His company combines AI with laboratory experimentation to develop enzymes for manufacturing valuable molecules. While its commercial ambitions still need to be judged against measurable results, the approach illustrates how businesses could use AI to create entirely new products rather than simply making existing operations more efficient. “Our measure of value is simple,” Heltzen says. “Can we make something highly valuable that only we can make?” Taub at Hetz Ventures argues that investors would benefit from distinguishing between three markets within the AI economy: compute, or spending on the infrastructure needed to develop and operate AI; intelligence, or what customers will pay to use AI models; and outcomes, or the economic value created when AI performs useful work. “These markets are connected, but they are not interchangeable,” he says. Consider a data center that generates billions of dollars in equipment sales and construction spending before anyone knows whether its computing capacity will generate enough revenue to justify the investment. Equipment suppliers may have already made money, while the data center operator still has years of expenses to recover. Further down the chain, the companies paying for that computing power have their own investments to justify. For Matt Calkins, CEO of the AI automation company Appian, creating more value from AI will require making the technology dependable enough for businesses to use in their most vital operations. “The top job of the AI industry today is not to drive blockbuster IPOs but to do the hard work of making AI useful in the biggest organizations for the biggest jobs. The AI value proposition isn’t complete. We need to focus more on safety and reliability,” he says in written commentary. A tool that saves employees hours of work during a demonstration may prove less economical once the costs of checking its output, correcting mistakes, and maintaining the system are factored in. We’re already seeing a version of this with AI-generated software. Developers can produce code faster than before, but companies still need people and systems to maintain it, investigate failures, and keep applications running. And even if AI companies get pricing and reliability right, they still need to build products people want. During a conversation at Dubai Future Week last week, Pat Pataranutaporn, an assistant professor at MIT, described what he called “the bubble of people creating technology.” He argued that AI developers can become disconnected from public concerns and the ways people experience their products. That presents a business risk, too. Companies could spend billions developing capabilities that impress researchers and investors but have limited appeal to the customers expected to pay for them. WHAT A CORRECTION COULD CHANGE A slowdown in AI funding could be good news for startups that have learned to do more with less computing power. Those struggling to retain customers or generate healthy margins, meanwhile, could find it increasingly difficult to raise money. O’Keeffe, the deep tech investor, believes such a shift could benefit companies building applications on top of existing AI models. “For the application layer, a reset would be healthy,” she says. “It clears out the me-too products and makes everyone focus on efficiency and real customer ROI.” But there’s a potential downside. Developing frontier AI models requires enormous capital, often years before a company can expect meaningful returns. If investors become reluctant to finance those efforts, the advantage could shift further toward tech giants with the cash and infrastructure to fund research themselves. Ironically, greater financial discipline could give the biggest technology companies even more control over the AI industry. Businesses might have thousands of AI applications to choose from, yet many could depend on models developed by the same handful of companies. Still, history offers some reassurance that a financial correction need not spell disaster for AI itself. Taub points to railroads, electricity, and the internet, each of which attracted extraordinary investment. Many of the businesses financing those technologies failed or disappointed their shareholders, but the infrastructure they built supported economic activity long afterward. AI may produce a similar outcome, creating opportunities for businesses that don’t even exist today while leaving some early investors with substantial losses. “We should be careful not to confuse a correction in AI valuations with a correction in AI’s potential,” Taub warns. Huang’s return may restore some confidence in Nvidia’s leadership, and Anthropic’s IPO has provided the company with a huge infusion of capital. Over the coming quarters, Anthropic’s earnings, the performance of infrastructure investments, and the AI contracts businesses choose to renew could tell us much more about where the industry is headed. For much of the boom, the industry has measured its ambition in chips purchased, data centers announced, and billions raised. The challenge now is turning those investments into enough paying customers, useful work, and profits to sustain the businesses behind them. Some of the companies spending the most today may discover that they financed the future without securing much of its rewards.
