Scientists Fear a Biological Apocalypse Could Destroy Us. Now They Have a Way to Stop It.

Some people fear a hostile alien takeover when there may be a threat lurking much closer to home (and it isn’t sentient robots). If a molecule such as DNA were to see itself in the mirror, its reflection would show the same molecule flipped around, much like the ubiquitous mirror selfie. But the mirror version can’t be stacked perfectly on top of the original, just as your left hand can’t be laid perfectly over your right. These hypothetical components of “mirror life” are not known to exist—yet. If they ever do, they could end up destroying life as we know it from the inside out.

Like humans, many molecules are left- or right-handed. Lack of actual hands aside, this means they come in two mirror-image forms. In DNA, that handedness highlights up in the direction its double helix twists. In amino acids, the building blocks of proteins, it comes from how four different groups of atoms are arranged around a central carbon atom, much like a left glove versus a right one. If one of these molecules really did have hands to hold a smartphone and take a mirror selfie, the image would show it with the opposite handedness. That mirror image is the version scientists never want to exist. It remains hypothetical for now, but bacteria and other forms of life built from mirror molecules could slip past an organism’s immune system and other defenses. Despite warnings that such organisms should never be made in a lab, a rogue scientist might want to play Frankenstein. Microscopic horrors could be unleashed as bioweapons, or a runaway experiment could escape into the world before anyone managed to eliminate it.

Mirror life is currently seen as a low-key but potentially apocalyptic biosecurity threat. Worried about what could happen if it ever emerges on Earth, a team of researchers from the University of Strathclyde and Durham University in the UK has proposed a way to detect it using polarized light. Their method relies on circularly polarized luminescence (CPL): light whose electric field rotates in a corkscrew as it travels, either clockwise or counterclockwise. Like a molecule, that rotation can be left- or right-handed. Scientists call this handedness chirality. Living things almost always use just one of the two mirror-image forms, or enantiomers, of each of their key molecules, which is thought to give them a biological and physical advantage.

Sources indicate that “This approach is based upon optical circularly polarized luminescence (CPL) signatures generated by fluorescent probes, which bind to subcellular targets (e.g. membranes, organelles, nucleic acids, etc.),” the researchers said in a study recently published in Royal Society Open Science. “Probes can either be achiral or be intrinsically chiral.”.

Sources indicate that most amino acids encoded in living organisms are left-handed, though some mirror forms do occur naturally and pose no threat. There’s only a slight chance that primordial mirror organisms survive from the early days of evolution or could arrive on meteorites. What worries the research team is artificial mirror life. They propose using fluorescent or luminescent probes that attach to biological structures and reveal whether those structures are mirror versions, based on their supramolecular chirality: the handedness that comes from how many molecules are arranged together, not just from each molecule on its own. This is only a thought experiment for now, but the approach could work and could be adapted to existing technology, such as cameras and microscopes, to search for mirror molecules and help prevent a bio-apocalypse.

It has been revealed that here’s how it would work: a fluorescent probe latches onto a target inside a cell, such as a membrane or a strand of DNA. When the probe glows, the handedness of the molecule it’s attached to shapes the light it gives off, so the light comes out spinning slightly more in one direction than the other. A probe bound to ordinary biological molecules would glow with one twist. The same probe bound to their mirror-image counterparts would glow with the opposite twist. That reversed signal would be the red flag for mirror life.

Industry observers note that mirror life doesn’t even need to exist for the CPL method to be tested. Researchers could try it on Z-DNA, a naturally occurring form of DNA whose double helix twists to the left instead of the right. Z-DNA isn’t a true mirror molecule, since it’s built from the same building blocks as ordinary DNA, just coiled differently. But its reversed twist makes it a handy stand-in for testing whether probes can tell one spiral direction from another. Using several types of probes could also help rule out false positives that might send the entire planet into a panic. Probes that have a handedness of their own may respond differently than probes without one, which helps flag misleading readings. Other probes that produce CPL have been designed specifically for RNA, which functions and behaves in especially diverse and complex ways. Some probes that incorporate amino acids also produce CPL when bound to specific molecules and could be added to the toolkit.

“Close coordination between biologists and chemists will be required to provide a library of fluorescent CPL-active biological probes that can robustly identify mirror life if it is encountered,” noted the researchers. “The CPL method could provide a first-line assay for identifying extant mirror life biohazards, while also offering a foundation for bio-surveillance efforts that would likely be necessary for practical safeguarding against artificial mirror life.”.
