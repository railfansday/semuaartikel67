Few literary spies are as synonymous with danger and lethality as James Bond. Ian Fleming’s 007 literally has a license to kill, proving that a capacity for violence isn’t just part of his job description, it’s one of his greatest professional strengths. Across Fleming’s 14 James Bond books and the dozens of continuations from other authors that followed, M16’s most famous spy permanently dispatched countless foes in a variety of painful and creative ways.

Sources indicate that james Bond may be the poster child for dangerous fictional spies, but in reality there are plenty who make him look relatively tame. The Bond of the novels is far less trigger-happy than his movie counterpart, but the same can’t be said for the generations of thriller spies he inspired. Plenty are far more lethal than 007, with the most dangerous completing missions through means Fleming’s vision of Her Majesty’s Secret Service would never allow.

Industry observers note that surviving the massacre of a presidential protection detail is an unusually brutal introduction to a thriller protagonist. That’s precisely how readers encounter Scot Harvath in Brad Thor’s The Lions of Lucerne, which establishes that his character has much more depth than his Secret Service credentials. Before protecting presidents, Harvath served as a Navy SEAL. His subsequent career in covert counterterrorism after the death of his security team combines both backgrounds, creating an operative who understands how protection works and possesses the military skills to dismantle it.

Sources indicate that throughout his series, Scot Harvath fights multiple sustained campaigns of violence under conditions that should finish him. For example, 2019’s Backlash strips away his support and leaves him fighting through hostile Russian territory, turning his survival skills and appetite for revenge into an especially vicious combination. It’s not uncommon for Harvath to kill dozens of people in a single novel. This is especially true when his friends and family are at risk and things get personal. Bond is dangerous when cornered, but Harvath’s special operations background makes pursuing him an exceptionally costly mistake.

An art restorer hardly sounds like the obvious choice to outclass 007. The civilian profession of author Daniel Silva’s Gabriel Allon, however, suits a killer whose defining qualities include patience, precision, and extraordinary attention to detail. Recruited into Israeli intelligence after the 1972 Munich Olympics massacre, Allon participated in the campaign to eliminate those responsible. His background establishes him as a specialist in targeted assassination long before the first novel in his series, 2000’s The Kill Artist, begins, with the consequences of that work following him throughout Daniel Silva’s novels.

According to recent reports, gabriel Allon is a notably lethal spy when he’s dealing with his targets up close. Daniel Silva describes his early assassinations as close-range shootings with a .22-caliber Beretta, emphasizing nerve and preparation over overwhelming firepower. He’s not a literary spy that only has violence to rely on, however. Allon’s linguistic abilities and talent for assuming convincing identities make reaching the target part of the same skill set as killing them. Whereas meeting James Bond isn’t an encounter those in his orbit forget in a hurry, Gabriel Allon knows how to become completely unremarkable until he makes his move. The fact that various organizations try to bring him back to his career as an assassin despite him wanting to leave that life behind is further testament to just how lethal he truly is.

You can purchase The Kill Artist, the first Gabriel Allon novel by Daniel Silva, here.

It has been revealed that losing his memory should make Jason Bourne an easier target. Instead, 1980’s The Bourne Identity introduced readers to a covert operative whose survival instincts and skills remain terrifyingly effective even after he’s forgotten honing them. Author Robert Ludlum builds much of the suspense of the Jason Bourne novels around that contradiction: the titular spy knows how to survive a world full of professional killers, but can’t remember how he acquired his lethal skillset. Bourne’s level of combat conditioning is so deep and thorough that his training kicks in on a subconscious level.

The novels also give Jason Bourne a considerably wider skillset than a straightforward assassin. Robert Ludlum’s original trilogy places Bourne in an elaborate operation that requires deception and infiltration just as much as armed and unarmed combat. Later authors expand the violence, placing Bourne against rival assassins and intelligence organizations equipped to anticipate his methods. Across those different incarnations, Bourne proves himself much more dangerous than 007 thanks to his incredible adaptability. Removing his support network, compromising his identity, or sending trained operatives after him never seems to make him any less of a threat.

You can purchase The Bourne Identity, the first Jason Bourne novel by Robert Ludlum, here.

The distinction between merely investigating terrorists and actively eliminating them is central to the appeal of the Mitch Rapp novels. Author Vince Flynn’s CIA operative specializes in the latter, approaching counterterrorism with a lethal urgency that repeatedly puts him at odds with his superiors. 2010’s American Assassin, which serves as a prequel, traces Rapp’s trigger-happy mentality back to personal loss and his recruitment into a covert assassination program. From there, Rapp honed himself into one of the most proficient killers at the CIA’s disposal.

It has been revealed that what makes Mitch Rapp especially frightening is his cold and calculated approach to even the most violent operations. His training encompasses all manner of firearms and close combat, but it’s his willingness to use his skills without a second’s hesitation that makes them so deadly. This is where his operations differ significantly to 007’s, and this difference reveals everything about which is the more dangerous. James Bond’s assignments frequently require him to uncover a larger conspiracy before confronting its architect. Rapp is often dispatched because the U.S. Government wants an enemy to stop breathing.

You can purchase Transfer of Power, the first Mitch Rapp novel by Vince Flynn, here.

It has been revealed that A body count only reveals so much about an assassin’s skills. Often how targets are killed matters just as much as how many have been dealt with. This makes John Rain an especially competent literary spy, since his victims often aren’t recognizable as murder victims at all. The Japanese American freelancer from Barry Eisler’s novels specializes in deaths that appear natural, making him particularly valuable to employers who need a person removed without attracting attention. Beginning with 2002’s Rain Fall, also published as A Clean Kill in Tokyo, Barry Eisler turns Rain’s ability to conceal violence the basis for one of the most lethal spies to grace the pages of a thriller novel.

As expected, the ability to make a murder look like an accident is far from the only trick up John Rain’s sleeve. Serving in the Special Forces during Vietnam and extensive martial arts experience mean he’s just as capable in an open fight as he is setting up an elaborate assassination. Still, it’s his penchant for creative and untraceable kills that define Rain. Surviving a gunfight is one thing. Arranging matters so that nobody realizes a killing occurred requires an entirely different level of control.

You can purchase A Clean Kill In Tokyo, the first John Rain novel by Barry Eisler, here.

Pike Logan belongs to an organization designed to operate beyond the normal limits of American law. In Brad Taylor’s novels, a black book group codenamed The Taskforce recruits from elite military and intelligence backgrounds. Even among a roster of highly experienced specialists, however, 2011’s One Rough Man establishes Logan as an especially lethal operative. His notably military approach makes him especially dangerous when a mission develops into a sustained confrontation.

Logan thinks about factors like surveillance, positioning, coordination, and escape as connected problems, and has no qualms about using lethal force whenever the situation demands it. Author Brad Taylor’s own Special Forces experience adds a notable level of detail that makes Logan’s professional competence feel legitimate. James Bond is an exceptional lone operative, but Pike Logan brings the tactical lethality and group-coordination of an elite military unit into the world of espionage. His lethality comes just as much from knowing how to use the people around him as it does any weapon.

You can purchase One Rough Man, the first Pike Logan novel by Brad Taylor, here.

In a recent development, most fictional spies have a life before whatever intelligence service they work for recruits them. Even the enigmatic James Bond went to Eton and served in the Navy before joining Her Majesty’s Secret Service. Evan Smoak’s preparation begins at twelve, when the Orphan Program takes him from a group home and starts conditioning him to be a highly covert government assassin on a team that doesn’t officially exist. By adulthood, Smoak’s entire world has been defined by violence, surveillance, and operational discipline. Gregg Hurwitz’s Orphan X, the first novel in the series of the same name, introduces the result: a man whose formidable resources and specialized skills make his disappearance from the organization that made him a matter of national security.

In a recent development, working as the Nowhere Man, Smoak redirects his abilities toward helping people, but that doesn’t make him any less dangerous to those who earn his attention. His lethality comes from obsessive preparation just as much as shooting or fighting. He assesses his surroundings, anticipates complications, and treats small mistakes as potential disasters, an approach that leads to watertight operation planning. James Bond is a man who has been trained for danger. Evan Smoak feels like he’s been psychologically conditioned into a living weapon.

You can purchase Orphan X, the first Evan Smoak novel by Gregg Hurwitz, here.

In a recent development, being hunted by the CIA would be a serious problem for any literary spy, including James Bond. For Court Gentry, aka The Gray Man, it becomes an extended demonstration of why the agency couldn’t simply let him walk free after his years of service. Author Mark Greaney’s former covert assassin survives long after his own employers mark him for death. He manages to build a successful career as a freelancer despite being hunted by one of the most far-reaching intelligence organizations in the world.

It has been revealed that as for why Court Gentry is more dangerous than James Bond, the central spy of The Gray Man novels has an advantage when it comes to sheer concentration of expertise. Unlike 007, who carries out a wide variety of types of missions, Gentry’s career is built on surviving encounters with other highly trained killers. Bond often comes face-to-face with dangerous foes, but this is rarely part of the blueprint. Gentry exists precisely to take out these kinds of threats. Bond may be the more well-rounded spy, but Gentry is inarguably the more lethal of the two.

You can purchase The Gray Man, the first Court Gentry novel by Mark Greaney, here.

Sources indicate that before adopting the name John Clark, John Kelly from the world of the Jack Ryan novels was a former Navy SEAL and Vietnam veteran whose personal revenge campaign develops alongside a military prisoner rescue operation. Tom Clancy gives Clark the skills for both undertakings when he made his debut in 1988’s The Cardinal of the Kremlin, and then spends multiple books showing just how ruthlessly he can apply them. Clark’s professional effectiveness comes from versatility accumulated over a long career. A potent combination of maritime, military, and clandestine fieldwork experience allows him to function across situations that demand very different kinds of expertise.

According to recent reports, john Clark’s later leadership of the multinational counterterrorism unit Rainbow showcases the full extent of those capabilities, as he turns a team of elite but disconnected specialists into a lethally coherent unit. James Bond may have formidable instincts when violence becomes the only option, but Clark’s professional capacity for it ensures that he rarely, if ever, needs to rely on anything but carefully applied brute force. He understands how to plan a mission, select the necessary people, and deliver operational success even when up against overwhelming firepower.

You can purchase The Cardinal of the Kremlin, the first novel to feature John Clark by Tom Clancy, here.

Espionage missions that heavily feature luxury, global travel, and impeccable presentation make Villanelle, aka Oxana Vorontsova, an obvious spiritual successor to James Bond. However, she doesn’t just build on his legacy when it comes to making the spy lifestyle look lavish. She also embodies the cold-blooded professional killer archetype that Ian Fleming’s 007 is often mistaken as being. Author Luke Jennings introduces Vorontsova in the Killing Eve novels as a Russian killer recruited by the secretive Twelve, whose intervention transforms an already violent young woman into an international assassin. Her handlers supply fresh identities and intensive training, turning her capacity for murder into a highly marketable professional skill.

It has been revealed that like 007, Villanelle is an extremely adaptable and capable spy. Where they differ is that she has an alarming absence of remorse. Bond’s conscience often causes him to hesitate before making a kill in the novels, or even change his mind and lower his weapon. This isn’t the case for Villanelle. What’s more, she appears to take a disturbing pleasure in this aspect of her work. Bond is only ever violent when he feels it’s necessary to complete his mission. Villanelle seems to go out of her way to find solutions that require bloodshed. This means that, naturally, she ends up killing much more frequently than James Bond ever has, and ensures she’s one of the most dangerous spies ever to appear in a thriller novel.

You can purchase Codename Villanelle, the first Killing Eve novel by Luke Jennings, here.
