Silver's spot price reached a record high of $121.67 per troy ounce on Jan. 29, 2026. That marked a 289% gain over the previous 12 months. But today, it trades at about $44 per troy ounce. Let's see why it pulled back -- and how much it could be worth by the end of 2027.

In a recent development, three catalysts drove silver's price to its record high. First, the Fed held its benchmark rates steady in 2024 and 2025, and investors hoped it would finally cut rates again in 2026. Lower interest rates weaken the U.S. dollar and drive investors toward gold and silver.

Recent findings suggest that second, silver is an industrial metal used in solar panels, electric vehicles, consumer electronics, and data centers. The rapid growth of the data center market has caused demand for silver to outstrip supply. Lastly, only about a quarter of the world's silver comes from silver mines. The rest is produced as a byproduct of mining lead, zinc, copper, and gold. That inelastic supply -- along with declining ore grades, higher mining costs, and tighter environmental regulations -- prevented miners from producing enough silver.

Industry observers note that however, those tailwinds dissipated in 2026. The outbreak of the Iran war exacerbated inflation, prompting the Fed to raise its benchmark rates for the first time in three years in September. The stronger dollar and higher yields on fixed-income investments weighed on silver.

The Democratic sweep of the U.S. midterms also flipped the governorships of three Tier-1 data center states: Texas, Ohio, and Georgia. The governor-elects of all three states have called for tighter regulations or moratoriums on newly introduced data center approvals.

Recent findings suggest that at the same time, macro headwinds reduced market demand for silver in other industries -- including silverware, jewelry, solar panels, and industrial machinery. All of that pressure is making silver an unappealing asset as investors shuffle toward less volatile investments.

According to recent reports, analysts expect silver to rise more than 50% to a median price of $66.75 per troy ounce in 2027, according to a recent Reuters poll. That outlook assumes the macro environment will improve with lower inflation, interest rate cuts, and lower Treasury yields. Ending the war in Iran, which has bipartisan support, could ignite those catalysts.

The gold-silver ratio, or the number of ounces of silver required to buy one ounce of gold, has also risen to 82.5 in recent months. A ratio below 50 implies gold is undervalued, while a ratio above 80 suggests silver is undervalued. That ratio isn't foolproof, but it suggests that silver is undervalued at current prices, as long as the macro environment remains stable.

In a best-case scenario, the macro environment would improve as the demand for silver across the data center, EV, industrial automation, and solar markets warms up again. The U.S. government could also ease its restrictions on silver mining to increase domestic production.

It has been revealed that however, the regulatory challenges facing data centers, solar panel makers replacing silver with copper to cut costs, and other industry-specific headwinds could throttle that recovery. So while silver could be bottoming out at these levels, it probably won't set latest record highs in 2027.

It has been revealed that the supply deficit for silver could widen from 40.3 million ounces in 2025 to 46.3 million ounces in 2026, according to the Silver Institute and Metals Focus. That would mark its sixth annual deficit, and it won't likely become a surplus anytime soon.

That strained supply makes silver a worthwhile long-term investment -- so the iShares Silver Trust ETF (SLV -2.60%) is still worth buying -- but I'd only buy it as a hedge against a weaker dollar rather than a core investment. Silver still tends to underperform the S&P 500 (^GSPC -2.02%) over the long run -- so I wouldn't go all-in on the precious metal today.
