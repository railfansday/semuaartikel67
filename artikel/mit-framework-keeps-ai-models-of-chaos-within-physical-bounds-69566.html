MIT researchers have developed a machine-learning framework that guarantees bounded trajectories for AI models of chaotic systems, addressing simulations that can drift into physically impossible values during long runs. MIT announced the work on November 20, 2026.

The research by graduate student Sunbochen Tang and professors Navid Azizan and Themistoklis Sapsis concerns models of weather, climate, turbulence and other nonlinear processes. Their paper in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences was published online on October 12, 2026, and appeared in the journal’s October 20, 2026, matter.

Chaotic systems are highly sensitive to initial conditions, so tiny differences can produce dramatically different outcomes. A neural network may match data over a short period and still fail when left to run: small errors can accumulate until its simulated values grow without limit, even though the real system remains bounded. That behavior can invalidate estimates of long-term statistics, a central goal of modeling dissipative chaotic systems.

Sources indicate that the guarantee therefore concerns long-term bounded behavior rather than exact point-by-point forecasting far into the future. It seeks to preserve valid long-run statistics without suppressing the complicated variation that makes the system chaotic. As Azizan put it, “Chaos does not mean anything can happen. These systems often remain bounded, and their models should, too.”.

The framework simultaneously learns a dynamics emulator, which predicts how the system evolves, and an energy-like representation. That representation is used to construct an algebraic dissipative constraint inside a projection layer, which adjusts the model’s predicted evolution. Outside a learned boundary, the energy-like quantity must decrease, drawing trajectories toward the bounded region while allowing chaotic behavior within it.

Sources indicate that the paper notes this construction enforces dissipation by design and provides formal trajectory-boundedness guarantees over arbitrarily long forecast windows. The learned energy boundary also supplies an outer estimate of the system’s strange attractor, whose complex geometry makes it difficult to characterize directly.

Recent findings suggest that tang said soft penalties or incentives applied during training need not hold when a deployed model faces fresh inputs, so unbounded predictions remain possible. Arbitrary caps can keep outputs within a chosen limit, but they can also restrict the model’s ability to represent complex features that should be learned from data. The projection layer is modular and model-agnostic, allowing it to work with different neural-network architectures.

Sources indicate that MIT reports that the approach closely tracked true outcomes in tests ranging from textbook systems to complex multidimensional cases, while conventional methods moved into impossible predictions. The published demonstrations include Lorenz 96 and a reduced-order model of the Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation, where the method produced bounded long-horizon forecasts that preserved invariant statistics.

A related Energy-Constrained Operator paper by Andrea Goertzen, Tang and Azizan was published on June 7, 2026. It introduced a closed-form quadratic projection layer and reported stable long-horizon forecasts that captured invariant statistics for systems governed by the Kuramoto–Sivashinsky and Navier–Stokes equations. Both papers use control-theoretic energy conditions to tackle predictions whose unbounded growth prevents meaningful evaluation of long-run statistics.

The algorithms and computer code have been deposited on GitHub, and the paper and open-source code are accessible online. The researchers hope other teams will build on the modular layer and test it on larger, more complex problems.

The team plans to test progressively larger systems and data-driven models of realistic weather. Realistic-weather performance therefore remains an open research question: the immediate contribution is a formal limit on runaway simulations, while the next tests must establish how that guarantee affects the usefulness of models’ long-term statistics.
