Researchers Just Sent an Unblockable Message Through a Solid Wall Using Gravity

Researchers have demonstrated an unusual way to transmit information through solid barriers using gravity rather than electromagnetic signals.

Physicists at the University of Queensland have transmitted a message through a brick wall using gravity instead of electromagnetic signals. Their unconventional communication system relied on a moving elevator counterweight and an extremely sensitive instrument capable of detecting tiny changes in the gravitational field.

Sources indicate that the experiment took place in UQ’s Parnell Building, where researchers moved a 1.8-metric-ton (about 4,000 pounds) elevator counterweight between two positions representing binary 0 and 1. A gravimeter positioned in a nearby bathroom detected the gravitational changes and decoded a single word: “Gravity.”.

Industry observers note that unlike radio waves and other electromagnetic signals, gravitational fields cannot be blocked by ordinary building materials. This means information carried by changes in gravity could potentially reach places where conventional wireless communication struggles.

It has been revealed that dr. Charles Woffinden from UQ’s School of Mathematics and Physics explained the advantage. “Unlike the electromagnetic signals used in modern wireless communication, a gravitational signal cannot simply be screened out by placing ordinary material in its path,” he said. “Anyone who has lost phone reception in a tunnel or struggled with Wi-Fi through a solid wall will understand the appeal.”.

“While the present system is far from practical, it demonstrates the possibility of communicating using a method that ordinary physical barriers cannot block.”.

The communication experiment grew out of an unrelated effort to measure vehicle weights without physical contact. Dr. Andrew Groszek and his colleagues were developing a system that could estimate the mass of trucks and other heavy vehicles by detecting their gravitational attraction.

Industry observers note that “We were aiming to determine the mass of trucks and other large vehicles by measuring their tiny gravitational pull,” Dr. Groszek said. “Once we had shown that the idea worked, we realized the same physics could be used to encode and transmit information.”.

It has been revealed that the researchers applied that principle to communication by moving a large mass between fixed positions. Each movement altered the gravitational field measured by the detector, allowing the team to encode binary information without transmitting electromagnetic signals.

It has been revealed that although the experiment successfully transmitted information, its data rate was exceptionally low. Professor Andrew White identified the aging elevator as a major constraint.

“Our lift was snail-paced – it’s from the 1950s – so our experiment transmitted just 1 bit per minute, meaning it took an hour to send a single word,” Professor White stated.

“The speed of gravity communication would come down to how substantial the mass is, how fast you can move it, and the sensitivity of the detector.”.

“It took more than 100 years to go from the first telegraph system to the mobile phone, so while gravity communication is not practical today, it could be in the future.”.

Recent findings suggest that professor Matthew Davis said the experiment established that information can be deliberately encoded in a gravitational field and recovered by a detector. The achievement demonstrates the underlying physics, although substantial improvements would be needed before the technique could serve as a useful communication system.

“It’s not often that you get to demonstrate an entirely newly introduced way of communicating,” Professor Davis noted. “We have shown experimentally that information can be encoded in the gravitational field and recovered at the other end.”.

Recent findings suggest that the researchers also modeled how more sensitive quantum sensors might improve the technique. Unlike the classical gravimeter used in the experiment, such sensors could potentially detect weaker gravitational changes at greater distances.

“Our experiment used a classical gravity sensor, but our modeling indicates that quantum sensors could, with rather ambitious assumptions, support ranges of up to about 500 meters (about 1,640 feet) and data rates of around 15 bits per minute.”.

Those figures are theoretical projections, not experimentally demonstrated capabilities. Gravity-based communication remains far too slow for practical applications, but more advanced detectors could eventually make it possible to exchange information in environments that radio waves, light, and other conventional signals cannot penetrate.

Reference: “Unblockable communication with gravity” by Andrew J. Groszek, Charles W. Woffinden, Michael D. Harvey, Andrew G. White and Matthew J. Davis, 18 November 2026, Science Advances. DOI: 10.1126/sciadv.aee0802.
