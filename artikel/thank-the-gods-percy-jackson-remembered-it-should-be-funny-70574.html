Recent findings suggest that disney+'s Percy Jackson and the Olympians series has done an admirable job of adapting the Greek mythology-inspired adventures of Rick Riordan's novels. But it has consistently struggled with a key aspect of of the books: their humor.

The book series are narrated by Percy Jackson (Walker Scobell) himself, whose internal monologue contains its fair range of sass and hilariously deadpan descriptions of the strange things that happen to him. Case in point, every book's irreverent chapter titles, which kick off with the iconic "I Accidentally Vaporize My Pre-Algebra Teacher" and go on to include such hits as "I Battle My Jerk Relative" and "Everybody Hates Me but the Horse.".

Recent findings suggest that the TV series borrows these chapter headings for its own episode titles, but loses a significant chunk of the humor that make them land on the page. It sands down much of the wisecracking banter between Percy and his questing companions, turning them into more stoic fantasy heroes. Yes, Season 2 features one of the books' funniest moments: the fan-favorite scene of Grover (Aryan Simhadri) wearing a wedding dress to escape from cyclops Polyphemus. (It throws Percy in a dress too for good measure.) But it also misses out on the smaller wordplay or misunderstandings that drew substantial laughs from the books, like when Percy mistakes the word "hubris" for "hummus." It's a solid joke, as well as a reminder that Percy is just a kid caught in the crossfires of a mythic war. In missing out on these gags, small as they may be, the show has failed to let Percy and his companions be kids, and also struggled to capture the books' distinct tone.

Thankfully, the first two episodes of Percy Jackson and the Olympians Season 3 prove to be a refreshing change of pace, right from their very first moments. The season begins with a tense monster fight for Thalia (Tamara Smart), during which Percy intervenes and helps her kills her foe. When she tells him she had everything under control, a car bursts into flames in the background.

Sources indicate that it's a classic gag, one that by no means reinvents the wheel. But it gets better when Annabeth (Leah Sava Jeffries) asks Percy to put out the fire. As she and Thalia have a serious chat, we see him exasperatedly playing firefighter in the background. Here is a perfect example of how to balance the Percy Jackson and the Olympians books' high stakes with humor, and the TV series keeps it up from there.

Sources indicate that turns out, the key to unlocking Percy Jackson's comic potential is the contentious relationship between Percy and Thalia. The pair butt heads throughout the first two episodes, over everything from how to deal with Kronos ally Luke (Charlie Bushnell) to undertaking a quest. There's real dramatic tension there, but Percy Jackson and the Olympians emphasizes it with a running undercurrent of sassy snapping. When Thalia mentions her dream of battling Percy to the death, she wonders if it will come true.

"No!" Percy says, affronted. Then, as a follow-up beat, he asks, "Who won?".

In addition to the often sarcastic back-and-forth between Percy and Thalia, Percy Jackson and the Olympians finds other ways to levels up its humor. Its fight scenes get the occasional jolt of laughter, like when Annabeth orders Grover to "goat kick" a foe. The show also leans further into the kinds of descriptions Percy would give in his internal monologue in the books. When he sets foot in the imposing military academy of Westover, he remarks it looks like students are "training for Dracula's army." That's not a line that's actually in The Titan's Curse (Percy likens it to "an evil knight's castle" instead), yet it feels like it could be ripped straight from its pages.

Elsewhere, the series finds genuinely funny ways to cut its intense storyline. After Annabeth reminds Percy he noted he'd burn down Olympus to save her, he utters a hapless, "I was having a bad day when I said that." A later scene involving Percy and Thalia waiting for a prophecy from the Oracle also left me in stitches. Who other than Percy Jackson would ask a mummified Oracle for a high five in the hopes of waking her up?

According to recent reports, even the gods are funnier this season. Apollo (Hubert Smielecki) being a hot-shot bro who jams out to "Walking on Sunshine" contrasts perfectly with his sister, fierce huntress Artemis (Dafne Keen). Hearing her snarl "We are twins" after he keeps calling her "kid sis" is exactly the level of petty family drama I want to see from the Olympians.

None of this increased humor detracts from Percy Jackson and the Olympians' dramatic stakes. Far from it! The dark moments — and book readers know that this season will get dark — still land, even more so now that they've been juxtaposed with genuine lightness. Like in the books, that lightness emphasizes just how young these characters are, which makes their brutal trials even more heartbreaking. And with The Titan's Curse's fan-favorite "dam" jokes on the way, confirmed by Scobell himself, we know we'll be in for a lot more humor combined with that heartbreak.
