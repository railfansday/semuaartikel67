‘Light and sound’ therapy for Alzheimer’s flops in long-anticipated trial

A device that uses flickering light and rhythmic clicking sounds to stimulate the brain failed to slow memory loss in people with Alzheimer’s disease, according to results from a highly anticipated clinical trial.

The device, called Spectris AD, consists of goggles paired with headphones that individuals wear for an hour a day at home. The flickering light-and-sound show is designed to induce brain activity called gamma waves, which have been linked to increased attention and memory.

But after a year of treatment, participants who used the device did not show any improvement in their cognitive function compared with those assigned to a sham treatment. The results were reported by US medical-technology firm Cognito Therapeutics, which makes the device, at the Clinical Trials on Alzheimer's Disease meeting held in Boston, Massachusetts, on 16 November.

Although disappointed by the results, Christian Howell, Cognito’s chief executive, said that the results show beneficial signs for people with mild cognitive impairment. The organization aims to file for approval from the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) next year, he adds.

Recent findings suggest that “The effect that we saw in the mild subgroup was so meaningful,” Howell mentioned. “It just gives us optimism as we move forwards.”.

Industry observers note that but researchers are not convinced. “The trial was negative,” says Eric McDade, a neurologist at Washington University School of Medicine in St. Louis, Missouri. “If anything is going to happen after this, it should be a follow-up study in that mild population,” he says.

Recent findings suggest that in the trial, 673 people with Alzheimer’s disease were randomly assigned to use either an active or a sham Spectris AD device for 60 minutes a day for 12 months. After the year, those who used the active device scored, on average, 8.97 points less on a 156-point scale that measured cognition and ability to perform daily activities than they did before using the device. The scores of participants who used the sham device declined by 8.82 points.

In a recent development, howell claims that the device was more effective in people with mild, rather than moderate, cognitive impairment. In the mild-impairment group, active-device users experienced 42% slower decline than sham-device users — a difference of three points on average.

For Rob Howard, a psychiatrist at University College London who specializes in dementia, these data are exploratory rather than a persuasive proof of efficacy.

According to recent reports, “If this was a drug, such a clear failure would mean that a company would not proceed to seek regulatory approval,” notes Howard. The safety of the device doesn’t make it any more useful to patients, he adds.

According to recent reports, previous trials of Spectris AD haven’t panned out either, adds Howard. In 2024, a smaller trial of the device failed to slow the decline of cognitive and functional abilities in people with Alzheimer’s disease1.

The results are a setback for researchers who have been working to understand how gamma brain-wave modulation could bolster brain health.
