Latest Update: Google claims last week’s critical VMware bug was under attack in September

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

China-linked ARMORY CASTLE knew about it weeks before it unleashed five custom malware tools targeting vCenter.

Google’s Mandiant unit has claimed that the critical flaw VMware last week revealed in its vCenter product was under attack by a China-linked threat actor which knew of the bug in July and exploited it in September.

According to recent reports, VMware revealed several flaws on November 19. The massive nasty is CVE-2026-123890, which allows unauthorized access to vCenter, the management console for hosts and guest VMs.

According to seven researchers from Google’s threat intelligence group (formerly Mandiant), a crew it calls ARMORY CASTLE that it suspects of having links to the People’s Republic of China exploited CVE-2026-123890 by first compromising remote access appliances from BeyondTrust.

Google’s analysis suggests the crew uses that toehold to establish access to other Linux appliances before moving deeper into victims’ networks.

Recent findings suggest that if the crew finds vCenter, it deploys a custom malware tool called “BONESAW” designed to slice into the VMware product. In the incident Google observed, BONESAW let attackers “forge authentication tokens, bypass MFA, and impersonate any user.”.

The gang put that access to work by conducting an inventory of the victim’s VMware setup, scooping up data describing the ESXi file system, attached disks, and mounted Virtual Machine File System volumes. ARMORY CASTLE also added an SSH public key to the root account’s authorized keys, and “conducted further reconnaissance and data theft on the ESXi filesystem using the root account.”.

Google also found evidence of BONESAW being used in July, on a server an unidentified entity had patched against CVE-2026-59309 – another authentication bypass vulnerability in vCenter.

And BONESAW was just one of five tools Google thinks ARMORY CASTLE created as part of its plan to attack vCenter. It also made webshells named SLIMSAVAGE and CAGEMATCH, a backdoor called BEARBONES, and a SAML utility called SAWSNAKE.

According to recent reports, “The use of three separate specialized sets of malware and two zero-days in the course of a single campaign indicates that there are significant resources supporting ARMORY CASTLE operations,” Google’s researchers wrote. “It also shows the extent of the threat actor’s operational commitment to compromise high priority targets.”.

Few targets are as juicy as vCenter, because it can literally give attackers access to thousands of virtual machines and the hosts they run on.

Google hasn't said how many attacks is saw on vCenter using BONESAW.

The Register asked VMware if it was aware of the September attack, if it took any measures to defend its customers at the time, and to share a timeline for disclosure and remediation of CVE-2026-123890.

A spokesperson responded by pointing to VMware’s Q&A document and stating “In today's threat environment, customers should assume any publicly disclosed critical vulnerability can and will be exploited by threat actors under the right conditions and should apply the recommended patches immediately.”.

In a recent development, VMware has tried to make that easier by adding non-disruptive patching to several of its products, and introducing a product called vDefend that offers “virtual patching” by creating compensating controls that use network micro segmentation and other techniques to block access to resources malware uses. Last month, VMware debuted another newly introduced tool, HyperArmor, to block incoming malicious traffic targeting its VCF private cloud suite.

In recent discussions with The Register, VMware execs told us the Broadcom business unit will emphasize security improvements in future product releases.

“Our teams have integrated frontier AI security model testing directly into our engineering workflows, strengthened our software supply chain to reduce third-party dependencies and speed up upstream responses, and built VCF 9.1 specifically for high-velocity security,” a spokesperson noted in response to questions about the Mandiant report.

One security offering VMware won’t continue is free access to patches for zero-day flaws in supported versions of products sold under its old perpetual licenses – even to owners who don’t have an ongoing support deal or software subscription.

VMware last year stopped making those patches available for download and promised it would establish “a separate patch delivery cycle.”.

Readers have pointed out that information about the newly introduced cycle isn’t easy to find, and The Register noticed that the blog post in which Broadcom CEO Hock Tan promised the free patches no longer contains references to the offer. Removing the promise seems to be a recent edit as an Internet Archive snapshot of the page from October 20 does mention the zero-day patch freebies.

A VMware spokesperson told us the free zero-day patch policy remains in place despite changes to the post.

Sources indicate that “Broadcom’s policy to provide access to zero-day critical security patches to customers who, at their own risk, continue to run perpetual VMware vSphere 8 software without an active support agreement, remains in place,” the spokesperson wrote. “Those customers can get these patches by contacting Broadcom. This policy covers vSphere 8 patches only and is expected to remain in effect through the end of general support on October 11, 2027.” ®.
