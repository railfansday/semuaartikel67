Breaking: AI upended entry-level jobs. Here’s what employers are actually looking for

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

AI is changing nearly every aspect of the workplace—but especially entry-level jobs, to the point where Gen Z is calling for millennials (who are only in their 30s and 40s) to retire to make room. Many are placing the blame on AI for making tasks in many entry-level jobs outdated; data seems to back that up. A recent survey of nearly 7,000 workers, managers, and executives found that 51% believe AI is reducing early-career opportunities, while 49% say tasks that traditionally helped employees build work experience are increasingly being handled by AI. Another shows that entry-level listings have plummeted 35% since 2023; another suggests entry-level jobs in Newly introduced York City have been cut in half. That means the pressure is on for early-career candidates to show employers what they can bring to a role beyond their ability to use AI tools. A catch-22 emerges: many employers increasingly turn to AI, yet many also want candidates who can think independently, come up with original ideas, recognize when to put the technology aside and trust their own judgment. Indeed, many young workers find themselves in a tricky-to-navigate, even unfair entry-level job market. Luckily, there are tips that can make you stand out in a labor market that both increasingly relies on AI, and that also demands workers show employers the human skills they’re looking for. Don’t let AI write your resume for you Sure, AI can be a helpful writing tool: it can help you brainstorm, organize your experience, even give you a first draft. But if every candidate is using AI to write their application, it can become harder to show what makes you different. According to Amitoj Sandhu, Manager of Global Early Talent at HR platform Dayforce, employers are starting to see more applications that all sound alike. “Every single profile looks the same. They all are the same on the paper, so it becomes really hard for us to shortlist them and present in front of leaders,” he claims. Sandhu says AI is a useful starting point, but candidates need to be able to use their own voice, and  stand behind what they submit. After all, if AI is writing your resume for you, when it comes time for the interview, “will you be able to validate the results?” he says. Sandhu recommends preparing two or three strong examples from internships, school projects, or extracurricular activities that you can actually speak to authentically without farming everything out to ChatGPT. Then, be ready to walk an interviewer through those experiences and what you learned from them. With fewer entry-level roles apparently available, layering this in can make your application pop. Do use AI in your cover letter, but with caution “AI can be a great tool for writing a better cover letter. It can help candidates organize their ideas, communicate more clearly and make meaningful connections between their own interests and the company they’re applying to,” says Dan Kejsefman, Talent Director at Avature, a software development company. He says AI can raise the bar in terms of an application’s quality—but at the same time, “the motivation behind it still needs to be genuine.”  “I’d encourage candidates to start with their own reasons for wanting the job: What interests them about the company? What would they like to learn? What experiences or interests connect them to the opportunity? Then use AI to help communicate those ideas more effectively,” he says. The crucial thing is being able to explain and stand behind what’s in your cover letter. “AI can help you tell your story better,” he says, “but the story still has to be yours.” Use AI tools where you can—many jobs will expect you to use them to some extent, anyway—but standing out in an increasingly crowded entry-level field means tapping into what makes you, you. Don’t leave your curiosity at home According to Sandhu, when it comes time for the interview, curiosity is one of the biggest qualities employers look for. Missing that in an interview is “kind of a red flag for us,” Sandhu says. Sure, one way to demonstrate this is by researching the employer before the interview, and developing a genuine understanding of what the company does and the industry it operates in. But preparation is only part of it. Kejsefman says candidates also need to show genuine interest in the conversation, rather than simply relying on a list of questions prepared with AI during your interview practice. “AI can help candidates research a company, understand a role, and prepare thoughtful questions. That’s useful, and I wouldn’t discourage it,” Kejsefman explains. “But there’s a difference between asking an impressive question, and actually being interested in understanding the answer.” For example, curious candidates listen carefully, ask follow-up questions and try to understand why things work the way they do. That’s a timeless strategy, regardless of AI or not. Young workers “don’t necessarily have all the answers, and that’s perfectly fine, especially early in their careers. What matters is whether they show a real desire to learn and understand,” Kejsefman says. Do bring your own judgment to the work Sandhu says employers want candidates who can question the information AI provides, apply their own judgment and build on what the technology produces. Think of a software developer who might use ChatGPT to generate code, but still needs to determine whether it makes sense or meets the requirements or passes the right tests. Human judgment, after all, has long been touted as something that will still prove crucial in the AI era—in fact, as more decisions get outsourced to robots, it becomes even more valuable. “Can you use your own judgment to evaluate a response you get from AI? Can you build on that context? That’s what we’re looking for,” Sandhu says. In fields such as communications and journalism, employers still want to see creativity and original ideas rather than generic content generated by AI. “They want to look at how you think and what kind of ideas you can spark, not just all the generic ideas AI is producing,” Sandhu says. Kejsefman says AI adoption is still uneven across organizations, creating an opportunity for early-career professionals. A recent graduate who develops strong AI skills can bring something valuable to a team, even when working alongside people with much more professional experience. But they also bring enthusiasm and perspective older workers don’t have, and that robots definitely don’t have. Kejsefman also cautions against focusing exclusively on learning individual AI tools. As those tools continue to change, the ability to think critically, understand problems and evaluate results will become increasingly important. As AI takes on more workplace tasks, your ability to bring your own ideas, judgment and perspective to the work can help you stand out—especially when looking to break into a field or organization. Do prioritize experience over job titles Regardless of best practices, as AI continues to change the skills employers need, recent graduates may need to be more flexible about where they start their careers. For starters, Sandhu cautions against focusing too heavily on a particular job title or starting salary. “The significant mistake which they’re making is they’re running after titles and what the compensation is going to look like,” Sandhu says. Instead, he encouraged candidates to prioritize gaining experience and finding opportunities to get started, even if the position isn’t exactly what they initially envisioned or in their precisely preferred field. “Don’t run after a specific job title in the world which is changing every day,” he says. And that experience doesn’t have to come from a traditional job. Kejsefman encourages recent graduates to draw on those other past experiences, like university projects or volunteering—even personal projects or side gigs, which a lot of Gen Z already has anyway. “They need examples of situations where they’ve taken initiative, faced a problem, learned something new, or adapted when things didn’t go as expected,” Kejsefman says. “For someone starting their career, demonstrating that mindset can be much more valuable than presenting a perfectly polished result.” Make no mistake—for new workers in the workplace, it’s rough out there. But keeping these strategies, which mix time-tested tactics with AI adoption and adaptation, could open the right door at the right time.
