Here’s how Google’s Android XR glasses are handling the camera & privacy

According to recent reports, google’s Intelligent Eyewear push with the first Android XR glasses from Warby Parker and Gentle Monster are forced into an unfortunate uphill battle. The “perv glasses” stigma is very real because, frankly, early movers in this category didn’t respect privacy. Here’s how Android XR and Gemini are handling the camera and privacy on “Intelligent Eyewear,” and why I think Google has struck a near-perfect balance.

In a recent development, to quickly summarize the problem at hand (and name names), Meta’s smart glasses developed a reputation of being abused by bad actors who would disable the visible privacy LED (which Meta only recently fixed) or use these glasses in tasteless ways. Combine that with improper handling of recordings on Meta’s part and it’s a recipe for distrust and an earned social stigma. It’s not like Google is a simple bystander here, either. The original Google Glass project was met with the “Glasshole” stigma, and it’s something that Google has clearly learned a lot of lessons from.

Industry observers note that it’s a shame, because a camera in smart glasses serves such a functional role, both for the user in just capturing pictures and for the AI assistant built into the experience. I recently talked about how the camera was my favorite part of smart glasses in an issue of our 9to5Google Weekender newsletter, but how it just feels a bit wrong now.

Anyway, with that context in mind, let’s look at how Google is handling this touchy subject.

It has been revealed that it starts with the outward-facing light on every part of Android XR glasses, which works similarly to the privacy LED on Meta’s glasses. The key distinction here is this is a “recording light” on Android XR glasses. It turns on whenever the glasses are capturing content that can be viewed later in some form or fashion by the user. So if you’re taking a picture or recording a video, it lights up. If you’re just using Gemini for context, the light doesn’t activate. That’s because any camera captures being used for Gemini are deleted immediately after use. They won’t show up in your Gemini chat logs and that’s not a choice for the user – these images are purged immediately after they’ve been used so that you as the user nor Google can use them. That includes Google not using visuals from Gemini captures for AI training or advertising. Users also have control over what apps on Android XR glasses can use the camera.

Sources indicate that importantly, the light on each pair of Android XR glasses is also designed to avoid any tampering. Android XR glasses automatically perform a system check to ensure the light is working on a regular basis – you might catch the light blinking in white and blue while charging which is a part of the system check ensuring the hardware is fully functional. If the light is found not to be working, the camera is disabled.

According to recent reports, one of the pieces of this package that really caught my attention is how Google is handling the privacy of those around you. When your Intelligent Eyewear glasses send a photo to Gemini, your phone automatically redacts faces from the image before it’s sent to Gemini. This happens locally on the phone, essentially as a part of the glasses app. Similarly, voice data sent to Gemini filters out other voices besides your own. The voice filtering bit is just one way in which Google is doing some impressive things with the microphones here, with another example being that the live translation feature works in a 60-degree “cone” in front of you, filtering out other voices in the space.

It has been revealed that continuing on with how the camera interacts with Gemini, Google explained in a conversation with 9to5Google that Gemini is tuned not to use the camera in cases where it’s not obvious that the user wants the camera to be active. An interaction such as “what painting is this” would very clearly imply that the user wants the glasses to view the scene in front of them and identify the art piece, where “how much money is on the table,” Google explained, would ask for a follow-up before using the camera seeing as it’s not obvious that the user expects the camera to be used in that sort of phrase.

According to recent reports, another way in which Android XR glasses will avoid the recording stigma is with their limits. Google is limiting Intelligent Eyewear video recording to 90-second sessions. While that does limit what the user can do, Google said that most recordings fell within that window anyway. Meta, by comparison, offers recordings up to 5 minutes.

For the most part, Google’s efforts around privacy on Android XR glasses aren’t groundbreaking. This is how it should work. But seeing as the bar is exceptionally low thanks to a rough start from competitors, I found myself reassured and even a bit impressed by the messaging here – a clear explanation of the indicator light and the automatic on-device face redaction and deletion of Gemini imagery both go a long way.

Google’s sentiment around privacy on glasses is pretty nearly summed up by a sentence in its privacy post today: “…when technology can see the world around you, it has a responsibility to the people around you, too.”.
