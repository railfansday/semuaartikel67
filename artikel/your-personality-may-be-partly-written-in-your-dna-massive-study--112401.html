Your Personality May Be Partly Written in Your DNA, Massive Study of 1 Million People Reveals

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The personality traits others recognize in us may also have roots in our DNA, with research uncovering genetic connections that go beyond how we see ourselves.

Industry observers note that could your DNA help explain why you are naturally outgoing, highly organized, or more prone to worrying than others? A large international study suggests that some of the personality traits that make us who we are may be partly rooted in our genes, raising a fascinating question about how much of our personality is determined before we are even born.

In a recent development, researchers at the University of Tartu have identified more than 1,000 genetic variants associated with human personality in a study involving more than one million people. Published in the journal Nature, the findings offer new insight into how genetics contributes to differences in personality, while also showing why DNA alone cannot explain who we become.

The DNA of two randomly selected people is approximately 99.9% identical. Differences in the remaining 0.1% help influence characteristics such as appearance, disease risk, and responses to medication. The new research suggests that these small genetic differences are also associated with five major dimensions of personality: openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism.

Industry observers note that scientists have known for decades that both genetics and life experiences influence personality. Finding the specific genetic differences involved has been much more difficult because personality is shaped by many genes, each contributing an extremely small effect. Detecting those subtle patterns requires enormous amounts of data.

Between 2021 and 2022, the Estonian Biobank gathered personality information from nearly 80,000 participants. Although substantial for Estonia, that sample was not large enough to reliably identify many of the genetic associations researchers were looking for.

The University of Tartu team therefore combined its findings with 45 other international datasets, creating a more geographically diverse study population. For certain personality traits, the researchers could analyze data from as many as 1.1 million people. The Estonian sample was the third-largest of all participating groups and used the most comprehensive personality questionnaire.

Industry observers note that the genetic associations were remarkably consistent across different populations, including people of varying ages and geographical backgrounds. However, the identified variants explained only about 4.8% to 9.3% of the differences in personality traits.

Industry observers note that that relatively small proportion highlights how much remains unexplained. Social relationships, environmental influences, and personal experiences may all play substantial roles, along with the ways these factors interact with a person’s genetic makeup.

One particularly interesting feature of the Estonian research allowed scientists to examine personality from two perspectives. Participants could evaluate their own personalities and invite someone who knew them to provide an independent assessment.

“No other biobank in the world offers such an opportunity!” said Uku Vainik, Professor of Behavioral Genetics at the University of Tartu.

According to recent reports, this approach helped researchers address an important question about personality research. Are genetic associations connected to characteristics that other people can actually observe, or do they mainly reflect how individuals think about themselves?

The researchers found substantial overlap between the genetic signals associated with self-reported personality traits and those identified through assessments by acquaintances.

“This reveals that measurable personality traits are truly real, publicly observable characteristics of people, rather than merely individuals’ own opinions about themselves,” explained René Mõttus, Professor of Personality Psychology and co-lead of the study.

The findings are also opening new avenues for understanding the relationship between personality and physical health.

Kerli Ilves, a junior research fellow, is investigating whether personality traits may have causal connections to health-related behaviors, including those associated with body weight, smoking, and alcohol consumption. Her preliminary findings suggest that personality could influence these behaviors, although the relationship may also work in the opposite direction.

Established behavioral patterns might themselves contribute to changes in personality over time.

“We especially see this kind of relationship in smoking,” Ilves noted.

Researchers emphasize that these early findings do not yet establish cause and effect. Understanding whether personality shapes health habits, whether those habits alter personality, or whether both processes occur will require additional research.

“The next step is to use other methods, such as longitudinal studies, to determine whether changes in personality are accompanied by changes in health behavior, and vice versa,” explained Vainik.

The Estonian Biobank is planning another study focused on personality and cognitive functioning. By examining how personality develops and interacts with everyday behavior, researchers hope to better understand what influences people’s health choices and eventually support more effective, personalized approaches to healthy living.
