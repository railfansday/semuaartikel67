Breaking: The fight to save one of Australia’s rarest frogs has taken a cue from farmers

Selective breeding could help Australia’s southern corroboree frog survive longer after exposure to the deadly chytrid fungus.

A breeding technique from agriculture might help one of Australia’s most threatened frogs.

Recent findings suggest that researchers found that southern corroboree frogs can inherit traits that help them survive longer after exposure to a deadly fungus. That means selective breeding could make the species more resilient.

According to recent reports, the frog is functionally extinct in the wild. The culprit is chytridiomycosis, an infectious disease caused by the amphibian chytrid fungus.

In a recent development, captive breeding has kept the species alive. But returning frogs to the wild is hard because the fungus persists in the environment.

Lead author Mikaeylah Davidson completed the work as part of her PhD at the University of Melbourne.

The study tested whether genomic selection could guide conservation breeding for the frog.

In a recent development, davidson and her colleagues combined laboratory experiments with genome-wide genetic information. That included a custom genotyping array built for the frog.

In a recent development, the team found that southern corroboree frogs differ in how long they survive after exposure to chytrid fungus. Some of that variation is inherited.

“Using genomic data, we were able to show that survival following chytrid infection is a heritable trait, meaning some of the differences in survival are due to genetic differences that can be passed on to the next generation,” Davidson said.

“This means there is genetic variation we could potentially use to improve survival through selective breeding.”.

“Excitingly, this approach is a potential way to move beyond simply keeping endangered frogs alive in captivity, towards actively improving their ability to cope with the threats preventing their recovery in the wild.”.

Modeling suggested genomic information could guide breeding decisions.

It could potentially increase average survival time after exposure to chytrid fungus by around nine percent in the next generation.

The model projected that gain while accounting for inbreeding and genetic diversity.

An established captive breeding program offered a chance to test the idea in practice. Zoos Victoria and the Taronga Conservation Society support that program through partnerships.

In a recent development, the research suggests improved disease resilience doesn’t need to come at the expense of genetic diversity. That is especially significant for small, critically endangered populations.

In such groups, maintaining diversity and limiting inbreeding are central to long-term population health and evolutionary potential.

Senior author Lee Skerratt from the University of Melbourne argued the approach may also have potential for other threatened wildlife affected by persistent disease.

That is particularly true for species already managed through captive breeding.

“Amphibians are an important focus, with hundreds of species now maintained in captivity for conservation as chytrid fungus continues to threaten populations globally,” he said.

“Genomic selection could help conservation breeding programs move beyond preventing extinction by moving animals into captivity towards actively supporting animals’ ability to survive threats in the wild.”.

This work builds on six years of effort.

In a recent development, first, researchers developed the genomic tools and disease datasets needed to explore new strategies for the frog. They then combined a newly mapped genome and a custom genotyping array with extensive disease-resilience data.

That let them investigate selective breeding as a proactive approach to species recovery.

The next stage is to improve and validate the predictions.

Industry observers note that the team will use genomic information from more of the breeding population. They will also explore other genetic approaches to improve disease resistance.

In a recent development, researchers will also test whether the predicted improvements show up in later generations. And critically, they will check whether greater survival in the lab translates into better outcomes in the wild.

Amphibians are in deep trouble worldwide. The chytrid fungus has caused catastrophic declines and extinctions.

More than 200 threatened frog species are now kept in captivity. These colonies have prevented immediate extinctions.

But they are temporary refuges, meant to hold the line until threats can be mitigated.

Selective breeding offers a different path. Instead of just keeping animals alive, it can improve their ability to cope. That could raise the odds of successful reintroduction.

Industry observers note that while genomic selection has transformed plant and animal breeding, it has rarely been applied to wildlife. The main barriers have been missing datasets and a lack of structured breeding programs.

This frog had both, which made it an ideal test case.

The species produces fewer than 30 eggs per year and reaches sexual maturity at 2 to 5 years of age. Those traits slow generational turnover.

According to recent reports, the trait is also polygenic. No single gene has a large effect. Resistance is shaped by many loci, each contributing a little.

Because many genes contribute to survival, the team used genomic selection, which draws on information from across the genome.

And the modelling covers only part of the breeding population. Future work could capture more genetic variation.

The team proposes a phased rollout.

According to recent reports, first, refine the breeding value predictions with more parental genotyping. Second, test realized responses in controlled and semi-natural conditions. Third, run multi-generational selective breeding alongside ongoing diversity management.

In a recent development, data from each phase would feed back into the program. That supports adaptive management and continuous improvement.

According to recent reports, for now, the result is a proof of concept. It shows that captive populations can become more than passive safeguards. For a frog on the brink, that shift could matter a great deal.

The study is published in the journal npj Biodiversity.
