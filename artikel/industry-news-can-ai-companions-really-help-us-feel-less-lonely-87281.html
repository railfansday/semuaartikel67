As more and more people turn to artificial intelligence platforms such as ChatGPT for a wide range of applications, researchers are now asking whether the digital relationships they can provide might also meet our need for social connection.

Recent findings suggest that for most of us, simple friendship is having someone to talk to, share experiences with, and turn to when things go wrong. Whether this friendship has endured for decades or involves someone we met recently, having a social connection can be invaluable, even as we grow older and more isolated. But what if the “friend” isn't actually human? Since the introduction of large language models and conversational artificial intelligence, ChatGPT and other services have become part of everyday life. Millions of people have already turned to AI chatbots for answering basic questions about everything from financial planning to medical concerns. Many people also ask for help with personal problems and look to these tools for emotional support and companionship.

Certainly, AI chatbots offer advantages when human contact is unavailable. They're available anytime, day or night, and you don't have to set up a meeting, worry about interrupting, or fear being judged for any personal confessions you make. Users can also keep talking for as long as they want without worrying that the chatbot will end the session early.

For people dealing with loneliness, this kind of care can be a tremendous source of comfort. But could having an AI pal make loneliness worse? This is a worry, especially for people dealing with psychological issues that require professional (human) help. An article recently published in the journal Translational Issues in Psychological Science addresses many concerns about social chatbots. In the article, researchers Zehra Merve Kaya and Edanur Kanbur at Boğaziçi University in Turkey present results from numerous studies into artificial companionship and how it can function as an alternative to the human social relationships we depend on. They conclude that artificial companions can help reduce loneliness, but only temporarily and to a limited extent.

In a recent development, loneliness is much more than simply being alone. Many people enjoy solitude without feeling lonely; others may have extensive social connections but still feel their emotional needs aren’t being met. For them, having numerous friends, supportive families, and an active social life might still leave them feeling incomplete. Ultimately, loneliness is defined by the gap between perceived and desired social bonds. And it is far more common than you might think. According to a 2025 WHO report, one in six people surveyed globally reported feeling lonely, often for long periods. Being chronically lonely has been associated with medical and psychological problems, including depression, heart disease, cognitive decline, and premature death. Though most commonly associated with old age, loneliness can strike at any age. Surveys of adolescents and young adults suggest high levels of loneliness, despite social media and other online communication.

Recent findings suggest that so, can artificial intelligence make a difference? Modern chatbots can display empathic statements, handle personal disclosures, ask follow-up questions, and otherwise respond in ways that mimic social contact and make lonely people feel heard and understood. Along with always being available, AI chatbots can also provide someone to talk to during a sleepless evening. People dealing with social anxiety can often view chatbots as a way of avoiding many problems with human connection. That includes the fear of embarrassment and rejection, as well as building a sense of trust. Still, when you interact with a (live) friend, your shared experiences often shape their response to a cry for help. That includes memories, emotions, and your shared understanding of your joint connection. Even when the friend doesn’t know what to say, that concern still comes through, along with the sense of being acknowledged by someone with their own ideas, feelings, and wants. Relationships are based on shared experience, disagreement, compromise, and trust.

AI works differently, however. While a very empathetic chatbot might respond to someone’s fears or even offer helpful tips, no human hears the user’s feelings. Instead, AI provides an online system trained to recognize patterns and deliver relevant language. Though it may seem real to many people in distress, no AI system today can mimic the qualities of friendship that a human can provide (yet).There are also practical concerns related to talking with chatbots, because they can save and use sensitive data without the user’s knowledge. Providing chatbots as psychological support agents means having to resolve issues of privacy, informed consent, and emotional dependency.

Recent findings suggest that AI is changing rapidly. Chatbots have evolved to extended, natural conversations, and future versions may become even better at memorizing personal details, responding to emotions, and adapting to humans. Whether this technology helps with loneliness or creates other problems remains unclear. Kaya and Kanbur note that most of the evidence they have presented has not yielded clear results (thus far). While AI companionship can help many people, too many questions remain unanswered to recommend it as a widespread option.

True friendship is more than talking with a perpetually upbeat companion that may not really understand what you are saying. We also need to form ties with others with various lives and experiences that may challenge our perspective. These relationships matter because they require effort to maintain.

Recent findings suggest that while artificial intelligence can imitate many qualities of companionship convincingly, it cannot form an emotional bond. That is what makes human relationships unique. Ultimately, artificial intelligence is not a substitute for human contact, though it may work as a helper in the short term. Whether it cures loneliness or keeps people apart is still an open question.

Kaya, Z. M., & Kanbur, E. (2026). Artificial intelligence as a social substitute in loneliness. Translational Issues in Psychological Science. Advance online publication. https://doi.org/10.1037/tps0000547.
