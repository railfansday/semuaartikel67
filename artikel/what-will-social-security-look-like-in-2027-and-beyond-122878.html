What will Social Security look like in 2027 and beyond?

With potential program insolvency looming in 2032, the next few years for Social Security are being defined by conflicting pressures — including the 3.6% cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) in 2027, quickly rising financial obstacles for older Americans, and yet another proposal in Congress that could either stabilize benefits or sharply increase federal debt levels.

Earlier this month, The Senior Citizens League forecast a 3.6% COLA for 2028, matching the Social Security Administration’s recently unveiled 3.6% COLA for 2027. But Morningstar noted this week that the 2027 COLA — the largest since 2023’s 8.7% adjustment — will translate into smaller take-home increases once Medicare costs are deducted.

Most Social Security beneficiaries are enrolled in Medicare Part B, and premiums are automatically deducted from monthly checks. Morningstar reported that the standard monthly Part B premium will rise by $6.50 next year to $209.40, a 3.2% increase. That will allow most beneficiaries to keep more than 3% of the 3.6% COLA after the deduction.

Morningstar calculated that a retiree receiving the average October benefit of $2,092 would see a net benefit increase of about 3.29%, assuming they pay the standard Part B premium and have no other changes in deductions. A higher-income retiree with a $3,500 monthly benefit would see a net increase of roughly 3.41% under the same assumptions.

The COLA is based on the Consumer Price Index for Urban Wage Earners and Clerical Workers (CPI-W), which compares the average readings for July, August and September on a year-over-year basis. Analysts cited by Morningstar noted that CPI-W may understate seniors’ actual cost pressures, particularly for healthcare, and pointed to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics’ CPI-E index for older households, which has historically risen faster than CPI-W.

The Senior Citizens League (TSCL) stated the projected 3.6% COLA for 2028 is likely to arrive against a deteriorating backdrop for older adults, including senior homeowners who may still be making monthly mortgage payments. The group said that seniors are now the country’s fastest-growing homeless population and that many forgo vision, dental and hearing care due to costs.

Sources indicate that TSCL’s release emphasized that Social Security’s primary trust fund is projected to reach insolvency in 2032, triggering automatic benefit reductions if Congress does not act. The Medicare Part A hospital insurance trust fund is projected to become insolvent in 2033, the group remarked.

That timing elevates the stakes for the next Congress, which will be led by Democrats after their decisive victories in the recent midterm elections. Senators elected in the current cycle will serve from Jan. 3, 2027, through Jan. 3, 2033, overlapping the projected insolvency date. TSCL framed these terms as a key window for any reforms to prevent automatic benefit cuts for millions of retirees.

TSCL pointed to Social Security Administration projections, which show that eliminating the taxable wage cap would add “decades” to the trust fund’s life. And the organization’s internal polling indicates broad bipartisan support among seniors for lifting the cap.

As advocacy groups push for revenue-raising reforms, a fresh proposal in the Senate would instead rely on federal borrowing to preserve full benefits.

It has been revealed that ron Johnson (R-Wis.), the chairman of the Senate Budget Committee, has introduced the Stop Scaring Seniors Act. The bill would allow the Department of the Treasury to transfer general fund dollars to Social Security’s retirement trust fund once it becomes insolvent, according to a press release published last week by the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget (CRFB).

Recent findings suggest that under current law, Social Security can only pay benefits from dedicated payroll tax income and trust fund reserves. When the retirement trust fund’s reserves are exhausted — presently projected to be in less than six years — benefits would be cut by about 22% without legislative changes.

CRFB estimated that backfilling Social Security with general revenue under Johnson’s bill would increase federal debt by $190 trillion over the next 75 years on an inflation-adjusted basis, equal to roughly 165% of gross domestic product.

“Paying for Social Security out of the general fund would absolutely explode the national debt, costing a whopping 165% of GDP over 75 years before even considering the economic implications,” CRFB President Maya MacGuineas said in the release, arguing that the policy could trigger a debt crisis or unsettle bond markets even before it is fully implemented.

CRFB characterized the bill as avoiding “the real decisions” needed to repair Social Security’s finances and urged lawmakers to pursue a mix of adjustments — potentially through a bipartisan commission — that would close the program’s funding gap without shifting the shortfall onto the federal balance sheet.

The trajectory of Social Security and Medicare benefits has direct implications for housing demand and credit risk among the senior segment.

Net benefit increases closer to 3% than 3.6% may constrain seniors’ ability to absorb higher housing costs — including the required property tax, insurance and association dues associated with reverse mortgages. The impact could be particularly acute in housing markets with rapid property tax growth.

Rising healthcare and prescription costs could drive more senior homeowners to tap their equity through home sales or reverse mortgages. Increased levels of downsizing would alter inventory and product mixes in senior-heavy markets.

In a recent development, A 22% across-the-board benefit cut in the next decade, if Congress fails to act, would likely raise delinquency risks among fixed-income borrowers and could affect investor views of senior-heavy mortgage-backed securities pools. Conversely, large-scale borrowing to backfill Social Security, as proposed in Johnson’s bill, could influence interest rates and broader funding conditions.

Recent findings suggest that localized affordability pressures could also emerge. Growth in senior homelessness and medical cost burdens might increase demand for affordable and supportive housing. It could also influence municipal planning, tax policy and zoning decisions that shape housing development pipelines.
