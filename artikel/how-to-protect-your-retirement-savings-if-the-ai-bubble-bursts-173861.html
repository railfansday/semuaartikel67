My own research with a colleague has found that accounts on systematic investment plans akin to 401(k)s earned better market-adjusted returns than accounts funded with lump sums. Last, don’t hand the decision to a chatbot. It will likely give you sound general guidance, such as diversifying and not panic-selling, and that consensus advice is where AI is reliable. After stock markets, including the tech-heavy NASDAQ, got pummeled on Nov. 20, 2026, many Americans are asking themselves whether the huge bet on artificial intelligence is heading south.

While markets rebounded the following Monday, this question still has profound implications for retirement savings, given how much these investment accounts are tied to stocks.

Recent findings suggest that before Monday’s surge, the NASDAQ had plunged about 18% since early October and had lost more than US$4 trillion since Nov. 16. The broad-based S&P 500 also took a beating and had shed all its gains for the year.

As a finance professor and certified financial planner, I can’t tell you whether the bubble has truly burst for good. But I can explain how the default model that most 401(k) plans use works, where it protects you, and what you might consider changing.

Whether AI stocks recover or not, the steps that protect a retirement account are the same: Know what you own, keep the mix of assets in balance, and hold enough in savings and low-risk funds to cover the years when you’ll need it.

Here are some basic facts to guide your planning.

Most 401(k) savers hold a target-date fund, which is named for the year of your “target” retirement and is often chosen as a default option by the employer. To understand how the current stock market volatility affects retirement savings, it’s important to first explain how these funds work.

About 55% of U.S. families have a defined contribution retirement account, such as a 401(k) or an individual retirement account. And inside a 401(k), the investing is increasingly done for you.

Six in 10 looks administered by the fund giant Vanguard signed workers up automatically in 2025 when they started a fresh job. And when those plans set a default investment, 98% of savers choose a target-date fund.

Recent findings suggest that about 60% of the nearly 5 million savers in those plans held a single target-date fund and nothing else. Taken together, target-date funds now hold $4.8 trillion.

It has been revealed that inside each target-date fund is a list of index funds, which are named so because they track a particular market index. Vanguard’s series, the largest, is built mainly from four indices: U.S. stocks, international stocks, U.S. bonds and international bonds. The mix changes the older the saver gets, gradually moving away from high-return, high-risk stocks to safer, lower-yielding bonds.

In a recent development, for example, Vanguard’s 2055 fund was just over 91% stocks at the end of September 2026, reflecting the relative youth of those holding it. That share drops to 16% for savers invested in the 2027 fund, who expect to retire imminently.

That autopilot model is now being tested, starting with Nvidia.

Industry observers note that in the span of a week, Nvidia went from riding the AI boom to getting mired in crisis. On Nov. 5, after the U.S. midterm elections raised the prospect of newly introduced curbs on AI and data centers, Nvidia shares fell more than 9%, while the broad-based S&P 500 index lost 4.4%.

Then, on Nov. 12, Nvidia announced that CEO Jensen Huang had resigned. By the end of that week, Nvidia had lost more than $1 trillion in market value within a month.

That slide may now be reversed. Nvidia’s board reinstated Jensen Huang on Nov. 23, causing its share price to soar.

More broadly, it’s too early to say whether the AI bubble is over. Some analysts argue that it’s just losing some air rather than bursting. But amid the turmoil in tech stocks, savers may wonder whether a retirement strategy on autopilot should adjust to this fresh volatility.

In a recent development, by the end of 2025, the 10 largest companies made up nearly 41% of the S&P 500, according to RBC Wealth Management. Yet those 10, almost all of which are in tech and AI, were expected to produce only about 32% of the index’s 2025 earnings.

In other words, investors are paying a premium for these shares because they’re expecting great profits later. Bad news can shrink that premium quickly.

In a recent development, furthermore, diversification doesn’t mean guaranteed protection all the time. In 2008, during the global financial crisis, funds built for people retiring in 2010 lost nearly 24% on average.

In principle, savers don’t need to do anything as long as they choose to stay on autopilot until retirement. Fund managers buy and sell assets to stick to the target mix – a practice known as rebalancing.

But rebalancing resets only the broad categories rather than trimming one particular organization for growing too large. As Nvidia’s share of the market rose, so did its share of the fund.

For people who hold just one target-date fund, I believe the right response to this month’s news is to leave their 401(k) account alone. The fund has been rebalancing all along, and your stake in any one firm is modest.

But a saver still needs to check three things.

First, consider the year you expect to retire. If you want to move it earlier or later, or want more or less risk, you should choose a fund with a different year.

Second, look at what the fund’s share of stocks is today, which is on the fund’s fact sheet. If you’re 60 and your fund is 60% stocks, a 30% fall in stocks would cut your balance by roughly 18%.
