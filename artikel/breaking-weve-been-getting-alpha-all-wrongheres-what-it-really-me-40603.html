Think of the first thing that comes to mind when you hear the term “alpha male.” Maybe you picture a John Wayne type or a Douglas MacArthur; maybe a "jock" or the captain of the football team; maybe even a criminal like Tony Soprano of the television show The Sopranos or Frank Underwood of House of Cards. When we think of the concept of the “alpha male,” we tend to imagine it having to do with rugged independence, aggression, and dominant behavior.

Popular belief has largely aligned with these archetypal depictions of the “alpha.” This view of masculinity as dominant, aggressive, and hyper-independent is widely held across the globe, from the concept of the “man box” in Australia, which describes seven principles of being a man (self-sufficiency, acting tough, physical attractiveness, rigid gender roles, heterosexuality and homophobia, hypersexuality, aggression and control) to the “manosphere,” a loosely connected network of online communities that promote rigid, often unhealthy, views of manhood that align with the “alpha” concept (MensLine Australia, 2022).

But ironically, natural science contradicts this rigid interpretation of the “alpha” concept. While popular culture and media have depicted the “alpha male” in a way that has become widely accepted and unchallenged, zoology directly contradicts the notion that alphas are aggressive, dominant, and isolated. Early research on animal behavior spawned the concept of the “alpha male.” When zoologist Konrad Lorenz studied animal behavior in the 1960s to understand aggression in the natural world, he concluded that there were three hierarchical categories found in the animal kingdom:.

Lorenz’s conclusion implied that most animal behavior was predicated on aggression and dominance, and that animals were classified by either being dominant or submissive. This early animal research laid the foundation for what gradually became a widely held (yet oversimplified) belief about male behavior, with the “alpha” being seen as preferred and elite.

In the 1970s, however, biologist and expert on wolf behavior David Mech drew a very different conclusion than had Lorenz: the idea of the “alpha” had been oversimplified and misapplied in earlier research. Mech’s research revealed that wolf packs acted as family units, relying on cooperation and caregiving, rather than on fighting and aggression. The alpha role was actually taken on by both wolf parents, not just the male, to protect wolf cubs, ensure survival, and establish a family hierarchy (Mech, 1970).

Society often perpetuates myths that simplify more complex ideas: in this case, it is easier and simpler to view “alpha” as a singular personality type than to see it as a collaborative model that cannot be rigidly defined. The “alpha” concept has become so oversimplified that the way we tend to view it today is light years from its actual meaning in the animal kingdom. And the collateral damage of this misattribution has profound implications: A 2025 survey concluded that “young men ages 16 to 25 consuming online masculinity influencers’ content reported higher rates of emotional distress,” including feelings of worthlessness, nervousness, and sadness (Fischer, et al., 2025). A 2023 survey found that men in America are five times lonelier than they were in 1990, and, while finding a community is a necessity, not all communities—such as those like the “manosphere” are helpful or psychologically healthy (Bledsoe & Smith, 2025).

Sources indicate that despite the scientific debunking of the “alpha” myth, by the late 20th century, the idea of the “alpha male” had seeped into society and become widely accepted, as it remains today. We still hear this term used, and it is still thought by many to accurately describe “preferred” male behavior. Though it is often seen as describing qualities of leadership and success, the “alpha male” idea actually holds no scientific credence and is more a social construct than a fact of nature.

Sources indicate that survival in the animal kingdom is predicated upon collaboration, not isolation. A wolf pack is a “pack” for a reason—it would not survive without its collaborative dynamic. A “lone wolf”—though romanticized in popular culture and media—would actually struggle to get by without the support of a collaborative environment. It is no wonder, then, that when we rigidly view the “alpha” personality as being preferred, we run into countless problems, which can result in serious societal fissures such as a normalization of aggression, isolation, and unhealthy behaviors and responses. Taking a cue from nature might help us to reframe our often toxic definition of what “alpha” really means.
