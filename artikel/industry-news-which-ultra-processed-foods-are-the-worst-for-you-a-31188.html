Research over the years has shown exactly how ultra-processed foods (UPFs) negatively affect the body. What’s still being studied is how many of these specific foods, and how much of them, impact the rise in certain cancers and other diseases. Although the internet is rife with stats showing an uptick in certain cancers among younger adults, a recent study conducted among a massive 460,000 Americans debunked one of the many fear-mongering rumors that ultra-processed foods are associated with increased colorectal cancer risk specifically. But before you place your next fast food delivery order, Caitlin Hosmer, MS, RD, LDN, a registered dietitian and co-founder of MenoPantry alongside Mark Gilbert, nutritionist from The 1:1 Diet by Cambridge Weight Blueprint, explains which parts of the meal are potentially the most harmful, and what you should know about the study’s findings to understand which ultra-processed foods are actually the worst for you.

Most researchers use a system called NOVA, developed by a Brazilian epidemiologist, Carlos Augusto Monteiro, who coined the term “ultra-processed foods.” Hosmer explains that NOVA sorts foods into four groups based on how much they’ve been processed and not by their nutrients.

Sources indicate that gilbert argues that the system is questionable. “Even nutrition experts were unable to categorize foods reliably or consistently into one of [these] four categories, from unprocessed to ultra-processed. Other systems, including IFIC, Nutri-Score, and the proposed CHIPS system, exist but are similarly controversial,” says Gilbert.

To better understand what these foods are exactly, Hosmer indicates the simplest way to put it is that a processed food is still recognizably food that’s been preserved or prepared. An ultra-processed food has been taken apart. “These foods are designed to be cheap, shelf-stable, convenient, and very easy to overeat. The key caveat is that NOVA describes how a food is made, not how nutritious it is. So the Ultra-processed category can hold both a hot dog and a packaged whole-grain bread,” says Hosmer.

Gilbert further explains that the processes many of these foods undergo often reduce healthy, essential nutrients, remove fiber, and make the resulting food easier to consume, absorb, and digest, leading to untoward blood sugar levels, less satiety and, in the longer term, poorer metabolic health.

Sources indicate that gilbert notes that it’s difficult to deem any one type of UPF as “worst” simply because genetic and environmental risk factors to various diseases and ill health vary so vastly. “So, just like no single diet is ‘best’ for everyone, no single food is necessarily worst. For instance, if someone has a high heart and vascular disease risk, they might benefit from less saturated fat. If someone has a high risk of diabetes, they should avoid sugar and other refined carbohydrates that raise their blood sugar levels,” Gilbert explains.

Recent findings suggest that however, Hosmer points out that when researchers break ultra-processed foods into subgroups, processed meat consistently comes out on top. The aforementioned study of more than 460,000 adults, conducted by the National Cancer Institute (NCI), found that ultra-processed food intake overall wasn’t linked to colorectal cancer—but higher consumption of meat from ultra-processed food sources was. Hosmer references a 2024 Harvard analysis in The British Medical Journal that found the same pattern for mortality.

“Across more than 30 years of follow-up, meat-, poultry-, and seafood-based ready-to-eat products revealed the strongest and most consistent associations, followed by sugar-sweetened and artificially sweetened beverages, dairy-based desserts, and ultra-processed breakfast food. That category includes processed meats such as hot dogs, deli meats, bacon, sausages, and many frozen breaded meat products,” Hosmer summarizes.

In a recent development, second to processed meat are sweetened drinks—both sugar-sweetened and artificially sweetened. “Sodas, sweet teas, energy drinks, and diet drinks give you sweetness and calories (or the expectation of calories) with no fiber, protein, or real food structure. They show up consistently across heart, metabolic, and mortality research,” Hosmer notes.

Industry observers note that sugar-laced and dairy-based desserts alongside sugary breakfast foods follow on the list. “Many sugary cereals and pastries are basically dessert dressed up as breakfast,” says Hosmer.

Industry observers note that these foods won’t necessarily give you cancer or lead to other diseases; Hosmer explains that the more useful message is that “worst” means low in what the body needs and high in what it doesn’t. In the NCI study, higher intake of whole grains, fiber, dairy products, and calcium from ultra-processed food sources was linked to a lower incidence of colorectal cancer. “A packaged whole-grain bread and a package of bologna are both “ultra-processed,” but they are not the same choice,” she adds.

It has been revealed that many UPFs are often made to be irresistible, meaning you’ll likely eat more than the recommended serving size. Gilbert explains that this leads to large spikes in blood sugar and doesn’t reduce satiety as much as most whole foods do. UPFs can also increase overall calorie intake. “Increased calorie intake and excess blood sugar spikes cause weight gain, high blood pressure, inflammation, diabetes, unhealthy blood lipids, generally poor metabolic health, and a variety of other effects which increase a person’s odds of the most common serious health conditions and diseases, reducing health span and lifespan,” Gilbert warns.

To illustrate, Hosmer points to what she calls a small but elegant 2019 National Institutes of Health (NIH) trial led by Kevin Hall, which she says has the strongest causal evidence. Twenty adults lived on a metabolic ward and ate either an ultra-processed or a minimally processed diet for two weeks each. The diets were matched in calories, sugar, fat, fiber, and sodium, and people could eat as much as they wanted. On the ultra-processed diet, participants ate about 500 more calories a day, ate faster, and gained weight—about two pounds; on the minimally processed diet, they lost about the same amount. “That points to something about the food’s form, not just its nutrients: it’s soft, energy-dense, quick to eat, and slow to trigger fullness,” says Hosmer.

It has been revealed that this depends greatly on the specific foods in question. “That’s what makes this newly introduced study that differentiates between the different kinds of processed foods particularly crucial,” indicates Hosmer. “The clearest evidence is for processed meat. In 2015, the World Health Organization’s International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classified processed meat as a Group 1 carcinogen for colorectal cancer. That’s the category for strong evidence that something causes cancer, though it says nothing about how massive the risk is,” Hosmer clarifies. More practically, she further explains that the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) estimated that eating 50 grams of processed meat every day—about one hot dog or a few slices of deli meat regularly—is associated with roughly an 18 percent increase in baseline colorectal cancer risk. “That’s a relative increase: for an average person, it moves a lifetime risk from approximately four to five percent to roughly five to six percent,” says Hosmer.

Industry observers note that for ultra-processed foods as a whole category, Hosmer indicates the picture is less clear. The latest NCI study is one of the largest ever done on this question. When the team looked by food group, whole grains and dairy were linked to lower risk and meat to higher risk. The researchers note possible opposing effects of some ultra-processed foods on colorectal cancer etiology—mainly that the helpful and harmful foods cancel each other out in the total. More specifically, the total consumption may matter more than the specific foods or food groups.

In a recent development, according to Hosmer, there’s also an indirect pathway. “Diets high in ultra-processed foods are linked to obesity, and excess body fat is an established risk factor for many cancers, including breast cancer after menopause, endometrial cancer, and colorectal cancer,” she notes. Hosmer warns, though, that this study’s findings shouldn’t be read as ultra-processed foods are fine. “Read it as a reminder that it’s hard to make a single recommendation about such a large and varied category of food.” When the researchers broke ultra-processed foods down by type, the results lined up with what we already know: whole grains, fiber, and dairy were linked to lower colorectal cancer risk, and processed meat to higher risk. Hosmer’s challenge is to check the new data against well-established research. “Headlines alone can be misleading, especially on topics as emotionally charged as what we eat.”.

Hosmer explains that the risks associated with ultra-processed foods include two things: how much of someone’s diet comes from these foods, and how much room their body has to absorb the negative effects.

It has been revealed that one caveat from the new study that Hosmer points out is that participants were mostly older white Americans, so results may not generalize to younger or more diverse populations. “That matters because colorectal cancer is rising in adults under 50, the group that has grown up recently, and with the most ultra-processed food available,” she cautions.

In a recent development, gilbert adds that knowing your family’s health history and getting various tests can offer some indications of individual risks, but predicting these risks is by no means foolproof. “Ultimately, those who have prediabetes, diabetes, high blood pressure, cardiovascular disease or cancer, or are living with overweight or obesity are the most at risk because these conditions can often be avoided or improved with better nutrition,” says Gilbert.

Myth: All processed food is bad.

Both Gilbert and Hosmer agree here. “Some foods can be intentionally processed to increase and improve their nutritional profile rather than reduce it,” Gilbert explains. Frozen vegetables, canned beans, plain yogurt, canned salmon, and bagged salad are strong examples. They are processed and still very good for you. Processing often makes healthy food more affordable and accessible. Gilbert’s 1:1 Diet is designed to align with this. “The products we produce are processed, but instead of reducing the essential nutrient content of the products, we actually increase it, adding vitamins, minerals, essential fats, and quality protein,” Gilbert notes, adding that extensive research has shown his diet improves blood sugar, blood pressure, inflammation, metabolic health, cardiovascular health, and reverses diabetes in about half of dieters.

Myth: This fresh study proves ultra-processed food is safe.

Hosmer notes explicitly that it doesn’t. “It looked at only one disease, colorectal cancer, in a mostly older population, and participants reported their diets just once, back in the mid-1990s. The authors themselves point out that ultra-processed foods became far more common and varied over the following two decades, so the study likely underestimated how much people were actually eating,” she clarifies. “The fairer reading is that which ultra-processed foods you eat matters more than the category itself: a whole-grain bread and a hot dog may both be “ultra-processed,” but they don’t affect the body the same way.”.

Myth: It’s only about nutrients, and processing doesn’t matter.

Recent findings suggest that kevin Hall’s controlled feeding trial suggests otherwise. People ate significantly more on the ultra-processed diet even when nutrients were matched, so a food’s structure and how fast you eat it matter too.

Myth: Organic, plant-based, or “clean label” means it isn’t ultra-processed.

Myth: You have to completely eliminate ultra-processed foods.

Perfection isn’t the goal. Hosmer points out that in the NCI study, replacing 10 percent of the total weight of ultra-processed foods with minimally processed foods was associated with a 2 percent lower incidence of colorectal cancer. “We don’t yet know exactly what that looks like on an individual’s plate. The study measured diets statistically, not swap by swap. But the direction is clear, and it fits decades of nutrition research: incremental shifts away from ultra-processed foods appear to matter, even when they’re modest. Start with categories that matter most: processed meats and sweetened drinks,” she concludes.
