Meerkats cross the desert together, but what happens when one finds itself alone?

Researchers tracked 20 meerkats in the Kalahari Desert to see how they navigated familiar territory after being separated from their groups.

Industry observers note that meerkats leave their burrows together each morning and forage as a group. How well can one find its way alone?

In South Africa’s Kalahari Desert, researchers carried 20 adult meerkats up to 0.9 miles from where they caught them.

Industry observers note that after release, 18 reached a familiar spot or found their group. Fourteen headed first to the burrow where the group had slept.

The team found no clear advantage for older or more experienced animals.

The researchers suggest that daily group travel may give meerkats enough experience to navigate alone.

The study was led by Mathilde Martin of the University of Zurich. The team worked alongside the Kalahari Meerkat Project, which follows about 15 wild groups accustomed to people.

The project is run from the Kalahari Research Centre, which is known as the KRC.

“At the KRC, our work with meerkats allows us to distract them with a water bottle and fit their collar in a few seconds while they stand upright drinking; a mere formality for them,” Martin said.

It has been revealed that A GPS tag on each collar logged the position of the animal once every second. A radio transmitter was also fitted, along with a small audio recorder for the meerkat’s calls.

Sources indicate that the team tested a total of 20 adults from five groups between January and March 2025. Fifteen of these animals were male, whereas only five of them were female.

Each meerkat was caught while its group was foraging and was released up to 0.9 miles (1.5 kilometers) away.

Such a distance is greater than the ground usually covered by a meerkat group in a whole day.

The release sites were located inside the territory of each group. The team chose areas that the group had not visited for at least 30 days.

Homing experiments of this kind have previously been carried out on honeybees and desert ants. Highly social animals that travel in tight groups have rarely been tested in the same way.

This is because the knowledge of one animal is difficult to separate from the decisions of the group.

According to recent reports, eighteen of the 20 meerkats left the release site. The sleeping burrow, the capture spot or the group itself was reached by all 18 of them.

Some of the meerkats departed almost at once, whereas others circled the release site for nearly 90 minutes.

The area covered by them ranged from half an acre to almost 17 acres (2,050 to 68,200 square meters).

A meerkat rarely strayed from its course once a direction had been chosen. Sixteen of the animals aimed for a fixed place.

The sleeping burrow was visited first by 14 of those 16.

On average, the straight-line distance to the target amounted to 82% of the distance actually walked by the meerkat.

Sources indicate that the release areas had not been visited by the group for a month. The team therefore concluded that the meerkats had been navigating from long-term memory.

Two males never left the release site, and they were carried back to their groups after 90 minutes. They were no younger or less experienced than the others.

The authors suspect that stress kept them there, and not a lack of knowledge.

Some meerkats had been expected to navigate better than others. Subordinate males sometimes leave the group and go alone in search of mates.

Other females are occasionally driven out for a while by the dominant female.

Industry observers note that solo trips of this kind could be counted as practice. The researchers also considered the age of each animal, together with the time it spent on guard duty.

Attention was given as well to how often it scent-marked the territory or dug at shelters.

None of those traits predicted how long a meerkat explored. Neither did they predict how accurately it set off or how straight it travelled.

No clear difference was found between males and females either, although only five females took part.

The authors think that the explanation lies in the territory itself. It is small and stable, with 0.3 to 0.6 miles (0.5 to 1 kilometer) covered by a group daily.

The whole territory has probably been crossed several times by each member by the time adulthood is reached.

The authors wrote that pigeons of different ages are likewise able to find their way home equally well.

Sources indicate that on the way home, the meerkats spent about two-thirds of their time searching for cues. Most of the remaining time was spent moving.

Few of them stopped to eat, and those that did only grabbed millipedes crawling on the sand.

According to recent reports, the animals stood upright or climbed a bush or tree trunk in order to scan the landscape. Overall, they spent 10 times longer on looking than on sniffing.

This balance between looking and sniffing was altered late in the trip. By that stage, a meerkat had reached its target and was searching for its group. Sniffing then took up more than 20% of its cue-searching time.

Along the way, meerkats were often seen sniffing around boltholes. These are the small shelters that the animals dig across their territory. An earlier experiment had found that meerkats remember where their boltholes are.

The authors suggested that boltholes may serve as landmarks in a meerkat’s memory of its territory.

The search was not ended by reaching the burrow, because the group could have moved on since the capture.

Sources indicate that thirteen of the 20 meerkats rejoined their groups on their own. The slowest took almost three hours.

The burrow or the capture site was reached by five others. The group had nevertheless not been found by them when the three-hour limit ran out.

These animals were caught again and returned by the team.

It has been revealed that the researchers could not explain why some meerkats succeeded while others did not. Success was not predicted by a straighter trip to the burrow.

Nor was it predicted by the number of scent marks left behind by the group.

Sources indicate that the tracking of a group was compared by the authors to a predator following its prey. The target keeps moving and its route is not known to the searcher.

Recent findings suggest that twenty animals make up a small sample, as the authors wrote. All of them were subordinate adults older than 10 months, so pups and dominant animals were not tested.

Recent findings suggest that every release was also made inside familiar territory. The results therefore do not show how a meerkat would manage in a place it does not know.

Sources indicate that two reasons for looking around could not be fully separated either. A lone meerkat that scans the landscape may be checking for predators as much as searching for landmarks.

If many group members know the territory, the next question is who leads when the group moves.

The authors want to know whether certain traits influence which animal takes the lead. The physical needs of an animal and its social rank are two examples.

Co-author Benjamin Robira mentioned the team now wants to understand what makes up a meerkat’s mental picture of its territory.

The researchers also intend to study how the animals get from one point to another.

The full study was published in the Journal of Experimental Biology.
