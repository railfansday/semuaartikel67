‘Line their own coffers’: independent MPs lambast Labor’s last-minute changes to election funding laws

Independent MPs say the government’s last-minute changes to controversial election funding laws would allow “an uncapped amount of money” to flow to the major parties through investment funds.

The amendments to the incoming federal electoral laws, which the government describes as “technical”, were introduced on Tuesday afternoon and come weeks before they are set to begin operating.

From January, political parties and candidates will face campaign spending limits and donation caps during federal elections. Disclosure thresholds for political gifts and donations will be lowered to $5,000, and public funding per vote received will be increased from $3.35 to $5.

But crossbenchers, who have long criticised the changes as favouring major parties and incumbents, say the latest tweaks further enable a lop-sided system.

They take particular aim at changes to allow political parties to invest and grow their funds without the interest earned contributing to donation ceilings.

“The amendments would enable a federal account holder to continue to grow their capital in a competitive manner and when the federal account funds are not required for incurring electoral expenditure,” the bill’s explanatory statement remarked.

“This would allow federal account holders a greater degree of flexibility to administer their financial assets as they wish, protecting the right to take part in the conduct of public affairs directly.”.

According to recent reports, major parties often set up investment arms to grow funds in time for election cycles. The Cormack Foundation in Victoria serves as a major financial backer for the Liberals, while Labor Services and Holding and Pilliwinks are Labor’s and the Nationals’ equivalents, respectively.

In 2024-25, Cormack donated $4.7m to the Liberals, while Labor Services and Holding donated $598,00.

The federal government intends to pass the eleventh-hour changes in both houses this sitting fortnight ahead of the electoral laws coming into effect from 1 January 2027.

The independent ACT senator, David Pocock, mentioned the bill continues the “major party stitch-up” and enabled “an uncapped amount of money to flow from their huge investment vehicles into their political accounts”.

“They like to talk about taking money out of politics but they legislate to drive more in,” Pocock said.

“And while the government is prioritising law changes that will line their own coffers, they’re failing to bring forward the overdue measures to strengthen our democracy like banning deepfakes in elections or legislating truth in political advertising.”.

Independent Curtin MP, Kate Chaney, remarked the changes didn’t address the bill’s serious flaws, and instead included tweaks to protect the existing investment vehicles and assets of major parties.

“Rather than being a new act of bastardry, this bill tweaks the act of bastardry performed on Australian democracy last year,” Chaney said.

“We don’t let Coles and Woolies make laws about supermarket competition, but this is what it looks like when major parties make laws about who can compete with them.”.

The electoral laws overhaul is already being challenged in the high court by former parliamentarians, Zoe Daniel and Rex Patrick, on three grounds.

The two are arguing the $800,000 cap on spending in each electorate impinges on the implied right of political communication.

While the expenditure cap applies to all candidates, the former independents argue newcomers are at disadvantage because major parties can also tap into their national campaign budget, which is capped at $90m.

They also argue two other changes unfairly disadvantage candidates without political parties: the $50,000 cap on individual donations, aimed at preventing Clive Palmer-sized campaigns, and rules stopping a single donor, such as Climate 200, from giving to more than five candidates in a state or territory.

Daniel and Patrick stated the latest amendments were not about getting big money out of politics but stopping competitive newcomers.

“Rather than trying to make this flawed law fair, the government is doing the opposite by introducing amendments that would entrench access to wealth for the ALP and the LNP through the enormous investment vehicles that only they have,” they mentioned.

Recent findings suggest that “No latest entrant could amass such wealth under the new laws. They are pulling up the drawbridge, and it is transparent to anyone who cares to look.

“It is classic cartel behaviour akin to Coles and Woolies land-banking to prevent competition from newcomers.”.
