Hera spacecraft gets 1st up-close look at stadium-sized hole punched in asteroid by NASA DART mission

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

NASA's DART Earth-defense mission punched a stadium-sized hole in a distant asteroid, and we have seen it up close for the first time thanks to the Hera spacecraft.

Recent findings suggest that NASA's DART (Double Asteroid Redirection Test) mission made history on Sept. 26, 2022, when it slammed into Dimorphos, the small moonlet of the much larger asteroid Didymos. The mission tested if such an impact could be deployed to divert an asteroid on a collision course with Earth. Scientists have known for some time that this deflection attempt was successful; the system's orbit around the sun was lengthened by 33 minutes, but the European Space Agency's (ESA) Hera mission released in October 2024 to travel the 120 million miles (194 million kilometers) to the Didymos system to observe the effect of the DART impact in greater detail.

Now, the spacecraft has revealed that DART flattened one face of Dimorphos with a stadium-sized crater and set it tumbling chaotically. The impact also appears to have blasted out hundreds of previously unknown boulders that are now circling the binary asteroids.

"Scientists debated whether Dimorphos had sustained an impact crater, changed its shape, or stayed the same," Hera principal investigator Patrick Michel, Director of Research at the Observatoire de la Côte d'Azur, said in a statement.

In a recent development, "Much to our surprise, the answer turns out to be all three at once! The face struck by DART has been flattened into an impact crater the size of a sports stadium, which extends across the asteroid's entire width.".

Michael Kueppers, ESA's Hera mission scientist, detailed some of the changes to the impacted moonlet seen by the spacecraft: "As Hera returned more images, it became clear that the DART impact has left Dimorphos tumbling wildly in its orbit.

"Another big surprise is that Hera is showing that the asteroids are surrounded by hundreds of orbiting boulders, just a few meters in size.".

It wasn't all change for Dimorphos. Hera discovered that the boulder fields covering the regions of the moonlet not impacted look just like they did when DART imaged it in 2022.

Industry observers note that hera isn't finished with Didymos and its moonlet. The ESA spacecraft is currently in a rectangular orbit around the asteroid binary system, using its thrusters to maintain a distance of around 18.6 miles (30 kilometers). This allows Hera to continue observing Didymos and Dimorphos.

Hera will soon come to within around 8 miles (13 km) of the asteroids, capturing even more detailed images of the space rocks. Following this, it will deploy two showbox-sized CubeSats, which will get even closer to the asteroids.

The CubeSat Juventas will use low-frequency radar to probe the interior of Dimorphos, the first time this has ever been done for an asteroid, and will eventually attempt to land on the moonlet to measure its gravity. Meanwhile, the Milani CubeSat will prospect asteroid materials and dust around the Didymos system.

Together, Hera and its CubeSats will more precisely determine the mass of Dimorphos, which, when combined with information about the orbital change of the system, will be crucial in planning missions to deflect threatening asteroids.

Hera's observations were revealed on Wednesday (Nov. 25) at the dinosaur hall of the Museum für Naturkunde Berlin, Germany. That location isn't a coincidence: It is a stark reminder of why this planetary defense mission is so vital. Especially if humanity doesn't want to go the way of the dinosaurs, whose rule on Earth ended when an asteroid fell from the sky."Sixty-six million years ago, an asteroid impact changed the course of life on Earth. Today, for the first time in history, humanity is validating how to prevent such an event from happening again," Hera mission manager Ian Carnelli explained. "NASA's DART mission demonstrated that we can change an asteroid's orbit. Hera is now revealing what happened to the asteroid itself. "By combining these two missions, humanity is taking a decisive step towards making asteroid deflection a reliable tool for protecting our planet.".
