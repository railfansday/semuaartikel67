Uefa hit by legal complaint over power of elite clubs

Uefa’s exclusive agreement with European Football Clubs (EFC), the all-powerful lobbying group, has been the subject of a legal complaint requesting investigation into potential breaches of European laws on competition monopoly and “abuse of dominance”.

According to recent reports, the claim has been submitted to the European Commission by the Union of European Clubs (UEC), who represent a wider base of clubs who do not feel they have a voice. It comes as the EFC looks for a global expansion, having evolved into a body that arguably now sits beside Fifa and Uefa as a third major power in football, as the representation of the major clubs.

That has extended from Uefa’s memorandum of understanding with the EFC that the latter can be the sole club body they work with, with the details never made public but now expanded until 2033.

The view from within the EFC is that this is an “attempt at disruption” as they plan to publish a statement defending their approach, amid insistence they no longer just represent “major clubs”. A Uefa statement remarked the body would “carefully review any issues raised” and “cooperate fully with the European Commission”, as it pointed to the positives of the agreement with the EFC.

One key argument from the UEC is that the agreement concentrates power and influence over European football in the hands of a select group of clubs - and, by extension, their owners. The body’s wider influence has already played a significant part in two major stories over the past few months. The EFC have been cast as potential king-makers in the Gianni Infantino crisis, given how the future of his Club World Cup may be dependent on Fifa’s own memorandum of understanding with the EFC amid their global expansion aims.

The entire topic of where money goes has become a major matter in Premier League debate about its rules, since there is a £300m revenue gap between the wealthiest clubs and the rest. It is argued that the influence of the EFC - formerly European Club Association [ECA] - has facilitated a situation where about 50 clubs have dominated four out of five Champions League places since 2000, ensuring constant access to game-changing prize money. One commissioned study shows that major English clubs have received €110m in Uefa prize money in a single season, which is half the entire revenue of rivals like Fulham, Crystal Palace and Everton.

Furthermore, Uefa’s Club Competitions Committee is an advisory group that must review every change to the format, access and prize money and has 20 members - but 16 of those are nominated by the EFC. The EFC would say that has 10 members from outside the “substantial five leagues” and that it is the Uefa Executive Committee - with representation from all major stakeholders - that actually ratifies changes.

The UEC argues this breaches Articles 101 and 102 of the EU Treaty on anti-competitive agreements and abuse of dominance, while also going against the Super League judgment.

Sources indicate that in its 2023 Super League judgment, the EU Court of Justice found that Uefa holds a dominant position, "or even a monopoly", over international club competitions. It also held that Uefa’s control over access must follow transparent, objective and non-discriminatory rules.

The UEC argues that giving one association exclusive representation of clubs fails that test.

Uefa themselves signed a co-operation agreement with the European Commission on 9 November 2026 committed to protecting “inclusive stakeholder governance” within the European sports model.

Amid criticism that 736 of the EFC’s members are seen as having “neither statutory voting rights nor representative voting Board members”, the body would contend it has in the last two months changed its statutes so that all 920 members vote to elect board members and the board is reconstituted with guaranteed positions for more smaller / medium-sized clubs.

The complaint further asks for the EFC to publish the memorandum, order restrictive clauses removed and require Uefa to change such rules.

Sources indicate that it comes out of long frustration from the UEC and their club base, who feel they have no voice in the system, let alone the capacity to rise on merit. One common view has been that there are too many glass ceilings - but also obstruction to even joining a group like theirs.

Four weeks before the UEC was debuted in March 2023, the then ECA asked they not launch and instead work within their body. This was declined.

Uefa subsequently told the UEC that they would have to bring 100 clubs together to have sufficient scale for recognition. This has not happened either despite growing to 138 clubs across 25 national associations, amid constant questions – including in The Independent – about a lockstep relationship between Uefa president Aleksander Ceferin and EFC chair Nasser Al Khelaifi.

Uefa have not even indicated what criteria for recognition would be, despite formal requests from the UEC.

This has put a series of clubs off membership, since they would be more comfortable with formal acknowledgement. As many as 59 clubs have asked that their existing membership not be made public, because of political pressure.

One account has it that the Baltic countries were making progress in the establishment of a cross-border league that would bring the revenue to better compete at European level, only to be told that the EFC would help them with media rights and Uefa approval - but only if UEC member clubs left that organisation and joined the EFC.

The legal complaint was written by Prof Miguel Poiares Maduro, former Advocate General at the Court of Justice of the European Union.

According to recent reports, “The Court of Justice was clear in the Super League and other sports cases,” said Prof Maduro in a press release. “Whoever controls access to European football must do so on transparent, objective and non-discriminatory terms.

According to recent reports, “No association can be given the exclusive right to represent all clubs while giving most of them no real voice. Such an arrangement simply hands a select group of clubs control over the interests of all clubs, and, in our assessment, that control has helped to consolidate their dominance of European and national football competitions.”.

“A system where a select group of elite clubs is given the power to decide who gets to compete in European competitions, and how the revenues are distributed, does not only harm competition in European and national football. It gradually erodes the model of open and balanced competition that Uefa itself claims to protect. If Uefa is truly committed to that model, it should welcome this complaint as a way to challenge the growing power of the elite clubs and better protect all football stakeholders.”.
