Oven Fires Surge This Time of Year—Here Is How Homeowners Can Protect Their Kitchen Over the Holidays

Let’s face it: The holiday season is when our ovens are working overtime.

Unfortunately, it’s also the prime time for an oven fire.

In a recent development, moneyGeek recently analyzed seven years of fire department reports to the National Fire Incident Reporting System and compared each state's Thanksgiving Day cooking fires with its own average November day, from 2019 to 2025. Nationally, departments reported 3.72 times as many residential cooking fires on Thanksgiving.

And it's not just the turkey that could get ruined.

While a professional kitchen remodel averages $26,994, depending on the scope of the project and the location, according to Angi.com, fire damage might amp up that pricetag depending on whether cabinets, drywall, appliances, or partial finishes need to be removed and replaced.

Major structural damage can reach $100,000 if flames or water damaged wiring and plumbing or if the fire spread to adjacent rooms.

This is why experts recommend tried and true precautions homeowners can take to keep their home safe.

In the days leading up to the holidays, be sure to inspect the inside of your oven.

“Look specifically for older grease buildups,” notes Steve Lockwood, owner of Mountain State Fire Protection, a fire safety equipment inspector and installer in Mesa, AZ.

“Over time, those can build up, become extremely hot, and they could ignite pretty easily.”.

If you’re someone who routinely stores items in your oven, be sure to empty the space entirely—before you preheat it.

“Often people don’t realize they have plastic cutting boards and casserole dishes with plastic lids stored in their oven,” Sam Taylor, disaster public affairs program manager with the American Red Cross, tells Realtor.com.

“You don’t want to run the risk of melting those items in the oven.”.

During the entire time you’re cooking a dish on your burners, stow all combustible materials away.

“Pay special attention to such items as plastic spatulas and utensils that can melt and catch fire, as well as wooden spoons, oven mitts, and paper products,” Taylor says.

Always make sure you’re not cooking foods on too-high temperatures, which can result in a fire; avoid dangerous bump-ins; and make sure pan handles don’t hang over the edge of the burners.

Industry observers note that “This can cause the contents to spill and could cause burns or fire,” Lockwood says. And never wear a shirt with long, loose sleeves while you’re cooking.

Sources indicate that “If you’re working over an open flame, that sleeve can catch fire,” states Taylor. “I always recommend wearing a different top that doesn’t have any fabric hanging near a flame.”.

Whatever you do, never leave the house when your oven is on, Lockwood says.

“Where most people go wrong is walking away from the kitchen,” he says. Instead, keep an eye on your oven at all times. Tip: If you have a newer digital stove, pay special attention.

Industry observers note that “A lot of these burners can be accidentally turned on just by leaning over them,” Lockwood notes. “That’s why it’s a good practice to put the stove into a lockout mode to avoid an accidental turn on.”.

Should a fire happen, keep the oven door closed, turn off the heat, reach for your kitchen extinguisher, and alert the authorities.

“Opening the oven door feeds the fire with oxygen,” Lockwood says. “In addition, while baking soda will work in a pinch, never use water on a grease fire.”.

And, if a pot on your burner catches fire, turn off the heat and put a lid or baking sheet on it immediately to smother the fire, he adds.
