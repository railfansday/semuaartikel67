How to plan your cash flow when clients pay late

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that A late payment stops being a surprise after the first one. If a client has paid you two weeks after the due date for three invoices in a row, that is not bad luck. That is the schedule. Planning cash flow around late payers is mostly the work of writing that schedule down and building the rest of your month around it.

Recent findings suggest that your invoice says 30 days. Your client's finance team pays on the last Friday of the month, after an approval nobody told you about. Those are two different dates, and only one of them belongs in a forecast.

Use the date the money actually reached your account, not the date you asked for it. After six months with a client you have enough history to know the difference without guessing.

Three columns next to every outstanding invoice: the due date, the date you expect to be paid, and what actually happened last time.

With those three lines you can see which invoices will fund this month and which will not.

Recent findings suggest that monthly totals hide the problem. Two invoices due in the same week, both paid late, leave a hole in the middle of the month even when the month ends fine.

A four-week view, with every expected payment sitting in the week you actually expect it, indicates you the hole while you can still do something about it.

Recent findings suggest that not a savings goal, a working rule. If your biggest outstanding invoice is 2,000 and that client always pays three weeks late, then 2,000 is the amount you need available to cover the gap, or the amount you need to avoid spending in advance.

Recent findings suggest that the buffer is not there to feel comfortable. It is there so a late payment does not turn into a decision about which bill to delay.

If two projects both bill their final invoice in the same week, and both clients pay late, you get one bad month and no lever to pull.

Sources indicate that move one final invoice a week earlier when you agree the schedule, or split the last stage into two smaller invoices. Clients rarely object to a billing schedule that was set before the work started.

Industry observers note that A deposit is the cheapest cash flow tool there is. It moves money to the front of a project, while you are doing the work, instead of the end, while you are waiting.

Write the rule before the month where it matters: if an invoice is two weeks late and above a set amount, work on that client's other projects pauses until it clears.

Recent findings suggest that A rule you decided in advance is a policy. The same decision made in the moment, with the client waiting on work, is an argument.

Recent findings suggest that put it in the quote when you agree the terms, not when an invoice is late. One sentence does the work: work pauses on any project where an invoice is more than 14 days past due, and resumes when it is paid.

When you invoke it later you are not introducing a new condition. You are applying the one they already agreed to.

The moment to look at the next four weeks is when you send a latest invoice, because that is when you know what is coming and you can still move a milestone or ask for a deposit.

Reviewing it when a payment is already late is a post-mortem, not a strategy.

Industry observers note that none of this works from memory. You need the list: who owes what, when it was due, when it actually arrived last time, and which invoices land in the next four weeks.
