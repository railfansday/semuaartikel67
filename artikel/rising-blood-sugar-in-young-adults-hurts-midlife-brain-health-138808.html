Source: American Heart Association.

While Type 2 diabetes has long been recognized as a risk factor for late-life dementia, cognitive impairment linked to blood sugar was historically thought to emerge primarily after age 65.

Now, a three-decade investigation published today in the Journal of the American Heart Association demonstrates that creeping elevations in fasting blood sugar starting in young adulthood can measurably undermine memory, processing speed, and executive function by middle age, even among individuals who never cross the diagnostic threshold into full-blown diabetes.

Led by first author Dr. Regina Paradela (postdoctoral researcher at the University of São Paulo Medical School and neuroepidemiologist at the Global Brain Health Institute at UC San Francisco) and senior author Dr. Kristine Yaffe (director of the Center for Population Brain Health at UC San Francisco), the study tracked metabolic and cognitive trajectories across 2,808 participants in the landmark Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults (CARDIA) cohort.

Industry observers note that enrolled between 1985 and 1986 at ages 18 to 30 across four U.S. cities, participants underwent repeated fasting blood glucose measurements over 30 years until reaching an average age of 55. At the 30-year mark, all participants completed comprehensive neuropsychological testing, and a subset of 605 participants underwent brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

An additional 345 participants developed Type 2 diabetes (fasting glucose ≥126 mg/dL) during the 30-year follow-up.

Even after adjusting for demographic and lifestyle factors, both the increasing-glucose group and the Type 2 diabetes group performed significantly worse on tests of memory, processing speed, executive function, and global cognition compared with the lower-stable glucose group.

“The fact that increasing glucose levels were associated with poorer cognitive performance, even among people who had not developed Type 2 diabetes during the 30-year study, is very important,” explained Paradela. “This suggests that changes in glucose metabolism may be relevant to brain health earlier in adulthood than we typically think about.”.

Furthermore, MRI scans revealed tangible anatomical footprints among participants who developed Type 2 diabetes, who exhibited less total gray matter and more abnormal white matter tissue, markers closely tied to accelerated brain aging and cognitive decline.

“Future research should evaluate whether monitoring and managing increasing glucose levels earlier in adulthood can prevent or delay cognitive decline and structural brain changes,” noted senior author Yaffe.

Commenting on the study, Dr. Elisabeth Marsh, professor of neurology at The Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine and chair of the writing group for the American Heart Association’s 2026 scientific statement Brain Health Across the Life Span, emphasized the value of early vascular and metabolic care: “The American Heart Association’s Life’s Essential 8 offers steps to improve heart and brain health and support cognitive well-being at every stage of life.”.

Can rising blood sugar harm brain health even if you don’t have diabetes?

Yes. Over a 30-year study, young adults whose fasting blood sugar gradually increased into the prediabetic range (110–115 mg/dL) scored significantly worse on midlife tests of memory, processing speed, and executive function than peers whose blood sugar remained low and stable, even though they never developed Type 2 diabetes.

What structural brain changes were seen in participants who developed Type 2 diabetes?

MRI brain scans revealed that adults who developed Type 2 diabetes by their mid-50s had less total gray matter volume and more abnormal white matter tissue compared to those who maintained lower-stable blood sugar levels from young adulthood.

When should adults start paying attention to blood sugar for brain protection?

Industry observers note that researchers note that changes in glucose metabolism begin affecting cognitive performance much earlier than age 65. Monitoring and managing blood sugar trajectories starting in young adulthood, using healthy nutrition, physical activity, and cardiovascular risk control, may help protect midlife and late-life brain health.

Trajectories of Fasting Blood Glucose and Brain Health in Middle Age.

Recent findings suggest that research on associations between long‐term fasting blood glucose (FBG) trajectories and brain health in early adulthood is limited. We investigated associations of FBG trajectories with cognition and brain structure.

In a recent development, participants were from the CARDIA (Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults) study, followed for 30 years, who completed ≥1 cognitive test at year 30. Among participants without diabetes with ≥3 FBG measurements from baseline to year 30, we identified FBG trajectories using latent growth mixture models. Participants who developed diabetes after baseline were included for comparison (n=345). Cognition was assessed using tests of processing speed, executive function, memory, fluency, and global cognition. Structural magnetic resonance imaging data from 605 participants included total gray matter volume and white matter lesions. Associations were examined using multivariable linear regression adjusted for demographics and health behaviors.

We included 2808 participants (mean age, 55.2±3.6 years; 56% women; 44% Black individuals). Three FBG trajectories were identified: lower‐stable (n=1387), moderate‐stable (n=938), and increasing (n=138). Compared with the lower‐stable group, increasing FBG and incident diabetes were associated with worse performance in memory, processing speed, executive function, and composite cognition; increasing FBG was also associated with worse global cognition. Incident diabetes was associated with lower total and parietal gray matter volume and greater parietal white matter abnormalities. No consistent sex or race interactions were observed.

Persistently elevated glucose below the diabetes threshold was associated with worse cognitive performance in midlife, while incident diabetes was associated with structural brain changes.
