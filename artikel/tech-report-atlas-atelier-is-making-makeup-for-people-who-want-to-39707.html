In a recent development, you know how everyone is debating whether smoking is back? Atlas Atelier is like the subject of the makeup version of that conversation, in both its long, skinny lipsticks that evoke a cheeky cigarette and its past-bedtime, maybe something a little naughty is happening vibe. The Paris-based brand makes just lipstick, blush, and a black liquid eyeliner, all engineered to be best shown off at night, maybe under the light of candles, or as you sip a cocktail or wear a jacket with strong shoulders. It’s makeup that is supposed to look like makeup. Severe is not too strong a word to use.

Industry observers note that the brand’s late-30s founder, Michelle Duncan, doesn’t smoke herself and doesn’t do barely there makeup along the lines of Rhode, Rare Beauty, or Glossier. “I am a Latest Yorker, and I’ve been living in Paris primarily for the last four or five years. When I arrived in Paris, everyone was so well-heeled that you would never see somebody wearing a gym look to go to dinner. This resonated with me about this idea of dressing up to go out no matter what you were doing, even if it’s going to a café. That was a huge inspiration for me,” she notes.

Sources indicate that duncan knew the industry she was getting into. She’s a veteran of brands owned by Estée Lauder, including both MAC and Tom Ford Beauty, and she also worked for Cartier. Atlas Atelier—the “atlas” is in homage to Ayn Rand’s Atlas Shrugged—just came out this fall in the US (now available at Violet Grey and Bergdorf Goodman). Vanity Fair spoke to Duncan about Paris expat life, rejecting celebrity brands, and what happens to makeup in unstable times.

The interview that follows has been condensed for length and clarity.

Industry observers note that vanity Fair: You know the challenges of the beauty industry. You’re not an influencer; you didn’t pick a celebrity to be the face of the brand or to partner with. Why not make it easier on yourself?

Recent findings suggest that michelle Duncan: Friends will ask, “Didn’t you want to do this with a celeb?” I wouldn’t want to. Culture and society is so focused on a quick buck right this second, which I’m totally into. I love making money very fast. This is so much about the world of Atlas, with the packaging and with the storytelling. The brand ethos of Atlas is about the night, about this seductive environment that, to me, will actually cut through the clutter in a long-term way. When a high-profile person rolls out a brand, the bet that they’re taking is on who they are as a person. For me, the bet is, do you buy into Atlas’s story?

Makeup has gotten very massive and plastic and in some ways looks like toys. That was sort of my first remit on it. The whole brief was: We want to create something that looks like an object that you want to be able to keep. My inspirations really are the 1930s, the Art Deco, Paris moment, and how do we make it leap into the ’80s in some ways because that’s obviously also just a second extension of the ’30s?

The 1980s version of Art Deco is so good. All that lacquer and glass blocks and chrome lamps.

Industry observers note that it felt like something that was radically different from what’s going on in the world right now, at least in beauty land. We’re exiting this no-makeup era, or hopefully exiting this quiet-luxury era. Those things were very pared back. Maybe focusing more internally about wellness and things like that. I understood that this was missing and that it was going to come back.

Recent findings suggest that the idea of self-expression in a sort of glamorous way. In the 1930s, it was not a great time in the world. And if we remember, the 1980s in Newly introduced York City was decadent and over the top and seemed very fun, but as a reaction to what was going on. I would argue that we are kind of in that place right now. We don’t really need to go into politics, but it is chaos right now. And so the answer to that is always this other form of self-expression.

According to recent reports, the name of the brand is a reference to Atlas Shrugged. Are you a significant fan of Ayn Rand?

In a recent development, I’ve always admired and been inspired by Dagny Taggart, one of the most iconic characters in 20th-century literature. She’s written as a fiercely independent entrepreneur in a male-dominated world, who is in control of her own life and embracing its complexity. When creating my business, I was trying to channel that vision of what a woman can be: intellectually formidable, professionally ambitious, and comfortable with her sensuality. I was especially struck by one moment in the novel, when Dagny draws on a statue using her lipstick, reminding us that makeup isn’t just to change our appearance—it can be a tool for women to own their own power. I wanted to create a makeup line that gave women that same feeling of confidence, ambition, and sensuality through tools like lipstick, blush, and eyeliner.

Were you a makeup lover growing up?

It has been revealed that yes. I have been wearing this winged-eyeliner situation for a very long time. Actually, since I was a teenager. If I don’t have my eyeliner on, I feel naked and I don’t feel like I have everything with me.

So you’re wearing eyeliner at home. What about the people who are reading this who may be thinking, I never go out, or I have kids, or I’m tired, or I work a lot, or all of those things? Do you want people to go out a lot? Do you go out a lot? Is it more just the mindset of going out at nighttime?

Recent findings suggest that the idea of my brand is it should be a pleasure to put on makeup, and I want people to experience pleasure again and just forget about all of the things that are happening, like picking up the kids, or going to the grocery store, or getting the dry cleaning, or whatever it is. Just remembering what it means to have a nice time and be inspired by the night. I, on purpose and by design, did not launch foundations or these technical components of makeup, because that’s just not really what it’s about.

In a recent development, how is expat life? I know so many people who have moved to France, or they would love to move to France. What do you think that’s about?

Sources indicate that in my social circle, it tends to consist of either people somehow in the fashion industry, the music industry, or the arts communities. A lot of those industries have just become quite commercial in America, as an example. And I haven’t lived in a lot of other countries, so I don’t really know expat life so well. But the amount of Italians that are here, the Spanish, the Germans, the Americans, and everyone just has this chaotic, frenetic energy of being around each other and doing things together. It’s pretty remarkable.

I know you’re not doing celebrity spokespeople, but who would you want if you didn’t have to think about money or access or if they already had a beauty contract?

Industry observers note that somebody like [the artist] Nadia Lee Cohen. She’s super glamorous. She’s quite creative. She’s a genius in a lot of ways, and she actually uses makeup as art. And then somebody like Dakota Johnson. I think she’s so chic now. Dua Lipa. I love her. She’s got the perfect energy for this. She’s there, she’s having a good time, and she’s powerful.

In a recent development, do you want to expand into other products ? It feels like you could do a fragrance. Or maybe a very chic makeup remover?

It has been revealed that I can’t wait for the day that we are able to launch a fragrance because that is one of those things you remember, along with lip color. You remember what they smell like, and when they leave the room, and so that’s the impression that Atlas is supposed to be giving—you’re remembered when you left the room.
