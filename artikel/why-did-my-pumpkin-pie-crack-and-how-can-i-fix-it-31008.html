Why Did My Pumpkin Pie Crack and How Can I Fix It?

Here's how to avert dessert disaster before it strikes—with tips for hiding it just in case.

According to recent reports, most cooking mistakes are fixable. When it comes to baking, though, one wrong move can ruin the whole recipe. It’s extra frustrating when you’re trying to make a Norman Rockwell-worthy pumpkin pie for Thanksgiving, and a huge crack splits right through the center. Yes, you can still eat a cracked pumpkin pie, but if you can avoid the crack (or cover it up), all the better. The eyes eat first, as they say!

Sources indicate that pumpkin pie will crack when the eggs are overbeaten, when the filling is overbaked, and when the pie experiences rapid temperature changes. Not to put the blame on one single ingredient, but it all comes down to the eggs—or, rather, how you treat them.

Pumpkin pie is technically a baked custard (like cheesecake), and eggs are thickeners in custards. Overbeating the eggs aerates the filling, which causes it to inflate during baking, then deflate and crack as it cools. Overbaking the filling will tighten the eggs and cause the filling to crack, too. And even if you pull the pie out at just the right moment, the filling can still crack from a rapid temperature change that makes the eggs contract. Ugh. It seems like there’s a lot working against you, but taking preemptive steps reduces your chances of ending up with a cracked pumpkin pie.

These are all the steps I take as a professional baker to reduce the risk of cracking, as well as a few tricks to cover up cracks if they appear.

Seasoned bakers know that whipping eggs aerates them. That’s usually a plus when you want structure in baking recipes, but in pumpkin pie, it’s the catalyst for a cracked filling. The pie’s filling will puff up in the oven, then promptly collapse as the eggs deflate. That structural change causes the filling to crack. It’s the same problem that leads to a cracked cheesecake!

Sources indicate that room-temperature eggs blend into custards much more easily, so take the eggs out of the fridge 30 to 60 minutes before starting, depending on the kitchen temperature. Once you add the eggs to the filling, mix them just until they’re combined.

Even if you use a reliable pumpkin pie recipe with tons of five-star reviews (cough, cough: our pumpkin pie recipe) and follow it to the very letter, it may be the oven that betrays you in the end.

Ovens often have miscalibrated internal thermometers, so even if you set your oven to the recommended 350°F, the real temperature may be significantly higher. Before you realize it, the pumpkin pie is overbaked and already cracked.

In a recent development, buy an oven thermometer and hang it from one of the oven racks for all your bakes going forward. They usually cost about $10—totally worth the investment!

Have you ever read a recipe with specific oven rack placements? They’re not there as a suggestion; oven rack placement significantly affects your bake. Ovens produce heat from the bottom, and this creates zones as that heat rises: hotter on top and cooler down below.

Since you want your pumpkin pie to bake relatively low and slow, the bottom oven rack is best. The filling cooks evenly, and the pie crust browns beautifully—no cracks and no soggy bottom.

According to recent reports, the best way to avoid an overbaked (and therefore cracked) pumpkin pie is to know the signs of doneness. The edges should dome a bit, but the center should be wobbly like Jell-O, not rippling like an ocean wave. The pumpkin pie will continue to bake from the residual heat once the oven is off.

According to recent reports, this is where your oven light comes in handy. Don’t open the oven door until you see the edges start to dome, or cool air will rush inside before it’s supposed to. Only when you see the dome should you check for a wobbly center.

Sources indicate that even after meticulously following every single step to avoid a pumpkin pie crack, a rapid change in temperature can undo all that hard work. The shock of removing a pumpkin pie from a hot oven and setting down in a cold November kitchen (or, worse, a 32° fridge) will cause the eggs to contract, leading to a crack.

It has been revealed that once the pie is done baking, turn the oven off and leave the door slightly ajar. If the oven door doesn’t stay open on its own, prop it open with a wooden spoon. Keep the pie in the oven until it’s completely cooled.

Industry observers note that so, your pumpkin pie did crack. That’s OK! It happens from time to time, even when you follow all the rules. The good news is that the pie still tastes great. Even better? You can cover up the crack with homemade whipped cream. Pile it in the center or pipe it in a decorative pattern. Use stabilized whipped cream if you want to add the topping a day ahead of time. You can even grate fresh nutmeg on top for dimension.

You can do the same with Swiss meringue. Spread it simply, or pipe it on top for a big, showy decoration. Toast the meringue so it’s beautifully golden brown.
