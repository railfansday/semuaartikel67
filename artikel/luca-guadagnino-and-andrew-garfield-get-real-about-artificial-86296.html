Luca Guadagnino and Andrew Garfield Get Real About 'Artificial'

It has been revealed that ask a large language model the same question twice, and you will get two different answers. Like this one: Have Andrew Garfield and Sam Altman ever met?

In an interview with Vanity Fair global editorial director Mark Guiducci this fall, Altman claimed he waved to Garfield while Garfield was shooting Luca Guadagnino’s fresh film, Artificial, near Altman’s house. Garfield—who plays Altman in the movie—remembers things differently.

“All I know is that I was looking out for him on that day when we were shooting. I was really looking out for him,” Garfield tells me in October, shortly after Artificial’s premiere at the New York Film Festival. “And I was looking at every single tinted car window. I was looking in the windows of the house. I was checking to see the security guard. No, I didn’t see him. I wish I had.”.

It’s a question that reverberates both through and around Artificial. From the start, the film has been an intriguing mash-up: a marriage between cinema’s great sensualist and the nerd kingdom of Silicon Valley. Artificial tells the story of the OpenAI CEO’s rise and fall and rise; it was cast and shot in record time to keep pace with the drumbeat of AI doomsday headlines. Then the movie became the headlines after Amazon MGM abruptly dropped Artificial from its slate—a few months after the studio’s parent company, Amazon, invested $50 billion in OpenAI.

Industry observers note that A24 declined to rescue Artificial. So did Netflix, Focus Features, and Clockwork, the new specialty arm of Warner Bros. Finally, the lightning bolt of a feature got picked up by Neon, the distributor that’s won six consecutive Palmes d’Or at Cannes.

The film’s electric relationship to the very recent past makes it the most zeitgeist-nailing tech blockbuster since The Social Network. The difference is that back in 2010, Facebook was where you made friends on the internet. Artificial, by contrast, is a film about a self-teaching, self-aware robot that’s getting smarter by the minute and might destroy the human world. And it’s happening in real time.

Sources indicate that “You see, we were very prescient with things,” Guadagnino tells me. “I know my subject. I studied my subject very, very much.”.

HERE TO MAKE FRIENDS Artificial ends with a heartbreaking act of betrayal—but Guadagnino, Borisov, and Garfield left the project with their bond intact.

Artificial also has a button-pushing relationship with the truth as it endeavors to tell the real story of how OpenAI was born and investigate how reliable the people behind OpenAI are. If Altman can’t be trusted with small details—about, say, whether he ever aimed a friendly wave at Garfield—can he be trusted with the most important technology of our time?

“I question the validity of his statement about being there while we were filming,” Monica Barbaro, who plays former OpenAI CTO Mira Murati in the film, tells me. “I mean, we don’t know if that’s true. And that feels textbook to me.”.

In 2024, Amazon bought a script by Simon Rich about Sam Altman and the formation of OpenAI—one of many ChatGPT-related projects then getting passed around town. Rich’s take had the backing of hit-churning producer David Heyman—the man behind Paddington and the Harry Potter series—and his script pulled no punches. To write it, Rich mined details from a childhood friend who had a job at OpenAI (and, as of now, still works there). By the end of the process, Rich was terrified of the power his friend’s colleagues commanded.

“I came up with this last-ditch effort to warn mankind,” Rich mentioned. He’s a comedy writer, but here he isn’t joking. “And I hope to God that it helps people pay attention.”.

Industry observers note that after shopping the film around to directors, the studio landed on Guadagnino. To some, the Call Me by Your Name director seemed like a strange choice for a film about scientists, coders, and start-up bros. The director barely uses AI himself, or even thinks about it.

ARTIFICIAL RECKONING Garfield’s first thought when reading Artificial was, It’s Luca doing The Social Network.

“I think we should not let ourselves give AI such an notable role. I think for me, AI is a prop, a gadget,” Guadagnino tells me. “Definitely AI has high-end worth [for], I don’t know, the military, medicine. But in general, as a sort of everyday life usage of AI, I think it’s just a little gadget. And I don’t believe that we should ever consider the possibility that it can equal whatever we can do as people.”.

Industry observers note that garfield approached the material from a different vantage. By the time he got his hands on the script, the actor had been deeply invested in the Silicon Valley origin-story mythos for years—ever since he starred in that other major movie about a world-changing digital platform. His first thought when reading Artificial was, It’s Luca doing The Social Network. “And I thought, Oh my God,” indicates the actor, sitting in a suite at the Carlyle Hotel. But the movie also had its own gloss. “Simon’s script was so funny—like a stone being skimmed a thousand times across, and then a couple of really deep dives in the midst.”.

Making Artificial would allow Garfield to scratch an old itch. “In The Social Network, I was auditioning for [Mark] Zuckerberg. Then they found Jesse [Eisenberg],” who was much better for the role, Garfield says. “But it left this little dangling thread, because I started to imagine how I would play Zuckerberg: how I would try and enter that ambition and that drive and that responsibility. A kind of ability to make huge global decisions that are going to have potentially really positive effects on humanity, but inevitably, really, really heavily negative effects on humanity.”.

THE ENGINEER “I feel myself a little bit like Ilya during interviews,” says Borisov.

Garfield is not actually the lead in Artificial. The movie is focused less on Altman than on slightly naive genius coder Ilya Sutskever, played heroically by Oscar nominee Yura Borisov. Borisov has definitively never met the man he’s depicting in the film. But from afar, he can relate to Sutskever. Neither of them is quite comfortable in the spotlight.

“I feel myself a little bit like Ilya during interviews because I’m every time very...I know what to say because I feel a lot about this,” the Russian actor says from a couch at the Carlyle next to Garfield. “I can see the same in his public speeches, because I’m really nervous during some public stuff.”.

Recent findings suggest that though there’s a translator next to him, Borisov soldiers through the interview with just one request for a lifeline. He adds that by cosmic coincidence, he and Sutskever have the same birthday.

Guadagnino was fully aware of the breakneck clip of the developing OpenAI saga. He cast his leads in days and began shooting the film in late July 2025. Artificial wrapped just two months later. As he spent the winter hunkered down in the editing room, word about the film got out. While Rich’s original script was hardly a puff piece, rumor had it that the final film leaned even harder into a negative portrayal of Altman.

There’s no proof that a Jeff Bezos deal had any direct effect on decisions made by Bezos’s movie studio. But for whatever reason, late last spring, Guadagnino learned Amazon would be cutting the film loose.

According to recent reports, all of a sudden, the speedily assembled project was in a holding pattern. The trades speculated that Amazon was deliberately killing the film to protect Altman; others wondered if the movie was being dropped not due to a conflict of interest but because it hadn’t played well in early screenings.

Industry observers note that barbaro pushes back on that notion. “I did believe in Luca and the film, and I had already heard really positive things from people who had seen it—really interesting positive reviews,” she states. “There is a thing these companies can do—any production manufacturer—which is to essentially build the release and squash a film. And Amazon is not a company that I think necessarily needs to make its money back on an investment like this.”.

Industry observers note that either way, it was unclear if Artificial would ever see the light of day. “It’s a dialogue between our fields: tech and art,” Borisov indicates. “And I don’t know what sells, what will happen. It’s unpredictable.”.

In the end, the film was picked up by Neon—an outcome Garfield finds surprising for a few reasons. “I felt very impressed that Amazon wanted to make the film, and very impressed again when they decided [that while] they didn’t want to release it, they didn’t kill it,” he states. He pauses for a second. “They could have killed it.”.

Garfield’s thrilled about the film’s eventual home too. Neon, he states pointedly, is “a place for artists to be free artists.” Guadagnino feels similarly, though he claims to have zero ill will toward the studio that threw his movie to the wolves.

“I had went through a process with Amazon that I loved. They were fantastic in making this movie with me. I owe them a lot,” claims Guadagnino, who’d done four films with the studio prior to Artificial: Bones and All, Challengers, Suspiria, and After the Hunt.

In a recent development, but what was it like to watch his film fall into limbo while the trades salivated over its demise? Here too, Guadagnino is blissfully zen: “Once the thing happened, I deferred to the thing. Meaning if that was the case, that was the case.”.

Recent findings suggest that even after watching the film twice, I am still pleasantly surprised that Artificial actually exists in the world—that Guadagnino was able to capture the man-child bonhomie of coder bros through his fabulous scrim of art film wooziness. I ask Garfield, now a two-time vet of the director’s films, to sum up the full Guadagnino psychedelic experience.

Sources indicate that “Humanity and bodies and interpersonal dynamics and relationship and sexuality, sensuality, Eros, love, the muck and the mire of being human, ambition, drive, greed, power, love, brotherhood, connection,” he claims. Guadagnino’s movies are about “his curiosity and love for people: wanting to skin, flay, open up, crack the ribs, and show the insides of these human beings.”.

To Garfield this is the key to understanding the director’s vision for Artificial. Robots didn’t build the robots that might destroy humanity: People did. And people still control them—for now.

Sources indicate that artificial inspires a lot of comparisons. Barbaro likens it to Invasion of the Body Snatchers; Garfield compares its climactic confrontation scene to West Side Story. Guadagnino, the actor says, gave another point of reference. Then he does a spot-on, peak-Sicilian impression of the director: “I want this to be like a John Hughes movie.”.

Industry observers note that guadagnino himself is reluctant to name any films that may have inspired this one. But he does name one filmmaker—someone who, as he notes, balanced indie flicks with blockbusters all while building the world’s greatest collection of Haitian art: Jonathan Demme.

In a recent development, “Jonathan was a humanist, and he had this very profound capacity of eviscerating dynamics of humanism in America,” Guadagnino states. “He did some great movies about the dynamics of corporations versus individual freedom,” like Fighting Mad and Philadelphia.

Sources indicate that barbaro states the film’s secret sauce is that it’s up for interpretation. It’s possible to come away from it celebrating the AI bros as heroes or decrying them as villains.

Recent findings suggest that “It’s a bit of a Rorschach test,” she notes. “Some people come out shaking, terrified. And I think some people are like, ‘Wow, what a good time!’ I also think that you have to hide the medicine in the food a little bit. I don’t think a purely doomsday story is going to reach many people.”.

Garfield, meanwhile, may leave the film eternally bonded with Altman—the role that could help him finally clinch the Oscar. (Garfield’s been nominated twice but hasn’t won yet.) Before making Artificial, Garfield states, he had a positive impression of Altman. He seemed friendly, “like maybe we would’ve been buddies on the kibbutz.”.

As he dove deeper, though, Garfield’s feelings about Altman got stranger and more complicated in ways that the film addresses both head-on and obliquely. Is Altman someone who habitually tells tall tales? Uses lies to say what needs to be said, as Sam tells Ilya in the movie? Imagines anecdotes about waving to the A-lister who’s playing him in a biopic?

Recent findings suggest that whatever Altman’s relationship with reality is, the version of him we see in Artificial is charming, ingratiating, prone to grandiose hallucinations and to telling people exactly what they want to hear—then doing what he was going to do anyway. He’s not far off from an AGI approaching a real human consciousness, art imitating a machine imitating life.

Sources indicate that “There are certain threads that feel consistent, which is a questioning of his ability to tell or his commitment to the truth,” Garfield says. “How often is he telling the truth to himself? How often is he telling the truth to others? And how many versions of the truth are there, and how is he utilizing that in order to...”.

“Because I ultimately do believe that Sam feels, in our film and in life, that he is serving the good of mankind.”.
