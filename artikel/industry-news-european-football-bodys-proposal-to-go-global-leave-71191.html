Industry News: European football body’s proposal to go global leaves Fifa on tenterhooks

Findings of ‘feasibility study’ into quest for world domination loom with Europe’s wealthiest certain to benefit whatever the outcome.

It will be one of the most eagerly devoured documents in football circles, assuming its contents are ever fully revealed. In September, the increasingly powerful European Football Clubs body unveiled a “feasibility study” into how it can become established on a more global scale, morphing into an organisation that represents clubs all over the world.

It has been revealed that the study’s findings could, some leading figures in the sport’s governance believe, define football’s long-term future. It has the potential to transform a landscape destabilised by the scandals surrounding Fifa and Gianni Infantino.

Once considered a lobby group representing clubs’ interests in the face of domineering governing bodies, EFC has mushroomed into a behemoth with almost 1,000 members that has the potential to supplant those bodies.

It has been revealed that “Now is the time to create World Football Clubs,” the EFC chair, Nasser Al-Khelaïfi, told delegates at its general assembly in Copenhagen seven weeks ago. The desire to expand exists in plain sight and is perfectly natural, some with a close interest in the process point out, given players and domestic leagues already have their own global representative bodies.

However, others warn of “a takeover by stealth” and fear it is merely a fig leaf for the tightening of the top European clubs’ influence over the game’s shape.

According to recent reports, sources familiar with EFC’s intentions say it is examining how to enter the international football market as a competition organiser, floating the idea of organising more effective tournaments in different formats. That is the role of Fifa or continental organisations such as Uefa and Concacaf but, should leading clubs find a way to forge events of their own, they could reap financial and commercial benefits far beyond those on offer through the confederations.

The prospect has some of the established powers on high alert. “You’re starting to see one of the major unspoken faultlines in football open up,” one source notes of EFC’s relationship with Uefa, which has become structurally closer in recent years, but is still conducted with an air of high mutual caution. “Friends close, enemies closer” is one characterisation of that relationship.

This particularly appears the case now that, in the wreckage of Infantino’s doomed Fifa Forward Enterprise proposal, all parties find themselves jostling for leverage. Beyond a dramatic increase in funding for the member associations, many senior figures in football have remarked that the biggest long-term consequence of FFE’s failure will be to increase the power of the biggest European clubs.

While the former development is already in motion, with Fifa announcing last month that it will hand over an additional $2.7bn (£2.05bn) from its reserves over the next four years, increasing their total investment to $6.35bn, the latter will not be far behind.

That is because EFC’s study is not alone in harbouring huge implications for football’s shape. As revealed in September, a joint venture between EFC and Fifa to run the commercial side of global club competitions like the Club World Cup has been in the works for some time, with the fallout from FFE emerging as the catalyst required to push it through.

Multiple sources say the joint venture, similar to the agreement under which Uefa and EFC have operated European club competitions since 2008 that was formalised last year with the creation of commercial entity UC3, is ready to proceed pending approval from Infantino.

One source with knowledge of the discussions claims Infantino had been holding up the deal until the FFE scandal broke in July, since when negotiations have accelerated. An announcement could be made at next month’s Fifa council meeting in Doha, although waiting until after March’s presidential election at the global governing body seems more likely.

The joint venture’s contents have caused particular concern within Uefa, whose hierarchy are believed to have made clear their wish that a deal between EFC and Fifa is not rushed through.

While Uefa have continued to attack Infantino forcibly and are on the verge of backing a candidate to stand against him in next year’s election, EFC has been largely silent since its chief executive, Charlie Marshall, sent an explosive letter to Fifa’s secretary general, Mattias Grafström, on 31 July.

EFC strongly condemned FFE, with the Guardian revealing the body threatened to boycott future Club World Cups (CWC) and withdraw its endorsement of the international match calendar. The sell-off plan was formally dropped the following day.

One particularly incendiary section read: “Unless a fully operational Fifa-EFC CWC joint venture, with exclusive authority over, among other things, all commercial and operational matters, is established well ahead of the women’s 2028 Fifa CWC rights cycle, EFC will not support – and its member clubs will not participate in – any future edition, including the women’s and men’s 2028 and 2029 Fifa CWC.”.

Al-Khelaïfi, who is also the president of Paris Saint-Germain, did not directly address the concern during his speech in Copenhagen two months later and there are suggestions a mutually beneficial solution has been reached. The crisis may have landed EFC the terms they wanted.

Sources indicate that “It’s not a matter of whether Gianni wins [the election], but what his mandate is, and who is behind it,” a senior source in European football said. “A large part of the answer is EFC. EFC is going to take over Fifa in the same way [it] took over Uefa. The massive clubs have gained yet more power and control.

“EFC initially came out very hard against Gianni, but were quick to row back. They’ve been silent on the presidential election and other governance complications, as it’s clear they’ve reached an agreement with Fifa. The joint venture is the price of their silence.”.

The result is likely to be an acceleration of trends already under way. The biggest European clubs look set to make even more money from new tournaments, particularly given they are likely to increase in size and regularity, beginning with a 48-team men’s Club World Cup in 2029.

Chelsea earned $115m (£85m) from winning the inaugural expanded tournament last year, with six European clubs in the top seven of the prize money table and Brazil’s Fluminense the only non-European side to earn more than $50m. Solidarity payments are also skewed towards Europe, whose clubs will receive 50% of the $250m pot despite providing only 12 of the 32 contestants. Those defending the status quo note that Europe generates about 70% of Fifa’s revenue.

A senior source at another confederation said the inevitable result of the joint venture would be less money for the rest of the world, certainly in proportional terms. “The most significant long-term result of the Fifa crisis will be to entrench the economic might of the biggest European clubs,” they said. “That means reduced access to competitions, less revenue and broadcast rights for the rest of us.

Industry observers note that “The European clubs are well organised, economically rational and have taken advantage of the opportunity to extract something from Fifa. Gianni Infantino is in the business of writing cheques and will now be writing big ones to clubs as well as member associations.”.

Recent findings suggest that these developments are, at least, easily foreseeable. It is less straightforward to analyse how football’s face will change if a putative World Football Clubs gains the power many believe lies in its sights. “Every club around the world deserves to be represented; to have a voice, and a better chance to succeed,” Al-Khelaïfi said in his September address. But the world’s wealthiest clubs stand poised to benefit from football’s crisis more handsomely than anyone.
