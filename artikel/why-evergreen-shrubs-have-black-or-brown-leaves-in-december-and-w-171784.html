Why Evergreen Shrubs Have Black or Brown Leaves in December – and When You Should Act Fast if You Spot This Leaf Damage

Evergreen shrubs are workhorses in the garden, providing year-round green foliage, privacy hedging, and focal points in beds. They're the backbone of a winter garden, which is why it's so disappointing to find brown or black leaf tips on a hebe, photinia, choisya, arborvitae or other evergreens after the first cold snap. And this year, evergreen shrubs with dead leaf tips are a problem many gardeners are facing as temperatures drop.

According to recent reports, the mild fall in many areas this year allowed many shrubs to delay dormancy and even put out fresh growth. Without the time to build cold resistance before winter weather arrives, evergreen foliage is left vulnerable to leaf damage.

Sources indicate that blackened or brown evergreen leaf tips don't necessarily mean your shrubs are dying, but they are cause for concern and a clear sign you need to prepare your garden for winter. Understanding what's caused the damage is important so you know what to do next to save your beloved evergreen shrubs. Come on in and let’s diagnose and treat your evergreen shrubs with black or brown leaf tips in late fall or early winter.

If your shrubs are experiencing damage related to our mild fall this year, you're likely to spot these symptoms:.

In a recent development, if the damage is confined to a few leaf and needle tips, there's no need to worry: your shrub will recover, and it's a good idea to take steps to prevent further damage. If the damage is more significant, there's still a high chance of recovery but you should better protect plants from frost and have plenty of patience.

There are two primary and most likely causes of dark, dry tips on your evergreen shrubs. The distinction matters because the underlying cause determines how you help your shrub recover.

Your shrubs naturally harden off for winter, a process triggered by less daylight and gradually dropping temperatures. They slow their growth and undergo some internal processes that allow them to better withstand freezing winter temperatures. To get more specific, soluble sugars begin to accumulate in the cells of leaves and needles. These sugars make it harder for water in the cells to freeze and allow the plant to better regulate water movement. As the youngest leaves go through this process, they become woodier, more mature, and more resistant to freezing injuries.

Industry observers note that during a mild fall, as many of us have experienced this year, some evergreens might continue producing new, soft growth instead of entering the dormancy period. This disrupts the vital process of winter hardening-off, leaving cells with high water content and underdeveloped protection. So, when temperatures finally drop, the water in cells freezes more readily. These frozen cells then rupture. Affected leaves, needles, and tips die as a result of the damage and turn brown or black.

Some of the evergreens most susceptible to this type of damage include hebe, choisya, camellia, and photinia. Even plants that normally tolerate cold winters can be affected after an unusually warm fall.

It has been revealed that desiccation or wind scorch, also known as winter burn, is another potential cause of black or brown evergreen foliage, and this can occur regardless of an unseasonable fall. Evergreen shrubs lose moisture through their leaves, even as growth slows, and cold air, winter winds and bright sun increase this moisture loss. Once the ground – and the moisture held in the soil – freezes, the shrubs’ roots can't take up water to replace what's being lost. This moisture imbalance can cause leaf and needle tissue to dry out and turn brown or black, and damage often begins at foliage tips and margins.

Distinguishing leaf damage caused by winter desiccation from latest foliage that's died in the first cold snap is easy: if wind scorch is the issue, damage will be on the windy side of the shrub. You’ll also see that the outer leaves and needles are most affected, with the inner foliage protected. The damage from wind scorch is typically gradual rather than suddenly appearing after a frost or freeze, too.

It has been revealed that evergreens with shallow roots like arborvitae, boxwood, rhododendron, yew, juniper and holly are particularly susceptible to winter desiccation. Any evergreen can be damaged this way, however, depending on moisture in the soil, wind levels and how protected the shrub is.

First – and most importantly – of all, don’t rush to snip off those black tips. You can’t recover that dead tissue, and cutting it off now can cause more damage. If your shrubs are victims of the poor hardening-off that resulted from a mild fall, trimming could trigger more vulnerable growth, and also expose at the moment sheltered foliage to further harm. Similarly, the damage on a wind-scorched shrub will protect the foliage beneath.

The next step depends on the cause of the leaf damage.

In a recent development, now you know your shrub isn’t ready for the cold, protect it from the rest of the winter weather to come. A zip-up plant jacket is an easy way to winterize shrubs that only need protecting ahead of a predicted cold snap rather than being wrapped up all winter. Another option is to wrap plants up in burlap – rolls of hessian are available from Walmart – or horticultural fleece such as this from Amazon, unwrapping plants after the cold weather has passed.

When covering shrubs, be sure to do so loosely and use stakes inserted in the soil to hold fabric away from leaves. If your shrub is growing in a container, then wrapping the pot in bubble wrap and burlap protects roots during a prolonged freeze, too. Choose the level of protection based on your zone and how badly damaged your evergreen shrub is.

Recent findings suggest that if those dead leaf tips are caused by a wind and moisture imbalance, first check the moisture in the soil around the plants. If you're using a moisture meter, make sure it's one with a long probe like this moisture meter on Amazon so you get a reading where it matters. If the soil is dry and the ground isn’t frozen, give the root zone a good soak.

Next, apply a layer of mulch made from natural materials like Back to the Roots Organic Mulch, available from Amazon, around the base of each shrub. This will help conserve moisture and insulate the soil. Just be sure to keep the mulch a few inches away from the stems to prevent moisture accumulating there and causing rot or fungal disease.

Now you need to protect your shrub from further damage from wind scorch. You could pop a frost jacket on your shrub whenever high winds are forecast, or use burlap to create a windbreak. You don’t necessarily need to cover the whole shrub; just provide a barrier from the prevailing winds. Keep checking shrubs throughout the winter, adding windbreaks and watering when the soil thaws as needed.

In a recent development, these steps will help prevent more damage, but they won’t reverse the damage that has already occurred. Now, you’re probably wondering when you can remove those unsightly dead leaf tips – and you're going to have to be patient. It’s vital not to prune off dead leaves or branches now as this can trigger newly introduced growth, introduce damage or disease at the cut areas, and leave interior foliage vulnerable.

Wait to prune until spring when there is no more risk of a severe frost, then use clean, sharp shears to remove dead tips. If larger areas have browned or blackened, cut these stems back to the first healthy, live bud. You can check if a branch might regrow by scraping back the outer layer with your fingernail. If it’s brown underneath, it’s dead and should be removed. If you see green, it could still sprout newly introduced growth.

Keep your pruning light in spring. Trim back the damaged parts and then let healthy branches and leaves grow back in before you do any heavier pruning or shaping.

The damage for this year is done and gone, but you can learn from the incident to save your evergreen shrubs from harm next year:.

It has been revealed that dead leaf tips on evergreen shrubs are usually a cosmetic glitch, not a death sentence for your favorite plants. Follow these guidelines and your shrubs should recover, ready to shine again next year.
