I run a global business called Brand of a Leader, which supports founders and executives in more than 12 countries by helping them build their visibility. When I first rolled out my business six years ago, all I had was a LinkedIn profile — no website and, naturally, no digital footprint of any kind yet.

It has been revealed that and so our first few clients came through my network. By being active on LinkedIn and consistently sharing my perspective, I would stay top of mind with people who already knew me. When they needed support, or when someone in their own network did, I was the one who came to mind and was hired or recommended.

Today, I continue to use LinkedIn actively to generate clients, develop relationships and stay connected with my network. I’ve been on the platform for over 20 years, and throughout the years, I’ve heard people constantly complain about one thing: the algorithm. I always chuckle because the discourse is consistently the same, yet it’s presented as something new. Through all of my work and my extensive work supporting founders across the globe with their LinkedIn, I’ve seen how much people overindex on what to post, when to post and how to post, while overlooking something else that is likely even more notable!

In a recent development, here are the three mistakes I see founders make all the time. And I promise that if you fix those, you will see better results from this platform.

Obsession over what, when and how to post is real. Short posts or long posts? Daily or weekly? With a photo? Video? No media? What should I even write about? Those are undoubtedly important questions, yet there’s another one: What happens when someone reads your post, does become interested in learning more about you and then clicks on your profile?

My experience indicates me that most founders don’t pay much attention to what’s actually on their profile. I know this because I constantly audit LinkedIn pages as part of the service that we provide at Brand of a Leader.

Too often, when I visit a LinkedIn profile, I find a bland banner or none whatsoever, and a short “About” section written in the third person. Whatever interest the person’s post may have piqued is rapidly lost when visiting their uninspiring profile.

Personally, I treat my profile as a landing page. And we do the same for our clients. For entrepreneurs, myself included, a well-designed LinkedIn page can be a practical alternative to a personal website when used thoughtfully.

Visit your profile, looking at it through the eyes of a potential client, and see what impression it would leave on them after spending a minute or two scrolling through it.

One of the things we consistently convince our clients not to do is include calls to action in their posts. Salesy or overly promotional messages on founders’ profiles don’t make a great impression.

As a reader, I find that repeated prompts to book a call can make otherwise thoughtful writing feel overly promotional. I prefer to keep my content, and my clients’ content, conversational, expertise-building or humanizing. Yet, I do understand the importance of giving interested readers a clearly visible way to take the next step.

Instead of salesy calls to action in posts, I leverage the custom call-to-action button included with LinkedIn Premium Business. You can have it say anything that’s most relevant to you and your goals (for example, “Visit my website” or “Book a call”). This link will appear in your profile, just under your name and above the post that you write. That feels more elegant to me.

Sources indicate that one of the biggest mistakes I see founders make is treating LinkedIn as a broadcasting platform and not a social media platform. This is not only the quickest way to reduce the overall engagement of your content on LinkedIn, but it’s also not great for your positioning overall. When you only post and never engage, it positions you as a taker, not a giver, and also discourages people from further engaging with you.

At a minimum, you need to reply to people who took the time to comment on your posts. No, not only a “like” — an actual thoughtful reply. They have taken time to respond to something you shared. Read their contribution and respond to the substance of it.

It has been revealed that and then ideally, extend that attention to other people’s content. Comment to share a relevant experience or ask a thoughtful question. If someone describes a challenge you have faced, explain what you learned. But don’t use AI. Generic, bland comments negatively impact your brand equity. Take 30 minutes to leave some authentic comments a couple of times weekly, and that consistency will compound over time.

Before publishing your next post, think about what interested readers will see on your profile if they click on it. Give them enough context to understand your expertise, an easy way to contact you and your attention when they engage in conversation with you.

According to recent reports, before you hit publish again, take a look at LinkedIn’s latest Small Business Playbook for practical ways to build your brand, grow your network and find customers. You can also explore LinkedIn’s tools for small businesses to make sure your profile is ready when interested readers click through.
