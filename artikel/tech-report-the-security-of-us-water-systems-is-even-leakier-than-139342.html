Tech Report: The security of US water systems is even leakier than we thought

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

And intruders don't always need to exploit bugs to break in.

American water systems are even less secure than we thought, according to a study of 236 technology vendors that provide gear to US drinking water, wastewater, and industrial water systems.

In a recent development, the federal government has issued 4,014 security advisories for industrial control systems and cataloged 1,792 vulnerabilities known to have been exploited in cyberattacks. A new index, called Water Supply Chain Watch and published by exposure management firm Securin, however, found almost twice as many vulnerabilities as public records identified – including 20 exploited flaws that weren't even in the federal government's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) catalog.

The security provider compared data on water-tech vendors with records from the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA), the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), the FBI, and WaterISAC, drawing on CISA advisory data through November 20 and KEV catalog entries through November 23.

It found that federal water advisories hit their highest level this year since 2020.

However, the volume of security alerts isn't a reliable predictor of real-world attacks, the researchers noted.

Sources indicate that five vendors – Rockwell Automation (58), Advantech (48), Schneider Electric (35), Siemens (26), and ABB (24) – account for 191 of the 376 water-tagged security advisories. The No. 2 firm, Advantech, accounted for the highest number of vulnerabilities rated critical, but none of the CVEs in its advisories appear in CISA's KEV catalog. Unitronics, on the other hand, accounted for only two water-tagged security advisories, but at least 75 of its controllers were compromised between November 2023 and January 2024 by attackers taking advantage of default or missing passwords.

The report also highlights another disconnect between water-related CVEs and real-world cyberattacks, which don't always require attackers to exploit software bugs. The July intrusions, which security researchers suspect were the work of Iran-linked attackers, targeted more than 100 water systems, with incidents reported across at least 12 states, and involved poorly secured internet-connected controllers.

According to Securin, 791 Rockwell MicroLogix 1400 controllers across multiple industries in the US were exposed to the internet as of November 17.

According to recent reports, additionally, research by Forescout cited by the security shop found that most exposed Rockwell controllers in the US – seven in 10 – sit on mobile carrier networks, where poorly secured cellular modems can provide an entry point for attackers. A CISA advisory from July describes this technique:.

According to recent reports, the authoring agencies observed Iranian-affiliated APT actors using several foreign-based IP addresses to access internet-facing PLCs manufactured by Rockwell Automation/Allen-Bradley, Schneider Electric, Siemens, and potentially other manufactured PLCs [T0883]. The actors used leased, third-party hosted infrastructure and manufacturers’ PLC programming software to connect to misconfigured victim PLCs. Inbound malicious traffic has been observed targeting PLC devices on the following ports: 44818, 2222, 102, and 502, as well as targeting modems on port 22.

These cellular modems, often installed by third-party contractors, frequently aren’t documented.

Unsurprisingly, the top recommendation for water utilities is to find every cellular modem in the environment, including those installed by contractors, make sure they aren’t exposed to the open internet, and put a gateway with multifactor authentication (MFA) in front of them to stop would-be intruders from gaining access via the modem and controller combo.

“Water utilities are spending time and money fixing the vulnerabilities they're told to worry about,” Securin CEO Srinivas Mukkamala noted in a statement. “Meanwhile, attackers are getting in through equipment nobody knew was connected or passwords nobody bothered to change.” ®.
