Latest Update: Fourth Circuit Upholds Virginia's Law Terminating Agreements to Help With Immigration Enforcement - but Strikes Down anti-Mask Law

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that in September, I wrote about the the federal district court decision striking down Virginia's new law terminating Section 287(g) agreements, under which various state and local law enforcement agencies agree to help with federal immigration enforcement. Today, in United States v. Virginia, the US Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit reversed that decision. But, at the same time, in a 2-1 ruling, they struck down Virginia's law barring law enforcement officers (including federal ones) from wearing masks except in relatively narrowly specified circumstances. Here is an excerpt from the court's opinion (written by Judge Heytens) on the 287(g) problem:.

We vacate the preliminary injunction that bars enforcing the 287(g) Law with respect to existing agreements. The district court concluded the Federal Government was likely to succeed on the merits because rendering such agreements unenforceable violate both the intergovernmental immunity doctrine and the Contracts Clause. We disagree. The intergovernmental immunity doctrine does not apply because the 287(g) Law neither directly regulates the Federal Government nor prevents private parties from contracting with it…. The 287(g) Law also does not violate the Contracts Clause for two separate reasons: (1) because the existing agreements expressly permit unilateral termination by the non-federal party; and (2) even if they did not, the Commonwealth would have constitutionally valid justifications for requiring state and local officials to terminate or renegotiate any such agreements.

Later in the opinion Judge Heytens expands on why Section 287(g) termination does not violate the Contracts Clause:.

According to recent reports, we conclude the Federal Government cannot satisfy that test for two different, yet complementary, reasons. First, all the agreements covered by the district court's injunction expressly permit the Commonwealth's agents to unilaterally terminate without cause, which means the 287(g) Law does not "substantially impair" (Sveen, 584 U.S. at 819) a contractual relationship at Step 1. Second, even if the agreements did not so provide, neither the Commonwealth's agencies nor those of its localities may contract away "an essential attribute of [the Commonwealth's] sovereignty." United States Tr. Co. of N.Y. v. New Jersey, 431 U.S. 1, 23 (1977). Here, that attribute is the Commonwealth's power to decide whether and on what terms to assist with the ongoing enforcement of federal law.

According to recent reports, I largely agree. In my post on the district court decision, I offered some additional reasons why terminating these agreements doesn't violate the Contracts Clause.

In a recent development, the anti-mask law presents more difficult problems because - as the majority emphasizes - it directly regulates federal law enforcement officials. On the other hand, the law imposes the same constraints on state and local officers, as well, and therefore doesn't discriminate against them. On balance, I agree with Judge Robert Bruce King's dissent:.

At bottom, a single ICE officer should never be able to override a duly enacted statute of the Commonwealth of Virginia in the absence of some federal law, regulation, or other valid supporting authority. Consider what purportedly conflicts with the Mask/Identity Statute. A federal statute? None. A treaty of the United States? None. A federal regulation or executive order? None. A written policy located anywhere within ICE or the Department of Homeland Security? None has been identified. What we have today is a single sentence in a Declaration asserting that individual ICE officers possess the discretion and ability to conceal their identities when they alone deem it appropriate. No supervision is at all necessary or required and the agency itself has no involvement in the ICE officer's daily decisions on whether to wear a mask. I cannot and will not accept the proposition that individualized discretion, untethered to any identifiable source of federal law, enjoys constitutional supremacy over a duly enacted statute of the Commonwealth.

As the majority recognizes, federal law enforcement officers are not generally exempt from state law while on the job. For example, they must obey state traffic laws when driving their vehicles. A valid federal law could override a conflicting state law. But here, there is no such overriding federal law, or even a regulation (though I'm not convinced the latter alone would be enough). Just the preferences of ICE agents. Those preferences are not enough to secure an exemption from state law. Federal law (when constitutionally valid) is supreme over state law. The preferences of individual federal officials or agencies are not.
