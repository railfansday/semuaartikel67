Pink turkey doesn't necessarily mean your Thanksgiving feast is ruined. Here's how to know if your bird is safe to eat so you can stay calm and turkey on.

I’ll never forget the Thanksgiving when I was convinced I’d ruined the holiday for everyone. I wasn’t an experienced cook at the time, and it was one of my first attempts at making a Thanksgiving turkey. I used a helpful guide for how long to cook a turkey and even invested in a meat thermometer to be sure it reached at least 165°F. It looked great, and I was so proud!

Sources indicate that imagine my surprise and horror when I carved that gorgeously browned roast turkey and discovered it was pink inside. I was so nervous my guests would assume the turkey was undercooked. I didn’t know then that pink turkey can be perfectly safe to eat—as long as it’s cooked to that vital internal temperature.

Cooked turkey can look pink for a few reasons that have nothing to do with doneness, including the age of the bird, how it’s prepared and natural pigmentation.

It has been revealed that “Poultry naturally can have a pink hue,” claims Heidi Diestel of turkey farm Diestel Family Ranch in Sonora, California. “The legs and thighs have more of the natural pigments that give meat its darker color. Those pigments, along with changes that happen during cooking, can leave some pink even when the turkey is fully cooked.”.

The pigments Heidi refers to are proteins called myoglobin and cytochrome c. The more of these proteins the meat has, the darker it will be. Older animals tend to have more proteins, and therefore darker pigmentation, as do any regularly exercised muscles. That explains why leg meat is darker than breast meat, as turkeys spend the bulk of their days walking and running.

According to recent reports, cooking can also cause a chemical reaction with these proteins that contributes to a pink color. Smoked turkey often stays pink from the reaction of the meat to the smoke inside the grill or smoker, but even oven-roasted turkey can have a pinkish hue. Myoglobin usually turns white during the cooking process, but cytochrome c is more resistant to heat and can maintain a pink blush.

Grace Natoli Sheldon for Taste of Home.

Using a meat thermometer is the best way to know if your turkey is safe to eat.

Recent findings suggest that poultry is considered safe to eat when it meets a cooking temperature of at least 165°. However, the Taste of Home Test Kitchen recommends cooking the leg and thigh meat to 170° for the best texture. For stuffed turkeys, don’t forget to take the temperature of the stuffing as well, which should reach at least 165° when the thermometer is inserted in the center of the stuffing.

If your turkey comes embedded with a round plastic doneness indicator that pops when the turkey is ready, don’t use that as an absolute; they are notoriously inaccurate.

To check if the turkey is done, insert the thermometer in a thick, meaty area, making sure the tip doesn’t touch any bones (bone will show a higher temperature).

If you’re using a probe thermometer that you insert before cooking, double-check its position once the turkey has been cooking for a while. Meat contracts as it cooks, so it may no longer be in the best spot.

In a recent development, if any part of your turkey is below 165°, you should continue cooking the turkey until all parts reach a safe temperature. It’s common for the thermometer to register different temperatures in different areas, since white meat cooks at a faster rate than dark meat. Don’t be tempted to cut off the parts that aren’t ready and return only them to the oven. It’ll increase the risk that undercooked juices will run onto your cutting board and contaminate the properly cooked turkey.

If the legs and thighs aren’t done but the breast is, I like to baste the white meat and loosely tent it with foil to try and lock in as much moisture as possible. If the dark meat is ready but the breast needs more time, there’s no need to sweat it. Dark meat has enough natural fat and moisture that it won’t become dry with a little extra time in the oven.

You should never knowingly eat undercooked turkey. According to the CDC, undercooked turkey can contain harmful bacteria, like Salmonella, Clostridium perfringens or Campylobacter, that can cause foodborne illnesses.

There’s also a risk of bacteria if the turkey isn’t thawed or prepared correctly. Give yourself plenty of time to thaw a frozen turkey, either in the refrigerator or in a cold water bath. Also, make sure to keep the counter or cutting board where you’re prepping the turkey clean and sanitized before and after handling the raw bird.
