According to recent reports, one major debate in investing right now, and a cause of concern for some economists, is the question: "Will the U.S. dollar keep its status as the world's top reserve currency?" Many Americans who go about their daily business, like shopping for groceries or other household goods, might not think much about this. But it's important.

Industry observers note that in the background of everyday life, Americans benefit from a unique privilege: Many countries around the world want to use and hold our currency. The U.S. dollar is the most popular reserve currency for central banks and governments around the world. According to Federal Reserve data, as of 2024, 58.5% of total global foreign exchange reserves were held in U.S. dollars. The euro was in a distant second place, making up about 19%, followed by the Japanese yen at 5.6%.

According to recent reports, there's been some debate about whether this will continue. What if the U.S. dollar becomes less popular than other international currencies? What if America has a government debt crisis and investors flee American assets? What if the world loses confidence in America?

Jamie Dimon, the CEO of JPMorgan Chase (JPM -0.57%), made headlines for casting doubt upon those doubts. Dimon believes that the American dollar's status as the global reserve currency is not in danger -- as long as two key U.S. strengths stay in place.

If Dimon is correct, that could be good news for investors in U.S. dollar-denominated assets. Let's look at what the JPMorgan Chase CEO had to say, and why it matters for your investments.

Speaking at the Economic Club of Chicago last month, Dimon expressed confidence in the U.S. dollar and stated he doesn't believe it will lose its status as the world's most popular reserve currency. He said that the two most important factors in the U.S. dollar's dominance of foreign reserves are the U.S. military and the overall strength of the U.S. economy.

"If we have the strongest military in the world and the strongest economy in the world, we will be the preeminent reserve currency," Dimon noted.

Foreign governments and central banks tend to hold dollars because the dollar is widely used in international trade and is generally considered a safe, stable, and liquid asset. It's easy to move money with U.S. dollars or exchange U.S. Treasury bonds for cash. Not every country offers these advantages. The U.S. dollar's role as the No. 1 reserve currency also reflects America's strengths as a place to invest: a stable government, a strong economy, and the rule of law to protect investors' rights and freedoms.

Industry observers note that dimon's argument is that as long as America's economy leads the world and the U.S. military helps ensure global stability and protects international free trade, America will be a safe and desirable place for investors worldwide to put their money. That's good news for the U.S. dollar and investors in American stocks and bonds.

In a recent development, since so many banks, investors, and governments around the world trust the dollar and want to buy it, it creates demand that keeps the dollar’s value strong relative to other currencies. It also helps keep U.S. interest rates lower, enabling American consumers, businesses, and the U.S. government to keep borrowing money.

What if that all went away? A June 2026 research paper by Stanford University finance professors found that if the dollar lost its status as a foreign reserve currency, there would be adverse consequences. According to their model, the dollar would depreciate by 8.8%, the U.S. real interest rate would rise by about 0.9%, and there would be an aggregate loss of wealth of about one year of U.S. GDP (about $30.8 trillion, as of 2025).

According to recent reports, the good news is that it seems highly unlikely that the U.S. will lose its status as the world's favorite foreign currency -- at least not anytime soon, and not all at once. Other countries' currencies have some disadvantages that make them unlikely to replace the dollar.

Industry observers note that if you believe that the U.S. dollar will stay strong, you might want to keep investing in U.S. stocks, such as those in the S&P 500 index (^GSPC -0.04%). The Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO -0.15%) is a simple way to buy shares of the 500 largest publicly traded companies in America. If the U.S. economy performs well and the U.S. dollar remains strong, the companies in this S&P 500 ETF will likely benefit in the long term.
