No mention of fossil fuels in latest COP31 draft deal

Climate negotiators have removed any mention of fossil fuels — the main source of greenhouse gas emissions heating the planet — in a latest draft deal published by COP31 summit co-host Australia.

The latest text released Thursday at the UN climate conference in Turkey stripped out two references to a transition away from coal, oil and gas and a call for an accelerated phaseout that had been in an earlier draft.

This slimmed down version follows days of tough negotiations, with representatives from nearly 200 nations debating problems such as the future of fossil fuels and climate funding for developing countries.

The text still includes a call to increase the global share of final energy demand met by electricity to 35% by 2035 — up from around 20% today — a proposal that was pushed by host Turkey.

It did not specify whether that additional electricity needs to come from clean sources rather than fossil fuels, a point that has drawn criticism from some countries.

Industry observers note that the draft did mention the electrification target alongside efforts to triple renewable energy capacity globally as well as other energy efficiency measures. But Jan Kowalzig, a climate finance expert with Oxfam Germany, mentioned negotiators had failed to "meaningfully" link them.

Kowalzig also criticized the text for not referencing a transition away from fossil fuels in a gradual manner — something that had been mentioned in the final decision out of the COP28 in Dubai several years ago.

"This is insufficient for a climate conference that already decided several years ago to phase out fossil fuels and that presents itself as a conference focused on implementation," he said in a statement.

According to recent reports, another hotly contested problem at the talks has been financial support to help developing countries deal with climate change. Divisions largely revolve around who should bear the costs and whether funding should come in the form of grants or loans.

The second draft called for further discussions on a tripling of funding for climate adaptation measures in developing countries. It also noted "with concern the gap between climate finance flows and needs," with the adaptation costs in developing countries estimated at $305 billion annually up to 2035.

The wording on finance prompted criticism from Meenakshi Raman, the coordinator of the climate change program at the Third World Network, who had been "hoping to see much more ambition on the side of finance from the developed world.".

"Coming here really confirmed the worst fears that developed countries are not only backsliding but putting nothing in terms of real meaning on the table," said Raman.

The European Union has noted it won't agree to higher climate financing pledges because it pays its "fair share" while others don't.

The draft text also expressed "deep concern" that global warming is projected to exceed 1.5 degrees Celsius (2.7 degrees Fahrenheit) above pre-industrial levels — the threshold outlined in the 2015 Paris Agreement to avoid runaway climate change.

It acknowledged that every increment of warming above current levels would "result in increasingly adverse, and in some cases irreversible, impacts and risks to human and natural systems.".

Australian Climate Change and Energy Minister Chris Bowen remarked the text aimed to communicate that "1.5 degrees of warming is not acceptable and will not be allowed to stand.".

The draft also recognized the need for "deep, rapid ​and ‌sustained" cuts to greenhouse gas emissions and called for efforts to ramp up action targeting the potent greenhouse gas methane.

The greenhouse gas emissions heating the planet are largely caused by burning fossil fuels.

The text additionally calls for the deployment of technologies that can remove carbon from the atmosphere to be sped up, many of which have yet to be tested and proven on a large scale.

Harjeet Singh of the Fill the Fund campaign to boost climate finance for developing countries said the second draft "trims away inconvenient mandates and accountability while leaving behind lofty rhetoric that won't protect a single vulnerable community.".

The climate conference officially ends on Friday, but it's not uncommon for the annual talks to run on for longer than expected. The latest text is expected to face further revisions before it can be adopted.

Decisions on the language need approval from all countries, meaning an objection from one party is enough to scupper a deal.

On Thursday, Panama remarked it would block any agreement that does not meet its demands for a cap on oil production and steps to eliminate methane.

The country's environment minister, Juan Carlos Navarro, mentioned Panama would "not lend its name" to "complacent or ineffective language" in the final pact.

Australian Energy Minister Bowen said he expected countries to bring forward their concerns, feedback and questions in the coming hours and overnight.

"We won't be leaving the rooms of Antalya until we have that consensus, until we have a plan we can put to the parties on behalf of the countries and governments they represent, and they can say, yes, we can do this: we can keep the world to 1.5 degrees of warming.".

The stakes are high for this edition of the COP summit, which comes in a year of major climate-related disasters, from wildfires and deadly heat waves in Europe to a glacier collapse in Nepal that killed more than 1,400 people.

It's also taking place against the backdrop of an intensifying "super" El Nino weather pattern, and geopolitical shocks that have sent energy prices rising globally.

Industry observers note that the conference is the first to be held under the acknowledgement by the UN that keeping the average global temperature under 1.5C is no longer realistic. Instead, scientists are talking about limiting the time spent above this threshold and bringing temperatures back down rapidly by the end of the century.

Countries are at odds about how to acknowledge overshoot of the temperature threshold in the final agreement, with some worrying that fossil fuel producing countries such as Saudi Arabia and Iran are seeking to jettison 1.5C entirely, which could lead to "backsliding" on climate action, according to AFP.

"It's critically vital for this COP to recognize, and this is really the first COP that will likely do so, that we are on the way to overshooting 1.5 degrees," stated David Waskow, the director of the World Resources Institute's International Climate Initiative.
