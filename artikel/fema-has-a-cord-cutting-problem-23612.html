On Tuesday, countless TV viewers will find their favorite highlights interrupted for a one-minute test of the Federal Emergency Management Agency’s Emergency Alert System (EAS). The test will cut into live broadcasts and cable reruns alike, and also be heard on radio stations across the country. However, anyone getting their TV fix from Netflix, Disney+, or any other streaming service likely won’t notice a thing. That’s bad news, especially when the next real emergency happens. According to recent Nielsen data, U.S. consumers are already spending half their TV viewing time with digital video platforms like YouTube and Amazon Prime. Federal alert systems have been modernized multiple times since those early days, including an expansion to mobile phones in 2012. But in recent years, regulators and emergency management officials have struggled to keep up with rapidly changing media consumption habits and shifts in the TV business. This has led to confusing gaps in the Emergency Alert System. One example: Consumers who have switched their TV subscriptions from a cable company to an internet-based TV service like YouTube TV, Fubo, or Hulu + Live TV may also miss out on alerts. These so-called “virtual” pay TV operators don’t participate in the EAS, which means they don’t interrupt their programming to carry such warnings. But if a YouTube TV subscriber happens to be tuned into a local broadcast network like ABC or Fox, they may see the alert after all. Those channels are frequently ingested from local broadcast TV affiliates, which in turn do work with FEMA. In other words: YouTube TV subscribers who watch Dancing With the Stars on ABC during an emergency may get a warning, while those tuned into Bravo’s Southern Charm may be blissfully unaware of any impending danger. “Regardless of the other aspects of regulation, when it comes to public safety concerns, [traditional and internet-based pay TV services] should be on equal footing,” says John Bergmayer, legal director of the nonprofit technology advocacy group Public Knowledge. Virtual pay TV services already need to know a subscriber’s location to serve up local broadcast channels, which should help them target consumers with the right alerts. “And like cable, they have the technical capacity to both interrupt a program or to add some sort of crawl along the bottom of the screen,” Bergmayer says. Streaming services and their media company owners have generally resisted the idea of being forced to carry emergency alerts. In a 2021 regulatory filing, the Motion Picture Association, which counts Netflix, Disney, and Skydance among its members, declared that it would be “enormously technically difficult, if not entirely impracticable” to expand emergency alerts to streaming. One problem the industry association pointed to is the federated nature of America’s Emergency Alert System. While Tuesday’s test alert goes out nationwide, real warnings about wildfires or flooding are necessarily local. Requiring a streaming service like Netflix to relay those alerts would mean building out extensive monitoring infrastructure in every state. Some hope that the fresh Advanced Television Systems Committee (ATSC) 3.0 broadcast standard, which brings modern technologies to antenna TV, will come to the rescue. In theory, modern TVs equipped with ATSC 3.0 tuners could interrupt a Netflix stream as soon as they receive an emergency alert via antenna. In practice, there’s no regulation in place to require this yet. Critics also fear that ATSC 3.0’s content protection technologies could hamper emergency warnings. In the end, the lesson from Tuesday’s test may be that emergency alerts can’t rely on just one technology. “There is no silver bullet,” says Art Botterell, author of To Warn Is Human: A Cultural History of Public Warning. Instead of trying to find a tech fix, he’d prefer that we invest in civic infrastructure and strengthen the bonds between neighbors so they can alert and help one another. “We need to facilitate that person-to-person sharing,” Botterell says. That, in turn, may also help people who happen to tune into the wrong TV show at the wrong moment.

It has been revealed that that’s bad news, especially when the next real emergency happens. According to recent Nielsen data, U.S. consumers are already spending half their TV viewing time with digital video platforms like YouTube and Amazon Prime. In other words: Half of America’s TV viewers are missing out on emergency alerts because they’re watching online instead.

“It is vital that emergency alert systems adapt to changes in consumer technologies,” states University of Colorado Denver communications professor Hamilton Bean, an expert in public alert and warning systems. “Whether through streaming services, mobile devices, or IoT [Internet of Things], emergency alerts work best when they reach people where they are—at home, at work, or in public settings.”.

The federal government first began testing emergency alerts in the 1950s to warn citizens of a possible Soviet nuclear attack. Federal alert systems have been modernized multiple times since those early days, including an expansion to mobile phones in 2012. But in recent years, regulators and emergency management officials have struggled to keep up with rapidly changing media consumption habits and shifts in the TV business.

Recent findings suggest that this has led to confusing gaps in the Emergency Alert System. One example: Consumers who have switched their TV subscriptions from a cable company to an internet-based TV service like YouTube TV, Fubo, or Hulu + Live TV may also miss out on alerts. These so-called “virtual” pay TV operators don’t participate in the EAS, which means they don’t interrupt their programming to carry such warnings.

But if a YouTube TV subscriber happens to be tuned into a local broadcast network like ABC or Fox, they may see the alert after all. Those channels are frequently ingested from local broadcast TV affiliates, which in turn do work with FEMA. In other words: YouTube TV subscribers who watch Dancing With the Stars on ABC during an emergency may get a warning, while those tuned into Bravo’s Southern Charm may be blissfully unaware of any impending danger.

“Regardless of the other aspects of regulation, when it comes to public safety concerns, [traditional and internet-based pay TV services] should be on equal footing,” claims John Bergmayer, legal director of the nonprofit technology advocacy group Public Knowledge. Virtual pay TV services already need to know a subscriber’s location to serve up local broadcast channels, which should help them target consumers with the right alerts. “And like cable, they have the technical capacity to both interrupt a program or to add some sort of crawl along the bottom of the screen,” Bergmayer says.

Industry observers note that streaming services and their media company owners have generally resisted the idea of being forced to carry emergency alerts. In a 2021 regulatory filing, the Motion Picture Association, which counts Netflix, Disney, and Skydance among its members, declared that it would be “enormously technically difficult, if not entirely impracticable” to expand emergency alerts to streaming.

According to recent reports, one problem the industry association pointed to is the federated nature of America’s Emergency Alert System. While Tuesday’s test alert goes out nationwide, real warnings about wildfires or flooding are necessarily local. Requiring a streaming service like Netflix to relay those alerts would mean building out extensive monitoring infrastructure in every state.

According to recent reports, some hope that the fresh Advanced Television Systems Committee (ATSC) 3.0 broadcast standard, which brings modern technologies to antenna TV, will come to the rescue. In theory, modern TVs equipped with ATSC 3.0 tuners could interrupt a Netflix stream as soon as they receive an emergency alert via antenna. In practice, there’s no regulation in place to require this yet. Critics also fear that ATSC 3.0’s content protection technologies could hamper emergency warnings.

Recent findings suggest that in the end, the lesson from Tuesday’s test may be that emergency alerts can’t rely on just one technology. “There is no silver bullet,” indicates Art Botterell, author of To Warn Is Human: A Cultural History of Public Warning. Instead of trying to find a tech fix, he’d prefer that we invest in civic infrastructure and strengthen the bonds between neighbors so they can alert and help one another. “We need to facilitate that person-to-person sharing,” Botterell says.

That, in turn, may also help people who happen to tune into the wrong TV show at the wrong moment.
