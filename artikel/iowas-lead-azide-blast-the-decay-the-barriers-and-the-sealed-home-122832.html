Iowa's lead azide blast: the decay, the barriers and the sealed homes

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The Army detonated its Iowa lead azide stockpile. See why it acted, how barriers limit the lead, and what households near the plant did.

Getting rid of the lead azide stored in Iowa forced the Army to choose among methods, and whichever one it picked would affect the people living around the plant. The Army had a whole stockpile to destroy rather than a single bad drum, with 68 barrels of bomb-making material in all, according to the Chicago Sun-Times. With that much material, the way the Army destroys it matters to everyone who lives nearby. Army officials weighed gentler-sounding methods, including chemical neutralization and thermal treatment, before they settled on a blast. The Army's case is that the blast is the least risky of these methods. "In every instance, the answer is controlled detonation is safest," Acting Secretary of the Army Adam Telle told PBS NewsHour. Once the Army chose a detonation, its neighbors no longer had to ask whether a blast would happen, only how to get through it.

Residents spent days planning around a date that kept moving while the Army's opponents fought the detonation in court. The date changed three times, PBS NewsHour reports, and the 8th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals cleared the blast only on the Monday before it. The court did not find that the blast was harmless. It approved the detonation by pointing to the barriers around the storage area and to the weather restrictions, so a safe detonation day depends on those conditions holding. That leaves people nearby with three practical questions: why the Army couldn't wait, how much lead it can keep on site, and what they should do themselves.

According to recent reports, KWQC put together a guide for households around the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant on the Army's strategy to blow up its lead azide, covering why the Army acted, how it planned to keep the lead contained and what families should do at home. A local outlet wrote the guide for the people who would have to act on it. Each part of the guide deals with the same trade-off from a different side. The Army acted because the drums were decaying, its barriers are designed to limit how much lead escapes, and residents' own precautions have to cover whatever gets past those barriers. None of these steps protects people on its own. Safety on detonation day depends on all three working together.

Sources indicate that the danger at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant has been building for years as old drums of lead azide slowly failed, and this year it reached a point where the Army could no longer leave it alone. Staff first noted deterioration in the barrels in 2009, and the decay since then has left them at risk of an accidental explosion, according to KWQC. The damage covered much of the stockpile, with several dozen degraded barrels found in March, according to the Chicago Sun-Times. The Army's question was no longer whether to act but how soon. Each month of delay meant more decay, not less.

The clearest warning sign was on the outside of the drums, and it made them dangerous to handle at all. Workers noticed crystals on the barrels' exteriors in March 2026. Army Col. Aaron Teller, a munitions emergency response specialist, warns that the slightest rubbing can break those crystals and touch off a chain of explosions, according to the Chicago Sun-Times. That reverses the usual intuition. Neutralizing the material with chemicals or treating it with heat sounds gentler, but in the Army's view workers would first have to lift and move the same drums that friction can set off. Leaving the drums where they sat offered no safe pause either, because doing nothing left the timing of any explosion to chance.

In a recent development, the explosion to fear is not the planned one but one that happens at a moment nobody picks. Army officials name a spontaneous, uncontrolled detonation as the danger. "The longer we wait, the longer the risk accumulates that they might have an accidental explosion," John Goodpaster, a chemistry professor at Indiana University, Indianapolis, mentioned, as reported by the Chicago Sun-Times. If the drums blew up on their own, nobody would choose the weather or warn the neighbors.

Acting now swaps an explosion no one can predict for one the Army controls, though a controlled blast still has to keep its lead from leaving the plant.

The Army is surrounding the blast with barriers because an open-air detonation with nothing around it has already shown how much lead can get away. In an open-air blast in the 1970s, 25% of the lead was released, according to KWQC. That is the one real-world result this strategy has to improve on. This time the Army is using sand barriers, a water wall, foam and soaked soil to cut how much lead dust reaches land beyond the plant. PBS NewsHour reports that the Army plans to use thousands of gallons of water. The Army's approach is to catch the dust at the spot where it forms, before any of it leaves the site.

According to recent reports, even the Army does not aim to keep all of the lead at the plant. "Our goal is to keep as much of it as we can here on our Iowa Army Ammunition plant to prevent it from traveling," Acting Secretary of the Army Adam Telle remarked, as reported by PBS NewsHour. Army officials are only hopeful that these measures will bring the share of lead that escapes below the level of the 1970s blast. The barriers can make a leak smaller, but they cannot seal it. Any lead that gets through becomes a question of where it lands.

In a recent development, when the Army chose the day of the blast, it also chose which neighbors would be downwind. With the detonation set for 9 a.m. Wednesday, PBS NewsHour reports that the plume is projected to move toward Missouri instead of north, as the earlier plan had it. People who were out of the path before are now in it. The Army moved the date for the wind conditions expected on Wednesday, and those conditions are a forecast, not a guarantee. Missouri officials under Gov. Mike Kehoe are only now establishing additional air and water monitoring. On the side that is newly downwind, measurement is catching up to the risk instead of arriving ahead of it.

The lead that gets past the walls and follows the wind becomes a problem for the people living under it.

For people living near the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant, the Army's trade-off became a set of household instructions, and distance from the plant decided which ones applied. Residents within five miles were first told to evacuate and those 5 to 20 miles away to stay sealed indoors, but an alert sent one hour before the 9 a.m. detonation reversed the evacuation advice, according to KWQC. The Associated Press reports that FEMA named Middletown, Augusta and Burlington, Iowa, in that final alert. Families who had been getting ready to leave home had to switch to staying in it, and the people closest to the plant were left with the same instruction as their neighbors farther out.

With evacuation off the table, residents' own homes had to provide the protection that the Army's barriers could not. Sealing a house means cutting it off from outside air through both the windows and the ventilation system. "STAY INDOORS with windows closed to block lead dust," FEMA mentioned in its emergency alert, as reported by The Associated Press. Households were also told to keep their heating and cooling systems moving only the air already inside. Dust was just one of the dangers named for people sheltering in the zone, and noise and vibration were the others. Residents can keep out the dust by closing up the house, but they can only wait out the noise and vibration. The guidance protects people who are under a roof.

A farmer can close up a farmhouse, but not the fields around it. The wind was expected to carry the plume southwest, away from Burlington and the Mississippi River, and farms lie in its projected path, according to The Associated Press. Fields and livestock cannot be moved indoors, and the shelter advice protects the farmer but not the farm.

The Army chose the blast it could schedule and built barriers to hold back what it could, but no one can seal the open ground in the plume's path.
