John Simpson: Ukraine is bowed but still unbroken as freezing weather sets in

Sources indicate that something weird happens when you reach Kyiv after the eight-hour drive from the Polish border: the clock on your mobile phone goes to midnight. It's not really for phones - it seems to be part of a wider strategy to disorient the missiles and drones the Russians are sending over.

Aside from little things like that, though, Kyiv seems fairly normal at first sight. You become well aware there's a war on when sirens begin to wail and people check their phones for the notifications which tell them there's a red alert and missiles are on their way. You can even buy gadgets that show you which direction the drones are coming from, and what type they are.

Industry observers note that the drones and ballistic missiles have been hitting in sizeable numbers over the last couple of weeks. On the morning of Monday 16 November, the battle of the bridges began. Ukraine hit the Kerch bridge which links Russia to illegally occupied Crimea. Then it launched a bigger wave of missiles and drones, damaging the central road-bridge arch. A few hours later, presumably in revenge, Russia fired seven ballistic missiles at Kyiv, disabling two of the nine bridges that cross the River Dnipro.

Recent findings suggest that the war has dragged on for more than four and a half years, killing what some estimate could be 100,000 Ukrainian troops and 18,000 Ukrainian civilians, according to the United Nations, and probably more. Its infrastructure, too, has been battered, as well as its morale. While temperatures drop to freezing at night, and with Ukrainians regularly suffering from no light, heat or water thanks to Russian strikes, can the country survive another difficult winter under bombardment? And what would a peace deal, if one could be struck, look like?

Kyiv is mostly dark at night, thanks to Russia's campaign of targeting Ukraine's electricity generating capability. In the blackness last week, the air raid sirens started wailing across the city, and our hotel's warning announcements came over the loudspeakers. Within a few minutes, we heard the first explosions. I jumped out of bed and stared out of the window into the darkness. After a few seconds two more missiles landed, lighting up the entire sky momentarily in a blaze of white and red. They were uncomfortably close.

Many people prefer to go to the shelters when there's a red alert. Others just stay in bed and hope the law of averages will protect them. It usually does. During one of the heaviest attacks last week, the number of fatalities was in single figures.

Recent findings suggest that I went to the shelter in our hotel one night. The clearly audible thumps and crashes above ground, the permanent lighting, the snores and grunts of other people, and the attentions of the little biting flies that seem to thrive in the warm, crowded shelters made sleep hard.

Kyiv is entering its fourth winter following Russia's full-scale invasion.

Recent findings suggest that to the people who live here in Kyiv, it's frightening, certainly, if a drone or a missile hits close by, but enraging too. Over the years I've been in several cities that were being bombed by high-tech weaponry and the reaction always seems the same, whether it's Lebanon, Iraq, Serbia, or Ukraine: a combination of weariness and fury. In Kyiv the attacks come by night and day, making normal life almost impossible. An uninterrupted night's sleep is a rarity. The morning after the bridges were hit, people in the streets looked tired and worn.

Recent findings suggest that yet although they long for it to stop, there's no sign they want Ukraine to surrender. On the contrary, just about everyone I spoke to felt they had to keep fighting. That's borne out by an opinion poll last month by the KIIS organisation, a Ukrainian market research company, which found that 62% of people were prepared to put up with the war for as long as it took. Of those polled, 79% believed that Ukraine would win the war in the long run, and only 7% thought Russia would win. These aren't the reactions of a country which is being beaten to its knees.

I don't have any doubt that Ukraine can survive the winter. Food is plentiful, though prices are high. Water is a problem, because the regular attacks on the power grid mean that it can't be pumped with any regularity. Kyiv underwent a large transformation under the Soviets, so there are large numbers of high-rise blocks of flats everywhere. Labouring up and down the stairs when there's no power is daunting, especially when you've got to carry quantities of water home.

Recent findings suggest that we came across a small crowd of people in their 20s who had gathered outside a nightclub for a rave at three in the afternoon, because of the 11pm curfew. "It's a way of getting a bit of respite from everything," remarked one young woman. "It makes me feel I can put up with everything," said a man.

It has been revealed that do people still support President Volodymyr Zelensky? My team and I met an elderly woman wandering around Independence Square in the centre of Kyiv holding a photo of her son, who was missing on the Eastern front. She blamed Zelensky; if he didn't insist on carrying on the war, she believed, her son would still be alive. It isn't a response most people seem to share. The same polling organisation, KIIS, found that Zelensky's trust ratings hover around 54% to 57% â way below the 90% he was registering in the early days of the war in 2022, but not too bad for a man who's been in power now for more than seven years.

Zelensky's ratings have dipped considerably.

It has been revealed that what comes across all the time in Kyiv is an absolute loathing of Russia. Putin reportedly launched his invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 because his intelligence chiefs assured him that Ukrainians longed to rejoin the Motherland.

According to recent reports, russian missiles have hit hospitals, clinics and schools. In many well-attested cases the Russians have used the "double-tap" method â sending in one missile, then waiting till the medics and rescuers gather before sending in another. At least 20,000 Ukrainian children have been abducted and taken to Russia, according to Ukraine's Ministry of Justice. In late March 2022, when the Ukrainians recaptured the satellite town of Bucha, on the outskirts of Kyiv, they found shocking evidence of torture and murder. Ukrainian soldiers call their Russian enemies Orcs, after the sub-human brutes in The Lord of the Rings. A country that has endured this kind of thing doesn't promptly forgive and forget. Russia denies that it targets civilians or civilian infrastructure.

Ukraine has never lost its own identity, even during the Soviet period. Some of Ukraine's culture was absorbed by Russia â "They call borsch [a famously delicious soup made of beetroot] theirs, but they got it from us," a waiter in a cafÃ© here told me proudly. After the Soviet Union collapsed in 1991, Ukraine overwhelmingly voted for independence. The war has brought a fierce renewal of this desire to be completely separated from Russia.

Recent findings suggest that it's not my usual beat, but my team and I went to film on the set of MasterChef Ukraine, at the moment one of the country's favourite television programmes. The studio was borrowed; the original one had been bombed. The executive producer had his phone in his hand the whole time, and was constantly glancing at it. The moment a red alert indicated, it was his duty to shepherd everyone off the set to a nearby shelter. "People see our cuisine as a way of expressing their national identity," he mentioned. It's one of the reasons the programme is so popular.

According to recent reports, there are other ways in which people express their Ukrainian national identity under the bombs. There has been a boom for books being written and published about Ukraine and Ukrainian identity. Ukrainian and Russian are close as languages, though vocabulary and pronunciation differ. Nowadays the differences are emphasised. "Woe betide a writer who uses Russian spellings," a Ukrainian novelist told me.

The war will end at some point; wars always do. But there has to be a clear winner before anyone backs down, and a lasting peace can be negotiated. US President Donald Trump and his advisers, people like Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff, with their business backgrounds, have approached the situation as though it's a form of mergers and acquisitions: make a deal attractive enough, and the other side will accept it.

Industry observers note that so, last week, Washington was rumoured to be offering a very Trumpian deal, that Ukraine should accept the loss of the land in the east which the Russians now control, and in return it was rumoured it could join Nato. It was a non-starter, because Russia would never accept it. The sweetener, in this US plan, was that the West's sanctions against Russia would be lifted, and both sides would benefit from unrestricted trading once again.

But neither side sees this as being about trade and profit. For Russia, it's about the dangers of being humiliated if it doesn't win. President Vladimir Putin has made it his personal war. By illegally taking Crimea from Ukraine in 2014, he made himself very popular in Russia. If he doesn't win this second stage of a 12-year campaign against Ukraine, the consequences for him personally could be disastrous. He's already had to face down one attempt against his rule: his former associate Yevgeny Prigozhin's march on Moscow in June 2023. In a much-quoted remark, reported last August, Putin is stated to have confided to his associates that if he didn't win the war "they'll hang me". My sense is he's not concerned about trade with the West and the profit that will bring, so much as about his personal survival.

Industry observers note that similarly, Ukrainians aren't interested in the precise details that have leaked out of the various negotiations between the Americans and Russians. Their contempt for the US is immense; "they just failed us," remarked one Ukrainian I spoke to.

And they don't believe Russia will stick to any future peace agreement anyway. "They signed a treaty to accept our borders, and Putin broke it," a young man told me as he sat hunched over his laptop in a bookshop. "The only solution is to defeat them.".

Tens of thousands of soldiers have died since the outbreak of war.

In a recent development, other people talked about the need to get huge reparations from Russia for the terrible destruction it has visited on their country. According to one estimate, reconstruction and recovery will cost more than $500bn (Â£383bn), an unthinkably huge amount for a country whose GDP was $200bn before the war. President Trump and his advisers don't seem to have addressed any of these challenges in their negotiations.

In a recent development, here in Kyiv, peace doesn't seem at all close. Russia's efforts to make life intolerable for Ukraine's citizens are certainly having a major effect. The war is often frightening for ordinary people, and it can be appallingly destructive of property and lives. But short of hugely escalating by attacking Ukraine with nuclear weapons, which is something China and the US are both thought to have warned him not to do, Putin can't seem to strike the knock-out blow against Ukraine that he needs.

According to recent reports, at present it seems to people in Ukraine that the war will never come to an end. Yet it will, at some point: and then the crucial requirement will be to work out ways to stop Russia coming back for another round. One way would be if Ukraine joins Nato, but that's something Russia would only accept if it had been comprehensively defeated. Ukrainians might think that can happen, but most Western onlookers are more sceptical.

Sources indicate that the daily and nightly sirens, the explosions of missiles and drones, the shortages and the fear and the darkness are all terrible. But they aren't enough to destroy Ukraine's will to continue fighting.
