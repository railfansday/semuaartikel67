It’s Time to Talk About “Duty Sex”

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

There are certain things that belong on your chore list: Doing laundry, washing dishes, taking the trash out, scrubbing the toilet. Sex, as you can see, is not one of those things—and it shouldn’t be. And yet, “duty sex” has become a pretty recognizable term in today’s relationships.

Industry observers note that duty sex typically stems from good intentions, Marisa Derr, LMFT explains. This happens when one partner wants to keep the peace or protect the relationship. It’s when you have sex because for some reason or another, you feel like you have to—not because you’re worried for your safety, but you believe that the success of your relationship lies on “fulfilling” some sexual duty.

This isn’t just an unnecessary pressure that can be demoralizing, it’s also just super unsexy.

“The problem is that sex out of obligation teaches the body that intimacy is another task, and desire fades even more,” she notes. “Over time, the partner initiating feels rejected, and the partner saying yes feels used, even though neither one wants that.”.

In a recent development, there’s an important distinction between duty sex and what we call “willing consent.” Willing consent is when you’re not necessarily feeling desire for your partner in the moment, but you consent to a sexual interaction with them because you’re open to desire showing up. It’s like when you’re not feeling particularly hungry, but you know that you really enjoy a certain meal so you go out to eat at the spot anyway. Duty sex is born from obligation, rather than curiosity—like eating that weird casserole your aunt brings on Thanksgiving when you really don’t want to, but do anyway because you prefer to not ruffle any feathers.

When did sex with our partners become that weird casserole or equivalent to scrubbing the toilet?

According to a recent study in Evolutionary Psychology, duty sex can be rooted in fear: Fear that a partner will cheat, fear that one could be less attractive than their partners and need to somehow “make up for it,” and fear that a partner will lose interest in them.

And while we typically assign duty sex as something that women mostly do—particularly because of the pressures to perform that disproportionately affect women—the study showed that men were having duty sex at similar rates as women. Though, more research is needed to find out if there is a difference between why men have duty sex and why women do it.

The thing that most people having duty sex had in common was that they were in long-term relationships.

According to recent reports, “Couples can start worrying about how often they're having sex, rather than whether they're actually enjoying it,” sex educator Anna Richards tells Cosmopolitan. “But intimacy shouldn't be about meeting a target or keeping score. It should be something you both genuinely want.”.

It has been revealed that sex should be about feeling pleasure and connecting with your partner in a fun, sexy, and intimate way. But for some couples, somewhere in the midst of all of the responsibilities of everyday life, sexual desire was put on the back burner. And instead of working to get it back, people are essentially forcing themselves to have duty sex to maintain their relationships. We need a serious overhaul of partnered sex!

In a recent development, “You can't demand desire, but you can create an environment where it has room to grow,” Richard indicates. Think about what the study said about why people have duty sex: they’re afraid of losing their partner. And one of the ways to bridge this gap of insecurity is to foster a relationship that comes with reassurance. If you’re the one finding yourself having duty sex, communicate with your partner about the pressure you feel. Talking about your needs—whether they come in the form of emotional security, verbal affirmations, or quality time—can help your partner learn how to help you foster desire. And if you’re the partner that’s noticing your S.O. initiate sex just to check it off a list, maybe it’s time to think about whether you’re reassuring them enough in the relationship. Do you create an environment of trust and comfort? Are you caring? Do you help them with their daily tasks?

One of the best ways to remove sex off your to-do list and onto your want-to-do list is to take it off your list completely. That may sound counterintuitive, but Richards says it helps alleviate the pressure.

According to recent reports, “Intimacy doesn't always have to lead to the bedroom,” she says. Flirting, spending quality time together, being affectionate, making each other feel appreciated, talking about what you enjoy—these can all help rebuild a connection between you and your partner.

“Sometimes removing the expectation is exactly what allows desire to return naturally,” she indicates. You also need to make sure that your relationship is a place where saying “no” feels safe.

“A yes only means something when a no is fully okay,” Derr explains. “Pressure, sulking, or keeping score kills desire.”.

Sources indicate that it’s also about the little things. Saying “thank you” when they do something nice, touching each other throughout the day, complimenting each other, and just being a good partner can all lend themselves into reigniting desire.

Recent findings suggest that life is way too short to be having sex because you feel like you have to. Plus, any healthy and happy relationship would not foster an environment where sex feels like a duty in the first place. Yes, making a relationship work longterm does take work and effort—but that doesn’t mean that every aspect of your relationship should feel like work. The parts that are meant to be fueled by fun and curiosity (like sex) should remain fun. And the second sex starts feeling like a chore, it may be time to clear your checklist and make reconnecting your first priority.
