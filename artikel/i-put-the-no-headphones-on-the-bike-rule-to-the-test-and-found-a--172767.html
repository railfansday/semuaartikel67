Sources indicate that many of us have been through a bad experience or two thanks to trail people listening to music on a ride. Sometimes it’s not even a dangerous situation—I can think of more than a few situations where I spooked a fellow rider listening to music while they pedal along in their own little bubble. It’s not just with cyclists. I’ve rolled up on cyclists, hikers, and trail runners alike who are surprised by my presence—even after ringing my bell, calling on your left numerous times, and letting my freehub ratchet away to try and get their attention.

However, not all who listen to music on the trail are this oblivious. I’ve been riding for close to two decades and I’ve spent a lot of time in the saddle both listening to music and not. There are times popping in a headphone makes sense and times when it isn’t the right call. But there is a right and wrong way to do it, even on those rides I consider to be the right times.

This year I’ve tried out different types of headphones to see what works, what doesn’t, and ways to avoid letting a little music ruin someone else’s ride.

Last year Matt Phillips, our Senior Test Editor at Bicycling, wrote a story about his almost literal run-in with another cyclist on the trail. “Bright red Beats earbuds. Music blasting so loud I could hear the song,” said Phillips about the rider who almost collided with him head-on. Phillips goes on to point out that riding with headphones can put people in a bubble. Riding in a way that can cause safety problems on the trail for yourself and other users is irresponsible, but there’s a better way to listen to music and navigate these shared spaces.

Sources indicate that I’ve been using traditional headphones for years—typically riding with only one headphone in while I ride. This year I’ve tried a few different bone-conducting headphones and I thought they were a huge step in the right direction. They give listeners a better ability to be aware of their surroundings while listening to music compared to traditional earbuds. And save those bulky headphones, like the Bose QuietComfort, for your trainer rides.

I’ve been using the Shokz Open Run 2 headphones for about six months now and I love them for riding outside. The headphones allow me to hear my surroundings and still get a musical boost when I need it. While I’ve been using these headphones, I typically only wear one earbud and let the other side just hang around my neck.

The Open Run headphones are not individual earbuds like common AirPods—the Open Run 2 has a band that connects the left and right, so if you want to only have one ear listening, the other side just hangs out by your neck. Bone-conducting headphones were a step in the right direction to be more aware while jamming out on the bike, but recently I’ve gotten to try a different style of headphones that may have solved this problem once and for all.

Of all the headphones I’ve tried for riding outside, my two current top choices go to the Bose Sport Open Earbuds and the Shokz Open Dots 2. Both of these headphones use what’s called air conduction technology. They essentially sit just outside your ear and direct the sound into your ear through the air. This style of headphone gives the situational awareness of bone conduction headphones, but without pressing on your outer ear to pump sound through.

Recent findings suggest that my initial concern was how secure can this style of headphone really be? When I first clipped them on my ears, I didn’t think they would stay on during a gravel ride, let alone bouncing around on chunky singletrack.

After testing both of them in situations where I was trying to bounce or knock them out I was pleasantly surprised—I still have two complete pairs of earbuds. Mountain biking with a half-shell and full-face helmet, fast road descents, bouncing off trees on the gravel bike, and even downhill trail runs on technical trails couldn’t bounce these out of my ears. These headphones make it easy to have one headphone in and maintain a healthy awareness of other trail users.

Recent findings suggest that if I had to pick between the Bose Sport Open Earbuds and the Shokz Open Dots 2 I would choose the Bose Sport Open Earbuds. On the Bose, the button to control the headphones was easy to use even with gloves on and when the headphones shifted a little on my ear the sound quality remained high. The Shokz Open Dots are a good pair of headphones, but the controls on the Shokz Open Dots 2 are a bit harder to use while wearing gloves. I thought the sound quality was a notch better in the Bose Open Earbuds compared to the Shokz Open Dots too. If you’re someone who likes to listen to music while you’re in the saddle, then check out these Bose Sport Open Earbuds and the Shokz Open Dots 2.
