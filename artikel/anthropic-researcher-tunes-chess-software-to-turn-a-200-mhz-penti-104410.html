Anthropic researcher tunes chess software to turn a 200 MHz Pentium Pro into a behemoth that would handily defeat Kasparov and Carlsen

Recent findings suggest that readers with back pain probably recall the much-discussed 1996 chess challenge, wherein the world champion Gerry Kasparov faced off against IBM's Deep Blue chess-optimized computer. The nascent clanker lost the match with a 4-2 total, but still stunned the world by being the first piece of electric sand to win a single game against a grandmaster. Modern chess engines will utterly destroy any human, but Anthropic engineer Nicholas Carlini wondered if granting old hardware fresh bytes would give the machine a better chance. Spoiler: yes, indeed it would.

To understand the task, here's a quick primer on chess scoreboards. A player is rated worldwide with an Elo system (first devised for chess), wherein they lose or gain points in proportion to the difference to their opponent's rating — meaning that winning a match against Magnus Carlsen would net you a lot of points versus defeating me, who only learned about the en passant rule a handful of years ago.

Sources indicate that at his prime, Kasparov ranked about 2800 points. Given that Deep Blue eventually barely won against him in a subsequent rematch, that is a good starting point for any. Contemporary chess engines like Stockfish have an estimated Elo of about 3,300, far higher than any human — guaranteeing it'll win most matches against a human.

Nicholas Carlini got hold of a 1995-era machine, packing a Pentium Pro CPU with all of its 200 MHz of clock speed, and 64 MB of RAM. For his first experiment, he grabbed Crafty, a 1996 chess engine, and measured how well it would do. It came in at 2,000 Elo with 1 second allotted, and 2,400 @ 10s. Running that software on a Ryzen 7 resulted in a 2,600 rating — good, but not enough.

Recent findings suggest that since Crafty's source code is available, he got Claude to mod it with modern features, and eventually reached a ceiling of 3,000 Elo @ 10s per move. That's quite an impressive feat already, especially considering that the lone Pentium Pro represented but a small fraction of the hardware that Deep Blue had. An Elo delta of +200 means this iteration would defeat Kasparov three out of every four games, while a +400 delta means a victory nine out of ten times.

Sources indicate that carlini then pulled out the substantial gun: Stockfish. He had to ask Claude to modify it to use only 32-bit arithmetic instead of 64-bit, and limit its workspace to the measly 64 MB of RAM, minus whatever the system harness took. Right off the bat, the figures were revealing: 2,500 @ 1s, and 3000 @ 10s.

The engineer performed a few optimization passes, and eventually got 2,850 @ 1s; 3,100 @ 10s; and almost 3,200 @ 30s — enough to put every human in their place, especially in a grandmaster tournament setting where there's no fixed time per move.
