Every Medicaid managed care initiative has them.They’re the members who remain outside the reach of traditional outreach and care management after the calls, texts, letters and emails haven’t worked. Their phone numbers may be disconnected, their addresses outdated. They’ve been targeted with different outreach strategies, even assigned to outside vendors that specialize in finding hard-to-reach members—and still remain disconnected.And they’re often among the members aims most need to reach.They’re Medicaid’s hardest-to-find, hardest-to-engage members: people with complex medical and behavioral health needs, often compounded by unstable housing, food insecurity, transportation barriers and other circumstances that make navigating healthcare extraordinarily difficult. Many are also among a plan’s highest-cost members, cycling through emergency departments and hospitals while remaining disconnected from care that could help stabilize their health.Plans have struggled for years to reach this population. What makes the challenge more urgent today is growing financial pressure on Medicaid plans to manage costs and demonstrate that every public healthcare dollar is yielding measurable results — and the recognition that some of the greatest opportunities to reduce avoidable spending and improve quality lies with high-risk, high-cost members traditional approaches still aren’t reaching.An estimated 4–8% of a managed healthcare plan’s Medicaid membership may fall into this hardest-to-engage cohort, driving an estimated 35-50% of an MCO’s total medical spend. he opportunity is to reach members who have remained disconnected and reduce the costly utilization that can occur when successful engagement never happens.When Traditional Outreach Has Run Its CourseManaged healthcare plans have built sophisticated outreach and care-management capabilities across multiple channels, supported by digital tools, data analytics and centralized teams. Many have changed tactics and brought in outside vendors. Those approaches work for most members.But the hardest-to-reach members are still left behind.For this population, simply finding the member is often the biggest challenge. Someone moving between temporary housing, shelters, encampments or other locations may be impossible to locate using the information a plan has on file. Finding that person may require combining data and technology with local knowledge, community relationships and people on the ground.And finding the member is only the beginning.A member may not trust the person who finds them. Housing, food, transportation or an immediate behavioral health need may matter far more at that moment than making a primary care appointment. Language differences and difficult experiences with the healthcare system can create additional barriers.That’s why the distinction between contact and engagement matters. Contact means reaching someone. Engagement means listening, building trust, addressing barriers and helping the member take the next step.What It Takes to Truly Engage MembersTechnology can help determine where to look, prioritize the next best action and make field teams more efficient. But technology doesn’t create trust. This is ultimately a human effort—technology-enabled but led by people.

Recent findings suggest that while finding members is often the hardest part, it’s only the beginning. What happens next is what can change outcomes.Experience with hardest-to-reach Medicaid populations has shown 25–40% reductions in ED utilization and 35–55% reductions in inpatient utilization after members were located and engaged, barriers addressed, and ongoing follow-through provided.*For intends, that’s where the financial opportunity lies. Reaching high-risk, high-cost members and helping them reconnect to care creates an opportunity to reduce avoidable ED visits and hospitalizations—and the costs that come with them.At a time when every avoidable ED visit and hospitalization matters, reaching and serving these members is more crucial than ever. It’s an opportunity to improve care while addressing a significant source of avoidable healthcare spending.
