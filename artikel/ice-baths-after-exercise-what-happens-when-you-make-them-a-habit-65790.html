Ice baths after exercise: What happens when you make them a habit?

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Ice baths may ease soreness after a hard workout, but regular cold plunges after lifting may limit gains in muscle size and strength.

An ice bath after a hard workout may ease muscle soreness. But taking one after every lifting session may make it harder to build muscle and strength over time.

A major review led by Edith Cowan University (ECU) found it can blunt long-term strength and muscle gains.

According to recent reports, researchers analyzed 15 published systematic reviews and meta-analyses. Together they covered 161 studies and more than 3,200 participants.

In a recent development, the takeaway is simple. Athletes, coaches, and fitness fans should be strategic, not habitual, with ice baths.

“Cold-water immersion is not a one-size-fits-all recovery tool,” said lead author Erfan Berjisian, a PhD student at ECU.

“Its benefits depend heavily on the athlete’s goals, the type of exercise completed, and when the next performance session is scheduled.”.

The review found ice baths are particularly good at reducing muscle soreness. They also lower markers of muscle damage and they help athletes feel recovered faster.

Muscle soreness dropped consistently for up to four days after exercise. Markers of muscle damage were lower between 24 and 72 hours after an ice bath.

Industry observers note that the review also noted a boost in heart rate variability right after exercise. That’s a sign the body is starting to recover.

For athletes who need to compete again soon, the benefits depend on what they’ll be doing and how much time they have to recover.

“Cold-water immersion appears especially useful when recovery time is limited, such as during tournaments, congested competition schedules or repeated high-intensity training sessions,” Berjisian said.

Sources indicate that researchers found endurance performance improved within the first hour after an ice bath. Power and jump performance often improved 24 to 72 hours later.

However, explosive performance actually dropped in the hours right after an ice bath.

That likely happens due to the temporary cooling effect on muscles.

“If athletes need to perform high-speed or explosive movements soon after cooling, cold-water immersion may be counterproductive unless sufficient re-warming occurs,” noted co-author Kristina Kendall from ECU.

Perhaps the most significant finding was about repeated use.

Athletes who constantly used cold-water immersion after resistance training saw smaller improvements in strength, muscle size, and power.

The evidence suggests chronic use may blunt some of the body’s normal muscle-building responses to training.

It has been revealed that the review also found a detail worth noting. The effect on strength may depend on the protocol.

Immersing just the exercised limb was linked to reduced gains. Whole-body immersion showed no clear detrimental effect.

For endurance athletes, it  was a different story. The review found no evidence that regular cold-water immersion harmed aerobic fitness or endurance adaptations.

The results suggest that cold-water immersion at 50–59°F (10–15°C) for 10–15 minutes generally helped with post-workout recovery.

Colder water, at 41–50°F (5–10°C) for the same duration, may help restore jumping performance and lower markers of muscle damage.

Both intermittent and continuous protocols worked, with no clear winner between them.

Sources indicate that the authors also assessed the quality of that evidence. They rated it low to very low.

Most of the samples were male, which limits how well the findings apply to women.

Industry observers note that the authors also flagged some overlap between the reviews, especially the ones on strength and muscle growth. Those conclusions rest on a fairly concentrated group of trials.

In a recent development, the review calls for more work on women and on different training types. It also wants research that tracks athletes over longer periods.

Ice baths may ease soreness when athletes have limited time to recover, and their effects on performance vary with the activity and the timing.

But the science notes athletes should stop treating them as a cure-all.

They should be more cautious when the goal is building strength and muscle mass.

Industry observers note that when races or matches are only a day or two apart, an ice bath may ease soreness before the next event. That can matter during a tournament, when athletes have little time to recover between rounds.

If your goal is to build muscle or get stronger, consider saving the cold plunge for competition days.

Making it a routine after lifting may limit the gains you’re training for.

The study is published in the journal Sports Medicine – Open.
