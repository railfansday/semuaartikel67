Industry News: Getting rid of antibiotic resistance: Challenges and possible solutions

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that "The threat is immense," said Jean Pierre Nyemazi, acting director of the World Health Organization's department of antimicrobial resistance, during a virtual press conference  recently. "An estimated cumulative number of 39 million deaths directly attributable to AMR [antimicrobial resistance] would occur in the next 25 years.".

It has been revealed that it was, however, the stated goal of World Health Organization, or WHO, member statesto reduce the mortality rate caused by AMR by 10% until 2030, Nyemazi added. "When we see the trend, the investments, the action, we don't think we are on the right track.".

A world without effective antibiotics would be like traveling back to the days in which even the smallest infection was life-threatening, mentioned Can Imirzalioglu, director of the Institute of Medical Microbiology at Justus Liebig University Giessen. "If antibiotics were no longer effective, modern medicine as we know it today would no longer be possible at all.".

It has been revealed that imirzalioglu is not only an expert on antibiotic resistance but also on "One Health," a concept adopted in 2004 by experts in human medicine, veterinary medicine and nature conservation. It recognizes that the health of humans, animals and the environment is closely interconnected.

After all, antibiotics and antimicrobial-resistant germs are not just a problem in hospitals. They can also be found in animal shelters, in animal products, in waste water, in fertilizers and in plants. "One Health tries to keep an eye on all of this," Imirzalioglu remarked.

Industry observers note that for hospitals and doctors' offices this means that antibiotics must be used as selectively and purposefully as possible. To this end, research is focused primarily on developing better and faster diagnostic methods.

Industry observers note that in Germany, antibiotics were frequently prescribed against respiratory or urinary tract infections, Imirzalioglu noted. But antibiotics are only effective against bacteria. If a virus causes the infection, they are ineffective.

Nonetheless, every antibiotics dose has consequences: it damages our gut microbiome. This consists of billions of micro-organisms, primarily bacteria, which are essential for digestion, metabolism and the immune system. "If we damage the [bacteria], resistant pathogens can spread and take their place," Imirzalioglu remarked.

Another problem was that such a spreading of resistant pathogens in the gut often went unnoticed and without causing symptoms, he added.

So people became "carriers of resistant pathogens, which they, in turn, release back into their environment.".

In a recent development, in this way, resistant pathogens make their way out of hospitals via toilets and sinks into wastewater and, from there, into the Baltic Sea, where they are ingested by wild birds. This pathway was traced in a study by Katharina Schaufler, a veterinarian and microbiologist at the Helmholtz Institute for One Health (HIOH).

Antibiotics have not only been used in humans on a large scale over a long time, excessive use in veterinary medicine has also led to resistance to nearly all available antibiotics, Schaufler said.

In 2006, an EU-wide ban on the use of antibiotics as growth promoters in animal feedcame into effect. Since then, antibiotics may only be applied as part of veterinary treatment.

According to recent reports, the problem is that due to factory farming the use of antibiotics for medical purposes is a permanent necessity. The mere fact that animals live together in confined spaces causes infections to spread rapidly.

Lack of space as well as boredom lead the animals to injure each other and this happens in an environment where, in Schaufler's words, "there's already a high level of bacterial pressure.".

Recent findings suggest that through fertilizers, antibiotic-resistant pathogens enter the environment. "It has been proven that, for example, resistant bacteria can still be found on lettuce heads," the veterinarian said. Resistant bacteria were also found time and again on meat.

In a recent development, national solutions are also falling short. In many countries, antibiotics are still a standard additive in animal feed, Schaufler remarked. And in such countries, antibiotics could be obtained in drugstores without prescriptions, Imirzalioglu added. Both say that all of this makes the global fight against antibiotic resistance more difficult.

Political will is of the essence when it comes to combating antibiotic resistance. Imirzalioglu works at Guardian, a project funded by the German Federal Ministry of Research, Technology and Space.

The aim of the project is to prevent outbreaks of diseases caused by resistant bacteria by monitoring and analyzing wastewater in the German state of Hesse.

At EU level, Imirzalioglu is also an advisor at the Antimicrobial Resistance One Health Network. He sums up his experience with policymakers: "The experts and working groups have great ideas, and then these are often scaled back or stifled during the initial stages.".

Perhaps the lack of political will so far is one of the reasons why the WHO's Nyemazi is issuing an appeal to every individual: Preventing antibiotic resistance means preventing infections, through hygiene, regular vaccinations and doctor's appointments as well as taking medications in accordance with prescriptions.
