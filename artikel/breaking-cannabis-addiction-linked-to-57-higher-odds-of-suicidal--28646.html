Breaking: Cannabis addiction linked to 57% higher odds of suicidal thoughts

It has been revealed that as marijuana becomes more widely available and accepted, researchers are uncovering notable questions about its effects on mental health. A major upcoming study suggests that people who struggle to control their cannabis use face a substantially greater likelihood of experiencing suicidal thoughts.

In a recent development, published in the Journal of Studies on Alcohol and Drugs, the research found that adults with cannabis use disorder had 57% higher odds of reporting suicidal thoughts compared with those without the condition. Despite major shifts in marijuana laws and public attitudes, this troubling association remained remarkably consistent over a decade.

In a recent development, the findings come from an analysis of more than 415,000 American adults who participated in the National Survey on Drug Use and Health. Researchers examined data collected between 2014 and 2023, a period when cannabis became increasingly accessible across the United States.

"What surprised me most was not that cannabis use disorder was associated with suicidal thoughts, but that this association did not appear to strengthen over time," noted lead author Tosin Philip Oyetunji, MPH, a doctoral student in public health at the University of California, Merced. "[Yet,] it remains an notable mental health concern that deserves attention.".

Cannabis and Suicidal Thoughts Show a Persistent Link.

In a recent development, the connection between cannabis use disorder and suicidal thinking was observed across different age groups, sexes, and racial and ethnic backgrounds. However, women and younger adults demonstrated particularly elevated risks.

Importantly, the association remained even after researchers accounted for depression, psychological distress, and other factors that could influence mental health outcomes.

According to recent reports, the consistency of the results was especially notable given how dramatically the cannabis landscape has changed. Recreational marijuana is now legal in half of U.S. states, and cannabis products have become increasingly potent. These developments have raised concerns among public health experts about whether broader access might contribute to worsening mental health.

Yet the study offers some reassurance on that particular question. Sidra Goldman-Mellor, PhD, a professor of public health at UC Merced and the study's senior author, noted that the association between cannabis use disorder and suicidal thoughts did not become stronger during the 10 years examined.

"Suicidal ideation does not seem to have increased among people with cannabis use disorder, despite all this legal and social change," explained Goldman-Mellor. Yet, "people with cannabis use disorder are still at a very substantially increased likelihood of experiencing serious suicidal thoughts, and that's not something that is widely understood.".

Why Cannabis Use Disorder Matters.

The researchers stress that these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that everyone who uses marijuana faces the same elevated risk.

The study focused specifically on cannabis use disorder, a condition in which people have difficulty controlling their marijuana consumption even when it causes problems in their lives. Someone with the disorder may continue using cannabis despite harmful consequences or repeated attempts to cut back.

This distinction is vital because problematic cannabis use differs from occasional or otherwise nonproblematic consumption.

According to recent reports, the findings also do not establish that cannabis use disorder directly causes suicidal thoughts. Because the research identified an association rather than a cause-and-effect relationship, it cannot determine whether problematic cannabis use contributes to suicidal thinking, whether underlying mental health difficulties contribute to both conditions, or whether other factors help explain the connection.

Nevertheless, the results point to a significant mental health concern that deserves greater recognition, particularly among people seeking help for problematic cannabis use.

Researchers Urge Greater Mental Health Screening.

One practical implication is that health care professionals should look beyond cannabis consumption itself when evaluating patients with cannabis use disorder.

Rather than focusing exclusively on depression or substance use symptoms, clinicians may benefit from asking patients directly about suicidal thoughts as part of a broader mental health assessment. This could help identify people who need additional care but whose struggles might otherwise remain unnoticed.

In a recent development, "Someone seeking help for cannabis use disorder may also be experiencing suicidal thoughts that are not immediately apparent," said Oyetunji. "The goal is not to assume that everyone with cannabis use disorder is suicidal but to identify those who may need additional assessment, treatment, or support.".

The study arrives at a time when marijuana is increasingly viewed as a relatively low-risk substance. Public perceptions have shifted considerably, and negative attitudes toward cannabis have declined alongside expanding legalization and social acceptance.

However, these changes have not eliminated the mental health challenges associated with cannabis use disorder. Even though the strength of the association with suicidal thoughts did not increase over the study period, the elevated odds remained substantial.

The researchers suggest that health care providers, policymakers, and community organizations can play an vital role by improving awareness, identifying vulnerable individuals, and making appropriate mental health support more accessible.

As attitudes toward marijuana continue to evolve, the findings serve as a reminder that the needs of people experiencing problematic cannabis use should remain a public health priority.

Said Oyetunji, "As cannabis becomes more accessible and socially accepted, we should ensure that the people struggling with its use are not overlooked.".
