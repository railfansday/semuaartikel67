📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Detection tools are getting smarter, but most deepfake scams still work because the person on the other end didn’t know what to check. That is a gap only awareness can close.

In January 2024, a finance employee at Arup’s Hong Kong office received an email from the company’s UK-based chief financial officer asking for a confidential transaction. He was suspicious. Then he was invited to a video call with the CFO and several colleagues he recognized. The faces looked right and the voices sounded right, so he went ahead. He made 15 transfers worth about $25.6 million. Every other person on that call was a deepfake.

It has been revealed that the incident is often quoted as proof of how convincing deepfakes have become. That is true. But one detail deserves more attention. The employee doubted the request at first. His instinct was working. What he didn’t have was the knowledge that a live group video call could be faked, or a clear rule on what to do when a request from the top feels wrong.

This is where most deepfake defense looks fall short. Companies are investing in detection software and platforms are adding labels, but very little is being done to help everyday customers understand what AI can do today. That understanding, or AI literacy, is the missing layer.

In a recent development, for years, the standard advice has been to look for giveaways – odd blinking, lips that don’t match the words, blurry edges around the face. These tips made sense when deepfakes were crude. They make much less sense now.

In a recent development, in 2025, identity verification firm iProov indicated 2,000 people in the UK and US a mix of real and AI-generated images and videos. Only 0.1% of them got every answer right, even though they had been told in advance to look out for fakes. Yet around 60% of the participants felt confident about their ability to tell the difference.

Low accuracy combined with high confidence is the real risk here. Teaching people a checklist of visual flaws may simply make them feel safer than they actually are.

Detection tools are improving, and they have an crucial role to play. However, they learn from yesterday’s fakes while upcoming generation tools keep arriving. Labels and watermarks help as well, but they can be removed. Moreover, many scams never pass through a platform that scans content. They arrive as a phone call, a voice note on a messaging app or a private video meeting.

Sources indicate that lawmakers are responding too. The TAKE IT DOWN Act, signed in May 2025, makes it a federal crime to publish non-consensual intimate images, including AI-generated ones, and gives platforms 48 hours to remove them once a victim reports them. Earlier, the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) ruled that AI-generated voices in robocalls fall under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act, opening the door to fines and lawsuits. These are meaningful steps. But laws act after the harm is done. They can’t stop a cloned voice that calls you at midnight pretending to be a family member, and they can’t help you decide in that moment whether to trust it.

Recent findings suggest that meanwhile, the financial stakes keep rising. Deloitte estimates that generative AI could push fraud losses in the US to $40 billion by 2027, up from $12.3 billion in 2023. McAfee’s 2026 State of the Scamiverse report adds that Americans see an average of three deepfakes a day, and one in three have lost money to a scam, with an average loss of $1,160.

Sources indicate that scammers are targeting people’s trust and everyday habits. That is why our defense needs to reach people as well.

AI literacy, on the other hand, goes wherever people go, whether it is a late-night phone call, a family group chat or a video meeting on a personal laptop. It is also one of the most affordable defenses an organization can build.

We have spent years teaching people not to click on suspicious links. Deepfakes need the same steady effort, with a upcoming message: what you see and hear online may not be real, and taking a minute to verify is simply good practice.
