Hikers can once again access the southern terminus of the Continental Divide Trail, more than two weeks after border wall construction closed down the southernmost 1.1 miles of the trail, the Continental Divide Trail Coalition (CDTC) says. But with the official end of the route now trapped between two sections of steel barrier, the obelisk that marks the terminus will need to be moved.

The southern mile of the trail had been closed since late October, when crews began widening the road that runs along the border fence and installing a second length of wall. Since 2025, that stretch has fallen inside the Fort Huachuca Fresh Mexico National Defense Area, created when the Trump administration transferred 110,000 acres administered by the Bureau of Land Management to military control.

Sources indicate that on Friday, the CDTC stated it had received confirmation that hikers would once again be able to access the terminus. As before the closure, anyone entering the NDA needs to apply online for a permit from Fort Huachuca and undergo a background check; additionally, hikers who aren’t U.S. citizens need to be chaperoned by a permitted hiker who is.

“This closure came at a critical time for southbound hikers,” the CDTC said in a statement. “Completing the 3,100-mile trail is a monumental achievement, and the construction activities at the border created dynamic changes to the permitted NDA access in place during the past year.”.

Industry observers note that however, the construction has created a latest issue: The Crazy Cook monument—the stone obelisk that marks the official end of the Continental Divide—is now sandwiched between the two steel barriers that make up the under-construction border wall. In an email, Audra Labert, the CDTC’s director of communications, stated that while the organization had received reports that hikers had still been able to reach the monument by passing through the construction area, that arrangement is likely temporary.

It has been revealed that “Authorities are now confirming looks to move the obelisk outside of the barrier walls for hikers to access, and passages between the barriers will eventually close,” Labert wrote. While it’s not clear when that could happen, the CDTC warned that the government would likely close the terminus to hikers again while it relocated the monument.

Industry observers note that construction on the border wall has complicated thru-hikers’ ability to access the southern termini of several long trails over the past year. In addition to the CDT, the federal government permanently closed access to the Arizona Trail’s southern terminus last November, a move that Matthew Nelson, the executive director of the Arizona Trail Association told Backpacker was “nothing less than a desecration.” In addition, authorities briefly banned Pacific Crest Trail hikers from touching the border wall this spring before abruptly reversing course. The under-construction barrier continues to figure in access and conservation fights along the southern border, with the Supreme Court recently ruling that the administration could continue construction of a upcoming stretch of wall in Substantial Bend National Park.
