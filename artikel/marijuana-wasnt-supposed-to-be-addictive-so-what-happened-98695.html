Marijuana Wasn't Supposed to Be Addictive, So What Happened?

Updated November 23, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk.

In Part 1, I explained how medicinal marijuana became a multibillion-dollar business.

According to recent reports, after more than 50 years studying addiction, from heroin and cocaine to nicotine, alcohol, and food, I have learned that assumptions many people make, including experts, are often wrong. Marijuana (cannabis) provides yet another example.

It has been revealed that for decades, marijuana was considered nonaddictive. The reasoning was simple. Heroin produced unmistakable withdrawal. Alcohol withdrawal could kill.

Marijuana did neither. Therefore, it wasn’t addictive. Case closed.

Sources indicate that but addiction isn’t defined by withdrawal. Instead, craving, compulsive use, and continued drug-taking despite the consequences matter much more.

We have seen increasingly potent THC products and widespread availability, producing millions of people who cannot stop using cannabis.

Some patients try to quit only to resume smoking to feel normal.

In 1972, the Shafer Commission, named after its chairman, former Pennsylvania Governor Raymond P. Shafer, evaluated existing cannabis research and found that many claims regarding its dangers were exaggerated.

The commission recommended opposing criminalization. This was quite reasonable. But opposing criminalization doesn’t require pretending cannabis is harmless “medicine.” Nor does recognizing limited legitimate medical uses justify unlimited THC exposure.

The 2025 National Survey on Drug Use and Health estimated that at least 19.3 million Americans aged 12 or older had cannabis use disorder (CUD).

Sources indicate that national Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA) researchers reported adult CUD increased from 15.3 million in 2021 to 19.4 million in 2024. Alcohol use disorder declined over that period. Different populations and ages, but this is an unmistakable warning.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) estimates 3 in 10 cannabis people have CUD.

Meanwhile, 21.4 million Americans reported daily or near-daily marijuana use in 2025.

According to recent reports, among cannabis owners aged 18–20, approximately 47–50 percent met CUD criteria in 2024, more than twice the proportion of similarly aged drinkers meeting alcohol use disorder criteria. So much for marijuana’s supposedly negligible addictive potential.

A Kaiser Permanente Northern California study examined cannabis among 637,461 adolescents and found upcoming CUD diagnoses declined before California’s 2016 legalization vote, then increased afterward. Cannabis, like other drugs of abuse, stimulates its own taking, which means use begets more use. At the other end of the age spectrum, among adults aged 40–50 reporting CUD symptoms, this percentage doubled from 3.3 percent in 2017 to 6.5 percent in 2025. Lastly, among cannabis consumers 65 years or older, 11.4 percent met CUD criteria. Most cases were mild, but near-daily users had substantially greater risks for addiction.

Opioids target the body’s natural opioid receptors, and THC acts primarily at CB1 cannabinoid receptors. Through these receptors, THC alters neurotransmitter signaling and networks involved in reward, motivation, learning, and memory. Repeated exposure produces adaptations underlying tolerance, dependence, withdrawal, and compulsive use.

Industry observers note that modern concentrates can overstimulate these receptors, downregulate CB1 receptors, and may turn off endogenous cannabinoid production. Today, modern products deliver high THC exposures unimaginable when marijuana was routinely dismissed as harmless.

A 2022 systematic review associated greater cannabis potency with psychosis and addiction. A subsequent review covering 99 studies and more than 221,000 participants found most relevant studies associated high-THC products with psychosis or schizophrenia and CUD. Of course, each user is unique. We can't accurately predict who will develop psychosis, addiction, or schizophrenia.

Industry observers note that of all groups, adolescents and young people have the greatest risks. Their brains are under development, and cannabis/THC can interfere with the brain’s developmental programming. Also, especially at risk are people vulnerable to psychosis. Cannabis use is associated with psychiatric and educational problems, although overlapping risk factors complicate causal interpretation. Prenatal THC exposure raises additional developmental concerns.

Alcohol, nicotine, vaping, and other substance use disorders frequently coexist with CUD.

One longstanding research question interests me particularly: Does smoking marijuana teach customers how to inhale drugs efficiently?

Recent findings suggest that data from Seattle-based PATH associate adolescent cannabis use with later tobacco smoking. Learning to inhale deeply, tolerate airway irritation, and receive psychoactive substances may be a gateway facilitating smoking or vaping any drug.

My group at the University of Florida developed animal models permitting direct investigation of cannabis smoke and effects of second-hand exposure to others.

Sources indicate that but physicians treating CUD should look for nicotine dependence, alcohol misuse, and other addictions. Finding one disorder doesn’t mean there aren’t others.

Emergency departments today report more episodes of severe THC intoxication, with patients complaining of panic, paranoia, psychotic symptoms, and cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome (CHS). Cannabis-related vomiting, sometimes called “scromiting” because patients may scream while vomiting, is a hallmark of CHS. Patients sometimes undergo repeated evaluations before cannabis is recognized as the culprit. CHS treatment is stopping cannabis. However, few people with CHS can actually follow this advice.

In a recent development, CHS is a bigger problem than most experts realized. The CDC has identified 199,565 CHS-related emergency visits from January 2023 through May 2026. After the introduction of a CHS diagnostic code, case reporting increased by 3.7 times.

Industry observers note that canadian studies also warned of unrecognized, notable long-term consequences for CUD. Cannabis-related emergency or hospital care among adults aged 45 or older was associated with a 23 percent higher adjusted risk of a upcoming dementia diagnosis than other acute care. Hospital-based CUD care was associated with increased subsequent mortality. Association is not proof of causation, but neither finding should be dismissed.

We have Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved medications for opioid, alcohol, and tobacco use disorders. What about the fastest-growing addiction—cannabis addiction or CUD? None.

In a recent development, marijuana Anonymous offers additional recovery support. Cognitive behavioral therapy, motivational enhancement therapy, and contingency management can help, particularly in the short term. Sustained abstinence continues to be challenging.

Columbia University researchers Christina Brezing and Frances Levin have looked for pharmacological treatments for cannabis withdrawal, craving, and reinforcement, so far without success.

GLP-1-based medications, originally developed for diabetes and obesity, also influence brain pathways involved in reward and motivation. Activation of GLP-1 receptors may dampen dopaminergic reward signaling, but the mechanisms and clinical effects in CUD remain uncertain. Early observational studies have associated GLP-1 medications, including semaglutide, with fewer CUD diagnoses and recurrences. Interesting, but not proof of efficacy.

The cannabis industry has developed increasingly potent products and an enormous market. Addiction medicine has yet to develop a single FDA-approved medication for the people who cannot stop using them. That is a treatment gap we can no longer afford to ignore.

Promising GLP observations are a signal, but they do not prove we have a new treatment for CUDs. GLP-1 medications deserve rigorous, placebo-controlled trials. We have been here before in addiction medicine. Biological plausibility and early clinical signals do not guarantee success.

It has been revealed that the large, unthinkable numbers of people, young and old, with cannabis addiction make it a public health problem that can not be ignored any longer. CUD is not rare or harmless.

We should apply the same lessons as we have applied to other addictions—earlier diagnosis, prevention, better behavioral treatments, medication development, and independent monitoring of cannabis-related harms.

We also need to stop confusing legalization with safety and commercial success with public health progress.
