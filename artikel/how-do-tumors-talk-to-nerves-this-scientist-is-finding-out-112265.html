Sebnem Ece Eksi did a double take. Proteins typically studied in the brain were turning up in prostate cancer cells from high-risk patients. These proteins, called neuronal adhesion molecules, play a key role in the circuitry associated with memory.

According to recent reports, the finding, made during her Ph.D. days at the University of Illinois Chicago, raised so many questions to which Eksi is still seeking answers. Now at the Oregon Health & Science University in Portland, Eksi has made it her lab’s goal to find out how nervous systems communicate with cancer. What were once two separate fields might be connected more closely than previously thought.

Sources indicate that “What we’re learning is that tumors are actively communicating with the nervous system,” Eksi says. “In a way, it makes sense to me that a growing tumor in our bodies, in an organ, would be immediately sensed and detected by the nervous system.”.

Eksi now plans to test an AI image-based recognition algorithm to identify nerve fibers in human patients. This is exciting, she claims, because these nerves aren’t normally visible in patient tumors. But through biopsies, she hopes to map out and observe how the nervous system — which impacts sleep, cognition and pain — changes as cancer progresses. The implications are significant, she says. Could experts use the nervous system to detect cancer early? Can they improve patients’ quality of life?

Sources indicate that “This field of cancer neuroscience can change all different stages of cancer diagnosis and treatments,” Eksi indicates. “It’s growing so promptly, the field itself.”.
