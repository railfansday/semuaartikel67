It has been revealed that what a strange title—“The Power of Play, and Second Chances”! And yet, the processes underlying play and second chances are linked in vital ways. Human development involves three information processing systems: feelings (affects), cognition (thinking, memory, etc.), and language (words to ideas and feelings). Second chances involve all three, but especially feelings.

Play is very key in human development and later life. The psychoanalyst Donald Winnicott writes:.

“It is in playing and only in playing that the individual child or adult can be creative and to use the whole personality, and it is only in being creative that the individual discovers the self” (1971).

Playing and creating can be seen at all ages and in many activities: art, sports, writing, relationships, psychological treatment, and science. Charles Darwin’s manner when he worked in his 60s was described by his son Francis as bright and animated—play-like!

His love of each particular experiment, and his eager zeal not to lose the fruit of it came out markedly in these crossing experiments—in the elaborate care he took to not make any confusion in putting capsules in the wrong trays, &c. &c. I can recall his appearance as he counted seeds under the simple microscope with an alertness not usually characterizing such mechanical work as counting. I think he personified each seed as a small demon trying to elude him by getting into the wrong heap or jumping away altogether, and this gave to the work the excitement of a game (Browne, 2002).

In everyday language, we are talking about joy, curiosity, and excitement—the wonder of living, and the joy of finding one’s own self through creating.

Here I use the term second chances to mean a change in any major aspect of one’s life—work, relationships, understanding of one’s self, and so on.

There is much interest in second chances, probably because humans are constantly trying to enhance their lives. Discussions most often focus on work or an notable relationship. I know one woman who for years did wonderful work for a financial firm, but she got tired of it. She had always wanted to travel, so she became a flight attendant and traveled the world, with much happiness. And as approximately 40 to 50 percent of first marriages end in divorce over a lifetime, second chances loom large for many people.

It has been revealed that one of the best books in this area is Second Chances: Shakespeare and Freud, by literary scholar Stephen Greenblatt and psychoanalyst Adam Phillips. They write:.

In the second chance we get, if there is one, there is always, then, the chance of finally realizing our preferred version of ourselves (if you want to picture your ambition for yourself you have to imagine what you would do if you got a second chance). The as-yet unlived life that is our second chance carries what we presume to be our potential. Our second chance must be, at least in prospect, better than our first. It is an opportunity for something to come to life, or to come to more life. It is an opportunity to formulate what we might want for ourselves, and for the other people in our lives. The second chance may begin as a wish—or even as a utopian fantasy—but everything depends on how it is worked out, and on how it works out (2024).

According to recent reports, often in life we come up against boredom, a lack of excitement, or a need for something newly introduced and fulfilling—in work, free time, or relationships. What causes a person to consider a second chance, and what leads one to try it or not?

Do fear and shame, or interest (curiosity) and enjoyment rule the day? That really is the question. And its roots run through every developmental phase: childhood, adolescence, early adulthood, adulthood, and older adulthood.

Second chances can be small or large. Small decisions occur all the time, at all ages. The larger dilemmas—picking a school, choice of a mate, work opportunities and changes, and, actually, life and death—arise from adolescence onward. Conscious as well as unconscious factors figure into the outcome, as do our assessments of the past, the present, and fantasies of the future. Feelings, cognition, and language are all involved, but feelings are probably the most notable.

Sources indicate that clinically, here is where past and present become especially interesting. Some people get overly excited and jump into things too swiftly, and need help using their cognitive processes. But more often we see people with significant feelings of fear and shame who have trouble trying something fresh because they are protecting themselves from potential hurt or loss.

This is conveyed nicely by the old “car” or “jack” story from years ago.

A person has a flat tire, and he has no jack and can’t fix it. He sees someone across the road who is fixing his tire. He starts across the road to get help, and begins to think—“Wait, I don’t know him…he won’t help me and loan me his jack,” and so on. When he finally reaches the other car, he blurts out tearfully and angrily: “Oh just keep your damn jack,” and stomps away. His past and fears and fantasies have influenced his actions profoundly.

This happens all the time: “These agents won’t like my manuscript.” “This school won’t accept me.”.

Fear and shame can seriously undermine the capacity to try something, to speak up. But when we do try and make a mistake, we learn crucial information about ourselves and how to improve.

The great hockey player Wayne Gretzky put it this way: “You miss 100 percent of the shots you don’t take!”.

Second chances really mean grappling with small and large decisions, conscious and unconscious, throughout the life cycle. Understanding our basic processing systems—feelings, cognition, and language—can go a long way toward helping us take advantage of them. This is especially true in using interest and enjoyment to counteract fear and shame—and that's the power of play!
