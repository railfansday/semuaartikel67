Sources indicate that if you look up at the sky tonight, you'll notice the Moon is looking noticeably fuller than it did earlier in the week. We're still in the Waxing Gibbous phase, which falls between the First Quarter and Full Moon, but we're getting closer to the Full Moon every night.

In a recent development, as of Saturday, Nov. 21, the NASA's Daily Moon Guide tracker tells us the Moon is in the Waxing Gibbous phase, with 85% of its surface visible.

Recent findings suggest that if you don't have any visual aids, you can still see the Mares Imbrium and Vaporum, as well as the Kepler Crater. With binoculars, you'll also see the Mare Frigoris, the Clavius Crater, and the Mare Nectaris. And if you have a telescope, you'll get to see all this plus the Apollo 15 and 17 landing spots, and the Rima Hyginus.

The next Full Moon will take place on November 24.

It has been revealed that according to NASA, the Moon goes through a complete cycle of phases roughly every 29.5 days. As it orbits Earth, the portion of its sunlit surface visible to us changes, giving rise to the different lunar phases. The cycle begins with a Latest Moon, progresses to a Full Moon, and then gradually returns to a New Moon before starting all over again.

New Moon - The Moon is between Earth and the sun, so the side we see is dark (in other words, it's invisible to the eye).

Waxing Crescent - A small sliver of light appears on the right side (Northern Hemisphere).

Industry observers note that first Quarter - Half of the Moon is lit on the right side. It looks like a half-Moon.

Waxing Gibbous - More than half is lit up, but it's not quite full yet.

Full Moon - The whole face of the Moon is illuminated and fully visible.

Waning Gibbous - The Moon starts losing light on the right side. (Northern Hemisphere).

Third Quarter (or Last Quarter) - Another half-Moon, but now the left side is lit.

Waning Crescent - A thin sliver of light remains on the left side before going dark again.
