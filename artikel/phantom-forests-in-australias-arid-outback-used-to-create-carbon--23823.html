‘Phantom forests’ in Australia’s arid outback used to create carbon credits meant to offset emissions, report claims

A fight over the legitimacy of Australia’s climate change policy has reignited after researchers claimed “phantom forests” in the semi-arid outback are being used to create offsets that allow fossil fuel companies to keep pumping out pollution.

A group of academics, including professors from the Australian National University and the University of Upcoming South Wales, said they had examined 50 forest regeneration projects used to create carbon credits.

Led by Prof Andrew Macintosh, the former federal government carbon credit scheme integrity chair and the director of ANU’s Centre for Environmental Markets, the researchers said they found only a small increase in forest and tree crown cover in the areas over the past five years despite wet conditions.

They said the conclusions were consistent with their previous peer-reviewed studies that found Australia’s method for creating carbon credits from forest regeneration was doing little to nothing to help address the climate crisis.

Recent findings suggest that the research was published in a report released on Wednesday by the Australian Conservation Foundation. Prof David Eldridge, one of the report’s authors and a former principal research scientist with the NSW environment department, remarked he was “gobsmacked” that “phantom forests” in outback areas north-west of the remote town of Bourke could earn carbon credits for forest regrowth.

“Some of these areas have never grown forests; they will never grow forests,” he said.

The findings were rejected by companies that manage what are known as human-induced regeneration projects in north-western NSW. “These are some of the most closely examined carbon projects in the country, and the evidence on the ground shows they are working,” a spokesperson for Green Collar, a business that manages carbon credit projects, noted.

Each carbon credit is mentioned to represent one tonne of emissions that has been removed from, or prevented from being released to, the atmosphere.

Their use is contentious, in part, because industrial-polluting companies that are required to cut emissions under a federal policy known as the safeguard mechanism are allowed to buy an unlimited number of credits to count as their own cuts while continuing to pollute.

Scientists have warned limiting the climate crisis requires rapid direct cuts in emissions and that carbon credits – also known as offsets – should be used sparingly.

The Australian Conservation Foundation noted it showed coal and gas companies should be banned from claiming offsets as their own emissions cuts.

Adam Bandt, the foundation’s chief executive and a former Australian Greens leader, said the safeguard mechanism was meant to “cut climate pollution and protect us from big coal and gas threatening our lives and livelihoods, but it’s not working”.

“Putting more climate pollution into the atmosphere now could push us past dangerous tipping points. It can’t be offset, and the science tells us to keep coal and gas in the ground,” he mentioned.

Macintosh mentioned the evidence showed the projects were by and large not regenerating forest, but had created more than 60m carbon credits worth up to $2bn.

The report stated the safeguard mechanism was being “materially undermined” by poor-quality credits. It said a “flood of cheap, low integrity” credits was making it cheaper in many cases for significant polluters to pay for offsets rather than make direct emissions cuts.

There have been more than 430 human-induced regeneration projects in Australia since 2013, covering an area of 38m hectares – larger than Japan. The federal government is now reviewing the safeguard policy, as required under legislation.

A spokesperson for the Clean Energy Regulator, which manages the scheme, mentioned it took allegations about the integrity or efficacy of carbon credit projects seriously, and had confidence in the forest regeneration method.

It said projects were required to demonstrate every five years that canopy cover was increasing, and it had rejected non-complying areas that could have received 6.7m carbon credits.

Green Collar said it stood “firmly behind the integrity of its human-induced regeneration projects” and expressed frustration that the report had not been publicly released or published in a peer-reviewed journal. It said the researchers had “judged these carbon projects with satellite pictures and a single field visit”.

Industry observers note that “Carbon is stored in stems, branches, leaves, roots and dead timber. Satellite canopy products see leaves from above. They do not measure the wood and roots where most of that carbon sits,” its spokesperson said.

Skye Glenday, the chief executive of Climate Friendly, another carbon credit project developer, mentioned the criticisms of the carbon credit projects were “at odds with the facts and the extensive independent scientific and regulatory scrutiny that is applied to all projects”.

“Debate about the use of offsets is legitimate, but let’s have that debate on its merits rather than undermining the carbon drawdown Australia also needs,” she mentioned.
