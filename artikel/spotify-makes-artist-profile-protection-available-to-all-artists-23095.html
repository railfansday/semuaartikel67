Spotify made Artist Profile Protection available to all artists on November 17, 2026, expanding an optional control that lets them review releases delivered under their names and remove incorrect associations before those releases affect their profiles, statistics or listener recommendations.

The expansion tackles a distribution problem that predates generative AI: metadata mix-ups, shared artist names and malicious uploads can place music on the wrong profile across streaming services. Spotify indicates easy-to-produce AI tracks have aggravated the problem, which can distort an artist’s catalog and statistics, interfere with Release Radar and change how fans discover the music.

Sources indicate that the feature is designed to control attribution on Spotify, not to determine whether audio was generated by AI. After an artist opts in, Spotify sends an email when a delivery carries that artist’s name. The artist can review eligible releases and remove an incorrect association; doing nothing lets the release go live as scheduled with the artist listed.

Industry observers note that A rejected release will not list that artist, appear on their profile, feed their statistics or enter recommendations to their listeners. The decision applies only to Spotify, so the same release can remain misattributed elsewhere and the artist may still need to contact a label or distributor.

Artist team administrators and editors can manage the feature through desktop artist settings or the mobile app’s music tab. The Help Center documentation describes two modes: Review releases lets music go live unless the artist removes their association, while Block releases, available only to some artists, requires approval before the artist can be listed.

It has been revealed that spotify recommends Block for artists with common names or repeated mismatches who can actively manage their catalogs. Legitimate releases can be delayed or blocked if nobody approves them. Artists can act before release or for up to 28 days afterward, while approvals can take up to 24 hours to process.

It has been revealed that review coverage includes main and featured artists, remixers and actors, plus composers, orchestras and conductors on classical releases. In Block mode, an artist key lets trusted distributors pre-approve legitimate deliveries. The key should not be posted publicly; it may not work with every provider, and releases from some providers may be ineligible for removal or approval.

Recent findings suggest that spotify says it tested Artist Profile Protection for several months with thousands of artists, including Samia, Diplo, Searows and Dave East. Robbie Mackey of Grand Jury Music, speaking for Samia’s team, remarked, “We’re glad Spotify is giving us more control over what appears under her name.”.

The wider rollout fulfills a plan set out in an October 20, 2026 platform patch, which still called the tool a beta and promised access for all artists by year-end.

Recent findings suggest that in that update, Spotify reported taking more than 50 million slop and spam tracks out of recommendations during 2026. Separately, more than 75 million spammy tracks had been removed from the platform altogether for serious or repeated rule violations.

That enforcement is distinct from profile review. Spotify indicates its recommendation systems combine listener engagement and feedback, upload patterns, metadata and multiple third-party AI-detection tools because no single signal catches every instance of slop or spam. The organization also acknowledges that AI detection has limitations.

It has been revealed that artist Profile Protection is separate from Spotify’s voice-impersonation policy, which covers unauthorized replicas of an artist’s voice, including AI clones. Spotify requires a claim from the artist or a representative because it cannot determine whether a particular use is a cleared sample or collaboration. After reviewing a submission, Spotify can remove music that violates the policy.

The voice policy can apply even when the impersonated artist is absent from the credits or the uploader presents the track as an AI rendition, with exceptions including certain parodies.

Sources indicate that for artists, the upcoming control moves a consequential decision—whether a release should shape their public catalog and recommendations—into their own review queue. Its reach is narrower than a deepfake takedown system: it depends on artist action and provider eligibility, and it cannot correct attribution on rival services or settle whether a voice was used without permission.
