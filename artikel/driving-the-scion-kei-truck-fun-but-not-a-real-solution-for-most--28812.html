Driving the Scion Kei Truck: Fun, But Not a Real Solution for Most Americans

What started as a government-subsidized program to provide cheap, industrious transportation for a Japanese economy that had been ravaged by World War II has become a genuine American fixation. In mountainous resort towns in Colorado, Faulkner farmland in the Delta, and Northeast metropolises, the pint-size Kei truck has become a popular target for importers bringing examples from Japan. Now Toyota is set to dump fuel on that fire, as a brand-latest kei truck from the resurrected Scion brand will be offered in the U.S. market in 2027.

Recent findings suggest that as with all vehicles not originally built to U.S. standards, federal Department of Transportation rules have limited American kei enthusiasts to importing 25-year-old examples of the miniature trucks. To be frank, most of these tired examples drive as one would expect of a work truck with a quarter century under its belt. Toyota’s incoming example is the exact opposite, as I got the chance to find out during a half-dozen laps of a half-mile cone course at Eagles Canyon Raceway outside Dallas.

Sources indicate that officially known as the Scion by Toyota Kei-01, the rebadged Daihatsu Hijet is powered by a 660-cc three-cylinder gasoline engine making around 53 hp, which is fed through a continuously variable automatic transmission. The Kei-01 is limited to 25 mph, far short of the 70 mph or so a Japanese-market Hijet can accomplish. That threshold was deliberately chosen to comply with federal safety requirements for low-speed vehicles, which mandate that the vehicle go faster than 20 mph but not faster than 25.

Yet that modest limit didn’t mean acceleration was lacking. When I moved the hilariously toylike shifter into drive and matted the gas, the Kei-01 jumped off the line like a puppy smelling sausages, growling and uncoordinated. The lack of gear ratios kept the tiny engine at high rpm, although the missing tach meant I couldn’t verify how fast it was spinning. Regardless, maximum speed was easily reached, according to the speedometer displaying kilometers per hour.

The ergonomics of a kei truck meant I sat on top of the front wheels and had the Toyota-badged steering wheel tilted into my lap. Also, this being a product designed for the Japanese market, the steering wheel was positioned on the right; Toyota has no plans to reverse the layout for us. With 14-inch wheels and what are barely soccer-ball-diameter tires, the Kei-01 was agile to a fault; I suspect you could pretty much turn it inside a single Costco parking space. Directional changes happened rapidly, although the steering is high geared, and even slow-speed maneuvers elicited a special charm. That said, I wouldn’t want to haul it around a tight corner at any rate of speed. And the ride quality could be described generously as rudimentary. I bounced up and down in my adjustable seat over Toyota’s provided gravel course, the interior loudly rattling the whole time. However, as a dedicated work truck, this was hardly disqualifying.

A button in the center console promised locking four-wheel-drive, though I didn’t get to use it. Slowing the Kei-01, with its curb weight of just over 2000 pounds, was as easy as letting off the accelerator. But front disc and rear drum brakes seemed powerful enough. Considering the modest limiter, you could just yank the analog, wire-operated parking brake.

Toyota hasn’t skimped on features. A central touchscreen tucked into the dashboard will supposedly have Apple CarPlay functionality when the Kei-01 arrives in the U.S. next year. Push-button start and slider-style climate controls will also be fitted as standard. For those who have bemoaned the rise of haptic-touch features, the Kei-01 will be the freshest breath of interior design air you’ve had in some time. Icons on the Kei-01’s dashboard even promised ADAS features like lane-departure control and collision warnings. Some of the touch surfaces were hard, and the doors felt featherlight, but who really cares?

The Kei-01’s payload capacity, scaled to its diminutive dimensions, is just 600 pounds. But the three sides of the load bed can be folded down separately, boosting utility, meaning that trips to Home Depot are more likely to be limited by speed than cargo weight. A storage deck behind the seats is well suited for backpacks and toolboxes, and there are flat storage spaces on the dashboard and built into the roofline.

Recent findings suggest that before you check your credit score, there is a brutal catch to the possibility of buying the Kei-01. Toyota has stated it won’t sell the vehicle to individuals initially, with access limited to fleet operators.

The plan is to target luxury resorts, university administrations, and other institutions with the need for such compact utility. This makes sense given both the likely lack of profit in individual trucks and the logic of putting the trucks into the hands of what are likely to be safe, responsible customers with their own private road networks. Toyota hasn’t revealed pricing yet, but the Kei-01 will almost certainly carry a sizable upcharge over the equivalent Hijet, which costs about $9000 in Japan, with U.S. versions being built in Indiana.

Recent findings suggest that the brutal truth is that a maximum speed of 25 mph means the Scion Kei-01 simply isn’t suited to road use in much of the U.S. Even in the center of cities like New York, where speed limits are largely set at that number, traffic normally flows considerably faster when free to do so. The prospect of trying to drive a Kei-01 on a faster-flowing highway surrounded by inattentive traffic is slightly terrifying. Toyota’s decision to keep the bulk of Kei-01s away from public roads is prudent, if a little disappointing. I’ve never had more fun going 25 mph.
