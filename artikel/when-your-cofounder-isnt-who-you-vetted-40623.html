When Your Cofounder Isn’t Who You Vetted

You’re driving home from the pitch, replaying the way your cofounder talked over you in front of the investor. The part that stings is that you did this carefully. You went through the cofounder dating questions together. You spent more time vetting them than most other founders you know. But you still ended up frustrated and disappointed, thinking to yourself: How did I choose wrong?

Recent findings suggest that most founders I work with put time and energy into selecting their cofounder. Many used Gloria Lin’s 50 questions, published by First Round Review. These questions are useful, but the problem is often that the answers were true when you gave them, and people change, especially under pressure.

Two founders I worked with demonstrated this clearly.

According to recent reports, one of them was leaving an established firm to start the business. Sarah was worried about what failing would do to her reputation and felt concerned she couldn't simply re-enter her role if she left. So she set a condition. She'd only make the leap if her partner, Tom, was willing to give up everything for the company: his sleep, his health, his marriage. Tom remarked yes, and he meant it (at the time).

Then Tom's wife got very sick. His priorities shifted, and he no longer wanted to prioritize the manufacturer above his marriage. He worked fewer hours. Sarah saw that as a breach of contract. Tom hoped that Sarah would change her mind and the two of them could keep building. But Sarah wasn’t willing to budge. She was quite clear on the level of sacrifice and commitment to the business she required, and they ultimately parted ways.

Looking back, the warning signs were present in their first conversation.

Sarah's demand was extreme. It highlighted unconscious anxiety about her inability to recover the prestige of her previous company and corresponding rigidity about sacrifice and commitment. The partnership agreement was also too inflexible. When Tom's life changed, they didn't have the capacity to change the terms.

I find it useful to separate aspirational values from lived values. Aspirational values are the ones you’d list if someone asked you. Lived values are what someone watching your behavior would see.

Sources indicate that when cofounders answer each other’s questions before they start, they’re mostly reporting aspirational values. They mean them. They just haven’t had to live them yet.

A core idea in psychoanalysis is that we’re of many minds, and that the conscious mind doesn’t always win.

Partnerships within a startup environment prove that point. Founders are often exhausted, sick, and under pressure, and in my experience, people regress under that kind of load. They fall back on older, less flexible ways of coping. It’s easy to say you’ll stay calm in a disagreement. It’s much harder when your heart is pounding, your revenue isn't growing, and you’re frustrated with the person across the table.

In a 1995 review in Psychological Bulletin, the psychologists Benjamin Karney and Thomas Bradbury drew on 115 longitudinal studies covering more than 45,000 marriages and proposed what they called the vulnerability-stress-adaptation model.

In their model, how a marriage fares depends on three things working together: what each partner brings into the relationship, such as personality and family history; the stress the couple faces; and how the two of them treat each other under that stress.

A couple can look steady when life is calm and struggle once the pressure rises. I see the same thing with cofounders, and a startup supplies plenty of pressure.

Two other cofounders I worked with had been colleagues at a previous company. Before starting, they agreed to a chart spelling out who was responsible for each area and who would be consulted or just informed on each decision.

According to recent reports, the technical cofounder signed off on being informed about areas outside his own. What he actually expected was to be consulted, because to him that’s what being a cofounder meant. His partner saw the cofounder title as little more than extra equity. What mattered to her was running separate areas and moving fast. Nobody asked what “informed” meant, and the mismatch only surfaced once they were building.

According to recent reports, gaps like this tend to get pushed aside, because they feel less urgent than shipping a product or landing customers. Founders tell themselves they’ll talk once the demo works, then once they have more customers.

In a recent development, in my experience, the company usually grows faster than the partnership does. Whatever got skipped early comes back later, often at a bad moment, like the middle of a fundraise, when you’re also deciding who’ll be the face of the company to investors.

According to recent reports, the questions are a starting point. The test is working together long enough, ideally several months, to say yes to two things: We’re aligned on what we’re building, and I know how you act under stress. It helps to think in milestones rather than a set number of weeks.

The first yes covers the practical side: where you want the business to go, how you’ll fund it, what financial outcome each of you wants, how much risk each of you is taking on, and how much time and sacrifice each of you can give.

In a recent development, the second yes is about the two of you. Have you seen how your partner responds when things get hard, and do you still enjoy working with them? Can you give each other feedback in both directions? Have you had a genuine disagreement and come back from it?

It has been revealed that that last one carries a lot of weight. In my work, trust between partners tends to grow through repeated cycles of rupture and repair, and the honeymoon period doesn’t offer many of those.

Sources indicate that before you sign anything, agree on how you’ll adjust equity, compensation, or roles if that were needed, and write it down. Most people’s life circumstances and values change at some point. What hurt the founders in my story was having no agreed way to adjust when that happened.

Sources indicate that if you are successful, you'll spend the next 5 to 10 years with this person. Building resilience in a relationship requires going through hardship together and coming through aligned on the other side. There's no question that can preempt that.
