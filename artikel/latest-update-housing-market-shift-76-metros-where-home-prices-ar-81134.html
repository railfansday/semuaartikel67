Based on ResiClub’s analysis of the Zillow Home Value Index, nationally aggregated U.S. home prices rose 0.7% year over year from October 2025 to October 2026. That marks another month-over-month deceleration, down from the 1.0% year-over-year increase from September 2025 to September 2026 and the 1.3% year-over-year increase from August 2025 to August 2026. After contracting over the past year, the number of America’s major regional housing markets seeing year-over-year declines is rising again, as the already soft national housing market (which is still amid the multiyear post-boom cyclical cooling window) sees another burst of softening amid the recent run-up in long-term yields and mortgage rates. —> 54 of the nation’s 300 largest housing markets (i.e., 18% of markets) had a falling year-over-year home price reading in the August 2025 to August 2026 window. —> 66 of the nation’s 300 largest housing markets (i.e., 22% of markets) had a falling year-over-year home price reading in the September 2025 to September 2026 window. —> 76 of the nation’s 300 largest housing markets (i.e., 25% of markets) had a falling year-over-year home price reading in the October 2025 to October 2026 window. We should point out that the nationally aggregated housing market is softer than the counts above suggest, given that year-over-year home price declines are at the moment skewed slightly toward larger metro areas. Home prices are still climbing a tad nominally (not inflation-adjusted) year over year in some pockets where active inventory remains well below pre-pandemic 2019 levels, such as certain areas in the Northeast and Midwest. In contrast, some pockets in states like Texas, Florida, Washington, and Colorado—where active inventory exceeds pre-pandemic 2019 levels by a solid clip—are seeing either material corrections, modest home price pullbacks, or simply flat pricing. Looking ahead, the substantial question is how much of a burst of softening the nationally aggregated housing market will see, now that the 30-year fixed-mortgage rate has jumped back above 7.0% amid an already strained affordability environment and the still ongoing post-boom cyclical cooling period. Many of the housing markets seeing the most softness, where homebuyers have gained the most leverage since the pandemic housing boom fizzled out, are primarily located in Sunbelt regions or the Mountain West. Many of these areas saw even greater price surges during the pandemic housing boom, with home price growth outpacing local income levels. As pandemic-driven domestic migration slowed and mortgage rates rose in 2022, markets like Tampa, Florida, and Austin faced challenges, relying on local income levels to support frothy home prices. That Sunbelt softening was further compounded by an abundance of upcoming-home supply in the Sunbelt. Builders are often willing to lower prices or offer affordability incentives to maintain sales, which also has a cooling effect on the resale market. As a result, some buyers who might have previously opted for existing homes are instead choosing new construction with more attractive deals—which added further upward pressure to resale inventory growth over the past few years. Of course, while 76 of the nation’s 300 largest metro-area housing markets are seeing year-over-year home price declines, another 224 are seeing year-over-year home price increases. However, most of the places posting “nominal gains” are seeing gains below income growth and consumer price growth, which economists call “real” (inflation-adjusted) home price declines. While the range [see chart above] between the strongest and weakest metro-area housing markets right now is fairly normal historically speaking, the bifurcation (i.e., direction) itself—the share of markets with rising home prices versus those with falling prices—is wider than normal, given that national appreciation is in a softer market with growth barely above 0.0%. And the longer some markets remain in the “rising” camp while others stay in the “falling” camp, the wider the gulf can become between the relatively more resilient markets and the weaker ones. For example, home prices in the Hartford, Conneticut, metro area are now 28.0% above their 2022 peak, while home prices in the Austin metro area sit 28.8% below their 2022 peak. Some of that bifurcation boils down to mean reversion, with many of the outright home price declines occurring in markets that overheated further during the pandemic housing boom.
