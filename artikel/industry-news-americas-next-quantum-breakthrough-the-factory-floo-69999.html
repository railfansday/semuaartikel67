📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that building a quantum computing firm has taught me something that wasn’t always obvious to me as a scientist. Developing a new technology is one challenge. Figuring out how to manufacture it reliably, at scale, is another. And the second can be every bit as difficult as the first. For years, the prevailing wisdom in technology was to focus on what you do best and outsource the rest. It made good business sense. Building manufacturing capacity is expensive, complicated, and often far removed from the work that inspired someone to start a technology company in the first place. But that approach comes with a tradeoff. When you depend on someone else to manufacture your technology, you also depend on their capabilities, priorities, and timelines. For an emerging industry like quantum information, those dependencies can become significant obstacles. And I believe they have implications well beyond any one company. A LESSON WE’VE ALREADY LEARNED The United States has seen what happens when technological innovation and manufacturing become separated. American companies designed many of the advanced semiconductors powering today’s AI revolution, but much of the manufacturing capacity needed to produce them was developed overseas. That arrangement supported decades of innovation and economic growth. It also created dependencies that have become increasingly difficult to ignore. Now, the U.S. is investing heavily in rebuilding domestic semiconductor manufacturing capacity. It’s a difficult and expensive undertaking, and one that will take years to fully realize. Quantum technologies give us an opportunity to think about these challenges earlier in an industry’s development. The U.S. has extraordinary strengths in quantum science. Our universities, national laboratories, and private companies have helped advance the field for decades. But the path from a scientific breakthrough to a useful product requires more than research. Much more. It requires specialized materials, fabrication processes, engineering expertise, and the ability to produce increasingly complex technologies consistently and economically. Those capabilities need to develop alongside science. WHY WE CHOSE TO BUILD I’ve experienced this challenge firsthand at Quantum Computing Inc. Our work in quantum photonics depends on highly specialized manufacturing processes. As we developed our technology, we had to think carefully about how much of our future we wanted to depend on outside manufacturing partners. For a growing company, investing in your own manufacturing capabilities is a consequential decision. It requires capital, specialized expertise, and a willingness to take on responsibilities that extend well beyond research and product development. We ultimately decided to build and acquire manufacturing capacity because we believe greater control over the critical and proprietary processes is essential to our long-term strategy. For other processes, we will leverage existing manufacturing capabilities and collaborate with industry partners to build a robust, reliable, and sustainable supply chain. We also see an opportunity to contribute to the broader ecosystem by making our manufacturing capabilities available to others. The quantum opportunity is far bigger than any one company. Advancing the industry will require collaboration and shared capabilities that benefit all of us. It’s time to work together to build that foundation and accelerate the growth of the quantum industry. NO ONE MANUFACTURING MODEL That decision has shaped how I think about innovation. When researchers and engineers have closer access to manufacturing, they can learn from what happens during fabrication and use that knowledge to improve the next design. Manufacturing becomes part of the development process itself. The experience gained along the way can be just as valuable as the equipment. There is no single manufacturing model that makes sense for every quantum company. Different technologies have different requirements, and outside foundries and specialized partners remain important. But access to reliable manufacturing capacity can have a profound influence on how rapidly an idea becomes something useful. BUILD AN INDUSTRY, NOT JUST A TECHNOLOGY Washington D.C. is beginning to recognize this challenge. A June executive order addressing quantum technology calls for strengthening domestic manufacturing capabilities and expanding partnerships between government and private industry. I welcome that attention. But building a manufacturing base requires more than funding announcements and policy commitments. It takes facilities, equipment, specialized talent, and years of experience developing processes that work consistently. It also requires demand from companies that are ready to move technologies out of the laboratory and into practical use. Other countries recognize the importance of these capabilities, with many having committed substantial resources to quantum technology. The United States has important advantages in research, entrepreneurship, and private investment. We should be building on those strengths now. That doesn’t mean every component needs to be manufactured domestically. International partnerships and global supply chains will continue to play important roles. But we should be deliberate about which capabilities are essential to our technological independence, making sure we have the capacity to develop and sustain them. FINAL THOUGHTS I’ve spent much of my career working to advance quantum technology. Like many scientists, I was initially drawn to the possibilities of what we could discover and what those discoveries make possible. Today, I spend just as much time thinking about what it takes to turn those possibilities into something people can use. That means manufacturing, supply chains, engineering, and all the difficult work that follows a breakthrough. The U.S. has an extraordinary foundation in quantum science. What we do with that advantage now matters. We need to invest in the ability to manufacture these technologies with the same conviction and intensity we brought to discovering them. Yuping Huang is CEO of Quantum Computing Inc.
