Tesla Cybercab orders open at $29,990 + $299/mo, only in Robotaxi zones

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Tesla is now inviting “select customers” to buy a Cybercab for personal use at $29,990, but the car won’t move unless you also pay $299 a month for an “Autonomy Subscription.”.

And it only works inside Tesla’s Robotaxi service areas in Texas and Florida, the same geofences that have turned out to be significantly smaller than what Tesla advertises.

Tesla’s official Robotaxi account announced the invites on X on Friday with screenshots of the order page and the invitation email.

Sources indicate that the order page lists the Cybercab at $29,990 before taxes, with an estimated delivery of December 2026 in Austin, Texas. You need to put down a $1,000 non-refundable deposit to order.

Industry observers note that the subscription isn’t optional. It’s pre-checked on the order page at $299 per month, with one month included, and the fine print states “Subscription and regular vehicle maintenance required to operate.”.

The email is even more direct. Its asterisk reads: “Only available in supported service areas. Subscription required for operation.”.

According to recent reports, the rest is what we already knew. Two seats, a 21-inch center touchscreen, 20.1 cubic feet of cargo space, and no steering wheel or pedals. Tesla calls it “fully autonomous” and indicates you’ll “manage your ride in the app.”.

In a recent development, musk had promised a Cybercab “below $30,000” when he unveiled it in 2024. He technically hit it. He just didn’t mention the $3,588 a year it takes to use it. Over five years, that’s about $47,600 for a two-seater, before insurance, maintenance, taxes, and charging.

It has been revealed that tesla runs its Robotaxi service in eight cities: Austin, Dallas, Houston, Fort Worth, and San Antonio in Texas, and Miami, Orlando, and Tampa in Florida. That’s where a Cybercab can operate. Everywhere else, it’s a $30,000 sculpture in your driveway.

Recent findings suggest that and those service areas aren’t what Tesla indicates they are. Independent tracker Koby Pierce tested thousands of addresses in the Robotaxi app and found that the areas where Tesla actually accepts pickups and drop-offs are only 59% to 78% of the advertised geofences in the six cities he checked. In Austin, the very city where Tesla is delivering these cars, it’s about 59%.

Industry observers note that railroad crossings are a significant reason why. In Houston, the creator of Robotaxi Tracker found the app quoting about 40 minutes to go two blocks downtown because the vehicle appeared to route around rail crossings. Tesla also lists San Antonio’s airport inside its polygon but doesn’t serve it.

In a recent development, then there’s the regulatory cloud. NHTSA has ordered Tesla to prove under oath that a car with no brake pedal meets federal safety standards that require one. Tesla answered on October 30 and asked that the entire response be kept confidential. NHTSA hasn’t ruled. Tesla is now selling the car to consumers anyway.

Sources indicate that I think this is going to be a total mess. It looks like Tesla is only doing this by invitation and delivering it in Austin to prove they can deliver an “unsupervised” vehicle in 2026.

Tesla is going to sell these to fans who are excited about owning a self-driving car, and many of them will find out the limitations aren’t worth $299 a month on top of a $30,000 car.

Sure, if your monthly Uber bill is well over $300 and you live and work deep inside the geofence, maybe the math works on paper. Until you realize you have to park it somewhere once you get to your destination. Or that it can’t take you to a friend’s place two neighborhoods over. Or that a 15-minute drive turns into an hour because the car won’t cross a train track. With an Uber or a Waymo, that’s someone else’s problem. With a Cybercab, you’re paying for the privilege.

No point trying to calculate what it will cost over X years. Tesla can change that monthly fee at will anyway (and shall be expected to, considering their overall pricing history).

Industry observers note that and here’s the part that makes no sense to me. The Cybercab runs on the same AI4 computer as the current Model Y. Tesla already runs unsupervised Model Y Robotaxis in these same geofences. So why can’t the Model Y owners who already paid for “Full Self-Driving” get unsupervised operation in those same zones? Wouldn’t be even easier to turn that on these vehicles which have accumulated much more data? Tesla keeps pushing that to “next year,” currently around Q2 2027, while asking people to buy a new two-seater that can do nothing else.

If the tech works unsupervised in Austin, turn it on for the Model Y owners in Austin. If it doesn’t, why should anyone pay $47,000 over five years for a car that only works where Tesla claims it does?

Like with every other Tesla product lately, the delivered version is going to be a lot more disappointing than the one that was sold on stage.
