📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, the U.S. Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) has released an interim final rule (IFR) [PDF] that will prevent Chinese AI companies from leasing data compute outside of China to access advanced U.S. chips like Nvidia Blackwell AI GPUs. However, Nikkei Asia reports that the latest regulations have also affected foundries making chips for consumers and the automotive industry, as TSMC has put several shipments to both Chinese and non-Chinese chip designers on hold as it reviews their compliance with the changes to U.S.

Washington’s previous export controls only focused on high-performance AI chips like Nvidia’s Blackwell and upcoming Vera Rubin chips. The latest rules expand it to include all chips, with front-end fabricators (chip foundries) or OSATs (outsourced semiconductor assembly and test companies), also known as packaging and testing companies, required to assume that semiconductors with a 16nm or 14nm node are automatically classified as export-controlled advanced computing chips. The ruling even mentioned that even if the designed chip has less than 30 billion transistors and has no HBM, it cannot be assumed that the chips aren’t built for high-performance computing.

Recent findings suggest that the 16nm and 14nm nodes have been around for around 10 years now, and they could even be found inside consumer devices like smartphones and TVs, as well as in automotive applications. This latest rule means that designers making chips using 16nm or newer nodes, even if they’re not specifically built for data centers, must secure documentation from an approved designer certifying the chip’s Total Processing Performance or performance density if they want to avoid additional scrutiny from the fab.

"The upcoming rule is very strict this time, and the affecting scope is far beyond only chips for data centers but covers smartphone processors, autonomous driving and other types of chips for consumer electronics as well as others if they are using nodes that are 14nm or 16nm or more advanced tech," a source told the publication. "The move would delay shipments to many chip developers worldwide before they can clear compliance and hit many emerging startups in many Asian nations.".

In a recent development, aside from this, the U.S. also now requires export licenses to ship advanced AI chips to the following countries: Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Brazil, and Thailand. The IFR says that these countries are often used as transshipment points for chips that are being diverted to China, as evidenced by the Supermicro and Megaspeed investigations, which used one of these countries as the destination of the Nvidia servers they ordered, before sending them forward to their final destination in China.

Chip smuggling has long been a simmering issue in the United States, but it seemingly has bubbled over after the revelation of Jensen Huang’s affair with Megaspeed executive Alice Huang, who’s also suspected of being instrumental in diverting $7.5 billion worth of Nvidia chips and passing on information to Chinese intelligence. While his alleged relationship with a direct subordinate was the apparent primary cause of his departure from Nvidia, sources say that the real reason behind his ousting is the mass smuggling of 1.5 million Nvidia AI GPUs that happened right under his nose.
