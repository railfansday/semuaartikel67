Tech Report: How data centers are driving a US natural-gas boom

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In May 2025, my colleague David Rotman wrote a feature story describing how AI could keep us dependent on natural gas. Just 18 months later, I’ve lost count of how many times he’s already been proved right.

Recent findings suggest that sure, it’s technically possible to meet the world’s rising electricity demand with other power sources, including renewables like wind and solar supported by batteries. But the reality in the US so far is that the data center boom is helping to fuel a massive natural-gas resurgence.

In a recent development, just one example: Looks for the Meta data center in Louisiana that David focused on in his story have expanded, and its power needs have too. The local utility went from three planned natural-gas plants to 10. And as I wrote in a fresh story this week, data center developers are now looking beyond the grid and planning to build their own off-grid gas power plants. How did we get here?

Industry observers note that if you want to understand the future of the grid, one way to do it is by looking at interconnection queues—lists of power plants that have requested a connection to the system. In recent years these queues have been dominated by renewables like wind and solar power, which are generally the cheapest way to build new energy capacity today.

Sources indicate that but there’s been a shift in some places, particularly for PJM, the country’s largest power grid operator, which covers parts of 13 states in an area stretching from Illinois to Washington, DC. PJM has seen a major uptick in natural-gas proposals. In August, the operator announced that it had accepted proposals for 715 total projects with a capacity of over 200 gigawatts to join its queue. Nearly half that total capacity represented natural-gas plants.

If all the projects in the queue were built, it would increase the fossil-fuel share on PJM’s grid from 61% to 67%, according to an analysis by Kevin Kircher, an energy modeler and assistant professor of mechanical engineering at Purdue.

PJM’s queue is reflective of a bigger trend across the US. Natural gas is already the single biggest source of electricity in the country, and it has basically become the default to meet exploding power demand.

Sources indicate that this AI-driven rush is already affecting greenhouse-gas emissions for tech companies. Meta’s emissions are up 45% over last year’s, according to the tech giant’s recently released 2026 Sustainability Report. That’s even higher than the jumps from fellow hyperscalers Google (18%), Amazon (16%), and Microsoft (25%). The company is also “reevaluating” its 2030 goal to reach net-zero emissions, according to the report, though there aren’t details about how or when it might reach a decision there.

And there’s another tidbit in that Meta report worth noting: The organization said that when the grid can’t deliver power “on the necessary timeline,” it will look to other methods, including building its own on-site power generation.

This fits into a related and growing trend that I’ve been tracking recently: Data center developers are looking to bypass long wait times to hook up to the grid by building their own power plants instead.

It’s not new for large industrial facilities to supply their own power. Chemical plants often have natural-gas infrastructure on-site to generate electricity and heat, for example (though they’re generally connected to the grid as well). Mining operations are often remote, requiring an off-grid power source.

But the desire to come online quickly means there could be a massive boom in off-grid power plants for data centers. Over 100 such facilities have been proposed in the US, with a total capacity of 115 gigawatts (that’s more than double the entire peak power demand of California). The vast majority of these sites have been proposed since the beginning of 2025.

There are alternative ways to help meet growing electricity demand: Wind and solar are inexpensive options, and pairing them with batteries could improve their reliability, for example. I’ve also written before about demand flexibility—efforts to limit power drawn from the grid when demand is high.

Right now, though, the evidence continues to stack up that the AI boom in the US is basically synonymous with a natural-gas boom.
