Key Takeaways From Luxury’s Q3 Earnings

🌐 Source: Vogue • 📅 16 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 9 min read (1898 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

China’s macro environment has been a major drag on luxury’s third-quarter earnings, causing most companies to fall short of market expectations.

Analysts expected growth to slow in Q3, but conditions in China turned out worse than anticipated. The sector’s average organic sales growth ended at 4% in Q3 2026, versus 7.8% in Q2 (calculated by Goldman Sachs on the basis of a simple average of luxury companies, excluding currency impact).

Industry observers note that kering stock plummeted 17%, following the announcement that growth at the tech giant had dropped to 1% in Q3 (vs +2% in Q2), including a 6% decrease at its largest brand, Gucci. (Gucci retail sales declined by 9% on a comparable basis.) Kering and LVMH stock are each down 37% year to date, while the Hermès share price has fallen 36%.

Industry observers note that “Everyone is staying on the sidelines,” Goldman Sachs luxury analyst Erwan Rambourg says, referring to investors’ cautious stance. While he believes that Q3 marks the trough in the luxury downturn, he thinks that investors prefer to wait for tangible signs of an improvement rather than catch a falling knife.

It has been revealed that and after a stellar performance in the first half of the year, jewelry brands began decelerating in Q3. Sales at Richemont’s jewelry maisons, including Cartier and Van Cleef & Arpels, rose 14% versus 24% in Q2. Sales at LVMH’s watches and jewelry division and Kering’s jewelry arm, the latter of which includes Boucheron and Pomellato, were up 6% and 11%, respectively (vs 11% and 18% in Q2). Sales at other Hermès sectors, including jewelry and home, were down 1% (vs 4% in Q2).

Sources indicate that “Richemont’s jewelry maisons growing 14% in the fiscal second quarter is a slowdown, but it’s still tremendously better than pretty much anyone else in the sector,” Rambourg flags. “That gives a first-half average of just below 20% top-line growth for jewelry, which is the core profit driver of the firm. For the first time in a while, margins have increased.”.

Here are the rest of the key takeaways from luxury’s Q3 earnings.

China’s slowdown deepened after three years of deceleration. Growth at LVMH decreased 5% in Asia, while Kering fell 8% and Hermès came in flat. “It should be noted that the macroeconomic environment [in China] remains unfavorable,” Hermès’s VP of finance Eric du Halgouët told analysts during the company’s earnings call on October 22.

While the broader picture in China remained largely unchanged, with continued weakness in the real estate market, two additional factors weighed on bottom lines: the decline in equity market performance, and China’s upcoming taxation of foreign assets. The law on taxation, which was announced in late July and applies to offshore trusts, a widely used vehicle among high-net-worth Chinese individuals, had a significant impact on the sentiment of affluent consumers; the rules have retroactive effect from 2023, and the deadline to declare and pay was just 90 days.

“The two growth engines of the industry for the past 20 years are the middle-income consumer and the Chinese consumer,” claims Morgan Stanley managing director Édouard Aubin. These two engines are now barely running.

Using LVMH as a proxy, the Chinese cohort was flattish in Q2 and down approximately 15% in Q3. Richemont also highlighted the challenges in the mainland China market, despite seeing a positive trend in Greater China. “ If anybody thinks we’re less affected by [market conditions in] China, on the contrary,” chairman Johann Rupert told reporters during a media call on Friday.

Industry observers note that some soft luxury brands are still doing well, including in China. Ralph Lauren’s Asia revenue saw 28% growth in Q3. Greater China led retail growth for Burberry (+5%), and the Greater China region was up 5.7% for Zegna Group. In the private space, Chanel and Longchamp are also understood to perform well in China.

According to recent reports, one category doing surprisingly well in China is prestige beauty. L’Oréal Group posted 7.8% growth in North Asia in Q3. “North Asia was back to high-single-digit growth for the first time since the first half of 2022. This marks a step change in L’Oréal’s growth algorithm in our view,” HSBC analysts, including Olivier Nicolaï, wrote in a note. Puig revenues rose 12.9% in Asia-Pacific. Does it suggest that Chinese customers are trading down — buying lower-ticket items like fragrances and skincare?

And is there a brighter outlook ahead for luxury brands in China? “There aren’t many hopes for things to improve in the short term,” Rambourg says. “I think China being very tough is probably going to be the case for the next two or three quarters. Visibility is quite poor. And if you leave aside bases of comparison, there’s no key underlying reason for China to do better.”.

“Q3 was a continuation of the K-shaped economy we’ve seen in recent quarters — where the top end grows, but the lower end lags as the middle-income consumer has been priced out. The brands at the top of the pyramid are doing well; the rest are not,” Aubin notes.

Zegna brand (+12.7% in Q3), Brunello Cucinelli (+13.5% in Q3), Loro Piana (“enjoying very strong growth,” according to LVMH CFO Cécile Cabanis) and Chanel (up double digits in Q1, Q2 and Q3, according to industry sources) are in the former category.

According to recent reports, at LVMH, Cabanis noted that Louis Vuitton sales came in just behind the group’s fashion division at -5%, while Dior was flat. Rambourg remains bullish on Dior. “One, Dior is more exposed to China than some other brands, so that’s painful. Two, they have a men’s business, which is not doing as well as women’s. Three, I think it takes a bit more time for [creative director] Jonathan Anderson’s products to gain weight within the brand, but it’s certainly coming,” he explains.

Louis Vuitton felt the repercussions of both its legal dispute with Chinese tea brand Molly Tea and an matter with a shipping partner — the latter weighed on quarterly growth by a little over one percentage point. Add to that a tough year-on-year comparison basis: Louis Vuitton opened a boat-shaped mega-store in Shanghai in June 2025, dubbed The Louis, which generated a surge in foot traffic. The brand is undergoing a change in strategy, according to Rambourg: “They’re in the process of moving under the influence of deputy CEO Damien Bertrand, from what Louis Vuitton was 15 months ago, which was colorful, cheerful, fashion-driven, to something more institutional, more focused on the origin of the brand, which is the art of travel.”.

A Shanghai Fashion Week attendee wears a full Louis Vuitton look.

Kering’s recent creative changes were on the agenda of the group’s earnings call. Shortly before its Q3 earnings, Kering announced the departures of Anthony Vaccarello from Saint Laurent and Seán McGirr from McQueen. And on November 5 and 6, the group announced the departure of Pierpaolo Piccioli from Balenciaga, with Martine Rose to succeed him.

While still outperforming the sector, Hermès’s growth decelerated in Q3 (+4.3% in Q3 vs +6.7% in Q2). “While Hermès’s leather goods category re-accelerated to nearly 11%, some investors questioned whether the weakness in other categories, such as ready-to-wear and accessories, could foreshadow a similar slowdown in leather goods,” Barclays director Carole Madjo says. Risk of [handbag market] saturation in China and the increasingly structured secondhand market could potentially weigh on demand over time.”.

After years of Miu Miu outperforming Prada, the trend reversed in Q2, with Prada (6.3%) surpassing Miu Miu (2.6%), and continued into Q3, as Prada grew 1% while Miu Miu contracted 3%, likely due to a higher exposure to China and the Middle East  relative to the average of the sector.

Rambourg also stressed the important polarization across brands: “In the soft luxury space, we consider Chanel is now the biggest brand — bigger than Louis Vuitton — and growing very quickly. And so that’s taking a bit of the air from the others, notably adjacent brands like Hermès. In hard luxury, Cartier is continuing to do very well and is also taking a bit of air away from their competitors. So in both soft and hard luxury, you have one dominant brand which counterintuitively is growing very quickly.”.

Industry observers note that the US might remain a luxury growth driver, but things began to decelerate this quarter, partly because of a tougher comparison basis. Hermès was up 8.8% in the Americas in Q3 (vs 13.7% in Q2), and Kering’s North American retail revenue rose 1% (vs 10% in Q2). “Investors are questioning whether this signals the beginning of a normalization in the US, and whether it will be confirmed in Q4 and beyond,” Barclays’ Madjo states.

According to recent reports, goldman Sachs’s Rambourg dismissed concerns: “I do believe that the little softness in Q3 is temporary. I foresee a re-acceleration in Q4 linked to the easier comparison basis and the reality that holiday campaigns, newly introduced products and more thoughtful pricing should motivate people to buy. Not to mention that you had a lot of wealth creation from equity markets. Luxury demand has been a bit lagging, but I think it’s bound to pick up.”.

It is unclear whether the US midterm elections — which saw the Democrats take control of Congress for the remainder of Trump’s presidency — impacted the third quarter. Some customers possibly adopted a wait-and-see approach to spending ahead of the results, which could now be released and influence Q4. “Midterm elections typically have less impact than presidential elections,” Madjo notes. Asked what impact the outcome might have on Richemont’s business, CFO Burkhart Grund remarked: “We’re just a few days after the midterm elections and we haven’t really picked up anything meaningful.”.

Recent findings suggest that europe was up 4% for LVMH, down 5% for Kering’s fashion and leather goods division (including a 9% drop for Gucci), and up 7.2% for Hermès. “It is Europeans who are spending in Europe,” said Hermès’s Eric du Halgouët. Hermès’s sales in France decreased in Q3 to 3.6% from 6.2% in Q2, as the heatwave impacted footfall in the Parisian stores.

In a recent development, as brands began gearing up for the crucial holiday season, protests erupted across France, Italy, Spain, Belgium and Portugal. France and Italy are significant European markets. France accounted for 8% of LVMH’s total revenue in the first nine months of 2026 and 9.6% of Hermès’s nine-month revenues. “The comments on the impact of the protests in Europe were quite vague. It’s too hard to say and hard to quantify,” Madjo states.

LVMH’s earnings call took place before the so-called Act I, the day of major urban unrest on October 17. On October 22, Hermès’s du Halgouët noted it was too early to assess impact on local stores’ business and tourism. (The Hermès store in Marseille was looted and store windows in Paris, Nantes and Lyon were broken.) “It’s not just a China problem. Now, you have a French-Italian problem on top of that,” Aubin notes, referring to the possible effects on Q4.

Recent findings suggest that despite the lack of visibility in China and Europe, Goldman Sachs’s Rambourg anticipates a progression in Q4 and next year. “Because we see the US re-accelerating, and importantly, we see self-help measures — product launches and better pricing — motivating consumers to come back,” he says.
