4 ways to check a suspected romance scammer, from the FTC's advice

🌐 Source: Qz • 📅 16 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 8 min read (1642 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

  • The danger in a romance scam is rarely one convincing lie.
  • It's tempting to think only the frail or naive fall for this.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The FTC says to stop contact, talk to someone you trust, search the person's job plus "scammer," and check their photo.

The danger in a romance scam is rarely one convincing lie. It is a whole relationship built so that nobody else ever gets a look at it. That isolation is deliberate. Scammers isolate their targets from loved ones, according to a 2018 paper by Prof. Monica Whitty of the WMG Cyber Security Centre at the University of Warwick in Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking. ABC News reports that scammers also tell victims to move their conversations to encrypted messaging platforms. The courtship then takes place in a private channel that no friend or relative can see. Only one check on the relationship remains, which is the target's own judgment, and the scammer has spent weeks working on exactly that.

It's tempting to think only the frail or naive fall for this. The evidence demonstrates that people of many ages get caught, and it disagrees about where the worst risk lies. Many victims are middle-aged. In a U.K. study published in 2018 in Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking, Whitty finds that 63% of romance-scam victims were between 35 and 54. Older people still lose heavily, though. In Australia, people 65 and over lost $11.7 million, more than any other age group, according to ABC News. The two findings point to different ages as the greatest risk, and neither suggests that any age is safe. Whoever the victim is, embarrassment can stop them as surely as the scammer can. Western Australia Attorney General Tony Buti told ABC News that a "shame factor" is at work. In the U.S., the National Institute on Aging tells people who suspect a scam that they have no reason for embarrassment, because anyone can be fooled. Officials in two countries treat shame as a real obstacle, and the silence it produces is what a scammer depends on. A warning sign helps only if the person who spots it feels free to act on it without humiliation.

The Federal Trade Commission laid out what to do when you suspect a upcoming love interest is a scammer, from checking whether a profile photo belongs to someone else to searching the job the person claims to hold. Each of its steps lets someone outside the relationship look in. That person might be a friend, a stranger who has heard the same story, or the real owner of the face in the photo. All of the steps serve one firm rule from the FTC: nobody should send cash or presents to an online partner they have never met face to face. Each step is a way to keep to that rule before any money changes hands.

The moment you first suspect a newly introduced love interest is a scammer is the moment to stop talking to them. Most people want one more conversation, a chance to ask the hard question, hear the explanation and settle it face to face, or at least screen to screen. The Federal Trade Commission notes that cutting off the person right away should be the first move once you suspect fraud, and that you should stop communicating immediately. Someone running a scam may use threats or pressure a target to act immediately, according to the National Institute on Aging. A final conversation lets the scammer keep setting the pace, and it gives that pressure one more chance to work. Going silent without an explanation feels rude, but it is the one move that lets the target, not the scammer, decide what happens next.

The real reason to stop now is what the scammer is likely to ask for next. Romance scammers ask to be paid with gift cards, wire transfers or cryptocurrency. Money sent by transfer or gift card usually cannot be recovered, according to the National Institute on Aging. A request for either one is a request for money that will most likely be gone for good once it is sent. That leaves the two possible mistakes far from equal. Cutting off someone who turns out to be genuine costs an awkward apology later, while sending a transfer or gift card to someone who isn't usually means losing that money. Silence is only a pause, though. Once contact stops, the next job is to let someone outside the relationship look at what happened.

Telling a trusted friend or relative about a new love interest does the one thing a romance scammer works hardest to prevent. Most scam advice asks you to find the flaw in the other person. This step asks for the judgment of someone the scammer has never had a chance to work on. The Federal Trade Commission advises people to talk to someone they trust and to ask whether friends or family are worried about the new partner. ABC News reports that scammers may give the opposite order and tell their targets not to mention the relationship to friends and family. The FTC's question asks what the people who know you think of the new partner. Those people were never let into the courtship, so they can judge it from outside. A request to keep a new partner secret is therefore not a sign of closeness. It is a warning sign, and it is the moment to call someone.

A conversation with a friend costs nothing, while keeping quiet can cost a great deal. In Western Australia, 63 people lost about $3.8 million to romance scams in 2025, according to ABC News. Those are serious losses for the people who suffered them, and an awkward talk is small by comparison. The talk helps most early, while a friend can hear the story for the first time and before the relationship comes with a request for money.

A friend's worry about a new partner is exactly what the FTC asks you to listen for.

The job a upcoming love interest describes is often the excuse for why the two of you can never meet. The Federal Trade Commission lists working on an oil rig and serving in the military among the reasons scammers give for not meeting in person. That makes the job the part of the story worth checking first. Scammers tell the same story to many people at once, and one scheme involving Akpieyi and Hall reached 31 victims, according to Fox 8. Checking takes no special tools. Anyone can type the claimed job and the word "scammer" into a search engine and see whether other people have posted similar stories about oil rig workers or Army impostors. When those posts describe your story almost word for word, the person was most likely using the same story on other targets.

Over time, the cover story also becomes the reason the person needs money. The request sounds like more of the life the person has already described. "They'll start talking about the need for money in whatever project or in their life and just sort of make references to it, so it looks like it's just normally part of conversation," former FBI agent and attorney Mike Zummer told Fox 8. The best moment to search is when the job first comes up, before the person has asked for anything and before the story feels too familiar to question.

According to recent reports, an arrest can come years after a scheme begins. The scheme involving Akpieyi started around April 2020, and the FBI did not catch Akpieyi until 2023, at a Georgia bank, according to Fox 8. A search takes minutes, and it lets a target test the story long before any investigation reaches the person behind it. A person can also run this check without telling anyone, and someone who finds a match has solid reason to bring a friend or relative in.

A scammer can make up a job, a backstory and a way of talking, but the face in the profile usually belongs to a real person somewhere else. The Federal Trade Commission indicates that a reverse image search can show whether a profile picture belongs to another name or appears beside details that don't match, and it treats either result as a sign of a scam. Scammers build their profiles with stolen photographs, according to research by Whitty in Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking. A mismatched photo is therefore no fluke, because it comes from the way these profiles are made. The outside view on this step comes from the real owner of the face, who never agreed to take part in the romance at all. A gut feeling depends on how much the target already trusts the suitor, but this check does not. The picture either belongs to the person in the chat or it belongs to someone else.

Running the search takes little effort, but stopping to doubt someone who has spent weeks making you feel wanted takes much more. Victims of romance scams tend to act on impulse, which is the opposite of the pause this check needs. People in the U.K. who had been taken in by a romance scam showed a stronger tendency toward rash action than people who had never been scammed, according to Whitty's research published in 2018 in Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking. A safety step that depends on stopping to check asks for a habit that runs against a trait many victims share. The search is worth running early, before attachment makes checking the photo feel like a betrayal. The Federal Trade Commission names Instagram and Facebook $META among the places where scammers get in touch with people, so a photo is worth checking even when the stranger writes through an ordinary social media account.

The moment a stranger's attractive photo starts a conversation is the moment to check the face, well before anyone asks for money.
