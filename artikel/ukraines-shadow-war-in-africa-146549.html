The highlights this week: Ukraine attacks Russian vessels in Libya in “response to Russia’s war,” Gambia wins a landmark case at the International Court of Justice, and African nations respond to a disappointing United Nations climate summit.

Industry observers note that kyiv claimed responsibility on Monday for a recent attack on two Russian vessels off the coast of Libya, including the corvette Merkury, one of Russia’s newest warships. The vessels had been docked at the Benghazi naval base when the attack occurred on Sunday.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky has described the operation as “exemplary” and noted that it was in “response to Russia’s war.”.

Yet security experts warn that conducting drone strikes within a region that is not involved in the Russia-Ukraine war could set a dangerous precedent, especially as they fear Africa is becoming a proxy theater in the conflict.

In a recent development, the United Nations mission in Libya, known as UNSMIL, has called for “an immediate, independent and transparent investigation” into the drone strikes. “Libya cannot afford, and must not become, a battleground for foreign conflicts,” UNSMIL wrote on X.

In a recent development, kyiv has confirmed that seven Ukrainian-made naval drones had been rolled out from a “mothership” in the region, though it did not clarify how they were transported there. Some unverified Libyan sources have alleged that Tripoli’s U.N.-recognized government may have aided Ukraine, which Tripoli strongly denied.

It has been revealed that both of Libya’s rival administrations—the government in Tripoli, led by Prime Minister Abdul Hamid Dbeibah, and a Russia-backed government based in Benghazi, controlled by Gen. Khalifa Haftar—have denounced the attacks.

Industry observers note that following the 2024 fall of the Assad regime in Syria, where Russia operates a naval base, Moscow has sought alternative military footprints in Africa, including by pursuing airbases in eastern Libya. Syria served as a logistical hub for transferring troops and arms to landlocked Sahel nations, where Russia’s mercenary Africa Corps helps fight jihadists.

With Syria growing closer to the United States and Europe, Libya now serves as that hub, explained Ulf Laessing, the head of the Syria/Iraq program at the Konrad-Adenauer-Stiftung and the author of Understanding Libya Since Gaddafi.

It has been revealed that “Ukraine is trying to hit Russia wherever they can. It’s difficult for Ukraine to hit Russian targets where the Africa Corps is based in the Sahel,” Laessing said. “It’s more efficient and easier to do it where Russia is basically bringing goods.”.

Zain Hussain, a researcher at the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute, said that “through this attack, Ukraine is showing that it can target Russia’s geopolitical interests as well as fighting a war with Russia at home.”.

Recent findings suggest that the strikes come amid increasing concern that the war in Ukraine is spreading to other African countries. Ukrainian drone specialists have been deployed to Libya, Mali, and Sudan, according to a September CNN report. A source also told CNN that troops from Chad, Niger, and Burkina Faso had traveled to Mali to receive drone training from Ukrainian soldiers, though several experts told me that the evidence for this is speculative at best.

Meanwhile, in the Sahel, multiple media reports have accused Ukraine of supporting and training northern Mali’s Tuareg separatists in recent years as a means of wearing down Russian forces there.

Sources indicate that experts suspect Ukrainian drone assistance in the Tuareg ambush that killed dozens of Russian Wagner mercenaries and Malian soldiers in 2024. At the time, Andriy Yusov, a spokesperson for Ukrainian military intelligence, mentioned that the rebels had been given the “necessary” information to carry out the attacks. Yusov’s comments prompted Mali to sever relations with Ukraine, accusing Kyiv of supporting a “barbaric” attack by terrorists.

Industry observers note that analysts also suspected Ukrainian involvement in the brief insurgent takeover of Mali’s strategic town of Kidal earlier this year by Tuareg and al Qaeda-linked jihadists. Ukraine’s Foreign Ministry has officially rejected the allegations.

Ukraine’s latest attacks are unlikely to disrupt eastern Libya’s ties with Russia, Laessing stated, especially as Moscow needs “a country on the way to the Sahel.” Still, they add a layer of complexity to long-standing violence and drone warfare in Sahelian nations, said Virginie Baudais, the director of the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute’s Sahel and West Africa program.

As Baudais put it, the “internationalization of the conflict” in the Sahel will only contribute to “the escalation of violence, with the highest cost for civilian populations.”.

Friday, Nov. 27: The deadline for councils in South Africa to elect their leaders.

Somalia-UAE rift. Last week, Somalia expelled the United Arab Emirates’ ambassador and recalled its own envoy from Abu Dhabi, citing ​violations of the country’s sovereignty that amounted to an “open invasion.”.

It has been revealed that the dispute stems from growing Emirati ties with Ethiopia and the Somalian breakaway regions of Somaliland and Puntland. Somali President Hassan Sheikh Mohamud also accused the UAE of having played a role in Israel’s recognition of Somaliland last December in a statement to Somalian state news agency SONNA.

The UAE’s state-owned DP World and its subsidiaries operate ports in those breakaway regions. They have expanded the Berbera port in Somaliland and are developing a road corridor connecting it to landlocked Ethiopia. In January 2024, Addis Ababa signed a memorandum of understanding to eventually recognize Somaliland’s independence in exchange for naval and commercial access to Berbera.

A New York Times report using satellite imagery recently found that the UAE has turned airports in Berbera and Bosaso, a city in Puntland, into military facilities.

In response to Somalia’s latest move, the UAE noted its embassy in Somalia had been closed since January, after Mogadishu canceled bilateral agreements, and it strongly denounced the “escalatory and hostile approach” by the Somali government amid the latest diplomatic spat.

Recent findings suggest that ICJ ruling. Gambia, the smallest nation in mainland Africa, won a landmark case last week when the International Court of Justice (ICJ) ruled that Myanmar committed genocide in its 2017 military campaign that killed at least 10,000 members of the Rohingya Muslim minority community and displaced more than 700,000 into neighboring Bangladesh.

A Muslim-majority nation, Gambia brought the case to the court in 2019. Now, the ruling has possible implications for South Africa’s ICJ genocide case against Israel for its operations in Gaza.

In a recent development, both Myanmar and Israel have defended their actions as counterinsurgency operations. But in its decision, the court considered a pattern of mass killings, the razing of homes, and forced exodus. That finding “gives South Africa more avenues ​to argue for genocide” in Gaza, Israeli legal expert Yuval Shany told Reuters.

COP31’s failures. This year’s U.N. climate summit, known as COP31, in Turkey ended on Sunday without an agreement after references to phasing out fossil fuels were removed from the draft text. Despite being the most attended COP to date, some observers have called it the “worst” one ever.

Industry observers note that at the summit, African delegations voiced discontent with financial commitments for climate adaptation. Wealthier nations pledged only $50 million in total to the climate adaptation fund, far short of the $300 million annual target. They also largely offered loans rather than grants. The United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, France, Italy, and Australia did not make any funding commitments.

Industry observers note that africa, which is home to 17 percent of the world’s population, is responsible for just 4 percent of global carbon emissions. Yet the continent faces disproportionate impacts from climate change.

The African Group of Negotiators (AGN), chaired by Ghana, rejected attempts to weaken both finance targets and the Paris Agreement’s goal of limiting warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius. It said that the debate on fossil fuels had “overshadowed” broken promises on adaptation finance.

Recent findings suggest that “Those who were arguing about whether we should get fossil fuels in the cover decision or not were producers, exporters, importers and users of fossil fuels,” AGN chair Antwi-Boasiako Amoah said. “It was hypocrisy.”.

It has been revealed that AFCON verdict. Senegal was reinstated as the 2025 Africa Cup of Nations champion in a much-anticipated ruling by the Court of Arbitration for Sport on Tuesday.

Industry observers note that senegal initially won the final against Morocco 1-0 in extra time in January. Two months later, however, the Confederation of African Football (CAF) stripped the country of its title, awarding it to Morocco because Senegalese players temporarily walked off the pitch to protest refereeing decisions.

According to recent reports, the court overturned CAF’s decision because the match was allowed to conclude. “By blowing his whistle to restart play, the Referee considered that the conditions for resuming the match had been met,” the court said in a statement. Morocco plans to appeal the decision.

In a recent development, sudanese excavations. Last week, archaeologists unearthed the original floor of the largest medieval church in Nubia, in northern Sudan’s ancient city of Old Dongola. It was part of Makuria, one of three Christian Nubian kingdoms that existed from the sixth to 16th centuries.

The church was first discovered in 2021, but excavations were suspended two years later following the outbreak of Sudan’s civil war. Operations resumed last year in the abandoned city, which has managed to escape widespread destruction from the conflict so far.

UNESCO estimated that more than 100 cultural sites have been damaged, looted, or destroyed since fighting began in Sudan in April 2023.

Troll farmers. In HumAngle, Shade Mary-Ann Olaoye reports on U.S.-based social media influencers exploiting false narratives of a Christian genocide in Nigeria for engagement and donations. These accounts have posted artificial intelligence-generated content and videos from elsewhere in Africa, including Somalia, as supposed evidence of persecution in Nigeria.

In a recent development, as the creators “continue to spread misinformation, concerns are growing that their online influence could have real-world consequences,” Olaoye writes. “Campaigns like these can deepen ethno-religious fault lines, shape political decisions, and fuel reprisal attacks.”.

AI bioweapons. In Rest of World, Danai Nesta Kupemba reports on researchers at a small AI lab in Ghana who “jailbroke” their own drug-discovery AI model, which allowed them to quickly design thousands of toxic molecules, to warn about the potential for AI bioweapons misuse.

“Scholars have long warned about AI’s bioweapons threat, but it is only recently that AI systems have truly outperformed Ph.D.-level biologists,” Kupemba writes.
