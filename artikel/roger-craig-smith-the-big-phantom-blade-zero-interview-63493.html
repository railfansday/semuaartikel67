Sources indicate that chances are, if you’ve played a video game, you’ve heard the voice of Roger Craig Smith. He’s the video game voice of Sonic the Hedgehog, plays Chris Redfield in the Resident Evil series, and portrays Ezio Auditore da Firenze in Assassin’s Creed.

His 20-year career has seen him appear in almost 200 video games, playing original characters, dubbing international ones, and voicing many of the medium’s greatest stories.

His latest role comes as the English dub of Phantom Blade Zero’s protagonist, Soul. Following the overnight success of the Game of the Year contender, we sat down with Craig Smith to discuss it, including how it came to be last minute, as well as, of course, some chat about Sonic.

It has been revealed that jia Xiongkai is the voice of Phantom Blade Zero’s Soul in its native Chinese. For the longest time, Xiongkai’s English counterpart was Jay Makensuk; however, at the last minute, Craig Smith stepped into the character’s shoes. I had to find out why.

Sources indicate that “It was, I guess, late into the game. But that's also fairly common for what this effectively was, which was a localization,” he tells me. “The game would have essentially been done before this happened. It's funny, I guess it's maybe a little unorthodox for a video game, but not so much for a dubbing project.”.

How much did he know about replacing Makensuk, though?

“The elephant in the room is the fact that the early dev examples released featured another actor. I don't know the circumstances, but obviously someone else was in the role before that,” he says. “I honestly don’t know why it was, this stuff just happens in the business. I’ve had it go against me in the same way. You’re doing something, and all of a sudden, somebody decides they want to change course.”.

It has been revealed that “I’ve done vertical slices where I’ve thought that I was going to be the final choice, and it was like ‘maybe not, maybe this was just to help obtain the financing they wanted for the game’. I’ve had things taken away from me at the last minute; I’ve had things fall into my lap, but I never celebrate until the check clears the bank and the project is released.”.

Despite playing the English version of Soul, Craig Smith feels it’s equally as notable, if not more, to play through the game in its native language, even if that means not hearing his voice.

“If you want to understand what the tone was intended to be and to understand and even broaden your horizons culturally, to kind of hear cadences and delivery styles and the way that protagonists are approached in different cultures, you would want to experience it in that way first,” he says. “I say that as somebody who's been lucky enough to be hired to be the English dubbing voice for tons of games.”.

Phantom Blade Zero is rooted in Chinese culture and folklore; does this make voicing a character like Soul more difficult?

“It’s really like threading a needle,” he tells me. “You need to understand the source material and the performance style while trying to blend in more of an English-speaking style of dialogue. You have to try and make sure it works a little bit better for the English-speaking ear.”.

“It’s a fascinating part of what we do, trying to make sure all markets are covered, and that people get to experience things in the best way possible for them”.

Dubbing is a major part of what Craig Smith does. He’s the English voice of Daigo Dojima in the Yakuza series, and has appeared in Naruto, Bleach, and Aggretsuko. How different is dubbing a character from being the original voice?

“It’s very different. You’re not creating the character, it’s more of an homage,” he claims. “You're trying to make sure that you're not stepping on the toes of anything. I don't know that you necessarily get to make it your own. You're trying to give reverence to an original performer and yet also do something in a style that makes sense for maybe localizing”.

It has been revealed that I love to have a deeper understanding, especially at this phase of my career. I want to give my all for the audience.

Recent findings suggest that craig Smith mentioned earlier that projects often come about last minute. It reminded me of a conversation I had 18 months ago with Alix Wilton Regan, Aliona Baranova, Rasmus Hardiker, and Pepe Balderrama about Lies of P. Two of the actors preferred to get their scripts in advance to prepare their lines, while the other two preferred to dive straight into them in the booth—which did Craig Smith prefer, the ones with more time to prepare or the last-minute projects?

Recent findings suggest that “Both have their pluses and minuses, I suppose. There can be an energetic element to the latter approach of just jumping in and going for it. And certain projects really lend themselves to that,” he claims. “Now that I'm 20-plus years in the business, I very often ask for stuff in advance. I love to get stuff because I want to come in with some choices. I want to understand some things. It depends on the project. It depends on the character. But I love to have a deeper understanding, especially at this phase of my career. I want to give my all for the audience.”.

With Craig Smith’s role in Phantom Blade Zero very much falling into the “dive straight into the booth” camp, was there anything he saw that made him think that the game would be as much of a runaway success that it is?

“I couldn’t believe it. It surprises me that anything gets an audience these days with every device vying for our time, so it’s amazing, but with this game, it wasn’t necessarily a surprise,” he tells me. “It’s right in line with how much work the development team put in. It’s aesthetically beautiful, the fight mechanics are incredible, and there is so much to chew on with the story. Maybe I’m a little surprised, but I’m just so thrilled, I really am.”.

Perhaps Craig Smith’s most notable role is the video game version of Sonic the Hedgehog. He’s played the character for 16 years, first appearing in Sonic Colors and Sonic Free Riders back in 2010. For many, he is the Blue Blur, at least until Ben Schwartz stepped into his massive red shoes for the live-action silver-screen adaptation.

Smith has always been super complimentary about Schwartz’s version of the character, but he’s never explicitly noted whether he was sad that he didn’t get the role, so I had to ask.

“What do you think? I mean, what do you think? I mean, like, no, I don't feel like being a part of some of the biggest movies on Earth,” he indicates chuckling. “It’s like asking me if I’d come back as Ezio in Assassin’s Creed. What do you think?”.

Sources indicate that “I mean, as actors we're inherently envious and jealous and, you know, we want to do it all. We want so bad for you to like us, you know. So, of course, I would have. That's always been my biggest hurt, that I never even got a chance, that it was not even an option to get in and have a meeting or anything. It just never happened. The industry's weird, but I get it. It is what it is. But yes, of course, I would have killed for a chance to get in there. I get I might not be right for you, but having the opportunity is the first and foremost thing for me.”.

He couldn’t talk about the Sonic Movie version of the character without heaping praise on Schwartz, though.

Recent findings suggest that “I've stated so many times, I'm like, you couldn't ask for a more wonderful human being and a more talented, creative genius in Ben Schwartz to be in the role, doing such a great job with it and instantly understanding the fan base. There’s so much attention on this franchise again, and a big part of that is the success of the films. It’s really reinvigorated the entire brand.”.

Recent findings suggest that there is nothing but good, good stuff. I look back fondly at every role.

With my time with Craig Smith running short, I wanted to ask more about his illustrious career. He’s touched many of gaming’s biggest franchises, but there are still a lot that remain free of his voice. Is there any he’s desperate to appear in, or sad that he hasn’t?

In a recent development, “No, not really. And what an unbelievably fortunate position to be in to say that. I look at it like lightning has struck many times in my life. And I'm very, very lucky,” he claims. “Maybe deep down there's some stuff in there. But the reality is, I would not be doing myself any service by dwelling on that. And instead just pinching myself repeatedly and going, ‘can you believe that you woke up on your 30th birthday with essentially no career?’”.

“I've reached a certain phase and an age where I just go. I cannot believe this. There is nothing but good, good stuff. I look back fondly at every role, and my attitude always remains the same: I just want to make everybody on the other side of the glass happy.”.
