Latest Update: Who Sat for Jan van Eyck’s Arnolfini Portrait? A Fresh Catalog Essay Suggests It May Have Been the Artist

The catalog for the exhibition “Van Eyck: The Portraits” opening at London’s National Gallery on November 21, has reignited a long-standing debate about the identity of couple in the Early Netherlandish master’s mysterious Arnolfini Portrait (1434). As reported in the Art Newspaper, one of the catalog’s essays suggests that the man in the painting, usually identified as a member of the Arnolfini family of Italian merchants, is in fact Jan van Eyck himself.

Sources indicate that in the painting, which is owned by the National Gallery and a centerpiece of the show, an elegantly dressed man and woman pose hand in hand in a private room. Every item in the chamber, including the couple’s fur-trimmed clothing, the oriental carpet on the floor, and even the orange resting on a windowsill—an expensive luxury at the time—signals understated wealth. In a recent development, the man and the woman are clearly husband and wife, though the work does not, as was once believed, depict a marriage ceremony. A 16th-century inventory of the holdings of Margaret of Austria, the painting’s owner at the time, identifies it as “Hernoul le Fin” or “Arnoult Fin,” which is widely interpreted by art historians as “Arnolfini.”.

According to recent reports, scholars have disagreed however, however, about which Arnolfini the man in the painting might be. Possible candidates are either Giovanni di Arrigo Arnolfini (though recent discoveries suggest he was not married at the time the painting was made), or more likely his cousin, Giovanni di Nicolao Arnolfini, who would have been in his late thirties in 1434.

Giovanni’s first wife Costanza Trenta died in 1433; thus, according to the National Gallery’s website, the woman is probably his second wife, whose identity is unknown. Alternatively, as art historian Margaret Koster and others have suggested, the painting may be a memorial piece.

It is also possible that the sitters were friends of Van Eyck’s. Reflected in the convex mirror behind them, two more men can be seen entering the room. Prominently written on the wall above the mirror is the inscription “Johannes de Eyck fuit hic. 1434” (“Jan van Eyck was here. 1434”), which is sometimes taken to mean that the men in the mirror are Van Eyck and his servant arriving for a visit.

But Berlin-based curator Stephan Kemperdick, writing in one of the catalog’s essays, asserts instead that the subjects of the painting are Van Eyck and his wife Margaret. Kemperdick is a curator at the Berlin Gemäldegalerie, which owns several Van Eycks, including Portrait of a Man in a Red Chaperon (1390–1440/41) of the same man in the Arnolfini Portrait at an older age. He bases his theory on the inscription’s placement in the Arnolfini Portrait next to the male figure’s head, arguing that this indicates a direct connection between Van Eyck and the man.

Sources indicate that if true, Kemperdick’s theory would throw the identification of the subjects in other Van Eyck paintings into doubt. The National Gallery’s Portrait of a Man (1433), of a much older man than the man in the Arnolfini Portrait, is presently tentatively identified as a self-portrait, while the entry for the Berlin work—Kemperdick’s theory notwithstanding—still notes that it is presumed to be of Giovanni Arnolfini. (Both Portrait of a Man (Self-Portrait?) and Portrait of a Man in a Red Chaperon (Giovanni Arnolfini?) are included in the exhibition, which brings together all nine of Van Eyck’s surviving portraits.).

For his part, Nicholas Flory, the exhibition’s co-curator along with Emma Capron, told the Art Newspaper that he found Kemperdick’s theory “interesting” but “speculative.” He notes that the sitter in another work in the show from the Musea Brugge, definitively known to be of Margaret based on an inscription, has greyish-blue eyes while the woman in the Arnolfini Portrait has brown eyes.

German art historian Till-Holger Borchert, the former director of the Musea Brugge—concurs, finding the 16th-century inventory reference near conclusive proof that the painting is of an Arnolfini. But, he added, given how little is known about Van Eyck’s life, “Can you tell me anything about Van Eyck that isn’t enigmatic?”.
