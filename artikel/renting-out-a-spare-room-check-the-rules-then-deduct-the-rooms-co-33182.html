Renting Out a Spare Room – Check the Rules, Then Deduct the Room’s Costs

Industry observers note that A lodger in your spare room can cover a big share of your mortgage. Before anyone moves in, confirm that zoning and your homeowners association allow it, and learn how your state lets you end the arrangement. Then screen the lodger, sign a written room agreement, and deduct the room’s share of costs from the rent you report.

Once a lodger lives down the hall, a bad match is hard to undo, and your state’s law decides how hard.

It has been revealed that california’s courts spell out a simpler way to end it. The rule covers a single lodger in a house you live in, as long as you keep the right to enter their room. Written notice giving them one rental period to leave, such as a month for monthly rent, ends their right to stay, and police may remove a lodger who stays past it. Rent to two people and the rule no longer applies, so ending it takes a court eviction.

Local zoning can cap how many unrelated people share a house, and a homeowners association’s rules may restrict rentals. If you rent, get your landlord’s written consent.

Check an applicant’s credit and eviction records with their written consent, verify their income, and call a past landlord. Then sign a room agreement that covers the rent, deposit, shared spaces, utilities, guests, quiet hours, and notice to leave. Before move-in, tell your insurer you’re taking in a lodger, and have the lodger buy renters insurance.

Sources indicate that under IRS Publication 527, the rent is taxable income, usually reported on Schedule E, the form for rental income. Only a home rented out 14 days a year or less escapes that tax. You split shared costs like mortgage interest, property tax, utilities, and insurance by number of rooms or square footage, then deduct the room’s share. Say the room is 10% of your floor space and rents for $900 a month, enough to cover 40% of a $2,250 mortgage payment. You’d report $10,800 a year in rent and deduct 10% of those shared bills from it.

Claim depreciation too, a yearly deduction that spreads the room’s share of what the house cost, minus the land, over 27.5 years. When you sell, the IRS doesn’t let the home-sale tax break cover the part of your profit equal to that depreciation. You owe tax on it whether you claimed it or not.
