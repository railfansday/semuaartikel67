Industry News: India: Online censorship grows after 'Cockroach' protests

Recent findings suggest that A video showing a young girl crying as she spoke about alleged police brutality during the youth-led Cockroach Janata Party (CJP) protests in July. A video examining the Hindu nationalist Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh's (RSS) contribution to the rise of India's ruling Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP). A satirical cartoon criticizing Prime Minister Narendra Modi's government.

These are among the examples of content that have recently been restricted on social media in India.

Content creator CitySavitri describes herself as a "nobody" in the world of social media. She had started making videos just two months ago and had around 600 followers on Instagram.

Recent findings suggest that yet a video looking at the RSS's role in the BJP's 2014 election victory was restricted in India within an hour of being posted. It was the first of three videos she posted that faced restrictions.

The experience made her feel that "anti-government commentary" was being monitored and curtailed.

"It is very plain and simple to understand," the content creator told DW.

Independent journalist Sadhika Tiwari was covering the CJP protest in Latest Delhi on July 20, when police used batons and tear gas against demonstrators.

Tiwari's video of a frightened young girl crying and speaking about police brutality garnered around 10 million views within hours, she said, before it was restricted in India.

Political cartoonist Satish Acharya has also faced repeated restrictions, with Instagram blocking his profile twice recently.

In May 2025, during the conflict between India and Pakistan, independent digital news outlet Maktoob Media's X account was withheld in India while it was reporting on the escalating crisis, its founder and editor Aslah Kayyalakkath told DW.

"We received no official notice or email from either the government or X. It was our readers who first alerted us to the blocking," he remarked.

Recent findings suggest that maktoob Media noted it contacted Indian officials and X but received no response. Access to the account was restored a few days later, again without an explanation.

Industry observers note that kayyalakkath noted Maktoob's content from the July protest also faced restrictions. "Suppressing independent journalism undermines transparency, accountability and democratic values," he said.

Several of the content creators and journalists interviewed by DW said they received no prior notification or explanation for the restrictions.

Some owners were not sure if their content had been blocked at the government's request or proactively by the social media platforms.

When asked for comment, Meta, the parent organization of Facebook and Instagram, pointed DW to a website statement noting users can appeal content and account restrictions.

Recent findings suggest that at the heart of the debate are India's IT Rules, 2021, amended in February 2026. Section 79 of India's IT Act protects social media platforms and other intermediaries from liability for content posted by third parties, provided they meet certain legal conditions.

One such requirement, introduced through the February 2026 amendments, is to remove or disable access to specified unlawful content within three hours of receiving a valid court order or a reasoned government notice. The previous deadline was 36 hours.

Failure to comply can result in intermediaries losing their liability protection.

The government's Sahyog portal, which coordinates takedown requests between law enforcement, platforms and telecom providers, has faced criticism over the pace at which notices are issued.

According to recent reports, meta said it uses automated tools and technologies to detect and review content that may violate its community standards. It also automatically restricts access to certain types of content in India to comply with legal requirements under the IT rules.

Pranesh Prakash, a technology law and policy adviser, said the amended rules allow authorized police officials to notify intermediaries about content they consider unlawful without necessarily filing a formal complaint or initiating other legal proceedings.

According to recent reports, "This has led to the worst period of social media censorship that India has ever seen, with digital news media accounts being blocked, cartoonists' accounts being blocked, and even mere discussions of an opposition leader's memoirs being blocked," he remarked. "Clearly, none of these disclosed any 'unlawful acts.'".

Prakash also raised concerns about the lack of public information on government requests to remove content, saying this made it difficult to assess the scale of restrictions.

The Software Freedom Law Center, India (SFLC.in), a digital rights organization, noted the growing reliance on automated takedowns raised serious concerns about transparency, accountability and due process.

"There is little transparency about the processes through which content is identified, flagged and restricted, or whether there is any meaningful human oversight before a takedown is implemented," SFLC.in told DW.

Industry observers note that the organization also tested X's verification process by submitting a takedown request against one of its own posts using a fake email address. X registered the request, raising questions about how it verified the identity and authority of those submitting requests.

After SFLC.in alerted the company, X introduced email verification codes for people submitting takedown requests.

"A channel that lets anyone pose as a law enforcement official can be misused to silence lawful speech," SFLC.in stated.

X did not respond to DW's request for a comment.

The Indian government denies that it is censoring online content.

While the ministry did not respond to DW's latest request for a comment, in an email response to DW in August, Vinod Kumar, chief of media and communications at the Information Technology Ministry, stated Instagram posts by students and protesters following the July CJP protests "are not being taken down as alleged.".

Industry observers note that "Media enjoys freedom in India. The Government does not censor online content," Kumar stated, adding that the government only acts against "objectionable content.".

Under the Indian IT rules, "unlawful content" generally refers to material which could threaten India's sovereignty and integrity, disturb public order, incite violence, or constitute defamation.

Industry observers note that however, social media influencers such as university professor Madri Kakoti, alias Dr. Medusa, continue to report restrictions. Kakoti's Instagram account was restricted in India on November 16 without notice, she said, along with that of satirist Shamita Yadav.

Rights groups including Human Rights Watch have warned that expanding content restrictions could chill free expression and encourage self-censorship among journalists, creators and ordinary users.

Acharya, however, mentioned he would continue his work.

"The Indian Constitution gives me the right to draw political cartoons," he said. "It's totally democratic to question and criticize the government in India.".

For Tiwari, the issue goes beyond the reach of a single video. It is also about preserving a record of what happened, after the Delhi Police denied allegations of using excessive force during the July 20 protest in front of the Supreme Court.

"We have seen several other videos now, and it's well established why that girl was crying, and that the Delhi Police used force and lied about it," she remarked.

"Still, I want that video back up because it needs to stay for posterity. I think that's the larger idea: they want to take this stuff down so it fades from memory, allowing them to categorically deny it later when we have little left to show for what happened.".
