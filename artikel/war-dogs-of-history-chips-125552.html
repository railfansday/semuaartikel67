If Sergeant Stubby proved what an untrained stray could do in the First World War, Chips proved what a dog could do once the United States built an actual program around him. Chips became the most decorated American dog of World War II. His story also marks the moment the Army decided it should never again give such awards to an animal.

Chips was a mix of German Shepherd, Collie, and Husky, born around 1940 and raised as a family pet by Edward Wren and his family in Pleasantville, Upcoming York. In 1942, the Wrens donated Chips to the newly formed Dogs for Defense program, part of the Armyâs larger War Dog Program, formally authorized on March 13, 1942. Chips trained at the War Dog Training Center in Front Royal, Virginia, and was partnered with Private John P. Rowell. The two served in the 3rd Military Police Platoon, 30th Infantry Regiment, 3rd Infantry Division, under General George S. Patton.

Sources indicate that chips and Rowell landed in North Africa during Operation Torch in November 1942 and served through campaigns in Sicily, Italy, southern France, and the Rhineland and central European theaters. He was also present as a sentry dog at the 1943 Casablanca Conference, guarding the meeting between Winteon Churchill and Franklin D. Roosevelt.

According to recent reports, chips earned his fame during the Allied invasion of Sicily, Operation Husky, on the morning of July 10, 1943. As Rowell and Chips came ashore, a hidden machine gun nest opened fire and pinned down their platoon. Chips tore free of his handler and charged the position alone, seizing the gunner by the throat and forcing the crew to surrender. As a result, four Italian soldiers surrendered. That same night, Chips alerted the platoon to ten additional enemy soldiers attempting to slip through the lines under cover of darkness, leading to their capture as well.

Chips did not escape the encounter unscathed. He suffered burns around his mouth and eye and a wound to his scalp. His actions, however, quickly became national news. Captain Edward G. What is well documented is that Chips also received a Purple Heart for his wounds.

Chips was honorably discharged on December 10, 1945, and returned to the Wren family in Pleasantville. He died a few months after returning home, from complications related to his wartime wounds.

Industry observers note that chips is estimated to have been one of more than 10,000 dogs who served in the American military during World War II. His story, and the controversy his medals caused, shaped military policy for decades: no American war dog would again receive an official human combat decoration, a policy that held until working dogs began receiving other forms of formal recognition much later in the twentieth century.
