Sources indicate that if you’re like most guys, you probably have plenty of little bumps and lumps on your skin. There’s the callus on your toe from training for that 10K, the razor bumps along your neck from this morning’s shave, and the skin tag on your underarm you keep meaning to get removed. While it’s often completely fine to ignore these bumps, that isn’t always the case. Some can be a red flag worth paying attention to—especially if they’re on your chest.

While male breast cancer is rare, estimates suggest that more than 2,600 men are diagnosed with an invasive form of the disease each year in the United States, and more than 500 die, according to the American Cancer Society. And one of its telltale signs is a firm bump under or near your nipple. Below, find out more about what a breast-cancer lump may look like and four other things that could be to blame for a newly introduced bump on your chest.

There isn’t a universal description of what breast cancer lumps look like in men, but there are some common characteristics to keep in mind. “Breast cancer in men typically presents as a painless, firm, or hard lump,” states Jose Pablo Leone, MD, the director of the Program for Breast Cancer in Men at Dana-Farber Cancer Institute in Boston.

Industry observers note that another sign that the bump could be breast cancer is if it’s behind or very close to the nipple and areola. “Men have much less developed breast glandular tissue than women, which is why breast cancer in men most commonly develops near or behind the nipple,” states Dr. Leone. As for size even a lump as small as a pea can be cancerous, but by the time men go to the doctor, tumors are often larger than a marble, according to Dr. Leone. “In part because of the lack of awareness and screening, most men present with larger tumors,” he says. “But any size is worth mentioning to your doctor. Not all breast cancers feel the same, so any latest or persistent breast lump should be evaluated by a healthcare professional.”.

Recent findings suggest that your chest has a lot of oil glands, which can set the stage for pimples. “Staying in a sweaty shirt after a workout can make chest acne flare, especially if the fabric is tight and rubs against your skin,” indicates Elizabeth Bahar Houshmand, MD, a board-certified dermatologist based in Dallas. While blackheads and whiteheads are typically small and easy to identify, inflamed pimples or nodules may seem more alarming because they can be hard, painful, and form deep under the skin. When it comes to nodular acne specifically, you can’t treat it on your own. If that’s what you think you’re dealing with, see a dermatologist, who can prescribe an oral or topical medication to help clear it up.

“This is a collection of fat that forms under the skin,” claims Sonya Kenkare, MD, a board-certified dermatologist at Illinois Dermatology Institute in Hinsdale, Illinois. “It is usually soft, not painful, and you can move it around in all different directions.” You don’t need to do anything about a lipoma, but if you want to get it removed, you can talk to a doctor about it.

When hair follicles get inflamed or infected, you can end up with folliculitis. “Folliculitis often looks like small pimples or pus-filled bumps centered around a hair follicle” states Dr. Houshmand. “They may itch or feel tender.”.

Blockage or damage to a hair follicle can also cause epidermal inclusion cysts. These cysts occur when dead skin cells get trapped under the surface instead of shedding. “They feel somewhat firm and often have a central little black spot,” says Dr. Kenkare. Sometimes simply applying a warm compress to the area can help a cyst heal on its own, but a doctor can also inject it with a steroid to reduce swelling, drain it, or surgically remove it.

It has been revealed that this condition occurs when breast tissue gets enlarged in men, and it can first appear as a lump under your nipple. “It often feels like a rubbery or firm disc beneath the nipple, and it may be tender,” says Dr. Houshmand. While gynecomastia can come on as a side effect of some medications, like antibiotics or antidepressants, or diseases like thyroid disorders, sometimes there is no clear cause at all.

If you’re starting to feel like it’s difficult to tell on your own what the lump on your chest might be, you’re not wrong. That’s why it’s best to have your primary care physician look at any new, unexplained bumps and tell you whether you need imaging, like a mammogram with an ultrasound. That may sound scary, but it’s really just covering your bases. “Uncommon things are uncommon, and breast cancer in men is uncommon,” says Dr. Leone. “You shouldn’t worry excessively about male breast cancer, but you should be aware of it.”.
