Recent findings suggest that ask a 17-year-old where their career plan came from and the answer is usually someone at the kitchen table. A parent, maybe an uncle. These are people who love them and want the best for them. They are also, in most cases, working from a job market map that is 20 or 30 years out of date. The school counselor rarely enters the picture. Most U.S. school counselors spend no more than 20% of their time on career counseling while serving hundreds of students. The available capacity works out to well under an hour per student per year, even before accounting for group programming and administrative responsibilities. Meanwhile, nearly the entire AI and jobs conversation is about what this technology will take away from young workers. That concern is fair. I’ve argued here before that AI will reduce the need for some entry-level white-collar roles, and employers need to plan for that now. But we have become so focused on what AI will do to young workers that we have stopped asking what it can do for them. And there is an irony sitting right in front of us. One of the hardest problems in workforce development is getting accurate, real-time labor market information to the people who need it most. That happens to be the kind of problem AI is unusually good at. THE DATA EXISTS BUT FEW CAN READ IT Labor market data is not scarce. Governments, job boards, and industry groups publish enormous amounts of it every month. The trouble is that it is dense, scattered, and close to illegible for anyone without a statistics background. A 16-year-old is not going to read a state workforce projection. Neither is her dad. So young people make one of the biggest decisions of their lives with the least information. We are preparing them for yesterday’s jobs while tomorrow’s technology sits on the phone in their pocket. They know the system is failing them. In the Broken Marketplace, a Schultz Family Foundation and HarrisX survey of more than 5,700 young Americans ages 16 to 24, more than four in 10 said the education system and the employment resources available to them are broken. Seven in 10 said they learn about career pathways on social media. We know that young people are already asking algorithms for career advice. They are just asking the wrong ones. WHAT A GOOD AI COUNSELOR SOUNDS LIKE At the International Youth Foundation, we’ve been testing this idea with a chatbot called Clara, a partnership we have with the Salesforce Foundation. The premise is straightforward: Take labor market data that would make most people’s eyes glaze over and turn it into a conversation. Picture a young woman who has just finished a technical program and doesn’t know where to apply. Instead of a spreadsheet, she gets something closer to a knowledgeable friend. A chat that says, “Did you know they’re hiring electricians a few miles from you? A couple of things in your background could make you competitive. Let me ask you some questions and see whether it’s worth pursuing.” From there, the tool can explain what training she would need, how long it takes, and whether that field is growing where she lives. That is the guidance a great counselor gives. But most students never get it. Nearly half of career and technical educators say their school’s counselors spend less time on career pathways than on college prep. AI can close that gap at a scale no hiring plan ever could. Substantial data sets, updated in real time, delivered to one person at a time in plain language. That is what these models do best. THE RISK AND FIX ARE REAL To be clear, this is not without risk. General-purpose AI models can sound authoritative while missing the details that matter. When researchers at Renaissance Philanthropy tested leading models on thousands of real career questions from young people, most would have earned a C-minus. For a young person making a first career decision, a confident wrong answer can cost years. That is a reason to build these tools carefully, and to build them now. If AI makes the labor market more volatile, good information becomes increasingly valuable. The work of measuring whether AI gives young people good career advice has barely started, and it deserves far more investment than it is getting. The AI labs should be paying attention, too. Millions of young people are already typing some version of “what job should I go for?” into their products. Making sure those answers are accurate and grounded in real local demand would cost a fraction of what is being spent on the next generation of models. AI can’t save every job. But it could help a young person find the next one. Employers, funders, and the companies building this technology spend a great deal of energy worrying about what AI will do to the next generation of workers. They should spend at least as much making sure it works for them.Christina Sass is president and CEO of the International Youth Foundation.
