Breaking: The “5,000 Episodes a Week” AI-Podcast Firm Has Pivoted

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

After launching a podcast network last fall with thousands of AI-generated podcasts and AI-podcast hosts, Inception AI has pivoted.

Over the last few months, the manufacturer has stopped work on its owned and operated podcasts, which numbered more than 15,000 and ignited strong reactions in the industry, essentially letting them fall dormant and no longer monetizing the shows. The company has instead moved into liquid content, helping other companies translate text to audio and create podcast videos from audio content, and rebranded under the name Liquid Studios, with another bold claim.

“I think we’ve solved podcast video,” CEO Jeanine Wright tells The Hollywood Reporter of the new plan.

The decision to pivot came in mid-July, as both a bet on growth and in response to uneasiness about AI in the marketplace. The launch of their content offerings had made a splash and invoked a degree of vitriol, with Wright noting that she had been boo’d while taking the stage at podcast conferences, though she stated she would later be pulled aside for business inquiries. The original vision for the company was to create content and provide services to other businesses, but the company ran into headwinds on the content side, which at one point included producing more than 3,000 episodes a week and creating more than 50 AI personalities to host content produced for $1 an episode or less.

The thousands of highlights had been monetized through programmatic advertising, but the team ran into an matter when some of the marketplaces were unable to clearly identify to advertisers which shows were made with AI. The episodes included the AI-disclaimer in the audio and episode notes.

In a recent development, “We started to get some feedback from our programmatic partners that their customers wanted to know when they were advertising in something that was AI, and that they hadn’t built out the infrastructure yet to be able to clearly identify to them when they were advertising in something with AI, and how it was performing,” Wright noted. She added that she mulled switching to direct sales instead, but that would’ve required more work from the small team.

Additionally, Wright had heard concerns about information contained in some of the episodes of the company’s back-catalog of more than 350,000 episodes. Pre-pivot, some of the AI-created podcast hosts had operated in the health and wellness field, and referred to themselves as doctors or mental health professionals.

While Wright mentioned the AI-made creators did not give medical advice – they offered home remedies for a cold or provided information on what depression and anxiety are – the concerns about representation were enough to cause the manufacturer to stop releasing new episodes from those creators in the winter. The team then went through and took down all related episodes from the back catalog. But that experience, as well as prior concerns they had received about the quality of past show episodes and show art, which were created with older versions of AI models, made Wright realize how much work would be required to maintain all of the back catalog, both editorially and as AI models evolve.

“It opened the question and the conversation of how are we going to look at our obligations and our responsibilities with respect to maintaining, updating what was this huge body of content that we had created in the past,” Wright stated.

It has been revealed that the company had closed a $5 million seed round led by venture capital firm Activate Venture Partners in January, and in keeping with its commitments to investors pivoted to B2B as a better growth strategy. With the pivot, and using AI to help translate human-led projects into other mediums, Wright added that the industry has also been more receptive.

“I thought that within a year, people wouldn’t care whether or not something was materially made with AI. I think people still care. I think I was wrong,” Wright stated.

The formerly eight-person tech giant has now grown to 11. But, after moving away from making their own content, the company lost two of its co-founders, lifestyle guru and former television host Katie Brown, who previously led the content team, and her husband, William Corbin, who was chief technology officer of the company.

In a recent development, as it stands, Liquid Studios has about a dozen customers who are expected to go public this month, Wright said, and about a dozen more that are confidential, with clients including media companies, publishing houses, and large independent creators, brands and one tech giant with a large television archive, with much of the current focus around turning audio into video, as well as text to audio and translation into other languages. The company went public in October and recently introduced a pilot program to turn industry news podcast audio from PodNews into video.

In a recent development, to translate audio to video and more, Liquid Studios is using AI orchestration, which selects and coordinates the best AI models, video generators and more to use for the project. Related images and animation are pulled to create the videos, in styles ranging from animated to realistic, and clients can decide whether to use AI-generated images or not. The company also has the ability to create photorealistic or animated digital replicas of the podcast hosts to appear in the videos, cloning both the actual host’s voice and look, but U.S.-based clients have been nervous to take it public because of how the market will react.

“In the [user-generated content] space, I think we’re seeing it a lot. But in the traditional creator space, I think it’s coming, but it’s not here yet,” Wright stated.

In a recent development, wright notes she believes the manufacturer’s upcoming model helps creators approach the increasingly visual medium of podcasting and help them visualize content without the costs associated with a larger production studio. As it stands, the company is going to market with a rate of $10 a finished minute of audio to video conversion.
