📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Comedian Gianmarco Soresi once mentioned that he procrastinated filming an hour special, “holding on to the most destructive thought a creative can have: I’ll do it when someone pays me for it.”.

Ultimately, he didn’t hold out, though that’s probably for the better: His self-released debut hour, Thief of Joy, broke out as one of the best reviewed of last year, while being recognized for impressive viewership on YouTube — surpassing a million views within a week, with over 6.4 million all time. And now, Soresi has secured the kind of mainstream gatekeeper payday that he originally desired, with his debut Netflix hour God Forbid set to premiere Dec. 3. In a recent development, named one of Deadline’s Comedians Ready to Break Out In 2025 — and subsequently, one of our Comics Who Won 2025 — Soresi has had one of the swiftest and most impressive rises of any stand-up in recent memory. A performer with roots in musical theater, he spent years chasing acting before deciding to explore stand-up. It took just a decade for him to garner a reputation as one of the art form’s rising stars, through a combo of kinetic live shows, solid social strategy, a hit podcast and popular side projects like the live show, Theater Adult. Watch on Deadline.

For Soresi, a goal consistently at top of mind is to offer fans an “elevated stand-up experience.” He says it took him four to five years to figure out what he could uniquely bring as a stand-up — how to synthesize his strengths as a joke writer with “performance abilities” accrued from his years as an actor. 2022 was a breakthrough year as that in which he was named a JFL Newly introduced Face, garnering industry recognition while showcasing a knack for “play logic and set pieces and movement and musicality” that sets him apart.

Whereas Thief of Joy afforded Soresi the opportunity to introduce himself and his comedic voice to a broad audience, God Forbid may well expand his audience further, as he delves into “the next level of what I’m thinking.”.

For the comedian, 2026 has been a time of connection — a year in which he’s begun to feel “much more connected to the world in a way that I didn’t, whether it be comedically or as a human being.”.

Says Soresi, in today’s episode of Comedy Means Business, “I went to Asia for the first time this year, let alone to do stand-up comedy. And there’s something that feels like it changes when you see just how big the world is. And the fact that I could go somewhere for the first time and sell out a theater and have people who know me, and I don’t know anything about the world, but we’re able to connect through some similarities…And also seeing all these other comedians and these different vibrant comedy communities, some which are much earlier in the trajectory of their stand-up evolution, some of which have taken different paths… I just feel like it’s made me want to expand the way that I think. I see humanity from more vantage points.”.

In conversation with Deadline, Soresi talks about his experiences traveling the world for stand-up in 2026, and the experience of building his career at a time when it’s “really tough to build a sustainable life” as a stand-up without being entrepreneurially minded. He explains how he writes and evolves an act on tour, the difference between the material you’re likely to get from him at an early vs. late show, why it took him a while to find his comedy community, and his experience seeing both great success and great challenges contending with volatile social media platforms. Soresi also discusses why “we miss the gatekeeper,” the brutal nature of touring life, the importance of “sh*t talk” in keeping artists honest, recent Fire Island reveals with comedian friend Jay Jurden, performing for two weeks this summer at the Edinburgh Fringe, his fascination with how the rules governing speech vary from country to country, navigating personal and professional life with girlfriend and manager Tovah Silbermann, and more.
