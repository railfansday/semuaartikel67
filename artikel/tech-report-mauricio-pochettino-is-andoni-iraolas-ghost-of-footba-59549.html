Industry observers note that mauricio Pochettino begins where Jose Mourinho finished, and some would suggest that ought to have been the order of succession at Manchester United anyway. But Mourinho’s last match in charge occurred at Anfield. His United lost 3-1 as Liverpool had 36 shots. Six months later, Jurgen Klopp had another notable victory to celebrate, over Pochettino’s Tottenham in the Champions League final.

According to recent reports, but a journey along the East Lancs Road for Pochettino’s bow with United has a pertinence for other reasons, and they stretch beyond the regular comparisons between English football’s two most storied clubs. Opposites in some respects, they are akin in others. When Pochettino looks at the home dugout on Sunday, he may see himself: not so much in the mirror as in the past.

Andoni Iraola is 44, a decade younger than Pochettino and is roughly at the point in his managerial career that the Argentinian was 10 years ago; indeed, that was when many felt United should have appointed him, in preference to the more decorated Mourinho.

It has been revealed that some of those similarities could be dismissed as superficial, even if they suggest Iraola was following Pochettino’s path. Each managed in LaLiga before England, then joining a South Coast club; controversially, too, given the English incumbent who had succeeded was ruthlessly sacked to make way for a man deemed more progressive.

The eventual verdict was that Southampton got an upgrade on Nigel Adkins, as Bournemouth would on Gary O’Neil. Pochettino took Saints to their joint highest finish for 24 years. Iraola recorded the best in Bournemouth’s history.

Each did so while improving players to such an extent that the established elite started buying them in bulk; indeed, Liverpool were to purchase four of Pochettino’s Saints players and, even before hiring Iraola, had taken Milos Kerkez from Bournemouth and shown an interest in Antoine Semenyo.

A reason was that they were playing a brand of football that seemed to equip them for clubs more accustomed to life nearer the top of the table, which in turn reflected the ethos of the managers. Pochettino and Iraola were not prospering by playing underdog football; a high-paced, attacking, ambitious gameplan meant they earned admirers.

Each was an evangelist for a pressing game. Each had learned from one of its great theorists, Marcelo Bielsa, even if Pochettino was more of a disciple while Iraola’s biggest managerial influence was actually Ernesto Valverde.

Recent findings suggest that parachuting either in to a superpower required a decision to prioritise potential over a proven ability to win trophies, a style of play over experience. Iraola’s only silverware as a coach has come in Cyprus. Ten years ago, and two seasons into Pochettino’s time at Tottenham, he had won nothing, and Mourinho had won everything.

A fundamental difference in thinking between Liverpool and United may have been apparent in some managerial choices. Liverpool sacked a Premier League winner, in Arne Slot, to appoint Iraola. For a time, United sought solace in silverware, in the Champions Leagues Louis van Gaal and Mourinho had claimed, but picked men who were past their best. Then they sought solace in the silverware secured in lesser leagues by Erik ten Hag and Ruben Amorim, but picked men who became exposed in the Premier League.

It has been revealed that liverpool may be banking on Iraola becoming a trophy-gatherer as a consequence of combining his methods with better players and when freed from a selling club. For much of the last 13 years, United have seemed to look at the manager’s CV to provide a shortcut to success.

Recent findings suggest that at times, it may have counted against Pochettino. But, as he finally takes charge of United, there is a way in which he represents a role model to Iraola. The Argentinian took the step up to bigger clubs and, without acquiring the medal collection, has offered the evidence he belongs at that level. Will Iraola, even if he does not last at Liverpool?

In a recent development, because others have floundered and dropped down: to clubs with less scrutiny, or expectation about the style of play. Think of David Moyes after United or Roy Hodgson after Liverpool, or some of Pochettino’s successors at Spurs, Nuno Espirito Santo and Thomas Frank among them. In contrast, since Tottenham, Pochettino has managed Paris Saint-Germain and Chelsea and remained a constant in the conversations for high-profile posts.

It has been revealed that for some of the last decade, however, it has been unclear if his career remains on the upward trajectory it definitely was in 2016, as Iraola’s has been in 2026. Ten years ago, Pochettino seemed to have captured the Zeitgeist, perhaps in alliance with Pep Guardiola and Klopp. Now everyone presses, Pochettino is in his mid-fifties and there is a question if he is still the face of modernity.

Liverpool can note their last two Champions League-winning managers, Rafa Benitez and Klopp, were given the job at 44 and 48 respectively. United’s only unqualified success among managerial appointments since Sir Matt Busby, in Sir Alex Ferguson, was 44 when he arrived at Old Trafford.

Now Iraola is 44; a coincidence, perhaps, but the age can seem a sweet spot, offering coaches some experience while being young enough to have a connection with their players, for their ideas to have a freshness and them to exude energy. Pochettino, another smiling Spanish speaker, may glimpse a vision of what he was when he looks at Iraola. But then his test is to show he remains the man for now.
