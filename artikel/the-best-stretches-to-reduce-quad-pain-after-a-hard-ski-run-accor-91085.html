The Best Stretches to Reduce Quad Pain After a Hard Ski Run, According to a Physical Therapist

Having sore quads after skiing might seem like a side effect reserved for beginners who pizza their way down the slopes. But athletes of all levels can suffer from them.

Industry observers note that even with good form, your quads are the dominant muscle in skiing—especially the lateral quad (vastus lateralis), which is the most active when you turn, James Spencer, a physical therapist in Aspen, Colorado, told Outside. The stronger a skier you are, the harder you can push your quads on the mountain, which means “you can find a way to make them sore, no matter your skiing level,” he explained.

The root cause behind this soreness is the same reason your legs are tender after an arduous trail run or a lower-body weightlifting workout. When you intensely and repeatedly load your quads, you create microscopic tears in the muscle fibers. That damage may sound bad, but it’s actually a good thing: it’s one of the triggers that tells your body to strengthen that muscle, Spencer said.

Still, the side effects can be unpleasant. As with any bodily injury, the microtears trigger swelling, which increases the pain you feel after exercise and causes that sore sensation, Spencer explains. Officially known as delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS), this feeling sets in after your workout ends and can peak as late as two to three days after. So if you feel fine boarding the shuttle at the end of a long ski day, but find yourself hobbling to the toilet the next morning, DOMS is probably the culprit.

The true cure for DOMS is time. “You really just gotta wait for those [microtears] to heal,” Spencer remarked. But there’s also something you can do in the interim to ease your pain slightly: quad stretches.

Recent findings suggest that by gently stretching your quads after a hard day on the mountain, you can decrease the intensity of your soreness by flushing out some of the inflammation in your muscles, Spencer remarked. It won’t be a cure-all, but it can help you move and feel better in the short term. Read on for the specific moves Spencer recommends.

The above stretches can be sufficient to help reduce quad soreness, but if you’re looking for more, Spencer also suggests other recovery methods.

Spend a couple of minutes foam rolling your quads, including the front of your thigh (from the knee all the way up to the hip) and the sides to hit the vastus lateralis (basically where your IT band is). Similar to stretching, foam rolling can bring some relief to the quads by reducing swelling in the area.

Another option: hop on a stationary bike and gently cycle your legs for ten to 15 minutes. This easy movement can also reduce soreness by pumping out inflammation from your quads, Spencer said. Just make sure to keep the resistance and your effort level low. “It’s not meant to be a workout,” he said.

Stretches can ease DOMS in the short term, but if you want to prevent intense soreness altogether, strengthening your quads is the ticket.

Industry observers note that “If you just build a little strength going into the season, you’re going to be less sore,” Spencer said. And you’re also less likely to get injured, he added.

Exercises such as quad sets, wall sits, and really any squat variation like traditional squats, Bulgarian split squats, and single-leg squats can all strengthen the muscle group.

“The thing I hear all the time is the only way to get ready for skiing is by skiing, and that’s a total lie,” Spencer remarked. There’s a lot you can do both in the off-season and throughout the winter to prime your body for the demands of skiing, he said. In addition to quad-strengthening exercises, he recommends hamstring, glute, and core moves, plus jumping exercises that train your ability to react to the unexpected.
