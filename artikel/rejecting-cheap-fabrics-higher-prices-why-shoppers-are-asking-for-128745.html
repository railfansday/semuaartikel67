Rejecting cheap fabrics, higher prices: Why shoppers are asking for better clothes

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Clothing isn't made like it used to be, and more shoppers are taking notice.

It has been revealed that the makeup of apparel imported into the U.S. has shifted in recent years. In 2010, about 60% of clothing was predominantly made from cotton. Now, that total is around 45%, while predominantly synthetic fabrics such as polyester and nylon comprise the majority of imports.

According to recent reports, apparel prices generally declined over the two decades leading up to the pandemic, according to the consumer price index. Since 2020, they have soared to record highs.

It has been revealed that it's starting to change habits for consumers who are paying more for food, gas, rent and other staples. As shoppers shell out more for clothes, they're increasingly making sure the garments are worth the cost.

Consulting firm AlixPartners' 2026 Consumer Sentiment Index, which surveyed more than 9,000 shoppers, found that consumers are scrutinizing labels more closely and rejecting cheap materials sold at a premium.

"If it's not a natural material, a retailer brand who passes it off as it is, they're really becoming very low on consumers' minds," mentioned Sonia Lapinsky, head of fashion at AlixPartners.

Sources indicate that fast fashion brands like Forever 21 and H&M exploded in popularity during the early 2000s and 2010s, bringing trendy clothing to shelves swiftly at low prices. Then came ultra-fast fashion, with brands like Shein and Fashion Nova pumping out new garments at even quicker speeds and lower price points — made possible in part through the use of inexpensive, synthetic fabrics.

Recent findings suggest that "We got to a point where the pendulum just swung too far in that direction. And we've actually begun to see a reversal," said U.S. Bancorp BTIG consumer analyst Janine Stichter. "Quality matters a lot more ... over the last three or four years than it has in the previous 10 or 20.".

But quality, which shoppers often tie directly to material, can come at a higher cost.

Recent findings suggest that the average unit price of apparel imported to the U.S. between January and August was $2.64 per square meter for garments that were predominantly synthetic fiber, like polyester and nylon, compared with $3.67 for items that were largely cotton, according to data from the U.S. Commerce Department's Office of Textiles and Apparel.

When comparing those 2026 figures to 2010, synthetic fiber apparel import costs have climbed around 11%, while cotton apparel costs have risen 21%.

"If you're appealing to a more value-sensitive customer, you either reduce the quality, or you negotiate with suppliers to try to share some of the cost or you maybe absorb the cost … and perhaps hope to crank a higher volume of product to make up for it," remarked Bernstein Research senior retail analyst Aneesha Sherman.

Though polyester is cheaper, experts point out that's not necessarily a bad thing. It can help clothing hold its shape and wick away sweat.

Recent findings suggest that sherman believes the athleisure boom is a primary factor in the shift toward polyester. She noted that from 2015 to 2025, sports apparel was growing at a 4.9% rate in the U.S., versus 2.6% for all apparel and footwear, according to Euromonitor data.

"There probably is some quality trade down as well happening there," Sherman said. "But I think both these trends are conflated.".

Industry observers note that nonetheless, backlash to synthetic materials is growing. Gen Z has even repurposed the word "polyester" as an insult to mean generally low-quality or fake.

Concerns have also grown about the environment and human health surrounding petroleum-based clothing, as more research indicates microplastics can leach from polyester clothing when washed.

It's led consumers to reexamine what materials are in their clothing.

According to recent reports, apparel prices have risen coming out of the pandemic, as demand outpaced supply and allowed companies to raise prices, mentioned BTIG's Stichter. Tariffs and the Iran war followed, raising costs for businesses — and prices for consumers.

Petroleum is the basis of many synthetic fibers, and oil prices have skyrocketed since the start of the Iran war.

The same factors that helped drive up prices of clothing — Covid, tariffs and war — also affected clothing quality.

"Companies had to move around their supply chains in the post-Covid period," said Stichter. "They had to find ways to take costs out, and sometimes that meant using less expensive fabrics.".

Those factors compounded a more-than-decade-long shift away from manufacturing in China.

Sheng Lu, a fashion and apparel studies professor at the University of Delaware, whose research focuses on the economics of the global textile industry, said China's established history with clothing production means it has higher-skilled workers, state-of-the-art-equipment and better material management compared with emerging manufacturing destinations such as Bangladesh, Sri Lanka and Cambodia.

It has been revealed that "They mostly serve the value market and the mass market. And I think the reason why they're not ready to make higher-quality luxury and premium products really reflects the kind of skill sets you can expect when you source from these countries," said Lu.

AlixPartners' Lapinsky said these countries that manufacture at a lower cost sometimes have to still ship the raw materials from China, which can extend the timeline to make clothing. That means taking earlier bets on trends and then discounting if products don't sell well.

"If you have to mark down that product because the consumer doesn't want it as much, your net margin might actually be worse than if you were to just invest in quality in the first place," mentioned BTIG's Stichter.

And as material costs rise for synthetic fibers, Stichter also said she believes it could further influence companies to use more natural fabrics moving forward.

Companies prioritizing quality are winning in this environment.

Ralph Lauren's stock is up around 85% over the past two years. Around 90% of its apparel is primarily made of cotton, leather, wool, viscose, cashmere or silk, according to its 2026 sustainability report. In its fiscal 2026, the tech giant remarked it sold more products at full price and reduced markdowns.

Meanwhile, direct-to-consumer clothing brand Quince, which centers its brand identity around natural materials, reached a $10 billion valuation this year.

The brand jumped 16 spots to No. 3 for best resale value in online consignment retailer ThredUp's 2025 report. Organic cotton retailer Pact jumped 20 spots on that list to No. 8, illustrating the potential role materials are playing in brand recognition.

Industry observers note that alternatively, the 2026 AlixPartners report, which ranks sentiment for brands in different retail categories, found shoppers are viewing some fast fashion brands less favorably. Both Shein and Zara dropped three spots — to fifth and seventh place, respectively — in terms of sentiment in the young adult specialty category.

Industry observers note that shein passed higher tariff costs onto shoppers in 2025 and then blamed a sales slowdown on those higher prices. The ultra-fast fashion retailer saw its year-over-year share of U.S. apparel, accessories and footwear card spending decrease in the fourth quarter of 2025 and first two quarters of 2026, according to Consumer Edge.

Shein did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

Sources indicate that though Zara fell in AlixPartners' Consumer Sentiment Index, it has continued to grow sales and profits year over year. Even so, Lapinsky said worsening consumer views are something to watch, even if the firm is doing well.

"What we found over the years with this consumer sentiment index is that sometimes it will be a bit of a leading indicator of what's to come," noted AlixPartners' Lapinsky. "I'm not saying that this is going to be true for Zara, but I think that's one thing that we'll be watching as we look in the upcoming quarters and into 2027.".
