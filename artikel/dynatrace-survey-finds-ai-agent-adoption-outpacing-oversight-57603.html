Dynatrace Survey Finds AI Agent Adoption Outpacing Oversight

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, AI agents are becoming common in the teams that keep enterprise software running, but the tools to monitor what those agents decide and do lag behind. In its State of Observability 2026 findings, published November 10, Dynatrace noted 87% of surveyed technology leaders reported moderate or extensive use of AI agents for observability and IT operations. Only 27% say they presently address visibility into agent decisions and actions.

Sources indicate that the results are based on an online survey Dynatrace commissioned in August 2026 of 1,202 enterprise technology leaders in 12 countries. They describe what respondents report about their own organizations, not what researchers independently verified in production. Dynatrace sells observability software, so the report is best read as a snapshot of the challenges its survey respondents describe, not a census of enterprise AI use.

The survey’s adoption figure describes a particular part of enterprise technology: monitoring software and running IT systems. It does not mean 87% of companies have agents authorized to make consequential decisions on their own. One organization might use an agent to summarize an incident and propose a response for an engineer to review. Another might allow an agent to carry out a predefined action inside a narrow part of its infrastructure. Both can report using agents, even though the agents have very different responsibilities.

Respondents estimated that people still verify an average of 66% of AI-powered decisions. Separately, 12% reported fully autonomous operations, while 44% said they were targeting that level within five years. That makes the latter figure a measure of ambition, not a forecast. The survey also found that integration complexity was the most frequently cited barrier to autonomy, at 31%, alongside challenges such as skills, oversight and data quality.

Recent findings suggest that standard system monitoring can tell an engineer that a service responded and how long it took. That does not reveal whether an agent selected an appropriate action or whether the action resolved the underlying incident. For instance, if an agent recommends restarting a service, a useful record would show which alerts informed the recommendation, whether a person approved it, and what happened to the service afterward. Engineers could then check whether the restart helped or merely coincided with recovery.

Dynatrace found that 43% considered visibility into agent decisions an notable observability requirement, compared with 27% that reported addressing it. That gap is not evidence that everyone else has no logs. It is a comparison between what respondents regarded as important and what they mentioned their organizations were addressing.

In a recent development, costs add another dimension. Some 81% reported higher compute and infrastructure spending from AI workloads, and 77% said they needed better visibility into how costs are allocated. A tool that measures how long operators spend on an incident without capturing the extra resources an agent consumes can make the automation look cheaper than it is. Connecting the two measures allows teams to compare the cost of different approaches to the same work.

In a recent development, in a November 17 follow-up, Dynatrace outlined a progression from gathering evidence to recommending actions, then acting with approval before moving toward broader autonomy where warranted. For an operations team, that suggests measuring an agent against a specific task rather than increasing its permissions because it performed well in unrelated work. An agent that reliably categorizes alerts, for example, has not necessarily shown it can choose when to change a production system.

According to recent reports, the survey points to a practical test for enterprises considering greater autonomy: can a team reconstruct what an agent did, justify the authority it had, and measure whether the action helped? Without those answers, a higher adoption rate may reflect more agents in the workflow rather than evidence that they are ready to run it.
