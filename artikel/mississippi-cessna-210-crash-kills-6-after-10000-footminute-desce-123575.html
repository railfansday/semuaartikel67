Sources indicate that A Cessna 210 Centurion six-seat light aircraft has crashed into the Gulf of Mexico between Pascagoula, Mississippi, and Horn Island after descending at a rate of 10,000 feet (3,050 m) per minute. All six passengers tragically died in the crash, including four adults and two boys, aged eight and 10.

In a recent development, the Federal Aviation Administration and National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) have started an investigation into the accident. The NTSB will lead this work and provide updates as more information becomes available.

Sources indicate that the Cessna involved in the crash took off from Upcoming Orleans at 2:00 PM local time and was bound for Fort Myers, Florida, with the tail number N29002. At 3:45 PM, the regional air traffic control tower in Mobile, Alabama reported that it had lost contact with the aircraft. WVUE, a New Orleans news station, has reported that those onboard were traveling back home after enjoying a fishing trip in Venice, Louisiana.

Coast Guard responders began a search of the waters over which the aircraft had been flying. They found only three life jackets, an unoccupied life raft, and the plane's floating cabin. Divers then went into the Gulf of Mexico and recovered the bodies of all of those onboard.

Commander Trevor Blount is the coordinator for the Coast Guard's search and rescue operations in the Mobile area. He mentioned that the situation is heartbreaking. "We are grateful for the strong inter-agency coordination that enabled us to swiftly locate those aboard the aircraft and provide closure to their families," he said.

The FAA and NTSB have started their investigation into the accident. Some information has already started to emerge. For example, the Coast Guard has remarked the aircraft was falling at a rate of 10,000 feet (3,050 m) per minute when it crashed.

The FAA's database indicates that the aircraft is registered to EAB Services, who are based in Cape Coral, Florida. It was active for the past week, safely completing a few trips. This included Fort Myers to Buras, Louisiana on November 21, and Fort Myers to East Rutherfordton, North Carolina (outbound and return) on November 19.

Sources indicate that cessna accidents and incidents made headlines again on November 22. An Air Canada Boeing 737 MAX 8 received a Traffic Collision Avoidance System (TCAS) alert due to an unresponsive Cessna in high proximity to it. The 737 MAX was climbing out of Palm Springs International Airport (PSP) and bound for Vancouver International Airport (YVR).

The pilots received a resolution advisory at approximately 11,400 feet (3,500 m) to climb an additional 600 feet (183 m), allowing the two planes to reach a safe amount of separation. Luckily, the two aircraft were kept apart from each other. However, the Cessna's identity and the minimum distance that separated the two aircraft remains unclear.
