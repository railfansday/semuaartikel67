Industry News: Saudi Arabia strategy challenged by anti-Houthi forces in Yemen

Accusations of incompetence follow retreat of Riyadh-backed forces in effort to relieve siege of Taiz and protect Aden.

Saudi Arabia has been accused by anti-Houthi forces over its military strategy after its Yemeni allies withdrew troops from the strategic Bab al-Mandab strait and pulled back forces from parts of Marib in the central part of the country.

The Saudi-backed government explained the repositioning by saying the strategic priority was to break the attritional siege on Taiz, Yemen’s third city, and to protect Aden, the southern port city in which the Saudi-backed and UN-recognised government is now based.

According to recent reports, many blame the Houthi advance on the absence of a consolidated military command and control among the disparate forces backing the Aden-based government. Saudi Arabia responded to the setback by sidelining the commander of its Yemen coalition, Lt Gen Fahd bin Abdulaziz al-Salman, and replacing him with the chief of staff, Gen Fayyad bin Hamed bin Ruqad al-Ruwaili.

Recent findings suggest that the Houthis’ steady advance towards the areas surrounding Bab al-Mandab, the narrow strait linking the Red Sea with the Indian Ocean on Yemen’s west coast, culminated on Sunday with a formal withdrawal of Yemen government forces, including some in the Southern Giants Brigades that had always been reluctant to defend territory outside the old southern Yemen. In 1990 Yemen had been divided in two, and many in the south want to return to a separated state.

The Saudi-backed government launched a fresh military campaign 42 days ago to drive the Houthis from Yemen but so far it has backfired.

The forces redeployed on Sunday have moved along the coast to al-Suqyah, 95 miles west of Aden, and inside the old Southern Yemen.

Industry observers note that the retreat seems to be acknowledgment of Houthi resilience and is all the more remarkable given the Saudi air superiority. More than 300,000 Yemeni civilians have been displaced.

Amr al-Bidh, special representative of the exiled Southern Transitional Council (STC) leader, Aidarous al-Zubaidi, a separatist anti-Houthi force that is opposed by Saudi Arabia, accused the Saudis of incompetence – and in effect warned Riyadh that unless it became more open to a southern state it would lack the cohesive forces required to prevent the Houthis becoming a permanent security threat.

Industry observers note that he said: “When there is no strategy and no plan, it is inevitable the Houthis would take advantage. The STC will defend the south to the last man but we are fighting with one hand behind our backs since January after Saudi Arabia attacks on the STC.

Sources indicate that “The STC forces retain a credible capability on the ground. But we need practical and political support from the region to counter the Houthis and create the basis for a negotiated settlement, one in which the pathway towards southern independence is fully recognised. We cannot continue like this or else the Houthis will keep expanding.”.

Industry observers note that the breadth of support for the STC, backed by the United Arab Emirates, however, remains disputed. The movement suffered a near fatal setback in January and since then some of the Giants Brigades have switched to backing Riyadh.

Saudi Arabia continues in effect to keep as many as 10 STC leaders hostage in Riyadh, and has refused to let al-Zubaidi to return from exile in Abu Dhabi. Al-Bidh argued the priority should be defensive, avoiding counteroffensives that backfire, improving command and control, and widening the coalition.

Al-Bidh stated reconciliation would require Riyadh to acknowledge the political declaration al-Zubaidi made about a timetable for a southern state in January, and that al-Zubaidi’s return would boost morale and help unify chaotic southern military factions.

He said a recent Yemeni government cabinet reshuffle, including a new information minister, was designed primarily to try draw southerners into fighting the Houthis and not to change policy toward the south.

In a recent development, he also urged the UK to use its diplomacy to bring in other actors rather than simply backing Riyadh. A group of southern Yemenis have written to the foreign secretary, Ed Miliband, seeking a review of UK policy. “We recognise that the longstanding advice from your office is to continue following the Saudi line while calling for unity among the anti-Houthi forces,” the letter mentioned. “But you must surely recognise that every time Britain calls for ‘the unity and territorial integrity of Yemen’, it is asking the southern people and their armed forces to fight for a political outcome in which they do not believe”.

It has been revealed that the former US special envoy to Yemen Tim Lenderking mentioned the US administration was treating Yemen like a sideshow adding “world powers do not understand the gravity of what it is at stake here”. He warned “the problem is only going to get bigger through apathy and lack of attention”.
