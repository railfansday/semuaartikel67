Wind turbines may have killed nearly 19 million bats in the U.S. since 1981

A new study draws on decades of wind farm surveys to estimate how many bats turbines killed across the lower 48 states from 1981 to 2024.

The bats that die under wind turbines are picked up one carcass at a time, by crews walking the ground under each tower.

For decades, those site-by-site counts were the main tally anyone had.

Combining those counts with a statistical model, researchers estimated that wind turbines killed about 18.7 million bats in the lower 48 states from 1981 through the end of the study period.

It has been revealed that since 2021, the toll has run above 1.5 million a year. That is a scale the authors wrote may rival the fungal disease that has devastated cave-hibernating bats.

Charles Labuzzetta of the U.S. Geological Survey‘s Fort Collins Science Center led the analysis with colleagues at the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and the National Laboratory of the Rockies.

The figures are model estimates with wide ranges, built from monitoring that was never designed to be added up, and the relationships they found are correlations, not proven causes.

In an interview with Earth.com, Labuzzetta said the data behind the study were richer than their patchy origins suggest.

“We found that there could still be a lot to learn from the rich monitoring data that has been collected since the late 1990s and continues to be collected,” he noted.

The team pulled together 499 monitoring reports from 277 wind facilities in the lower 48 states, filed with the Fish and Wildlife Service since the late 1990s.

Each report counted the carcasses found under turbines and estimated how many were missed. But the searches differed in length, area and method, and many reports are confidential.

A statistical model pooled them, corrected for what the searchers could not see, and projected fatalities back to 1981.

The median came out at 18,696,140 bats across ten species, with a plausible range of about 13.3 million to 25.7 million.

That is the estimate for the lower 48 states that the compliance reports could never produce on their own.

Four species account for nearly all of it. The authors estimate about 6.7 million hoary bats, 5.8 million eastern red bats, 3.4 million silver-haired bats and 1.5 million massive brown bats.

According to recent reports, on average, a turbine killed close to six hoary bats a year. At the other end, the rare Indiana bat accounted for about 6,000 deaths over the whole period.

The last national figures date from 2013, and they put the toll in the hundreds of thousands a year.

Those estimates leaned on a single rule of thumb, about 1.7 hoary bats per megawatt of capacity.

A projection built on it assumed roughly 113,000 hoary bat deaths in 2012, and the new model estimates about 268,000 for that year. The old figure was less than half the new one.

The gap matters because of what the old projection, built on expert judgment, already said.

Run on the lower numbers, it warned that hoary bats could lose 50% to 90% of their population within 50 years. Eastern red bats have been dying faster still, more than 500,000 a year since 2021.

Rated capacity, the usual yardstick, turned out to be a poor predictor of deaths. The area swept by the blades was a better predictor.

On most turbines built since 2020 that is 2.5 to 5 acres (10,000 to 20,000 square meters).

Researchers estimated more deaths among several bat species at turbines whose blades swept a larger area or passed closer to the ground.

For all ten species, estimated deaths were higher at wind farms closer to rivers. The study couldn’t determine whether being near a river was itself responsible for the higher toll.

Labuzzetta told Earth.com those patterns point to siting and design as levers, but not to a single fix.

“There is still a great deal to learn about what strategies may be optimal and could provide the greatest conservation management return on investment for individual species and across all species,” he said.

White-nose syndrome, a fungal disease first seen in 2006, has cut some cave-hibernating species by more than 90%.

The authors compared their total with one rough estimate of the disease’s losses and found the two comparable in size.

It has been revealed that the two threats mostly hit different bats. The disease kills bats that hibernate in caves, while turbines mostly kill the migratory tree-roosting species.

Across North American bats as a whole, the authors wrote, the two may compound.

Whether 18.7 million deaths translate into shrinking populations is the question the study can’t answer.

Industry observers note that data on how many hoary or eastern red bats exist, how long they live and how fast they breed are largely unavailable, the authors wrote. So the same number of deaths could mean a crisis or a dent.

“Our research alone cannot answer whether fatalities from wind energy are leading to similar population-level declines for migratory tree-roosting bat species,” Labuzzetta remarked.

Population trends from the North American Bat Monitoring Program could begin to fill that gap.

The authors also called for monitoring at wind farms to be collected and shared in a form that can be pooled.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
