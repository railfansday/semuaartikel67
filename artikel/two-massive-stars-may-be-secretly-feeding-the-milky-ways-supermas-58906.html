Two massive stars may be secretly feeding the Milky Way’s supermassive black hole

Sources indicate that latest observations and computer simulations have revealed a surprising possible source of mysterious gas clouds near the Milky Way's central supermassive black hole. An international research team led by MPE has traced these compact clouds to a massive pair of stars whose powerful winds appear to produce material that could eventually help feed the black hole, known as Sagittarius A*.

In a recent development, the center of our galaxy is an extraordinarily crowded and active environment. At its core sits Sagittarius A* (Sgr A*), a supermassive black hole surrounded by stars, gas, and dust moving through a region dominated by intense gravitational forces. This extreme setting gives astronomers an opportunity to investigate how matter moves near black holes and where the material that sustains their activity comes from.

Industry observers note that over the past two decades, infrared observations have revealed several small, dense gas clouds traveling close to Sagittarius A*. These mysterious "clumps" may provide vital clues about how material makes its way toward the black hole. However, scientists have struggled to determine where the clouds originate and what causes them to form.

Mysterious Gas Clouds Near the Milky Way's Black Hole.

In a recent development, one of the first major discoveries came in 2012, when astronomers identified a compact cloud of ionized gas called G2. Containing several times Earth's mass, the cloud produces emissions from hydrogen and helium, consistent with hot gas mixed with dust. It follows a highly elongated orbit around Sagittarius A* and has a faint extension trailing behind it, known as G2t.

Sources indicate that soon afterward, researchers examining older observations recognized another similar object, G1, traveling along a closely related path. The similarities suggested that G1, G2, and G2t might not be independent objects. Instead, they could represent concentrations of gas within a larger, continuous stream.

Recent findings suggest that even relatively small differences in gas density can make such a stream appear broken into separate clouds. This happens because the brightness of the gas increases in proportion to the square of its density, making denser regions stand out much more strongly.

According to recent reports, more recently, astronomers observed gas in G2's trailing material gathering into another compact cloud that follows a similar orbit. Although this newly formed object might naturally be called G3, that designation had already been assigned to a different object. Together, the related clouds trace a connected flow of material through the Galactic Center, known as the G1-2-3 streamer.

Industry observers note that these clouds could play a surprisingly crucial role in sustaining the black hole. Calculations suggest that if approximately one Earth mass of gas reaches Sagittarius A* every ten years, it could provide enough fuel to maintain the black hole's current level of activity. Discovering how the clouds form could therefore help explain how the black hole receives its supply of matter.

Astronomers Trace the Gas Clouds to Their Source.

Researchers have considered several possible explanations for the clouds, including powerful winds from massive stars, stellar explosions such as novae, and gas pulled away from objects by the black hole's gravity.

According to recent reports, to investigate, the MPE-led team used two advanced infrared spectrographs, SINFONI and ERIS. Both instruments employ adaptive optics to produce sharper observations. By examining a specific hydrogen emission feature called the Brackett‑γ line, the astronomers measured the clouds' positions and velocities and reconstructed their paths around Sagittarius A*.

The results revealed a striking pattern. G1, G2, and G2t all follow orbits with nearly the same shape and orientation. It would be extraordinarily unlikely for three unrelated clouds to share such closely matching orbital characteristics, strongly suggesting that they originated from the same source.

Two Massive Stars May Be Creating the Clouds.

By working backward from the clouds' observed motions and their velocities along our line of sight, the researchers identified a promising source: IRS 16SW, a massive contact binary system consisting of two stars in extremely close proximity.

According to recent reports, the stellar pair belongs to a disk of young stars that revolves clockwise around Sagittarius A*. Its location and motion provide a plausible explanation for the gas stream. Even the slight differences between the clouds' orbital paths can be accounted for by the movement of the two stars around one another.

Computer simulations of how gas flows and interacts provided additional support for this explanation. The models indicate that powerful stellar winds from the binary system can collide with surrounding material, creating a shock in the region between the stars. Gas then accumulates and becomes compressed, forming dense concentrations that can eventually separate from the system.

According to recent reports, once released, these compact clouds can travel inward along paths resembling those observed in the G1-2-3 streamer. This provides a physical explanation for how one binary star system could produce several distinct clouds moving along closely related orbits.

A Fresh Clue to How Supermassive Black Holes Are Fed.

The findings suggest that massive stars near the Galactic Center may provide a continuing supply of gas capable of fueling Sagittarius A*. Rather than relying only on material already circulating around the black hole, the system could receive fresh gas from the powerful winds generated by nearby stars.

The discovery also brings together several notable processes in astronomy. The evolution of massive stars, the movement and compression of gas, and the supply of material to supermassive black holes may all be closely connected. By identifying a possible source of the mysterious clouds near Sagittarius A*, researchers have uncovered a latest way in which stars could help sustain black hole activity at the heart of our own galaxy.
