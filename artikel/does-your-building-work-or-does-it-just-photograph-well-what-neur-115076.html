Architecture reaches people through whatever medium carries it, and each new medium changes our picture of what a good building looks like. For example, when 3D modeling software arrived, the novelty of complex shapes drove the starchitecture era of the 2010s, and projects built on those experiments earned awards such as the Pritzker Prize. Today the medium is the social media feed. A building exists first as a model and then as a render, long before it exists as a place. Meanwhile, research in neuroarchitecture is building a different picture based on how people actually experience buildings. So how could those two seemingly unrelated topics close a loop that may lead to the profession's next creative frontier?

Industry observers note that in the book Cognitive Architecture, Ann Sussman and Justin Hollander examine how the brain reacts to the built environment. In their research, people looked at photographs of buildings while their eye movements were tracked. Viewers tended to skip blank, featureless facades but lingered on details, contrast, and other people. One case study, the Stapleton Library in Latest York, demonstrates how much fenestration patterns matter: windows provide areas of contrast that the eye seeks out and stays with. Their study suggests that architecture tuned to these cues could serve its occupants at a layer deeper than aesthetics. Sussman's broader argument is that the brain works at walking speed and eye level, hunting for faces, contrast, and activity at the ground floor.

According to recent reports, anjan Chatterjee's lab at the University of Pennsylvania adds nuance to this picture. They summarize architectural experience in three elements: coherence, or how organized and legible a space is; fascination, or how rich in information an environment is; and hominess, or how comfortable it feels. Everyone weighs these differently, but people with an architectural background seem to give coherence more weight than non-architects do. At the same time, he also makes a second point: architects often predict how a building will work, but they rarely collect post-occupancy information to test those predictions.

In some cases, this might even put the architectural brief at risk of becoming a framing exercise: a legible silhouette, a dramatic angle, a frontal moment that survives a person's attention span. Therefore, it is possible to see BIM as one more medium that represents a building, while the interesting part could be the shift from representing intent to recording use. Imagine adding the missing column to that spreadsheet: how the building is actually used. Temperature, daylight, noise, density, and anonymized demographics, from every building a firm completes. Because neuroscience is about perception, not just physical conditions, the column would pair those sensor readings with simple occupant feedback on how coherent, engaging, and comfortable a space feels, and with observations of where people actually linger. Over time, this could become a database of what worked, for whom, and in which climate and specific conditions. Architecture's next creative frontier may not be bolder forms, but a humbler habit: go back, measure, and let the building tell you what actually worked.
