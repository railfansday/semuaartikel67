Venice Biennale Awards Selected by Public Vote Go to Nick Cave, Indian Pavilion

It has been revealed that after a Venice Biennale of bitter controversy, the exhibition has at last revealed the winners of its top honors, which were chosen this year by public vote instead of a jury of esteemed curators because that panel resigned in protest. This time around, the awards went to the American artist Nick Cave for his participation in Koyo Kouoh’s main exhibition and India for its national pavilion.

Dubbed the Visitors’ Lions, these awards replaced the Golden Lions, which are regularly given to participants in the main exhibition and artists representing countries with pavilions in the exhibition. This time around, just two awards were given out, one for an artist in the main show and one for a national pavilion. The Biennale was forced to instate the Visitors’ Lions after the five-person Golden Lion jury resigned in May, in advance of the show’s opening. By that point, the curators had already said that they would “refrain from considering those countries whose leaders are at the moment charged with crimes against humanity by the International Criminal Court,” a move that would exclude Israel and Russia from consideration. More than half of the artists in the main show said they would not accept the Visitors’ Lions in solidarity.

Cave’s contributions to the Kouoh’s show, titled “In Minor Keys,” included a decidedly major work: Amalgam (Origin), a 26-foot-tall bronze sculpture in which a person’s body sprouts branches and wildlife. The work, which appeared outdoors at the Arsenale, derived from a similar-looking proposal for a piece to replace a toppled Confederate monument.

It has been revealed that inside and outside the Arsenale, there were yet more large-scale Cave sculptures that composed Two Points in Time – At Once, which featured more figures whose bodies fuse with plants. Needlepointed portraits accompanied those figures. “The works address the stages of grief toward mourning, rebirth, and radical connection,” Cave wrote on social media in May, when the Biennale opened.

India’s pavilion, titled “Geographies of Distance: Remembering Home,” dealt largely with how objects act as vessels for memories and featured such grandly sized pieces as Sumakshi Singh’s Permanent Address, in which the walls of a house were remade in see-through thread, causing the domicile to appear flimsy and impermanent.

According to recent reports, alongside Singh, the pavilion featured works by Alwar Balasubramaniam, Ranjani Shettar, Asim Waqif, and Skarma Sonam Tashi. Organized by curator Amin Jaffer, the pavilion was the first staged by India at the Biennale since 2019.

In a statement posted to social media, the Indian ministry of culture mentioned, “The recognition highlights the talent of Indian artists and the growing global presence of contemporary Indian art.”.

The Biennale’s spare release unusually did not include citations for why the Indian Pavilion and Cave won, likely because neither had been selected by a jury. Instead, the release included a statement from Biennale president Pietrangelo Buttafuoco, who remarked, “The public of the Exhibition embraced this recognition – the Visitors’ Award – as an opportunity to bring Pablo Picasso’s practice to life: a painting only comes to life through the person who looks at it. I can say without hesitation that La Biennale is brimming with ever-renewed energy thanks to the vibrant presence of everyone.”.

When the Biennale opened, dozens of participants in Kouoh’s main exhibition, as well as several dozen more with national pavilions, signed a statement saying that they did not want to be considered for the Visitors’ Lions as a form of solidarity with the resigned jury. If these artists were selected as winners, they said, the Biennale could expect legal action. “Not only is this confusing for visitors, it is blatantly disrespectful towards the artists who have made the request for removal of their names,” the artists said in a statement.

Though it did not directly respond to the artists, the Biennale said in its release about the Visitors’ Lions that “all national participations” were eligible, since the exhibition is about “openness, dialogue, and the rejection of any form of closure or censorship.”.

Notably, neither Cave nor the artists representing India signed the e-flux statement, which was endorsed by many of the artists with most talked-about pavilions in Venice—including Florentina Holzinger, whose Austrian Pavilion generated long lines and emerged as an early favorite during the professional preview.

In a recent development, the Visitors’ Lions bring to an end a tumultuous Biennale marked by protests over Israel and Russia’s pavilions, which faced an outcry both before and during the Biennale, with many calling for both countries to be ejected altogether. The Biennale sought to proclaim itself a neutral entity, saying that it cannot kick out any nation recognized as a state by the Italian government. But the logic proved unpersuasive for many, including the European Union, which mentioned it will revoke a 2 million euro grant that it typically awards to the Biennale as a protest against the Russian Pavilion’s return this year, during a period when Russia continues to face EU sanctions over its war in Ukraine.

Lifetime achievement prizes are typically revealed ahead of the Biennale’s opening, but none were awarded this time because Kouoh, the curator of the main show, died in 2025 before she could select the winners. A Silver Lion, for a promising young artist in the main exhibition, was not awarded during this round, either, likely because that prize is given out by a jury who was not present to offer it.

The Biennale has positioned this year’s fraught edition as a resounding success. Upon its closure this past weekend, the Biennale said the show received more than 714,000 visitors, the second-highest attendance figure in the exhibition’s history. “This edition of the Biennale, so rich in events and complex developments—so immersed in the harsh affairs of the world—was pervaded by a vivid spirit, rewarded indeed by the number of visitors,” Buttafuoco, the Biennale president, said in a statement this past weekend.

According to recent reports, the Biennale was initially expected to announce the Visitors’ Lions when the exhibition concluded its run on Sunday. Asked by ARTnews about the delay, a Biennale spokesperson stated that extra time was necessary to “ensure that sufficient time is available to complete the necessary overall vote count.”.
