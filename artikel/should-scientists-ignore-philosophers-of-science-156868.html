Should Scientists Ignore Philosophers of Science?

Posted November 26, 2026 | Reviewed by Kaja Perina.

According to recent reports, the eminent evolutionary biologist and modeller John Maynard Smith was once asked about Karl Popper and the philosophy of science. His answer was not flattering. His impression was that scientists who take the philosophy of science seriously, and allow their research to be influenced by philosophical preconceptions, are "far more likely to do themselves harm than good". He loved reading philosophy of science and was always ready to argue about it, he added, but he did not believe that one should allow oneself to be influenced by it when actually thinking about science.

In a recent development, this is why I've recently published an academic article on the topic. These are quite radical words, and though Maynard Smith's generalisation is untenable, there is an crucial insight to be extracted.

If modellers approach the philosophical literature on models with the goal of figuring out what models are and what a good model is, they might easily find themselves lured into naive monistic views of models that fail to represent the full epistemic richness of modelling practices, thereby turning them into worse scientists than they could have otherwise been.

In a recent development, this could happen in several ways. For instance, models with epistemic functions not recognised by narrow monist accounts might be discarded. Consider, for example, models with weak predictive power, but great generality and insight into complex systems.

In a recent development, perhaps Maynard Smith's worry is most strongly illustrated in economics, where Milton Friedman and Karl Popper are taken to have the "last word" in any methodological debate, and are frequently invoked to both defend and criticise the work of modellers. They are perhaps paradigm cases of monist thinking. Friedman argued that the only relevant desideratum in science concerns predictive power, while Popper argued that what matters is falsifiability.

These accounts of science have received extensive criticism over the last decades. Indeed, the philosopher Alex Rosenberg argued that Friedman's naive picture single-handedly justified the existence of philosophy of economics as a distinct profession. Economists, however, are unlikely to read much if any work on methodology beyond Friedman and Popper. As the economist Dani Rodrik notes, economic methodology is relegated to specialised journals and, more importantly, is not really being taught in graduate school.

The result is that economists seem to believe that their science operates according to a simple form of falsificationism, a picture of science that, most contemporary philosophers of science would agree, both misrepresents scientific practice and provides the wrong ideal of how science ought to function.

The obvious counter to Maynard Smith's concern is that it is not an argument against the philosophy of science, but rather against bad philosophy of science, which limits, rather than fosters, insights into the richness of the epistemic tools available to scientists.

Sources indicate that philosophers are not innocent here. Some debates in the philosophy of science rest on such monist motivations to seek univocal accounts of laws, explanation, species, functions, preferences, and many more. As Peter Godfrey-Smith emphasised, one of the hazards of philosophy is the temptation to come up with theories that are "too broad and sweeping". This hazard has not been avoided in the philosophical literature on models. Many philosophers of science seem to be under the impression that once we figure out what "models" are and how they work, we can finally get a handle on all the other pressing questions in the philosophy of science. I argue that this way of thinking about models is grounded in a mistake.

A philosophy of science worth reading takes the plurality, diversity, and richness of models and modelling practices seriously. It does not hand scientists a single criterion for good science.

In a recent development, economists, Rodrik argues, need a better story about the kind of science they practise, and some economists have recently engaged with philosophical questions themselves, seeking contact with philosophers of economics in the process. Philosophers of science, in turn, should turn more of their attention toward both the social sciences and the philosophy of the social sciences. Their lack of engagement with this rich literature is a historical omission that may have provided a misguided understanding of how science works.

Should scientists ignore philosophers? They should ignore those who tell them that there is only one way of doing science well.
