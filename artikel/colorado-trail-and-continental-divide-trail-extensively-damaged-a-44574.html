Colorado Trail and Continental Divide Trail ‘Extensively Damaged’ After Severe Flooding: Here’s What We Know

This article was updated on November 18, 2026, at 11:48 a.m. with additional information regarding the condition of the Colorado Trail section 28 and the Continental Divide Trail.

In just over a week, two massive flooding events hit southwestern Colorado, destroying homes, businesses, and infrastructure across numerous mountain towns. But property is not all that the floods affected. Parts of both the Continental Divide Trail (CDT) and the Colorado Trail (CT) suffered damage that locals and trail organizations are calling “extensive” and “severe”, and which could take a very long time to repair.

“Rebuilding these damaged sections will not be quick, easy, or cheap,” Paul Talley, executive director of the Colorado Trail Foundation (CTF), said in a November 5 enhancement on the floods. “But we are committed to rebuilding.”.

As many of these areas are still inaccessible, there is very little confirmed information on their current state. Videos circulating online show missing bridges at key river crossings, and reports from the USFS and other agencies indicate that trailheads are damaged, and “heavy debris” is blocking some sections. But there has yet to be a full, official report on damage to these trails.

Here is what we know so far based on official updates, local reports, and videos.

Junction Creek is the CT’s southernmost section. Its trailhead, just outside of Durango, is also the CT’s southern terminus. Junction Creek Trail crosses multiple footbridges over the creek along its 19-mile stretch. A video posted to Instagram shows the Junction Creek trailhead sign that many hikers use for their CT terminus photo now standing directly at the edge of a tall wash filled with flood debris.

In a recent development, “Junction Creek Trail #553 suffered a near-total loss in places where it parallels and crosses Junction Creek,” a USFS conditions report from November 13 reads. “Miles of trail as well as multiple bridges crossing Junction Creek were wiped out, replaced with massive piles of boulders, trees, and other flood debris.”.

Sources indicate that according to the report, both the lowest trail bridge at 2.6 miles from the trailhead and the Walls Gulch Trail Bridge at 14.6 miles were destroyed. One social media video shows the first footbridge washed away completely. The USFS posted its own video of the road to Junction Creek to Facebook, showing a “20-foot waterfall” over the edge of the destroyed road.

“There is no doubt that trails across the forest have suffered severe damage,” the San Juan National Forest wrote.

In an interview with The Gazette, Talley stated, “The Colorado Trail Foundation has never experienced an event that damaged this much trail. This is the largest section of trail ever damaged in our 52 years.”.

According to recent reports, authorities claim that recovery work will likely begin in spring and carry into summer 2027 due to weather. They discouraged hikers from even attempting to set out on the area’s trails.

“Record flooding across Southwest Colorado has left San Juan National Forest roads, trails, and other infrastructure inaccessible, severely damaged, and, in many cases, impassable,” a USDA Forest Service Facebook post reads. “Don’t become a statistic—rescue will not be readily available due to the current demand for search and rescue services.”.

The CTF noted that it will be working over the winter with USFS, local partners, and volunteers to determine timelines and project budgets for the restoration efforts.

“It will be months before we can better understand what the next steps need to be on the sections damaged by climate events this season,” wrote Talley in his November 5 update.

There is even less information regarding damage to the CDT, which also passes through the flood-affected areas of Southwestern Colorado.

The Continental Divide Trail Coalition (CDTC) posted on Facebook on October 23, warning hikers that flooding had made “travel to and from the CDT trailhead at Wolf Creek Pass impossible,” and that “hikers may need to make alternate intends to avoid the area.”.

Industry observers note that on November 2, the CDTC posted another revision for CDT travelers. “The extent of damage to the CDT, access roads, and CDT communities is still unknown,” it stated. While the interactive map on its website demonstrates no current closures of the trail due to flooding, the CDTC advised hikers to use “extreme caution and check with local authorities before visiting.”.

Recent findings suggest that outside of Colorado, Latest Mexico also experienced severe flooding. However, Audra Labert with the CDTC told Backpacker, “The area of New Mexico near the southern terminus did not experience significant damage from flooding.”.

While the full extent of the damage to these trails remains to be seen, what’s certain is that the CTF, CDTC, USFS, and other state agencies have a great deal of restoration work to do next summer. Volunteers will be in high demand, and Talley, of the CTF, promised to be open about how hikers can help.

Sources indicate that “While we do not yet know what it will take to rebuild, we do know what we will need,” Talley wrote. “We will need our supporters, we will need our dedicated volunteers, and we will need that community spirit that makes the CT so special.”.
