iPhone Duo, One Month Later: I’m Not Ready to End the Love Affair

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that one month after getting the iPhone Duo, I’ve stuck to my commitment to keep using it even after publishing my review. Well, mostly.

Recent findings suggest that apple’s debut foldable has proven to be a practical everyday device, whether I’m streaming movies, texting or getting work done. I’ve grown so accustomed to using it that I sometimes forget how much attention it draws when I pull it out of my pocket, especially given its limited availability and lengthy preorder windows – not to mention its sky-high $1,999 starting price.

According to recent reports, still, there have been times when I’ve reached for the iPhone 18 Pro Max instead for its more advanced camera system, as well as its familiar, straightforward design. The Duo’s cameras pull their weight with casual photography, but while filming social content at F1’s US Grand Prix and chatting with filmmakers at an SFFilm screening, I couldn’t resist going for the best cameras at my disposal on the Pro Max. There are times a conventional slab phone just does the trick – without all the frills and novelty of a folding one.

That hasn’t stopped me from keeping the Duo as my primary device. It’s an enjoyable phone to use, with its compact, palm-sized build and spacious inner display. Plus, as CNET’s ongoing folding test demonstrates, it has a durable build that ensures I can fold and unfold it to my heart’s content.

And if I’m being honest, I kind of love how much of a statement piece it’s become.

Industry observers note that the Duo’s versatility has really shown its appeal the more I live with it. I find myself using the 5.4-inch cover screen and 7.6-inch inner display pretty equally, demonstrating the practicality of each.

Sources indicate that when I’m on the move, the cover screen suffices for sending texts, responding to emails and checking notifications. Lounging around at home, I’m more inclined to use the larger inner display to endlessly scroll through Instagram or binge YouTube videos.

Recent findings suggest that the matte inner display remains one of my favorite elements. It reduces the appearance of the crease as well as glare, making it easier to see content even outdoors. Yes, the coating can dim the vibrant colors in images and videos, even with the screen’s 3,000-nit peak brightness, and smudges become prominent. But I can live with both of those drawbacks, and a microfiber cloth fixes the latter.

Apple’s phone-tablet hybrid came in clutch when I was on a recent flight and didn’t want to pull out my laptop. Instead, I used the Duo’s split-screen mode to place the Notes app on one half of the screen and an informational video on the other so I could promptly jot down the main points. The Duo has also become my gym buddy; I prop it open on the treadmill so I can watch Magpie Murders on a tablet-like display, then fold it up and toss it in my pocket when I’m done.

It has been revealed that apple Pencil support really helps the Duo feel like a mini iPad, and the matte coating on the inner display adds a satisfying resistance that more closely emulates the feel of writing on paper than a glossy surface. It gives jotting down notes, highlighting book passages and marking up documents a more tactile feel. I’m not much of a doodler, but I imagine that’s fun, too.

The Apple Pencil can magnetically attach to the outer surface of the hinge, though it definitely sticks out, so there’s not really a practical, secure way to carry it around with the Duo (at least not that Apple has shared, but case-makers are sure to cook up a solution). I’m glad the stylus also works on the Duo’s cover screen, though the smaller size and glossy display make that experience slightly less satisfying.

In a recent development, the number of apps optimized for the Duo at launch was relatively small, but it’s steadily increased. I gasped aloud on the train platform one morning while sending an email, realizing Gmail had finally been adapted for the Duo. It’s refreshing to see apps that feel intentional and fill the screen, rather than being awkwardly letterboxed.

Sources indicate that apple noted in early November that more than 100,000 apps built for the iPhone Duo were live in the App Store. A week later, that number ballooned to over 200,000 apps. So despite a rather slow start, virtually all apps I regularly use now work seamlessly on Apple’s foldable.

One of my favorite apps on the Duo is Instagram’s Edits. When holding the phone open in landscape orientation, I can view the video timeline and make edits on the right side of the screen while seeing a preview of the video on the left. Rotated to portrait orientation, the layout resembles the interface on a traditional smartphone, but on a larger scale, which is helpful especially for making smaller tweaks.

It’s great to see more apps that don’t just expand to the larger screen, but make meaningful use of the Duo’s design.

As I found during my review, the Duo’s battery life has been pretty good. It consistently lasts through the day, even as I hop between displays. I usually charge my phone at night, but even if I don’t, I know I’ll have some remaining battery in the morning.

For instance, one day I started at 6:30 a.m. with a full battery. After my usual routine of checking emails, texting, scrolling through social media and browsing YouTube throughout the day, my battery was at 55% by 9:12 p.m. The next morning, I woke up with 48% battery, and that charge lasted through a similar routine, along with phone calls, texting and music and video streaming, before the battery finally died at 7:30 p.m.

The Duo has yet to give me a reason to stress about its battery life.

I will acknowledge I’m a tad pretentious when it comes to smartphone cameras. But if I’m documenting an event or photographing my fancy tea sets, I want the best cameras I can get. Unlike the Duo, iPhone 18 Pro models have a telephoto camera, more advanced optical zoom capabilities and larger main-camera sensors.

According to recent reports, most of the time, the Duo’s 48-megapixel wide and ultrawide cameras do the trick just fine. Colors are vivid, details are sharp and images are bright. Videos look good, too, though the 18 Pro Max does a noticeably better job with nighttime footage, which can look more muddy on the Duo. Digital video on the Duo also tops out at 6x, versus 24x on the Pros. Most of the time, though, I’m quite content with what I capture with the Duo’s cameras.

As I noted in my review, the under-display FaceTime camera on the inner screen isn’t great, but I never really take selfies using that camera. It feels much more natural to use the 12-megapixel selfie camera on the cover screen, which serves up sharp photos every time.

The cover screen selfie camera plays a critical role in an patch that makes using the Duo even more seamless. In its latest iOS 27.3 developer beta, Apple added an option to unlock the iPhone Duo using the front camera.

In a recent development, in the absence of Face ID, which I have felt rather painfully, this has been a solid workaround, even if it’s not as secure as Apple’s biometric facial recognition and authentication system. You’ll still need to use Touch ID or a passcode when using Apple Pay or accessing passwords, and to unlock the phone after leaving it alone for around 45 minutes. But a win is a win. (You can also unlock the Duo using an Apple Watch, but I don’t have one.).

In a recent development, I’ll admit I still feel a little bashful when pulling out the Duo because of how much attention it drums up, despite being far from the first foldable phone. But its limited availability and hype paired with the unique design make it eye-catching, for better or worse. Using the Duo has led to some fun conversations, whether it’s with a sales associate as I pay for a pastry or with Project Hail Mary directors Phil Lord and Chris Miller, who spotted the phone in my hand during an interview and geeked out over it. That’s been a fun side effect of using Apple’s first foldable in my everyday life.

In a recent development, I’ve been living dangerously without a case; I don’t want anything to detract from the phone’s sleek feel. This is one of those “do as I say, not as I do” moments, as I’d recommend slapping a case on a phone that costs nearly $2,000. I did put a tempered glass screen protector on the cover display to guard against scratches. I can feel its edge against the otherwise flush surface of the phone when I hold it open, but aesthetic sacrifices must be made.

Sources indicate that the Duo isn’t perfect, even if I love using this phone. After holding Samsung’s Galaxy Z Fold 8, the iPhone Duo can feel slightly chunky. The Z Fold 8 measures 4.5mm thick when open and 9.7mm when closed. Meanwhile, the Duo is 5.2mm thick when open and 11.3mm when closed. It’s not a huge difference, but it makes me look forward to Apple hopefully slimming down the second-generation version, if and when it comes.

According to recent reports, the Duo is also slightly heavier than the Z Fold 8, weighing 254 grams (compared to 201 grams for Samsung’s foldable). I’m always surprised when people remark how heavy the Duo is, but I think the reason I don’t share that sentiment is that I’m accustomed to the iPhone 18 Pro Max, which weighs 249 grams.

Recent findings suggest that when the Duo is open in landscape orientation, I’m glad there’s an option to split the keyboard so it’s easier to type across the wider expanse, but I wish the swipe functionality worked in that configuration, too. It’s a little thing that would make a noticeable difference for those of us who rely heavily on swipe-to-text.

One drawback of the Duo’s compact design is that some existing MagSafe wallets and other accessories won’t stick to the back without awkwardly jutting out. So you might have to invest in some newly introduced accessories specifically designed for the Duo.

Until the recent addition of face unlock, the Duo’s biggest drawback was the lack of Face ID. It’s a feature I’d expect on such a premium device, especially given that even the budget-focused iPhone 17E supports it.

Sources indicate that touch ID works reliably on the Duo, but it’s slow compared to fingerprint unlock on the Galaxy Z Fold 8 (which also supports facial recognition). And while the new face unlock capability on the Duo isn’t an exact replacement for Face ID, which is more secure, it helps make accessing the phone’s contents more convenient and in line with the Duo’s modern aesthetics.

In a recent development, I’ve finished my homework: My review is done, and now, so is this one-month follow-up. What’s next?

In a recent development, I’m not sure I’m ready to throw in the towel on Apple’s foldable just yet. The novelty of the iPhone Duo hasn’t worn off, and I’ve become quite used to my passport-size companion. It does just about everything I need, despite some slight imperfections, and it’s fun to use. It’s also amusing to see how people react when they see it in my hand.

Recent findings suggest that so for now, it looks like the Duo will remain my daily driver. Whether the honeymoon phase eventually wears off remains to be seen, but I’m not in any hurry to end this love affair.
