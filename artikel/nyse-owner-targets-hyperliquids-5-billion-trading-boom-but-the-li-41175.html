NYSE owner targets Hyperliquid’s $5 billion trading boom, but the liquidity may not follow

Intercontinental Exchange (ICE), the New York Stock Exchange’s owner, is working with Trade[XYZ] to bring Hyperliquid-based perpetual futures to the US.

The proposed venue would have to attract its own buy and sell orders, Hyunsu Jung, CEO of Nasdaq-listed Hyperion DeFi, told CryptoSlate.

Jung said firms quoting on both the proposed US venue and existing overseas markets could help keep prices close if the venues match orders and handle funding similarly.

The amount a trader can buy or sell at those prices would depend on the orders available on the US market. Jung’s firm builds on Hyperliquid and earns revenue from work in its ecosystem.

ICE announced the agreement on Oct. 29 and mentioned it would be the exclusive operator of the regulated markets, using a dedicated retail venue and clearing structure separate from its institutional futures business.

ICE also declared a minority investment in Trade[XYZ], subject to regulatory approvals, including approvals needed to support leveraged trading. ICE’s announcement sets out who would operate and clear the market, leaving the trading rules and exact order-book design unspecified.

Recent findings suggest that perpetual futures let traders take positions on the price of a reference asset through a contract with no set expiration date. Trade[XYZ] already offers contracts on equities, stock indexes, commodities and currencies to eligible customers outside the US.

ICE expects customers who pass identity checks to access the proposed market through participating futures brokers, formally known as futures commission merchants, and also expects its joint venture with OKX to help distribute the contracts.

That route depends on separate approvals for the venture’s broker-dealer and futures commission merchant businesses.

The existing HIP-3 design gives each builder-deployed venue its own order books and margining on Hyperliquid’s shared trading infrastructure.

Recent findings suggest that in its November quarterly filing, Hyperion DeFi described ICE and Trade[XYZ]’s strategy in the context of HIP-3, an optional extension that lets operators control access. The filing tied the prepares it discussed to regulatory approvals, finalizing the extension, and making it available on the live network.

If a comparable contract traded at a higher price on one venue, a firm able to trade both could buy on the cheaper venue and sell on the more expensive one. Those trades would tend to bring the two prices closer.

A market maker that takes the other side of a customer’s trade in the US could manage some of the resulting exposure overseas if it can access both venues.

In a recent development, jung said margin and leverage rules for futures broker accounts would help determine how much liquidity firms can provide. Margin is the collateral a firm sets aside to support a position, and the more collateral a trade requires, the more of a firm’s capital it ties up.

Trade[XYZ] recorded over $4.9 billion of the $5 billion traded across tracked HIP-3 venues in the 24 hours through Nov. 18, representing 97.6% of the total. EntropyIO, the next-largest venue by volume, accounted for 2.3%, according to UnderHyper’s venue breakdown.

UnderHyper’s measure of rolling dollar volume counts completed trades across the HIP-3 venues it tracks.

Jung attributed Trade[XYZ]’s lead to listing key markets early and making them available in the Hyperliquid interface traders already used.

ICE hopes to reach US customers through brokers and distribution partners, but the size of that customer base and how often it trades will become clear only if the US venue opens.

If the US venue opens, traders will be able to judge its liquidity by the gap between the best buy and sell prices and how much they can trade near those prices. The test for ICE will come when traders try to move a large position near the price they first saw.
