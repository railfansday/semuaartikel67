Industry News: Her friend died after giving birth. Now she’s writing legislation to save other moms

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that mothering America is a regular column from Motherly and Chamber of Mothers sharing moms’ stories from across the country, showing the power of coming together to make change. Each piece is part of a larger effort to ensure every mother and family in the U.S. has the support and care to be well.

In 2008, Lauren Underwood was in graduate school at Johns Hopkins, earning a master’s in public health, when she met a woman with a substantial, bright smile named Shalon Irving.

According to recent reports, she never would have imagined the impact that friendship would have on her life’s work. Underwood would eventually become a U.S. representative for the state of Illinois, fighting to protect moms like Irving, who died before she could see her friend take office.

At the time, Irving, who already had her PhD, was coming back to school for additional training in public health. “We were just like regular girlfriends, passing notes in class and everything,” Rep. Underwood says.

Recent findings suggest that the two—Rep. Underwood, a registered nurse, and Irving, an epidemiologist—stayed in touch through the years. In January 2017, Rep. Underwood served as a senior advisor at the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, and Irving worked for the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). She had also given birth to a beautiful baby girl named Soleil.

It has been revealed that three weeks later, though, Irving wasn’t feeling well. “She went to the doctor several times, explaining that something wasn’t right,” Rep. Underwood remembers. On January 28, Irving died of complications related to high blood pressure. She was 36.

It has been revealed that “Shalon had a career focused on ending health disparities, and she was so excited to become a mom,” Rep. Underwood indicates.

Today, Rep. Underwood is the architect of a series of 14 bills called the Momnibus, which was reintroduced to Congress this spring. The Momnibus addresses “every clinical and non-clinical factor” leading to preventable maternal deaths and disparities in this country; it’s evidence-based, data-driven, and offers states, communities, providers, and nonprofits the resources to help save moms like Irving’s lives. For Rep. Underwood, the Momnibus is personal.

It has been revealed that while a member of Congress, Rep. Underwood is also a woman and a friend who, like too many in this country, lost a dear friend and felt a fire to make change.

Sources indicate that rep. Underwood recalls Irving’s funeral as devastating—particularly because many of those in attendance held positions of the highest authority in public health in this country. She remembers the head of the CDC speaking at Irving’s funeral and expressing bewilderment at her death.

According to recent reports, for Rep. Underwood, it was no mystery why mothers like Irving die in childbirth and shortly thereafter. As a clinician, she had seen firsthand health disparities in action; as a Black woman, maternal mortality was an unfortunate “phenomenon where it’s just so common when you talk about the maternal crisis.”.

In a recent development, today, we know about 84 percent of pregnancy-related deaths like Irving’s are preventable—meaning that most of the moms who aren’t here today should be here, and could be here, with the proper supports in place. Black moms are more than three times as likely to die in pregnancy or the postpartum months than white women—a disparity that persists across income and education levels.

Sources indicate that “People think higher education or higher income is protective. They think that if you are a healthcare worker, you’re going to get the best healthcare. If you know the risks, are self-aware, confident, and have agency, and you can speak up for yourself, then these kinds of things won’t happen to you. Shalon and her death fly in the face of all of those assumptions, and it breaks your heart.”.

It has been revealed that when she made the decision to run for Congress, Rep. Underwood knew that if she won, maternal mortality would be an concern she’d work on.

In 2018, Rep. Underwood became the youngest Black woman ever elected to Congress. She teamed up with Rep. Alma Adams (D-NC), who also had personal experience with maternal health: Adams’ daughter had experienced a very challenging labor and delivery, known in medical terms as severe maternal morbidity, or an experience that has lasting impacts on a mother’s health.

Together, in April 2019, the two formed the Black Maternal Health Caucus. At a crowded press conference, Rep. Underwood fought back tears. “To be in this moment with the dome of the Capitol behind us and these cameras and people asking questions and it’s this diverse, multi-generational, multi-ethnic, male and female moment of speaking about an concern that was so personal to me—I was just overcome,” she claims. “It was a moment of power and resolve. It was an invitation. We were planting a flag, saying, ‘We’re here. We’re going to do this work. We know that there’s an opportunity to bring change.’”.

Rep. Underwood didn’t expect the Black Maternal Health Caucus to grow into one of the largest bipartisan caucuses on the Hill. “We left that press conference and our phones started ringing, and it never stopped.”.

Recent findings suggest that rep. Underwood has dedicated her career to ensuring everyone—moms included—has equitable healthcare so we can all feel our best. She’s also dedicated her career to ending the maternal mortality crisis. “We know exactly what to do. We just need to invest, have the resolve, and to say, ‘This is unacceptable. Let’s fix this concern.’”.

She also reminds us that Congress works on the issues that members raise. “If a Member comes in and claims, ‘This is crucial to my community, and this is an essential issue that we tackle,’ that’s how solutions advance.” Many of her ideas for bills, she says, come from your stories.

“Often, people think that their story doesn’t matter, and when they come in to advocate, they think they need to have statistics or be a walking encyclopedia,” Rep. Underwood indicates. “But we are in a relationship business, and connection is currency. The fastest way to build a connection is to share your humanity, tell your story, and invite space for that vulnerability.”.

What that means for you: If you have a story to share, Members of Congress are home in their districts through the end of the month, with offices open Monday through Friday and available for meetings. (Not sure how to set up a meeting? Check out Chamber of Mothers’ ‘Moms at Recess’ guide, which includes step-by-step looks and tips for hosting a meeting with Members of Congress.).

Rep. Underwood also underscores the importance of being loud about solutions and progress in maternal health. She highlights the power of doulas, lactation consultants, and community support in maternal health—and the fact that, to date, one of the Momnibus’ 14 bills has been signed into law. Others have passed the House. The Momnibus has also unlocked more than $250 million for states, community organizations, researchers, and providers to save lives. (You can find out which projects are being funded with Momnibus dollars in your community through the Caucus’s Momnibus Money Tracker.).

“We want the news of a pregnancy to be a time of joy and hope and eager anticipation and wonder,” Rep. Underwood states. “This can’t just be a conversation around problems. We want people to know that we’re working on this, that we have their backs, and about the opportunities, help, and the resources that are available.”.
