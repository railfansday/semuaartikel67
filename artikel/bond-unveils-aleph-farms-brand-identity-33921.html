BOND Unveils Aleph Farms Brand Identity

🌐 Source: Abduzeedo • 📅 15 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 2 min read (445 words, 📖 Standard)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

BOND builds the Aleph Farms brand identity from a single flipped letter for cultivated meat: the ancient aleph, turned over, becomes an ox.

BOND calls it turning steak on its head. Cultivated meat is one of the most significant developments in food production in generations, and the studio treats it as a story about continuity, not the future. Aleph Farms grows meat directly from animal cells, without raising or slaughtering animals, and BOND was asked to build both the parent brand and its first consumer product brand, Aleph Cuts. The name comes from the first letter of one of humanity's oldest writing systems, first drawn as the pictogram of an ox, and the identity took Gold at the European Design Awards in 2024.

Sources indicate that the wordmark does the talking. Aleph Farms reads in a sturdy grotesque, white on a deep green field, with the flipped letter tucked into a circle as the mark, the ox reappearing as a small geometric head. Aleph Cuts gets a different voice entirely: soft, rounded, almost liquid letterforms in dark green on coral, the same ox mark sitting beside it. The parent brand is the steady statement, the product brand is the appetite.

The flip is the organising principle of the whole system. BOND runs it through typography, motion, imagery, and communication, so every panel reads as one argument. The palette stays tight: deep green, coral, and pale blue against off-white, with the ox mark repeating at every scale, from card to plate. The parent brand and the product brand keep distinct roles while sharing the same idea and visual language.

Recent findings suggest that the material language is quiet and tactile. Business cards carry the mark blind debossed into dark green and white stock, the circle and the ox pressed into the paper rather than printed on it. The same mark is pressed into the center of a plate, a small stamped seal in the middle of an empty dish, waiting for the food. Nothing shouts. The identity holds its nerve and lets the mark do the work.

Julia Stotz photographs the food the way the brand thinks. A skewer comes off a smoking grill against a dark green backdrop, sliced steak stacks on a white plate with mushrooms and peppers, and the meat stays the subject, never a prop. The photography keeps the palette of the system, green backdrops, pale cloth, so the product shots sit inside the identity, not next to it. The result is a brand for something that never existed before that still looks like it has always been at the table.
