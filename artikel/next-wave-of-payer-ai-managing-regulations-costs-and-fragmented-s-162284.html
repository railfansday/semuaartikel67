According to recent reports, payer technology leaders are faced with AI sprawl – where system-embedded AI tools and third-party agentic AI platforms are promising faster audits, quicker decisions, and fewer manual interventions. The demos look impressive, but due to workflow integration and user adoption challenges, we find that core operations staff are still manually gathering context, interpreting policies, coordinating work across multiple systems, and pushing cases through the same queues.

The NAIC put US health insurers’ aggregate profit margin at just 0.4% in 2025. Administrative expenses, meanwhile, went up by $5.8 billion. Blue Cross Blue Shield Association research estimates that more intensive hospital coding practices, potentially linked to AI, may be associated with about $2.3 billion in additional healthcare spending. Hence, rising administrative spend, investments in legacy modernization initiatives, provider and regulatory mandates such as price transparency and electronic prior authorizations are pushing Payers to move towards faster, more transparent audit decisions and optimized core operations.

With these pressures mounting, Payer technology leaders must accelerate AI adoption and deliver measurable ROI within the next 6 to 8 months – without disrupting ongoing legacy modernization initiatives and ensuring regulatory compliance, including robust data security and governance across AI agents, knowledge, data, integration and platform layers.

Industry observers note that today many Payer operations rely on siloed and disintegrated legacy core platforms and monolithic applications. Claims data, benefit documents, contract clauses, and medical records often sit in separate applications. Every process requires many manual touchpoints for information handoff and validation. An audit analyst or frontline operator may have to open several screens, reconcile conflicting information, and chase missing documents before a case can move forward. Adding AI to one step may save some time, but it does not optimize the overall cost or turnaround time if the rest of the process stays fragmented.

However, replacing legacy systems and workflows may not be a financially viable approach for achieving short-term ROI.

An alternative approach is to enable AI-based coworkers that acts as virtual teammates to Payer operations staff and integrate with existing systems to gather relevant records, apply approved rules, identify missing information, and complete routine follow-ups. When an exception is found or there is a situation that needs human judgment, the AI coworkers can route the case to the right employee with the evidence and policy references attached. Key Uses Cases: Audit Analyst (claims, prior authorization, FWA), Care Outreach Coworker, Member Services Coworker.

Sources indicate that auditors spend a fair amount of time just establishing whether a claim has been paid correctly. They need to check coding and benefits against provider contracts, while prior authorization reviews can get held up over missing documentation. Fraud, waste, and abuse (FWA) cases need detection of unusual billing patterns with perfectly legitimate explanation, but someone still has to examine the history behind it.

An AI coworker can pull together the relevant records into the review and flag likely overpayments with supporting evidence. Auditors then decide whether to pursue recovery or raise a dispute, while qualified specialists retain responsibility for clinical and fraud judgments.

A care manager calling a member may need to piece together information from several encounters to plan outreach or assign members to a CM/DM program. An AI coworker can take care of that preparation, leaving the manager with more time for the conversation itself.

It has been revealed that there are two potential financial benefits here. Care managers can reach more members without increasing staffing costs. Earlier engagement may also help lower medical spending, with care management programs showing fewer avoidable inpatient stays among members who engage early.

Member inquiries account for a regular part of a Payer’s operating costs. Even a seemingly simple question about whether a specialist visit is covered may require a representative to check network status in one system and benefits in another. If the information doesn’t match, the member may need to call again.

An AI coworker can pull the relevant policy and coverage details together while the representative is speaking to the member. More inquiries can then be resolved on the first call, reducing repeat contacts and the staff time spent handling them.

According to recent reports, the potential savings are clear in each of these examples. But there’s another cost to consider as Payers begin deploying AI coworkers across departments. A claims auditor and a member-services representative may both need access to the same benefit rules. If each team builds its own connections and maintains those rules independently, the Payer ends up duplicating the work and the expense.

Avoiding another layer of fragmented technology requires some decisions about how AI coworkers will be built, managed, and used across the organization.

To realize early value from AI, Payer technology leaders should not wait for broader enterprise AI platform enablement or legacy modernization initiatives to go-live. The pragmatic next step is to choose one high-volume, high-impact workflow and get started with an AI coworker model. Once initial, measurable value is delivered and proven, then reuse the capabilities built during the first 100 days to expand AI adoption in adjacent areas.

This approach will help the organization gain a clearer view of what AI costs and what it saves, and they can start reconsidering how technology and services are contracted. While contracting with vendors, fees linked to agreed outcomes can become a more realistic proposition. And if the savings from one deployment can help finance the next, AI investment becomes easier to sustain. Payers could gradually build an operating model where each latest coworker costs less to introduce, with more of the underlying capabilities already in place.
