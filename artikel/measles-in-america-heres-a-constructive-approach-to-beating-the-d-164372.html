Measles in America: Here's a constructive approach to beating the disease, based on Brazil's experience

According to recent reports, health experts saw it coming — the fact that after 26 years, the US would lose its status as measles-free. Back in July 2026, DW reported how one epidemiologist had suggested an 'amnesia' about the measles risk was pushing a growth in US infections.

Four months later, it was official. On November 17, 2026, the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) stated the US had lost its measles elimination status, along with Mexico.

But it doesn't have to stay this way.

Recent findings suggest that look at Brazil, where in 2019, with more than 21,000 measles cases, the country also lost its measles-free status. But within five years — during the COVID-19 pandemic — it fought and won back its measles-free status. And since 2024, Brazil has stayed measles-free, even as the highly contagious disease spread across the rest of the Americas.

Recent findings suggest that in 2026, with the data available up to the time of writing this article, Brazil had only had 70 measles cases. They were all linked to infections brought in from abroad, often from neighboring Peru.

Industry observers note that brazil has fewer people than the US, about 213 million compared to the US's nearly 350 million, but the two countries are similar in size. So the comparison is worth considering.

Brazil moves fast. The PAHO asks countries to respond within 72 hours of a suspected measles case. Here's Brazil's own rule:.

It has been revealed that it has also made hunting for cases a national habit. In a program called "Dia S," health workers knock on doors and visit schools, daycare centers and churches to find people who may be sick but have not seen a doctor.

Brazil also holds mass vaccination days called "Dia D." There was one in November, in the area where Brazil borders Colombia and Peru.

If someone is diagnosed with measles, health workers try to track down every person the patient may have met ("contact persons") and vaccinate them and other people living nearby.

The country has so far provided more than 16 million doses of the measles vaccine in 2026 and has donated 500,000 doses to Peru.

Sources indicate that brazil isn't perfect, though. According to official estimates from the World Health Organization, only 76% of children got both required doses of the vaccine in 2025 — well short of the 95% target. Experts warn this leaves the country at risk because measles is so highly contagious. A country needs to be as close to 100% immunized as possible to protect people and stop the disease from spreading.

"Elimination is not a trophy that we win once and put on a shelf. It must be protected every day," notes PAHO Director Jarbas Barbosa.

The US lost its measles-free status because the PAHO found that the same strain of the virus had been spreading in the country for 12 months or more. Mexico and Bolivia lost their status on the same day as the US.

The US has had more than 5,400 cases in 2026 (at time of writing), more than twice as many as in all of 2025, and about 19 times as many as in 2024. Nearly everyone who got sick with measles was unvaccinated or had an unknown vaccination status.

It has been revealed that US President Donald Trump signed an executive order on fresh vaccination recommendations for children in August 2026. It came at a time, when, as we reported, experts said people had stopped getting vaccinated against measles because infection rates were low. But like PAHO's Barbosa said, neglecting vaccination when infection rates are low is a mistake.

Recent findings suggest that to be fair, Trump's executive order listed the measles vaccine in a top category of vaccinations recommended for every child. But the administration's stance on vaccination in general is unclear.

Recent findings suggest that health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has often declared opposition to vaccination, including against measles, advising people to take vitamin A instead. In Texas, this led to children being hospitalized with toxic levels of the supplement, and left them with no protection against measles.

Before the COVID-19 pandemic, 95% of US kindergartners had their measles shots. Now, it's 92%, according to the US Centers for Disease Control and Protection (CDC). That leaves about 280,000 kindergartners in the US unprotected.

In the Texas counties where a 2025 measles outbreak began, coverage was as low as 77% at the time.

US children usually need vaccines to start school, but most states let parents opt out for religious or personal reasons, with no doctor involved.

But vaccination rates have fallen elsewhere in the world as well, including in Europe. In Germany, where measles infections have also risen, experts blame anti-vaccination rhetoric that emerged during the COVID-19 pandemic.

A person sick with measles can infect up to18 other, unprotected people. With vaccination, it is possible to increase the number of protected people and lower the number of people the sick person can infect.

Recent findings suggest that to prevent a measles outbreak, a community needs about 95% of people to be vaccinated and/or immune. The vaccination consists of two doses of the MMR (measles, mumps, rubella) vaccine.

But even after the two doses, a person can expect to have only 97% protection against measles — 3% short of full protection — so, nearly everyone in the community has to be vaccinated to keep the average level of protect as close to 100% as possible.

"For some diseases like measles, even a small decrease in vaccination rates can lead to more outbreaks of disease [so] community immunity requires over 95% of susceptible individuals to be immunized," says Andrew Racine, president of the American Academy of Pediatrics.

Recent findings suggest that and the 95% has to hold everywhere. A good national average can be affected by, for example, a single school where only 70% of the children are vaccinated. And that is where the outbreak is likely to start.

Around the world, measles deaths have dropped by 88% since 2000, thanks to vaccines.

It has been revealed that but progress has stalled. Only about three out of four children worldwide get both doses of the vaccine. The biggest outbreaks right now are in Bangladesh, Indonesia and Guatemala.

Data from Europe shows that things can get better. In the European Union, cases fell from more than 35,000 in 2024 to about 2,300 the following year, with no measles-related deaths.

Romania went from more than 30,000 cases in 2024 to about 4,200 in 2025.

In Hungary, where 99% of children are vaccinated, there have been no measles cases in the past year.

Industry observers note that but in January, six European countries lost their measles-free status, including the UK, Spain and Austria. Across Europe, eight out of ten people who got measles in 2025 were unvaccinated.

The Americas are seeing a major surge in measles infections, with more than 58,000 cases in 2026, over four times as many as by November in 2025. Guatemala has been hit hardest, with almost 35,000 cases.

The deadliest outbreaks are in Africa. African Union countries have so far reported nearly 1,800 measles deaths in 2026, about 1,650 of them in Democratic Republic of Congo. Yet, Sudan, despite the war, raised first-dose measles coverage by 22 percentage points in 2025, which was the world's largest gain.

In a recent development, measles is extremely contagious. One person can infect up to 18 unprotected people, which makes it about 12 times more contagious than the flu.

In a recent development, the virus can linger in the air for up to two hours after a sick person has left the room. And people spread it before they know they are sick. Someone with measles is contagious for four days before the rash appears and four days after.

Industry observers note that it's not just a rash. About one in five unvaccinated people who get measles in the US ends up in the hospital. Measles can weaken the immune system for years, making it harder to fight off other infections.

But it's preventable. Two doses of the MMR vaccine (which also protects against mumps and rubella) protect 97% of people for life.
