According to recent reports, two federal judges have now declined to protect Kalshi from Latest York's state-court gambling enforcement. The case moves to a state judge, and Kalshi disputes how far the proposed order would reach beyond New York.

Sources indicate that U.S. District Judge Lorna Schofield denied the CFTC’s renewed emergency request on Friday, November 20, after a telephone hearing that began at 5 p.m. ET, ruling from the bench in Manhattan.

Schofield found the CFTC was unlikely to show that the Commodity Exchange Act overrides state gambling laws. That includes licensing rules applied to prediction market operators. Her ruling came a day after Judge Vernon Broderick sent the case back to state court.

Sources indicate that the agency’s earlier bid had gone before U.S. District Judge Jed Rakoff.

With the federal route closed for now, the case sits with Justice Melissa Crane of Latest York County Supreme Court.

The proposed order would bar Kalshi from running an unlicensed gambling business within or from Newly introduced York, or for people in the state. It covers sports, culture, election and other event contracts. It would also block users under 21 from trading.

The reach of that language is Kalshi’s central concern. In a Thursday-night letter to Crane, Kalshi’s Milbank lawyer Sean Murphy warned that the order would close the exchange to all customers everywhere, because the manufacturer is based in Upcoming York. The letter also pointed to the likely forced liquidation of all open positions. James’s office has noted that shutting Kalshi down nationwide is not what it is after.

According to recent reports, the CFTC has tried to place event contracts beyond state reach. On April 24, 2026, it sued Upcoming York in the Southern District of New York. It sought a declaration that it alone regulates event contracts. Similar suits had already targeted Arizona, Connecticut and Illinois.

On August 11, the agency issued an emergency order telling Kalshi to keep performing its functions as a designated contract market. Kalshi had warned the CFTC on August 1 that New York’s pending motion created a market emergency. The order was meant to reassure traders that one state cannot shut down a CFTC-registered exchange.

The financial exposure is large. New York seeks a permanent injunction and full restitution to people. It also wants a $100,000 penalty for each attempt to offer sports wagering, plus a further penalty of three times Kalshi’s gains.

Industry observers note that the case lands on top of a 3-1 run of federal appeals court rulings against Kalshi. That record raises the chance the Supreme Court takes up whether states can regulate prediction markets. Separately, the Second Circuit denied relief on November 3 in Kalshi’s own suit against New York regulators.
