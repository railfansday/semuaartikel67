One of the problems with the classic end of the automotive hobby is that everyone wants a clean car. Whether your goal is to keep it that way, preserve and use it, or modify it to suit your tastes, the road will be a whole lot smoother if you start from a healthy place. Trust me, buying a good example of an older car is better for your health. I’ve been there.

This story originally appeared in Volume 38 of Road & Track.

Why would you buy something with obvious complications? To save money, right? But guess what—you’re undervaluing your own time, and, except for a few rare occasions, the savings never materialize. Trying to fix up a bad car without already being an expert bad-car fixer is like trying to treat someone with a spinal injury without being a doctor: It’s going to be brutal, painful, and expensive in ways you can’t anticipate.

In a recent development, A clean car has fewer unknowns. With a not-so-clean car, damage from accidents may not have been repaired to factory spec. Paintwork may not quite match. “Just a little bit of rust” is almost never an accurate assessment of that metal-eating menace. “You’re not going to believe what we found in here” will become your own sad catchphrase.

This is particularly true for Aughts-era cars because they are potentially usable for everyday driving. The fantasy of dailying something like a sweet E39 BMW M5 can run into the reality of owning a 20-something-year-old specialty product. You might be thinking: It’s not like buying an old Ford Woody and needing artisan-­level skills. Most Aughts cars should be reliable enough to drive regularly at freeway speeds. The worst that could happen is I might need to replace a few still readily available parts, right? Maybe. Maybe not. You’ll swiftly find out which local shops won’t touch cars that are over 10 years old.

Now, if I were a talented hobbyist mechanic, or a professional one, I could probably spot a deal where my skill set could be applied to a particular car’s needs, creating unique value for me. And yes, anyone could learn those skills with some time and effort. But do you want to spend your nights and weekends wrenching, not to customize your car but to repair it? Or do you want to spend that time driving?

In a recent development, for everyone who does not fix cars for a hobby and wants a cool car to drive for fun and maybe modify a bit: Get the clean car. Time is money, and if your money has to buy professional time un-shitboxing a previous owner’s mistakes, it’s going to cost you both dollars and days. You’re not just losing what you may have saved on the purchase price in repairs but also paying in the lost opportunity of a car that’s not on the road being enjoyed.

One of my recent purchases is an old Mercedes that I am having professionals modify for me. The ­difference between a great and a rough example of this car is about $15,000. Real money, but to make a bad example into a good one would be more than ­double that figure.

It has been revealed that so yeah, you pay more on the way in. But guess what? When you go to sell it, you’re on the other end of the same equation. Money out, money in. Because everybody wants the clean example, and you’re ­holding the goods.
