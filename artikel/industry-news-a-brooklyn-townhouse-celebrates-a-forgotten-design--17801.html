In a recent development, in the early 1920s, the Pittsburgh-based manufacturer Armstrong Cork Firm (now Armstrong World Industries) made the prescient move of tapping a young arts educator named Hazel Dell Brown to act as its in-house decorator. Hers would be an under-recognized yet wildly influential career. Over the course of more than three decades, she designed countless rooms for advertisements, booklets, catalogs, and more that showcased the brand’s products, chief among them linoleum flooring. With their bold colors, fearless mix of patterns, and daring pastiche, her interiors shaped an entire generation of homes in the Rust Belt and beyond.

For two contemporary artists setting out to renovate a Brooklyn town house for their young family, Brown’s little-known work recently served not only as inspiration but also as a litmus test. When interviewing architects to take on the project, the couple first presented examples of vintage Armstrong schemes. “A lot of people would take one look and then show me something different,” reflects the wife, who learned about Brown while working on a project in Pittsburgh. Not so in the case of Tal Schori and Rustam Mehta of GRT Architects. The AD100 duo instantly responded to Brown’s surface-heavy approach and inward-facing embrace of small spaces, or as Schori puts it, “rooms within rooms.” As for their interior designer, Penelope August, a friend of the wife’s since preschool—she implicitly passed with flying colors.

A circa 1980 Gio Ponti chandelier crowns the living room, which is furnished with a vintage Vladimir Kagan sectional sofa and a Jens Risom chair upholstered in a custom fabric based on a Macgregor Harp painting; artwork by Rose Marcus and wallpaper by Antoinette Poisson.

The kitchen features art-glass cabinet fronts by Paul Wissmach Glass Company and a custom terrazzo floor inspired by ancient roman mosaics of unswept debris.

The project evolved into a dynamic collaboration as the couple and their team gutted the town house, set in Carroll Gardens and previously renovated in the 1990s. Together they took care to preserve historic details like the original doors and staircase, with its original mahogany newel posts and handrails. Wherever appropriate, they embraced imperfections, filling in the gaps between old floorboards with contrasting tinted epoxy and leaving levels ever so askew. “This house shows signs of age and that’s not a bad thing,” says Schori. Adds Mehta, “As artists, they understood what is and isn’t precious so that the home does not feel overly restored.”.

The backyard was updated by Brook Landscape, with a shed and swing set by GRT Architects.

According to recent reports, brown was nothing if not a gleeful problem solver, presenting flexible solutions to the needs of everyday life, often through inventive secondary spaces. (“Your rumpus room can be anything you like” proclaimed one Armstong campaign.) True to that strategy, GRT devised a floor initiative full of versatile surprises. Adjoining the dining room is a cave-like lounge for watching television or just relaxing, with a pass-through to the kitchen for the easy arrival of snacks. Opposite the cookspace—more on that showstopper later—is a cozy breakfast area, with a load-bearing skylight that offers glimpses of the roof terrace (and sometimes curious children) above. And on the second level, a secret passage leads from the primary suite into the library, one column of shelving concealing a door. “All these appendages to the main spaces allow multiple things to happen at once,” indicates Schori.

In the daughter’s room, Shaker-style trim is painted in Farrow & Ball’s Blue Ground.

The kids’ bath mixes terrazzo walls with custom floor tiles by Haand.

Oak millwork incorporates vintage Piero Fornasetti panels in the dining room, where Guillerme et Chambron chairs surround an antique farm table; painting by Jo Nigoghossian, and custom fireplace fabricated by Daniel Armitano.

Industry observers note that surfaces and furnishings tend toward the eclectic and one-of-a-kind, with a mix of wallpapers and fabrics, some deadstock, setting the stage for vintage treasures. In the living room, a Vladimir Kagan sectional sofa found at auction doubles as a jungle gym of sorts. The primary bath is anchored by a salvaged earthenware tub, since glazed a light blue to complement the retro checkerboard tiles and scalloped vanity. And the dining room’s custom millwork incorporates a series of midcentury Fornasetti panels. Doorknobs, lighting, motifs, art by their circle of creative friends—it’s all a mishmash. “We liked things independently of how they worked together,” states the wife. Adds August: “It was so fun to stitch all these elements into an old house—I love a space where you come upon something unexpected.”.

Floor to floor, the mood shifts. The son’s and daughter’s rooms each riff on classic Shaker woodwork, with rails and built-in cabinetry that respond to the exposed ceiling joists. Enlivened by a splatter-painted floor and celestial configuration of ceiling lights, the plywood-lined attic operates as a kids’ club, craft room, and family theater in one. (How’s that for a rumpus room, Hazel?) Thanks to some clever excavation and engineering on the part of GRT, the combined garden level and cellar is now a double-height studio for the wife, complete with a gantry crane for hoisting heavy sculptures. Outside, meanwhile, Brook Landscape transformed the backyard into a romantic swath of greenery.

Vintage globe lights from a one-time optometry office add a surreal touch to the powder room.

As for that kitchen, here the team took inspiration not only from Brown but from two additional touchstones. Cabinet fronts nod to art glass at the Upper East Side’s late great Glaser’s Bake Shop, the salvaged interiors of which August and the wife chanced upon at The Demolition Depot. The floor, meanwhile, riffs on ancient Roman mosaics of unswept detritus, an example of which can be found at the Vatican Museums. “We wanted a kitchen that wouldn’t look bad if it got messy,” notes the wife. Working closely with GRT and August, she and her husband evolved that historic precedent into a contemporary series of shapes, some abstract and some legible (a knife, a carrot, a wine glass). Rendered in scraps of stone or metal, their forms now appear to float in poured terrazzo.

“The way artists think about objects and architecture is as a constant work in progress,” notes Mehta. “These are visual people with an incredible mental library of references.” Would Brown approve? Without a doubt. As the designer once wrote: “Whatever you do, use your own ideas. Don’t be afraid of them.”.
