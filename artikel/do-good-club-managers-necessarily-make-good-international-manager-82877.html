In a mailbag edition of our newsletter, we look at Mauricio Pochettino, Pep Guardiola’s international credentials and the lack of elite English managers.

According to recent reports, who should replace Mauricio Pochettino as the USMNT manager? – Tom S.

In a recent development, although the salary I’m sure will be excellent, I think the US job is going to be a really difficult sell to a top-level coach. The 2030 World Cup is a long way away and I’m not sure the Concacaf Nations League, the Gold Cup or even the 2028 Copa América carry much appeal for a manager with European experience or aspirations. But I’m not entirely sure that sort of coach is necessarily a great idea. The last two World Cups, after all, have been won by low-key coaches who have come from within the federation and have worked with many of the players at youth level. That’s the positive from appointing from within; appointing from without is almost certainly more expensive and there’s the danger the next foreign manager does just what Mauricio Pochettino does, and has his head turned by the first vaguely attractive European job that comes calling.

The United States’ interest in Pep Guardiola, and his talks with Italy earlier, have me wondering – what separates club and international management? Could Pep, as successful as he’s been, do well in either? – Joe P.

International and club management seem to me related but essentially different jobs. It’s one thing to have the players every day and be able to drill them over and over to impose a philosophy; quite another to pick them up for perhaps a dozen training sessions over a calendar year, plus major tournaments. International football is necessarily less sophisticated than club football, more based on individual excellence or heroism. Guardiola, you suspect, would be able to adapt, but it’s not certain. Plus there’s the fact that at major tournaments players are with their manager for a month or more, cooped up in the same hotel with no release. With the more intense coaches, such as Marcelo Bielsa, that seems to become problematic. And it’s notable that at the last World Cup the nations with the big-name managers – England, Brazil, the United States, Germany – fared less well than those who had appointed technocrats from within.

Michael Carrick’s firing highlighted the lack of top-class English managers. Why has an Englishman never managed a Premier League club to a title? – Ricky M.

Sources indicate that I think there are two related issues here. Are there any good English coaches? To which the answer appears to be that there are nowhere near as many as there ought to be. The various changes in player development enacted by the elite player performance plan and the England DNA project have, over the past 10-15 years, led to the development of far more technically proficient English players than there have ever been before, which has been reflected in the uptick in England’s performances at major tournaments. St George’s Park was supposed to be about developing coaches as well as players but, as yet, that seems not to have happened.

Industry observers note that that remarked, it’s probably also the case that it’s very hard for British coaches to be appointed to top Premier League jobs. Say they get their start at a mid-sized Championship club and win promotion; they then have to amend their approach in the Premier League to scrap for points in a relegation battle. If they fail they are written off as not good enough for the top division; if they succeed, they’re seen as not playing the right sort of football for an elite club. It’s happened to Scott Parker … even the likes of Gary O’Neil or Eddie Howe seem somehow not quite right for the elite who, perhaps understandably, prefer to turn to somebody with experience of European competition.

Recent findings suggest that is there any part of you that feels sympathy for the current Man City players, who will presumably have to play out a “lost season” thanks to actions they had nothing to do with, and which probably happened long before their time at the club? – Jason D.

To an extent, yes, but they’re pretty naïve if they didn’t realise the potential issue they were walking into. I have far more sympathy for those players who played for clubs who were denied success by City’s cheating. It’s not just the teams who finished second in the league, lost to City in cup competitions or missed out on European qualification because of City; it’s those teams, most obviously Arsenal and Aston Villa, whose development was derailed by City taking their best players, offering wages and transfer fees that it turns out they had no business offering.

Recent findings suggest that italy have gotten some big results in the Nations League. Are they back? – Nicholas A.

Industry observers note that this question, to be fair, was sent in before the 4-1 defeat to Belgium on Sunday. But anyway, beating France at home may be a big result, but that and two wins over Turkey seems limited evidence to say they’re back. They have definitely looked sharper under Roberto Mancini than they have probably since winning the Euros under him five years ago, but that’s a long way from being back. Even if they were to win the Euros in 2028, it wouldn’t alter the fact they haven’t qualified for three World Cups in a row. They need to qualify for 2030 and then probably get to at least the quarter-finals before you can start to say they may be back. And even then, Serie A is way off what it was at its peak – and how many of this squad would get in a Spain or England squad? Even being generous, probably no more than four or five.
