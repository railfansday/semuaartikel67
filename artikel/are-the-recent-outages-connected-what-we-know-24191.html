The FAA grounds all U.S. flights due to an air traffic control telecommunications issue on Nov. 13. Chase bank suffers an outage due to a software issue on Nov. 16. Later that same day, United Airlines' website goes down, affecting all departing flights. On Nov. 17, an internal revision issue disrupted some of Apple's services.

Recent findings suggest that major companies and services appear to be experiencing outages all at the same time. Conspiracy theories surrounding these events are spreading, and people are asking what in the world is going on with all these recent outages — and are they connected?

It indeed appears there have been an unusually high number of outages recently.

Industry observers note that "Compared with the past 12 months, the past week has been unusually busy," Ookla's Lead Industry Analyst Luke Kehoe said in a statement provided to Mashable. "Americans sent nearly 6 million reports in the past seven days to November 16, more than in any other week over that period, and the number of services with problems flagged on Downdetector was higher than in all but a few weeks.".

According to Ookla, the two-day Microsoft 365 outage was the largest reported downtime problem on Downdetector "since the AWS outage of October 2025, with 3.9 million US reports in the 60 hours after it began.".

However, the reason it feels like outages have occurred more frequently is that the affected companies have been fairly large.

As Kehoe explains, the data shows what "appears to be a cluster of big events." More people are reporting problems because the services suffering from outages have larger user bases. There have been numerous periods with more widespread outages, but among services with smaller user bases.

The answer, as uneventful as it may seem, is no. They are not connected, per se, at least not in the conspiratorial sense. And while investigations are still ongoing into some of these major outages, it does seem that they are, for the most part, isolated internal incidents.

Sources indicate that however, there are reasons why it appears major technology networks are more vulnerable to outages lately. And, they are somewhat connected, however, it's in a not so nefarious way.

“When major organizations and critical services experience disruptions in close succession, it can create the impression that the incidents are connected, but that isn’t necessarily the case," Shane Buckley, CEO of cloud security firm Gigamon, told Mashable in a statement.

Sources indicate that "Organizations today operate within a highly interconnected ecosystem of applications, networks, cloud infrastructure and third-party services," Buckley explained. "A failure in one part of that ecosystem can have downstream effects across multiple services, while unrelated technical complications can also surface at roughly the same time. Until the root causes are confirmed, it’s notable not to assume these incidents are connected.".

Basically, many essential services in the tech industry have consolidated into just a few major providers. As a result, many companies depend on the same service providers for their online platforms. If that service provider goes down, multiple platforms that depend on it will go down with it.

For example, many online companies use Cloudflare to manage their traffic and ensure maximum uptime. However, when Cloudflare went down last year, many platforms that depend on the service suffered downtime challenges along with it. This happened even though those individual companies' systems were not experiencing any problems.

Recent findings suggest that another example of how many systems are dependent on one another is the Chase online banking outage from just this past Monday. Users initially reported that multiple financial institutions were suffering from outages, including services like Zelle. It turns out, only Chase was suffering from complications. But, because customers at other banks and financial services were experiencing issues when trying to transfer money to Chase accounts, it gave off the perception that there were wider problems with online banking systems.

Industry observers note that so, as I said, nothing nefarious here. It's just the normal, everyday consolidation of essential online services into the hands of just a few major conglomerates. No need to worry!
