Menopause Was a Hushed Conversation—Until Gen X Changed Everything

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that sharon Cancio, 28, didn’t grow up knowing much about menopause. She didn’t learn anything about it in school, nor from the women in her family. But when she was a teenager, she started noticing her mother’s mood shifting. Around her mid-to-late 40s, “she became much snappier, more easily irritated, and sometimes just seemed angry in a way that felt completely different from the mom I remembered growing up,” she recalls. “I genuinely thought life had simply worn her down.”.

Cancio didn’t start connecting the dots until she came off birth control at 25 and noticed how dramatically her own mood changed around her cycle. Once she realized her mother had entered perimenopause, she went down an internet rabbit hole to learn about it. Women were discussing—in detail—what was happening to their bodies on TikTok and Reddit. Some women were going through menopause early, others were seeking answers for their symptoms.

It has been revealed that these discussions are a far cry from what conversations around menopause used to look like: nonexistent. In the past, there wasn’t much education—or even real discussion—on what happens to the female body after 40, forcing women to hustle through hot flashes and mood swings behind closed doors.

But within the last few years, there’s been a shift: Gen X women are the first to go through this hormonal transition in the age of social media—and they’re not afraid to talk about their experiences, claims Gillan Goddard, MD, an endocrinologist and an adjunct assistant professor of medicine at the NYU Langone Hospital. They’re educating an entire generation of younger women in the process. Posts about brain fog, inconsistent sleep patterns, and unfamiliar-feeling bodies have become the first real health literacy that this generation’s daughters, Gen Zers, have gotten about their own hormonal futures. These small conversations have big implications when it comes to learning more about their future health.

Industry observers note that the lack of menopause education starts in classrooms: Almost all U.S. states require teenagers to learn about puberty, reproductive health, and sexual disease prevention through sex education classes. But the content and depth of those classes may not favor women’s health: A 2024 study in the Journal of School Health looked at the state of menstrual health education in U.S. K-12 public schools. Researchers analyzed the education standards from each state’s Department of Education site, searching terms like “menstruation,” “menopause,” “period,” “reproduction,” and “puberty.” It found that menstrual health education—especially menopause—is profoundly rare.

Industry observers note that there’s also a lack of menopause education in medical schools. Only 31.3 percent of U.S. ob-gyn residency programs include a menopause curriculum, according to a 2023 study in Menopause. The result resembles a serious disconnect: The transition affects nearly half of the population, but the majority of the doctors tasked with treating menopause have received almost no training on how to do it.

It has been revealed that menopause just isn’t prioritized during residency training. Instead, the “focus during training was placed on surgical skills and labor and delivery responsibilities, both considered more urgent and time-consuming,” claims Alyssa Dweck, MD, chief medical officer of Bonafide Health and a Menopause Society Certified Practitioner located in Fresh York City. Ob-gyns can earn certifications in (peri)menopause, from organizations including the Menopause Society and Integrative Women’s Health Institute—but they’re not required. Clinicians should have more comprehensive training in medical school and beyond, says Dr. Dweck. But there should also be more funding and resources for women’s health research for treatment options.

In a recent development, when doctors aren’t familiar with menopause, “mood changes, cognitive changes, and body aches are often misunderstood or ignored,” says Dr. Goddard. This can lead to misdiagnoses, and to women not receiving proper treatment for symptoms caused by menopause. This is pretty common: A 2025 survey conducted by health care firm Biote of over 1,000 women ages 30 to 60 in the U.S. found that 39 percent of women have been misdiagnosed and prescribed medication by a provider for conditions like depression.

TikTok and Instagram have filled the menopause education void in the U.S. Conversations about life in menopause have spread awareness about what the condition is actually like, crushing stigmas. This visibility is not only helping younger viewers learn what they can expect later in life—it’s also helping mothers start more health-forward conversations with their daughters.

Milan Parker, 25, has always had open conversations about health with her mom. “She’s always been really honest with me about what she’s going through, even the more personal parts that people sometimes consider taboo,” she states. But social media increased this dynamic tenfold: Parker’s mother underwent a hysterectomy due to fibroids at 48, which made her enter early menopause. Following the surgery, her mother joined a menopause support group on Facebook. “Seeing that there were women all across the country going through similar things made her more open to talking to me about it,” Parker says.

For instance, Parker’s mom finally explained how debilitating her hot flashes really were. (She’d stopped going on trips with her because she was embarrassed about looking sweaty.) “There were times when I thought maybe my mom just didn’t want to go out with me,” she notes. “I’m really grateful that she made it something we could actually talk about instead of something that had to stay a secret.”.

Sources indicate that for daughters, it can be hard to watch their mothers struggle in menopause and not know how to help, but the experience can also help them grow closer. “The emotional experience can be a mix of empathy, grief, anxiety, and closeness,” notes Ashley Peña, LCSW, a clinical social worker and the vice president of outpatient operations at A Mission for Michael.

Even though Cancio’s mom didn’t tell her when she was going through menopause, the experience helped her gain compassion for her mother and better understand her own body. “As a teenager, I honestly thought she was just becoming grumpy or ‘getting old,’ which I feel terrible admitting now,” she states. “As an adult woman, I have so much more empathy.”.

In a recent development, having a raw look at what menopause is actually like—whether through a screen or a conversation with their mother—shapes how younger women view aging and how to prepare themselves for what’s ahead. Hearing that they may one day inevitably experience sleepless nights, sudden anger, brain fog, and the loss of identity can be scary, to say the least. But “open conversation about menopause helps women hear the full scope of menopause experiences and how women dealt effectively with their symptoms,” Dr. Goddard says. It can make the condition sound less scary to younger women.

In fact, growing conversations around menopause can actually inspire younger women to learn about it earlier. “I am seeing patients of all ages, Gen Z included, who have become more inquisitive and engaged in their health overall,” Dr. Dweck states. Some of her young patients have told her they’ve already started talking to their older relatives about menopause and hormone health. They’re also more open about discussing fertility and egg freezing.

Industry observers note that even though menopause is now common dinner table conversation, the root of the (non-communication) issue still needs to be fixed. “The only way we normalize talking about women’s health is by talking about it,” Cancio indicates. “Ask questions, stay curious, and don’t be afraid to learn from women who have already been through it.”.

Sources indicate that mothers going through (peri)menopause can have a series of casual conversations about it with their kids. (The earlier, the better.) They can keep the conversation casual and tailored to the child’s developmental age. For instance, moms with children under the age of 13 can briefly mention that they’re “going through a body change.” Then, as the kids get older, they can talk more openly about what they’re experiencing, states Dr. Dweck. Encouraging their children to ask questions about menopause can also build empathy and normalize conversations surrounding health.

According to recent reports, on the flip side, Parker wants Gen Z women to pay attention to what their mothers are going through, and not wait until the right moment to spark a conversation. “Having those conversations with my mom has made that feel a lot less scary and a lot more human,” she claims. “Every stage of life comes sooner than you think, so it’s worth paying attention to your health long before you feel like you have to.”.

Cancio didn’t feel like she could have those conversations with her mom, which is part of what inspired her to start making content around women’s health topics for younger women. “I started thinking about the girl who was sitting at home Googling something because she was too embarrassed to ask her mom, or the girl who thought something was wrong with her because nobody had ever explained that what she was experiencing was normal,” she claims.

It has been revealed that even though the way that Gen Z is learning about menopause isn’t perfect—whether through a viral post or their mother—it’s still a step in the right direction. By doing her part, Cancio hopes to have a completely different relationship with her future daughter “where no topic about her body is off limits and she never feels like she has to search for answers alone.”.
