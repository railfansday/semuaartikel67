Second Amendment Roundup, II: Much Ado About Bowie Knives

In Viramontes and Grant, Cook County and Connecticut argue that 19th century restrictions on Bowie knives provide historical analogues under Bruen for banning what they call "assault weapons."  They further contend that the widespread use of Bowie knives disproves application of the Heller test that arms in common use are protected by the Second Amendment. If Bowie knives in common use by law-abiding citizens may be banned, the argument goes, so too may semiautomatic rifles.

A word of caution. To paraphrase Freud, sometimes a knife is just a knife. Knives generally have a point and an edge on one side, sometimes also an edge on the other. Any knife can be used for good or bad. Bruen distinguished heavy weapons of war like lancegays from daggers, noting that in medieval society "[a]lmost everyone carried a knife or a dagger in his belt," "[c]ivilians wore them for self-protection," and "the smaller medieval weapons … strike us as most analogous to modern handguns.".

No doubt Bowie knives were in use as tools for skinning game and self-defense, and ruffians are noted to have carried them concealed. But for a variety of reasons, it's unnecessary to decide whether Bowie knives met the Heller test of being in common use. Four of the amicus briefs are right on point on this glitch: National Rifle Ass'n (NRA), National Shooting Sports Foundation (NSSF), Senator Ted Cruz, and the United States (U.S.).

First, a few States' restrictions are not enough to establish a historical tradition. Under Heller, a handful of outlier laws cannot justify a ban. "Most States regulated Bowie knives in some manner, but, except in a few outlier jurisdictions, adults were free to buy, possess, and openly carry them. Regulations typically restricted concealed carry, sales to minors, or imposed enhanced penalties for criminal misuse." NRA Br. 5.

Second, "No State banned Bowie-knife possession in the nineteenth century." NRA Br. 36. "And one of the only laws that likely did actually ban Bowie knives (along with pistols and an array of other items) is the same 1837 Georgia law that was promptly held unconstitutional other than as applied to concealed carry—because it was too restrictive to comply with the Second Amendment. See 1837 Ga. Acts 90, §1 (1838); Nunn v. State, 1 Ga. 243 (1846); cf. Bruen, 597 U.S. at 54 (singling out Nunn as "particularly instructive" on the Second Amendment's meaning)." NSSF Br. 21.

These were concealed-carry restrictions, not complete bans. "This Court has already concluded that restrictions on concealed carry cannot justify carry bans, see Bruen, 597 U.S. at 48-49, 53-55, let alone possession bans, see Heller, 554 U.S. at 628-29." NSSF Br. 21.

Third, the same laws covered handguns. "If those laws justified rifle bans, then handgun bans would be justified all the more, because they typically also regulated handguns but not long guns." NRA Br. 38.

Industry observers note that where Bowie-knife restrictions were upheld, courts applied the same standard Petitioners advance here: arms in common use for lawful purposes are protected, while weapons used only by criminals are not. As Judge Richardson's dissent in Bianchi explains, it does not matter that courts sometimes disagreed about whether Bowie knives were in fact in common use, because they were applying the same yardstick. "State courts have long applied the common-use test to judge such laws under state constitutional provisions securing the right to bear arms." U.S. Br. 12.

Fourth, even a court that viewed the Bowie knife as exceptionally deadly held that the right to carry it was protected. "And Texas's Cockrum v. State held a Bowie knife to be a protected arm—"[t]he right to carry a bowie-knife for lawful defense is secured, and must be admitted"— while sustaining an enhanced penalty for its abuse, treating the weapon's dangerousness as a reason to regulate its misuse, not a reason to deny that it was an arm. 24 Tex. 394, 402-403 (1859). Cockrum is the mirror image of the decision below: alleged dangerousness bore on regulation, not on coverage." Cruz Br. 18.

The historical answer to misuse was punishing criminals, not disarming law-abiding citizens.

"[T]he evil must be prevented by the penitentiary and gallows, and not by a general deprivation of a constitutional privilege." U.S. Br. 30 (quoting Wilson v. State).
