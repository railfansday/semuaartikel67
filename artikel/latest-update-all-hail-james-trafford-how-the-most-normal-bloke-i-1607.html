Latest Update: All hail James Trafford: How the most normal bloke in elite football became England’s No.1

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

James Trafford doesn’t immediately strike you as one of the world’s best goalkeepers.

Industry observers note that you half-remember him from your student union days. Nice enough fella, just sort of there. Stoke City fan, you think. Went on to play guitar for a short-lived indie band that reached No.33 in the album charts.

Sources indicate that maybe that’s why he and Scott Parker didn’t immediately hit it off at Burnley. Trafford has described their “very, very weird start”, admitting that neither particularly liked the other and they struggled to connect.

According to recent reports, no great surprise, really. Parker is very much a proper football man, the archetype of a limited but committed noughties English midfielder. The final boss of Tom Cleverleys. All the while Trafford is nobody’s idea of a typical footballer. He grew up riding tractors in Cumbria.

Yet here we are. Leeds United’s record signing, the most expensive English goalkeeper ever and the fourth-most expensive in history, is worth every penny. Of the 52 goalkeepers to have played at least 10 matches across Europe’s significant five leagues this season, Trafford ranks first for save percentage, stopping an extraordinary 81.4% of shots he’s faced (credit to OPTA’s Jonny Cooper).

England Pod co-host George Elek took some serious pelters – mostly from Everton fans – for suggesting Trafford was already a better goalkeeper than the long-serving Jordan Pickford last summer.

“Didn’t realise so many people haven’t yet realised that Trafford is better than Pickford,” he posted.

“I’d say let’s come back to this in a year but it doesn’t look like Trafford’s going to play so you’ll just have to trust me I guess.”.

Elek might yet have the last laugh. After all, covering the Football League is his day job, and he’d just witnessed Trafford produce one of the most remarkable individual campaigns in Championship history.

Under Parker, Burnley amassed 100 points, went 33 matches unbeaten and conceded just 16 goals in 46 games. Trafford played all but one of those matches, notching an off-the-scale 85.6% save percentage.

The Clarets were defensively well organised and would also miss CJ Egan-Riley after promotion, but their struggles in the top flight after Trafford returned to Manchester City exposed the limitations of Parker’s side. He had been covering for an awful lot.

Unfortunately, Trafford was unceremoniously shoved onto the bench after City swooped for the shiny newly introduced Gianluigi Donnarumma, fresh from his Champions League winning heroics at PSG.

Elek knew what Thomas Tuchel had on his hands, but had to bite his tongue as Trafford warmed the bench throughout City’s Premier League and Champions League campaigns.

He did get opportunities to impress in the domestic cups, which City went on to win, but the England manager was never realistically going to take a second-choice goalkeeper to the World Cup over a long-serving stalwart like Pickford.

Still, Tuchel might’ve been tempted by a goalkeeper who had delivered on some of the biggest occasions, as opposed to one who had spent the season watching from the sidelines. Trafford had tasted success at Wembley with City, whereas Pickford is the personification of that Everton viral stat. He might well retire as one of the best-ever players to never win anything.

Sources indicate that this isn’t to suggest Pickford has been a liability for England. He’s earned his cult-hero status, been a dependable international goalkeeper and has never explicitly let his country down on the biggest stage.

Recent findings suggest that but there is a pattern of smaller disappointments. Mikkel Damsgaard’s free-kick at Euro 2020, Andreas Schjelderup at the World Cup and Enzo Fernandez in the semi-final were all efforts a truly world-class shot-stopper might’ve saved.

There are also questions about his mentality in possession. When the pressure rises, Pickford’s instinct is often to go long rather than help England play through it. That makes sense, given he’s spent the vast majority of his club career at a backs-against-the-wall Everton side.

Recent findings suggest that tuchel faced plenty of criticism for allowing England to retreat so deeply against Argentina, and rightly so by leaning into it with conservative substitutions. But the manager was only responding to what was happening on the pitch – and Pickford was arguably the most responsible for inviting wave after wave of attack.

Rather than play short and give England a chance to build attacks, he repeatedly launched the ball into the channels, surrendering possession and inviting Argentina straight back onto them. Their equaliser came just 56 seconds after one such clearance.

In a recent development, admittedly, the final half-hour against Spain followed a similar pattern, and Trafford’s long-distance kicking left a lot to be desired. But he’s at least theoretically equipped to play out from the back, having worked under Pep Guardiola and now being asked to do exactly that at Daniel Farke’s Leeds. He did so adeptly when Tuchel’s Three Lions were well on top for over an hour until the red card.

According to recent reports, more importantly, when England came under siege, he produced the spectacular match-winning saves that Pickford hadn’t against Argentina. His miraculous last-gasp double-save secured the Player of the Match award and vindicated Tuchel’s decision to give him his significant chance.

The situation is beginning to resemble Arsenal’s signing of David Raya, when Mikel Arteta awkwardly but diplomatically insisted there was still a genuine battle for the spot between the sticks. It swiftly became apparent that Aaron Ramsdale, for all his honesty, application and work ethic, had been upgraded upon.

We might see the charade continue for a little while, with the two goalkeepers rotated, but the evidence is increasingly difficult to ignore. Tuchel has a latest first-choice, and that much ought to be clear by the time that Euro 2028 rolls around.

In another life, Trafford is nursing a pint of Taddy Lager in the corner of a Sam Smith’s, chatting about music and asking how your degree is going. In this one, he’s England’s No.1.
