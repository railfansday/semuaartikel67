For winter snowshoeing on hiking trails and in the backcountry, you want a durable snowshoe with aggressive crampons for traction and a secure binding system that locks your boots to the snowshoes. If you want to get off the grid and snowshoe through backcountry terrain, here are our 10 recommended snowshoes. For more information, see our buying advice below.

Sources indicate that winter hiking snowshoes serve two key functions: they provide traction on icy trails and when climbing steep terrain, and they provide flotation over snow, so you don’t sink or posthole, which can be quite exhausting. While all the winter hiking snowshoes listed above meet both requirements, some excel in traction, like the MSR Lightning Ascents, MSR Evo Ascents, and Tubbs Flex VRTs, while others emphasize flotation, like the Tubbs Mountaineers.

According to recent reports, as a rule of thumb, teardrop-shaped snowshoes with synthetic, riveted decks tend to emphasize flotation, while rectangular snowshoes focus more on traction. If you’ll be climbing ice-covered mountains mostly, you’ll probably want a snowshoe that emphasizes traction; snowshoes that focus on flotation are better for areas where deep, powdery snow is the norm.

If you’re trying to choose between different snowshoes, there are four key properties that should guide your decision-making:.

Industry observers note that most winter hikers carry multiple traction devices and switch between them during the day. If you’re hiking a packed trail, you might start in bare boots, relying on your boot treads for traction because less weight on your feet means you’ll fatigue more slowly. If you hit slick or icy terrain, you might switch to microspikes, then snowshoes if you encounter fresh, uncompacted snow or slush.

To have these traction aids when you need them, you need to carry them. Microspikes are pretty easy to pack, but snowshoes aren’t because they’re big and bulky. Their bulk comes from their length, width, and thickness, which depends largely on the binding style they use. Lay-flat bindings, like the simple straps on the MSR Lightning Ascent and the MSR Evo Ascent, make snowshoes easy to attach and carry in a backpack, while snowshoes with Boa binding systems tend to be the bulkiest and hardest to pack.

Weight is also a key factor when choosing a snowshoe. Most snowshoes weigh four to five pounds, and they’re probably going to be the heaviest thing in your backpack, after water. That weight adds up over the course of a day, whether it’s in your backpack or on your feet.

We’ve already considered the packability of snowshoe bindings, but you should also consider comfort, security, ease of use while wearing gloves, whether the binding can freeze and become inoperative, and how easy it is to repair if it breaks. For example, some people worry that Boa closure systems can freeze if they get wet and stop working until they thaw. It’s a valid concern. One advantage, however, is that they are easy to use while wearing gloves and provide a secure grip that’s unlikely to come undone once set. Contrast that to the flat straps used on MSR snowshoes. They’ll never freeze up, they’re easy to replace if torn or lost, but they can be hard to attach when wearing gloves, and they tend to pop open once or twice during a hike.

Snowshoes come in a wide variety of sizes. These are determined by the total weight you want to carry (body weight + pack weight) and the flotation you need. Men’s sizes are usually larger than women’s because men are taller and heavier, while women’s snowshoes tend to be narrower because their gait is narrower.

If you’re buying a more traction-oriented snowshoe, you can sometimes drop a size below the manufacturer’s recommendation, especially if you’re hiking in an area that doesn’t get much snow or on trails that other hikers have already broken out. Sizing is directly correlated to gear weight and this is a tactic you can use to lighten your load. If flotation is a priority, you can buy tails, add-on snowshoe extensions that make them longer and increase their surface area. This is another way to cut down on the weight of a snowshoe because you can bring your tails when you need more flotation, but carry a lighter-weight snowshoe in less challenging conditions.
