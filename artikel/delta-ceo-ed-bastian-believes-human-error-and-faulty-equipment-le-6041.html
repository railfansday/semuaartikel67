Delta CEO Ed Bastian believes ‘human error’ and faulty equipment led to FAA ground stop

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Delta Air Lines CEO Ed Bastian thinks Friday’s nationwide FAA ground stop came from “a combination of faulty equipment, coupled with human error.” He dismissed other explanations as at the moment lacking evidence. “There’s no evidence that there’s [an] AI trigger. There’s no evidence that there’s cyber at play.”.

Bastian, speaking with Fortune’s Alyson Shontell at the Fortune 500 Innovation Forum in Detroit, said he has no information beyond what has been reported. “We’ll find out when it comes in,” he said.

The FAA halted departures nationwide for more than four hours Friday, canceling roughly 12,500 flights that day and thousands more on Saturday. It was the first nationwide stop since a January 2023 system outage, and only the second since 9/11.

In a recent development, bastian noted he has talked to Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy and FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford, and that Duffy “has been very transparent.” Duffy has said the trouble started during a copper-to-fiber switch at an FAA radio site in El Dorado, Ark. A router at a radar site in Amarillo, Texas, then “started to flood our system.” Contractor L3Harris did the installation, and Duffy has asked publicly whether the fault was the install or the router itself. L3Harris says it is supporting the investigation. Bedford said there was “zero evidence of anything nefarious and zero evidence of anything AI related.”.

Bastian said the router work wasn’t new. “I think they’ve replaced, I understand, close to 70% of the routers already,” he said. L3Harris indicates the switch to fiber is more than 70% done. “So I don’t know. I can’t explain it.”.

He said the system came back once the routers were taken offline, but finding them “took hours.” He put the cost of the disruption “in the billions.”.

Asked if more nationwide stops are coming, he called Friday “clearly an anomaly” and said he wouldn’t extrapolate “until we know what happened.”.

It has been revealed that bastian used the outage to argue for spending. Investors are putting “hundreds of billions, if not trillions of dollars” into AI and data centers, he stated, but “we can’t even get $50 billion to fix our skies.” Congress approved $12.5 billion for air traffic control last year. Duffy has said he wants $50 billion on top of that. President Trump has asked for $30 billion.

“The terrible thing would be if we use this instance to ‘lose confidence in what we’re doing,” Bastian said.

It has been revealed that he also pointed out that it takes longer to fly from Atlanta to LaGuardia today than it did when Delta rolled out the route in the 1950s. He said more capacity in the skies is the only way to lower fares. “We’re congested, we’re gridlocked.”.

Industry observers note that bastian stated Delta’s profit this year will be “pretty similar” to last year’s despite a $6 billion jump in its fuel bill. Delta’s ticket prices are up 15% to 20% year-over-year, he remarked with amazement: “We have never seen that dramatic growth.” Delta’s adjusted revenue rose 16% in the third quarter, and the organization expects about 20% growth in the fourth.

Delta could either raise prices or cut flights, he stated, arguing that “people are willing to pay more rather than have their options limited.” Most of its customers, he said, live “in the upper end of the K” and want to spend on travel, he said, referring to what economists call the “K-shaped economy.”.

Shontell noted that a planned Delta route to Riyadh, first revealed at the Fortune Global Forum in October 2025, was pushed back after missile attacks on the Riyadh airport. Bastian said service hadn’t started yet when the airport was hit.

Recent findings suggest that shontell also asked about Elon Musk, who has been going after Delta on X since the airline picked Amazon’s Leo satellite service over SpaceX’s Starlink. In early October, Musk posted that Bastian “will lose his job over this” and that “Delta will lose a lot of customers.” He also joked that a Delta flight was a good place to sandbox an AI model because there’s no internet. Delta revealed the Amazon deal in March. Leo is slated for 500 Delta planes starting in 2028. United is already installing Starlink across its fleet.

Recent findings suggest that bastian remarked Delta “helped them get started in aviation,” giving SpaceX planes to test on during COVID. “We just couldn’t come to an agreement on a business model.” He said customers don’t pick airlines based on Wi-Fi, and that when Leo arrives, “we’ll be at least as good as Starlink, if not better.”.

Sources indicate that leo has a new problem, though. On Sunday, a United Launch Alliance Vulcan rocket failed to reach orbit and lost all 40 Amazon Leo satellites it was carrying. Amazon had about 400 in orbit before the launch.

In a recent development, on AI, Bastian stuck to his line that the technology is there to help Delta’s roughly 100,000 workers, not replace them. “I’m not getting on any planes operated by AI with one pilot,” he noted. Bastian previously told Fortune that he hates the phrase AI and prefers to use “augmented intelligence” instead.

Sources indicate that customers who call about the AI never praise it, he noted. “They always complain about the AI.” The biggest issue with AI in the U.S. is trust, he said. Workers whose jobs change will be moved “to the extent capable into service, at airports or on the phones.
