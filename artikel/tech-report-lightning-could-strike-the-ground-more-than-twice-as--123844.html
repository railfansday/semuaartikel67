Tech Report: Lightning could strike the ground more than twice as often by the end of the century

If greenhouse gas emissions stay high, lightning strikes that reach the ground could rise 76% to 140% worldwide by 2100, with even larger increases in Alaska.

Lightning strikes that reach the ground could become 76% to 140% more frequent worldwide by the end of this century, compared with 1995 to 2014, if greenhouse gas emissions stay high.

The numbers are far above previous estimates, and they surprised the researchers who produced them.

During that baseline period, most of Alaska averaged fewer than one strike per square kilometer per year. By the end of the century, the statewide increase could reach 594%.

The study was led by Andreas Krause, an Earth system scientist at the Technical University of Munich.

Lightning has been genuinely hard to forecast, because its two essential ingredients pull in opposite directions under a warming climate.

In a recent development, it starts with heat. Air near the ground warms, becomes buoyant and rises, a process called convection.

In a recent development, that rising air stirs up the much colder atmosphere above it. In the turbulence, tiny ice crystals in the frigid mid-level clouds collide and swap electric charge.

The lighter crystals get carried upward while the heavier ones sink, separating positive from negative and building an electric field between them. When the field grows strong enough, the air breaks down and a bolt fires.

Warmer air near the ground can fuel stronger updrafts, increasing the chances that ice particles collide and build up an electric charge.

We can already see the pattern: lightning peaks in the early afternoon, the hottest part of the day, and the tropics get far more of it than the high latitudes.

Warmer air aloft could mean fewer ice crystals, making it harder for storms to build up the electric charge that produces lightning.

In a recent development, “There is actually quite a large uncertainty about how lightning will respond to climate change,” Krause said. “It’s not quite clear which of these is more important.”.

Rather than build the physics from scratch, the team trained a machine learning model on a single year of observations from 2019.

The researchers paired actual lightning records with the weather conditions that produced them: temperature, rainfall, humidity, air pressure, wind speed, and incoming solar radiation.

Then they ran it backwards. Applied to the climate of 1901 to 1920 and again to 1995 to 2014, the model indicated cloud-to-ground lightning rising 11% over the 20th century. That works out to roughly 12% more lightning per degree Celsius of warming.

The forward projection is where it gets uncomfortable. Under a high-emissions scenario, that sensitivity nearly triples, to about 30% more lightning per degree.

Compared with the 1995 to 2014 baseline, ground strikes would become 76% to 140% more common by 2100, depending on which climate model you feed in.

According to recent reports, for comparison, the highest previous estimates put the figure between 8% and 17% per degree. The gap comes mostly from a different mathematical approach and from this study’s use of a newer, more reliable global lightning dataset.

“I was quite surprised that the projections were so high,” Krause stated. “I expected smaller changes.”.

The projections apply specifically to cloud-to-ground lightning, the bolts that actually touch down.

Most lightning never reaches the surface, staying within or between clouds, and it does little harm there.

It has been revealed that what comes down is another matter. Cloud-to-ground strikes ignite wildfires, damage wind turbines, power lines and offshore oil rigs, and kill several thousand people and hundreds of millions of trees worldwide every year.

Industry observers note that the increases won’t be evenly spread. In absolute terms, the biggest rises come where lightning is already thickest: the Amazon, Southeast Asia, and Oceania.

The Amazon projection carries the largest caveat, and the researchers are upfront about it.

Their model learned from the Amazon as it exists today, and that forest may look very different by 2100. Some studies actually predict less tropical lightning in the future, not more.

The biggest proportional changes happen at high latitudes, where lightning is currently rare enough that any increase looks dramatic.

According to recent reports, many of those regions could see strike density more than double. Alaska is the extreme case, somewhere between 116% and 594% above its baseline.

Whether more lightning translates into more danger is a separate question, and it depends on timing.

Krause pointed out that the risk to people may hinge on whether the extra bolts arrive during the early stages of storms.

That’s when someone outdoors is most likely to be caught off guard, before the sky has made its intentions obvious.

It has been revealed that the study didn’t examine how forests would cope. In general, Krause said, conifers show more visible damage from a direct strike than broadleaved trees.

Sources indicate that within the tropics, some studies suggest that forests already accustomed to frequent lightning handle it better. That resilience is something he wants to look at next.

According to recent reports, “Trees basically evolved to adapt to the local lightning frequency,” Krause noted. “But are there any species that are particularly tolerant of lightning strikes and more likely to survive them? There’s not much information out there.”.

The study is published in the journal Earth’s Future.
