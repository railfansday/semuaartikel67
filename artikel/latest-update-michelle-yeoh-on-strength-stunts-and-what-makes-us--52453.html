Latest Update: Michelle Yeoh on Strength, Stunts, and What Makes Us Human

An action hero used to have to be substantial and brawny and brusque. Michelle Yeoh is none of those things—but you feel her presence and power the instant you meet her. She is kind, warm, and lovely, but she’s also intense…in a really good way. This is a person who listens closely yet also seems ready to launch into a wheelhouse kick at any moment.

Starring as Olwen, a bioengineered artificial human (or “replicant,” as they’re called in the Blade Runner universe, which includes the fresh series Blade Runner 2099, out November 25 on Amazon Prime Video), the 64-year-old global megastar literally wrestles with people and replicants alike on-screen.

But in real life, she dances with even larger issues. Like what separates humans from the bot world, how we define strength beyond the physical, why we all need to stretch every morning, and, finally, how she gets over a very specific fear. (If you’ve ever watched her in movies like Tomorrow Never Dies or even Everything Everywhere All At Once, you’ll understand how shocking this particular fear is for her. The solution is one six-letter word…and you’ll just have to read on to find out what that is.).

I sat down with Michelle—who is classically trained in ballet and does many of her own martial arts stunts—in NYC for a wide-ranging convo that starts with robots and ends with what she calls the “Michelle prayer.”.

Women’s Health: What was it like to play a replicant, and how did you think about preparing and training for that mindset?

In a recent development, michelle Yeoh: We have a brilliant writer, Silka Luisa, who had thought out the story for Blade Runner 2099 [a continuation of the original series] and where it has gone. All these replicants are now in charge of a Los Angeles that is no longer what we know. But it’s very interesting because [the question that arises is] What is humanity? The replicants do give the humans hope. As long as you’re good, as long as you do things the way we want them to be handled, you could go to a better place, because now Los Angeles is not exactly the most habitable place.

Replicants are made to look human. In fact, they are flesh and blood, except they’re stronger, faster. They are able to withstand the new worlds. But empathy is what gives you away as a human. Replicants are born without that emotion. They’re born with memories. They’re given memories to keep them sort of happy and at peace with what they are. Are you born with humanity or does it come with experience and as you live your life with compassion and kindness?

WH: You noted that you’re excited for people to see the series and to share ideas and to talk about it. Is that why? The humanity complexity?

Michelle: The worst is they say, “Oh, yeah, it was interesting.…” What is “just” interesting? Hopefully you go home, you think about it, and then you come back and the next day at work or in school, you’re chatting with your friends— “Well, what are you? A replicant or a human? What would you rather be?”.

WH: How did you prepare, physically, for this role?

Industry observers note that michelle: I think the older you get, the more you have to do to keep up your muscle strength, to keep your muscles there. Without that, you start walking differently. I have the advantage of understanding my body a lot more because I’ve been doing this for the last, well, ever since I was born. I was dancing, doing sports, everything. I do core-strengthening exercises and tai chi every day.

The core was the most important part of the training because we wanted to nail the way Olwen moved. It was with precision. Because if you want to move with motivation, it has to have that strength, that inner strength, rather than just speed. So when you move, it’s almost like you glide. As a replicant, all the moves are sharp, precise, because they’re so strong and powerful. Actually, when you take a human down, it’s too easy. I think all exercises are core-focused. You can’t lift weights or do pilates without working on the core first. Everything has to start from there.

Recent findings suggest that WH: Has your own relationship to risk changed over the years? Are there stunts that you used to do in your 30s that now you’re like “nah,” or do you actually feel more powerful and in control?

Sources indicate that michelle: I think that’s really interesting and a good way of putting it. Feeling powerful doesn’t mean being reckless and silly. I feel more powerful in the sense that I have a better assessment of the risk factors. But naturally, because when I was in my 20s, when I first started out, you’re brave and foolish and you’re also so fit. And I was really, really, really fit at that time because I didn’t have any crazy injuries. And, so, fearless is a good word, but fearless and stupid is not good. That’s a bad combination.

Industry observers note that sometimes the smallest stunts are the worst because you think they’re easy, and then you don’t pay full attention. You’re not focused enough. You’re focused, but you’re like, “Yeah, yeah, yeah. I’ll get it done.” And that’s when something bad will happen.

In a recent development, in the past, when you are beginning, you listen and you do what they tell you to do, and then you have a lot to prove because you don’t want them to think that you’re unable or you’re not capable, so you do it. But now you go, “How about we…” And then you can speak with power because you know your character. And then I’ll go, “But Olwen won’t do it like that.” And who is going to challenge me?

WH: I understand that you became a bit of a mentor to Hunter Schafer. So talk to us a little about the stunt piece.… Did you help her figure out how to have that voice for herself?

Michelle: To be honest, when I look at her, I go, “Oh my god, I see my young self.” When they show her something, she’ll just throw herself into it because she wants to do it. She’s eager to do it. But then I have to keep reminding her, “You don’t have the same kind of training the stunt people have. So when you can’t do it, it doesn’t mean that you’re not good.”.

Recent findings suggest that what I do is try to remind her, like, “Hunter, knee pads, Hunter, elbow pads. What is the stunt that you’re doing? Get them on.” Because sometimes you go, like, No, no, no, it’s okay. It’s not okay. Get it on.

Industry observers note that it’s a joy to work with her. It’s just a joy to be with her, which sometimes can be difficult because we’re supposed to be against each other in Blade Runner 2099. I mean, she’s a human pretending to be a replicant, pretending to be a blade runner. But somehow their missions intertwine, so they have no choice but to learn to work together. So we have a lot of fun.

WH: What are your nonnegotiables? What are the things you, Michelle, must do every single day for your health that you are consistent with?

Michelle: Water, sunblock, and a lot of stretching. That is a must.

It has been revealed that I do tai chi, I do my ballet and tai chi. I’ve been doing that for a long time now. I try to do that every day because I’ve unfortunately had injuries in the past—my back, my knees, my shoulder. I don’t know where I don’t have an injury. As I’m in bed, I do a lot of stretching. When I wake up, the first thing I do in my bed is to go through the motions so that the body wakes up with the mind and everything comes into place.

According to recent reports, I think that’s what you have to find: your own moves [or routine] so that you don’t give yourself an excuse when you go, “Oh, it’s raining. No time to go to the gym.” So you just do it even in the bed, you’re stretching, you’re doing all those core exercises to wake up the mind, to wake up the abs, the back, and the legs, everything. And that helps when we’re actually on-set because then I can step into it and know I am strong and ready to go.

Industry observers note that I love being outside, going on hikes. Then if I can’t do that, I’m on the elliptical, because I think cardio is still very key. But now, with time, I do a lot more weights. I’m picking up dumbbells or water bottles, whatever works. You have to combine the strengthening of muscles with cardio exercise.

WH: I’m curious if there’s something that is better now in your 60s than it was in your 20s—anything?

Michelle: I think the only thing that’s a little bit better might be this up here [motions to head].

Recent findings suggest that I do a fairly decent job at keeping up the energy, keeping up the spirits, because I also think it’s the inner strength that is very important. Your mind is very important. If you don’t have the inner peace, it’s very hard to keep control of your body because negative vibes are the worst thing for any human being. When you have negative vibes around you, in you, then you’re down. I honestly believe that because you’ve gone through so many experiences, you’re better for it. I wouldn’t give that up.

Sources indicate that michelle: I have a fear of heights. I know. See, I’m claustrophobic and have a fear of heights.

It has been revealed that michelle: But the magic word is action. Camera rolling…action. And I am focused. Fearless. It’s really, really bizarre. If I don’t hear the word action, then it’s a no go. If I’m in the mall, you know, sometimes you have those walkways that look over the first floor. I walk right in the middle of those. I’m taking no risk. But once action is said on set, I’m good.

In a recent development, WH: You mentioned a mantra. What, exactly, is it?

According to recent reports, michelle: When I wake up, even before opening my eyes and stretching, I sort of have my own little mantra to wake myself up. I try to talk to my body, thank my body, apologize for anything I’ve done bad, and then welcome everything that’s good because I’m deserving of it.

In a recent development, I don’t sit there and meditate. I don’t understand that at all. Just as long as you feel it yourself that I have what I need for me…because everybody is different.

And that’s why I start my day with a prayer, a mantra. I guess you could call it my own Michelle prayer. Be thankful and be willing to accept anything good that comes your way. I think we have to look at ourselves and have self-worth because I think the inner strength, the inner peace, that sense of compassion and kindness and contentment, is very notable. Because if you’re always wanting, needing, and thinking about something else, someone else, then you’re always in conflict, first of all, with yourself.

In a recent development, WH: What does the word strength mean to you? When you hear that word, what’s the definition that comes to mind?

Michelle: I think when you hear that word, first of all, it’s always very top layer. You walk into the gym, I have strength, I have power. I put on a power suit. That’s powerful. But for me, true strength is right in there [points to heart]. Showing weakness is strength. Doing a lot of things that people don’t understand, but for you, when you make a choice, it’s strength. There are so many ways of looking at it, and I don’t think there’s one definition unless you check the encyclopedia and they tell you what it is.

WH: And then we’ll just ignore that and go with the Michelle Yeoh definition anyway.
