Public-Land Sales, Energy Dominance, and Data Centers: What Interior’s Master Proposal Really Means for Outdoorsmen

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Less than two months after a dinner at the White House with leaders of hunting and angling groups in which President Trump and Secretary of the Interior Doug Burgum confirmed support for conservation priorities, Burgum has formalized the Trump administration’s plans to shrink the federal estate and to site industrial data centers and energy infrastructure on America’s public lands.

As we reported last week, the intentions are outlined in the Department of the Interior’s five-year strategic initiative that extends through 2030. The document serves as a blueprint for implementing the priorities of the Bureau of Land Management, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Bureau of Reclamation, National Park Service, and Bureau of Indian Affairs, among other Interior agencies.

The 28-page plan is short on details but long on statements about the department’s role in domestic energy “dominance,” “unleashing the potential” of America’s public lands, and pursuit of “public-private partnerships.” In addition to his role at the helm of the Department of Interior, Burgum also chairs the White House National Energy Dominance Council.

The few detailed directives contained in the five-year proposal note Interior’s intention to “decrease the size of the federal estate by offering to states, localities, and Tribes the opportunity to purchase federal lands” in order to better focus the department’s resources on “nationally significant federal lands.” The strategic plan further emphasizes Interior’s pursuit of “strategic rights-of-way agreements, land exchanges, and direct transfer of lands in support of reliable energy production and transport.”.

And the strategy specifically calls for redirecting revenue from the Land and Water Conservation Fund, which is funded by offshore oil and gas leasing revenue, to private-land easements rather than outright purchase of land by the government for recreation and resource purposes. The fund provides about $900 million annually to acquire land, improve existing properties, and invest in communities’ cultural and outdoor recreation assets.

Sources indicate that the Department of Interior’s strategy’s mention of possible land sales provoked quick push-back from lawmakers from both parties who recall the tsunami of opposition to inclusion of federal land sales as part of the 2025 Significant Beautiful Bill. Language that would have funded tax breaks by selling large parcels of public land was eventually withdrawn from that bill, which was later signed into law by Trump.

According to recent reports, both of Montana’s Republican senators, including retiring Sen. Steve Daines, who sits on the powerful Senate Energy and Natural Resources Committee, told the news agency Politico that they were not consulted on Interior’s strategic plan and would oppose the idea of land sales. Yesterday, Burgum, responding to growing criticism of the land-sale language, told Fox News that national parks would not be sold. He called public-land supporters’ reaction to the strategic plan “manufactured hysteria.”.

But the renewed conversation around the topic, along with the proposal’s focus on energy development and investment in “Super Intelligence” infrastructure, raises the question: How seriously should America’s hunters, anglers, and outdoor enthusiasts take Interior’s blueprint, or is the strategic plan simply glossy “shelfware” that will be ignored as a new Congress convenes in January and other priorities shove it to the background?

“The thing about these intends is that they don’t really hold a lot of authority,” says a government affairs executive who didn’t have permission to speak on the record. “The agencies have to put them out periodically, but this is not a chapter-and-verse regulatory document or a change in the statute. Instead, you can read it as a sort of statement of purpose.”.

According to recent reports, in order to execute sales or transfers of federal land, they note, the Interior would have to undertake a formal land-use planning process with opportunities for public comment. That’s a core provision of the Federal Land Policy and Management Act. But some conservation groups are signaling that a newly introduced bill working its way through the lame-duck Congress this week, the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act would undermine many of those land-disposal requirements. And the administration’s undercutting of the BLM’s Resource Management Prepares further weakens norms that have kept land transfers a rare exception.

A more present worry isn’t outright land sales, which are overwhelmingly opposed by America’s hunters and anglers. It’s the long-term lease of federal lands for hyper-scale data centers and energy facilities, says Randy Newberg, a longtime public-land advocate and podcaster.

In a recent development, “Within this vision plan is an awful lot of what I’d call greasing the skids for private development of public land, which in all aspects is quasi-ownership of public land,” says Newberg. “Given the transactional nature of this administration, I worry about sweetheart deals that help your buddy who is friends with the president or cabinet secretary to develop data centers or oil and gas facilities, you name it.”.

Newberg claims the outcome is the opposite of conservation.

“Under that vision document, you can just about bet that our public lands will become far more industrialized than any of us probably ever expected.”.

Recent findings suggest that no one should be especially surprised to see land transfer discussed at this vision-document level. After all, it’s a plank in Republican Party platforms in several states, including Montana, where party leaders last summer amended the position confirming their support for public-land transfer but also encouraging public access.

Montana’s GOP ratified the updated platform in June to read “Support transferring federally managed lands to the state with a transition initiative and continued public access.” The previous version had simply encouraged transfer of federal lands to the states “in order to secure statehood equality and provide for better management of public lands.”.

Interestingly, even the polarizing Project 2025 document, organized by the Heritage Foundation to prioritize policies of the incoming second Trump administration, doesn’t explicitly advocate transferring federal land. On the other hand, the document’s section outlining Department of the Interior priorities — maximizing energy production on federal lands, rolling back protections for national monuments, and weakening environmental reviews — are all reprised in Burgum’s five-year proposal.

Industry observers note that one of the few times the current Congress pushed back on administrative priorities in the second Trump term was to remove the land-transfer provisions of the Big Beautiful Bill. That opposition was led by Montana Rep. Ryan Zinke (R), the former Secretary of Interior in Trump’s first term, who later founded the Public Lands Caucus in Congress. But the next Congress, with majority Democrats energized by successes in this month’s mid-term election, is likely to demand oversight and review of federal agencies, including Interior.

One of the incoming freshmen, Democrat Sam Forstag, took Zinke’s western Montana seat by about 9 points. It’s the first time in 30 years that Montanans elected a Democrat to represent them in the U.S. House of Representatives. Forstag is a U.S. Forest Service smokejumper and union leader who ran on issues of affordability and against politics that “puts working people last.”.

Speaking from Washington, D.C., yesterday as he rushed between meetings and caucused with fellow freshmen, Forstag claims that public-land management has the potential to be a rare bipartisan issue.

“The notion of shrinking the federal estate and selling off our public lands sounds damned concerning to me,” Forstag said. “I think particularly as we’re seeing a change of leadership in Congress, this is the matter I see as having the most potential for not just bringing the [Democrat] caucus together but building bipartisan support. Republicans who may not have stood up publicly when we needed them the last couple of years are coming around that when it comes to actual people, public lands are not a partisan issue. They’re one of the last places where you don’t have to be rich to get access to it. [Public lands] are a reason Montana is a place worth living while costs skyrocket.”.

Forstag, who hopes to land an assignment on the House Committee on Natural Resources, states he considers talk about selling or leasing federal lands for data centers or energy infrastructure to be a “distraction.”.
