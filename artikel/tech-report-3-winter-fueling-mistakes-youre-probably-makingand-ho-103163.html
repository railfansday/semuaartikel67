Tech Report: 3 Winter Fueling Mistakes You’re Probably Making—and How to Fix Them

For many runners, winter presents a greater challenge than other seasons when it comes to fueling. Our regular eating schedules shift around social gatherings, holiday menus influence our usual food choices, and staying hydrated can take more effort without higher temperatures to drive our thirst.

As we approach the start of winter, it’s a good time to check in on your nutrition habits and make tweaks to set you up for stronger running through the season.

Three registered dietitians, who have years of competitive athletic experience themselves, share the most common fueling mistakes they see runners make this time of year and explain exactly how to fix each one.

Recent findings suggest that vitamin D deficiencies are common among athletes in winter, Maddie Alm, RD, a registered dietitian and the founder of Fueling Forward Sports Nutrition in Colorado, tells Runner’s World. That’s because shorter days and colder temperatures can mean less exposure to the sun’s UVB rays, which help our bodies produce vitamin D.

Recent findings suggest that alm explains that this vitamin not only supports strong bones, but also helps keep our immune and digestive systems functioning at their best. It plays an notable role in brain health and mood, too. According to the National Institutes of Health, low blood levels of the vitamin have been associated with a higher risk of depression.

The Fix: Get outside daily to catch some sunshine, and focus on eating more vitamin D–rich foods, with the goal to consume 600 international units (IU) per day.

Sources indicate that good sources of vitamin D include milk, yogurt, cottage cheese, tuna, salmon, egg yolk, and mushrooms. Some cereals and orange juices are also vitamin D–fortified. For reference, three ounces of salmon contain 450 IU, one cup of cow’s milk contains 180 IU, one large egg yolk contains 40 IU, and one cup of fortified cereal contains 40 IU.

It has been revealed that it’s a good idea, Alm adds, to check your levels through bloodwork once or twice a year and to consult a professional if your numbers come back low. If you have a hard time hitting your daily quota through food alone, you may benefit from taking a vitamin D supplement.

Runners can also overlook vitamin C intake this time of year, says Sakiko Minagawa, RD, a registered dietitian for the Division of Sports Medicine at Nationwide Children’s Hospital in Ohio and two-time Olympic Marathon Trials qualifier. Vitamin C, also called ascorbic acid, supports immune health, which is especially key during cold and flu season.

Recent findings suggest that our bodies also need vitamin C to produce collagen, a protein that’s essential for tissue growth and repair. “While vitamin C may not prevent you from outright getting sick, some research demonstrates that it may decrease the duration of cold symptoms,” Minagawa explains.

The Fix: Up your consumption of foods with vitamin C, such as bell peppers, citrus fruits, greens, and strawberries. Women should aim for 75 milligrams per day, while men should aim for 90 milligrams daily.

You’ll get about 210 milligrams from one large red or orange bell pepper, and 90 milligrams in a cup of raw strawberry halves—each of which achieves your recommended daily dose.

Industry observers note that because vitamin C helps with iron absorption, Minagawa suggests pairing the two whenever possible. Some meals she recommends are a spinach salad with strawberries, an omelet with spinach and red bell peppers, and tomato soup with lentils or black beans.

In the winter, you might not end your run with a dry mouth and sweat-soaked shirt like in the summer, but it’s still critical to hydrate. “Even when it’s cold, you still sweat,” states Minagawa, which means you still need to replenish fluids throughout the day, as well as before and after a workout.

Similarly, your midrun fueling and hydrating remain just as crucial for performance. It’s true: Tearing open an energy gel with gloved hands can pose a real challenge. But, if you’re doing long runs through the colder months, you still need to consume enough carbohydrates during your effort.

In a recent development, the Fix: Minagawa suggests carrying a water bottle that you can easily drink from and refill throughout the day. If you have a hard time sipping on plain water, remember that soups, broths, and hot chocolate can also help you stay hydrated—and can double as recovery fuel.

Don’t forget about fresh fruits and vegetables, either, as they can help with hydration. Some great in-season options that can contribute to your overall fluid intake include grapefruit, clementines, pomegranate, kiwi, squash, Brussels sprouts, sweet potatoes, pears, kale, and cabbage.

According to recent reports, during your winter long runs, experiment with fueling options other than energy gels and chews. Some great alternatives you can easily carry in a small plastic baggie include salted Medjool dates, boiled red potatoes, dried fruit, pretzels, and even candy.

Bad weather conditions, scant daylight hours, and more limited racing opportunities make winter a time for many athletes to dial back their training. Taking an off-season can benefit you in the long haul, but Minagawa encourages runners to still stay active—even if that means swapping some runs for walks, bike rides, easy swims, or elliptical sessions.

Recent findings suggest that you don’t want to depart from those year-round healthy habits, Brianna Butler, RD, a registered dietitian, co-owner of Stef & Bri Wellness in Texas, and former professional WNBA athlete, previously told Runner’s World. The same applies to your nutrition.

In a recent development, “The biggest mistake runners make is that, when it’s the off-season, they’re off everything—they say, ‘I’m not doing anything, I’m going to eat whatever’—they’re just winging it,” Butler says. “It shouldn’t be all or nothing. We want to make sure we’re nourishing our bodies well year-round.”.

If your training load changes significantly, pay closer attention to your hunger cues to make sure your nutrition matches your energy needs.

The Fix: Maintain a daily exercise routine you enjoy, prioritize nutrient-dense meals and snacks, and resist making drastic changes to your diet.

It has been revealed that if you do find yourself feeling sluggish this time of year, it can be tempting to repent with cleanses, detoxes, and extreme exercise regimens. But such quick fixes rarely pay off in the long run, Alm warns, as they put you at risk for injury, burnout, and subpar seasons to come.

Alm says not to stress if you ditched your typical eating and training schedule for a few weeks and are feeling less energetic as a result. When you return to your usual healthy habits, your body will naturally find its sweet spot. “Trust the process and don’t panic,” Alm advises, because our bodies may change as we move through different training phases. “It is okay to not be at your leanest year-round.”.

Industry observers note that the smartest path forward? Do your best to move your body daily, fuel it with what it needs, and give yourself permission to rest when you’re tired. That way, you’ll find yourself feeling strong and ready to train hard again come spring.
