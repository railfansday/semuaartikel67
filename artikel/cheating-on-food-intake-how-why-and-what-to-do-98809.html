Cheating on Food Intake: How, Why, and What to Do

Posted November 23, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk.

According to recent reports, at any given time, millions of American adults (and some younger) are on a diet. That means they are watching their weight. Or trying to. Some diets actually incorporate “cheat days” or “cheat meals.” Some people on diets institute their own cheats—as in, “I’ll just splurge on this tonight, and get back to my diet tomorrow.”.

Unfortunately, food cheating can lead to weight gain, failure to lose weight, and diet-disease consequences.

Industry observers note that where does this come from? Is cheating a “thing” in its own right or does it have more to do with food cravings, impulse control, metabolism, brain function, gut health, and any number of other body and brain processes?

In a recent development, to take a closer look at this, we can check out research on people reporting their food intake. After all, that is the only way we can find out what people are eating. Or think they are eating.

Sources indicate that how is this tracked? There are a variety of ways, including self-report questionnaires, 24-hour recall forms, recording food intake in the moment on an app, taking pictures of food being eaten, and wearing cameras throughout the day. There are some problems with all of these methods. For example, people may report differently when they are part of a study that watches what they eat. Still, there have been some discoveries that are fairly consistent.

It has been revealed that people are not great at reporting what they eat. They typically underreport snack foods, readily available foods, and foods that are high-calorie and feel more rewarding than vegetables. Most commonly, people tend to forget the snacks they grab between meals. Interestingly, people also tend to overreport their intake of healthy foods, like proteins or vegetables.

Digging deeper, there are some nuances.

Social pressure can work both ways. If someone has unveiled to their family and friends that they are on a diet or following a certain initiative, they may feel pressure to eat only healthy items when in that group. On the other hand, some may just go along with a group if they are enjoying some party foods. Being observed versus not being observed may also be part of the mix.

Distraction can cause people to grab a food and forget about it.

Negative affect can be associated with emotional eating and giving in to the impulse to seek highly palatable foods, and then have it slip from their memories.

Memory and judgment complications: One study (2020) discovered that negative affect is associated with decreased memory suppression in the hippocampus of palatable food cues. In other words, palatable food cues become more salient and can lead to wanting those items.

Another study (2019) noted that the Western diet, which includes processed foods, can impact the hippocampus and several other areas to impair judgment.

The hippocampus is a center of memory and can be implicated in confirmation bias. An article in the Washington Post (2022) noted that “one of the biggest problems to correcting information is the fact that hearing the truth doesn’t delete a falsehood from our memory.” Our brains store the original piece of misinformation, and it takes a different tagging system to get at the truth. To put it in the context of cheating with food, perhaps the eating was unconscious, or perhaps the person just doesn’t want to identify as a person who sneaks food. Either way, there is the memory that the cheat didn’t happen. The memory can become part of an identity or even a system of belief.

Metabolism: Excess weight can affect leptin signaling, so that hunger signals are inhibited, leading to food consumption unrelated to hunger signals.

Industry observers note that excess weight can lead to diabetes and insulin resistance. A study (2019) revealed insulin resistance to be associated with deficits in episodic memory.

Sources indicate that one study (2023) used a trial of supervised machine learning, which did, in the end, mitigate food underreporting. Unfortunately, this is not a sustainable process, or one that could be used widely.

Recent findings suggest that another study (2015) used Inhibitory Control Training with some success. However, the gains they made were only short-term.

Sources indicate that A study (2016) noted that accurate monitoring is “synonymous with the most weight loss.” However, studies that provide opportunity to do so are necessarily time-limited. The process needs to nurture long-term skills to maintain success.

Irregular eating patterns have been associated with cheating. So, one strategy would be to encourage people to eat regular meals and double down on planning.

It has been revealed that another strategy includes education or coaching. Awareness of the potential areas where pitfalls can happen and presenting other options may give people a strategy they can use long-term.

Education could include some of the reasons why cheating is so prevalent. It’s not a moral failure. It’s human nature, and it’s the way our bodies work. Knowledge can start to make some inroads.

Monitoring is a good tool. It is an external process that can lead to awareness. However, the strategies above point to an overarching process that is internal—changing attitudes, beliefs, and behaviors. These are changes that can be realized over time and can be lasting.
