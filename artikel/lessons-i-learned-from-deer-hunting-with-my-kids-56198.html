For a span of maybe a dozen years, from when my twin boys were 11 until their kid sister went to college, we together killed an average of six deer a year, mainly bucks but a few antlerless whitetails and mule deer when we needed the meat.

Industry observers note that that’s a lot of early mornings in northeastern Montana, a lot of cold feet and wintry weather, and a lot of cutting and wrapping and sausage grinding. The body count is a poor measure of our experiences together, though, as it doesn’t tally countless blown stalks, missed shots, and those curiously memorable days when we didn’t see anything worth shooting but we found arrowheads and bison skulls and watched waves of sandhill cranes migrate south. Or those days we talked about fickle friends, where grandparents go when they die, and the notion of self-reliance.

What’s most remarkable about my kids’ deer hunting journey is that almost all of it took place on our family’s farm and ranch, which we bought when my boys were 9 years old and which in most years holds a satisfying mix of mule deer in the sagebrush-and-gumbo uplands and whitetails in the irrigated bottomlands.

My kids are freshly gone from the home place. My boys have grown-up jobs in cities on opposite coasts. My daughter is in her first year of graduate school in California.

In the years they’ve been away, I’ve realized how little I care about all the deer-season conversations that used to pique my interest: best days of the rut, the perfect deer caliber, various hacks to unlock locked-up bucks. Instead, I now nourish myself during deer season with a handful of rich lessons accumulated with my kids. If you can excuse the musings of an empty-nest father, here are some of our experiences that still speak to me, years later.

How else can I explain the fact that my wife of 29 years still finds me funny or that the nib of a lead pencil that Eric Thompson stabbed me with back in third grade is still in the roll-over flab of my waistline?

On my son Ellis’s very first deer hunt, after we decided a mule deer doe would be a great way to begin a long hunting career, we were set up on a cottonwood limb that offered a solid rest for Ellis’s .243. Our little stand overlooked a corner of an alfalfa field that spilled into sagebrush and then into a prairie stream that’s a favorite bedding area for mule deer.

It has been revealed that on cue, the deer started filing toward the alfalfa field. Ellis had practiced dry-firing and panning his gun on the branch. Now he was loaded and ready, and, as if scripted, a yearling mule deer walked into view, not 80 yards away, just on the other side of an old barbed-wire fence. Strangely calm as the deer turned broadside to contemplate mule-deery things, Ellis sent one.

Watching from over his shoulder, I expected the deer to crumple, or maybe take the hit and lurch. But the deer just stood there looking at us, and I saw no dust or other evidence of the shot. Now El was rattled.

Recent findings suggest that “What do I do?” he hissed over his shoulder. “Rack another one,” I whispered back. But the deer had had enough and was gone.

Industry observers note that I was sure El had pulled the shot. After all, hadn’t I missed the first couple deer I had shot at when I was his age? It’s natural to be twitchy when confronted for the first time with the reality of taking an animal’s life. Only Ellis insisted that he had been calm and steady.

According to recent reports, “Let’s go make sure you didn’t cut hair or draw any blood,” I stated. Ellis cleared his chamber and we walked toward the first fence, crossed it, and inspected the spot where the deer had been standing when El shot. No blood. No hair. No evidence of a hit. We walked back to our hide, and that’s when I saw it, a freshly clipped vertical cross wire in the first fence.

It has been revealed that ellis went back to the cottonwood limb, and used a straight stick to line up where his rifle had pointed at the deer. It was perfectly aligned with the fence. His .243-caliber bullet had cut the .150-inch-diameter wire, deflecting his shot and saving that deer.

On the way back to the house, Ellis seemed to metabolize the events that had just unfolded.

“Maybe there’s a reason I missed,” he mused. “Maybe it wasn’t time. Maybe I’ll find that deer later.”.

Industry observers note that I have no way of knowing if any of the future mule deer Ellis killed descended from that lucky yearling, but in the years since, I’ve hit limbs instead of vitals, had bullets deflect off unseen sagebrush, and once had an eagle spook a dandy buck just as I was about to shoot. It’s the hidden hand of happenstance. Some people call it fate. Some call it luck. Whenever it happens to me, it conjures Ellis and the pigtailed wire that spoiled his first sure-thing hunt.

It has been revealed that ellis’s twin brother, Merlin, is the most coachable kid I’ve known, and I say that as a longtime coach of track and cross-country runners, some of whom are not related to me. Merlin listens well, follows directions, and is committed to a shared outcome.

So it baffled me just a little when, after graduating from Montana’s Hunter Education course and living under my tutelage about the proper place to shoot a deer, he proceeded to take a neck shot on his first deer.

The doe went about 3 feet, straight down. Lucky, I told him. A few inches high or low and you miss. A couple inches right, you blow the jaw off and the deer dies a horrible death, ultimately of starvation. But Merlin correctly noted that a proper neck shot saves a lot of meat.

According to recent reports, his next couple deer also fell to neck shots. Every time, I reminded him of the risks of the shot, and about the forgiveness of the “boiler room” behind the front shoulder that takes out both lungs and maybe the top of the heart.

It has been revealed that merlin’s rifle was a Ruger No. 1 single-shot chambered in .243. It’s not the most accurate rifle in my collection, but Merlin shot it well, and over the years I came to learn that he had supreme confidence in that gun, and in his one-and-done shot. It’s not my nature to stay quiet — especially when I’m right — but I piped down, and was quietly overjoyed to skin and butcher deer that had four intact quarters and no internal injuries.

I came to realize that Merlin knew something I didn’t. He knew precisely where he was sending that bullet, because he wouldn’t take a shot outside the proficiency zone of his gun and his abilities. When a shot exceeded that distance, he aimed for the boiler room. I came to recognize that his quiet belief in his own abilities has given Merlin opportunities that might never come to a blustery self-promoter.

It was hard to know if hunting would take hold of Iris after her first year. She was a little tentative, in part because we hired a videographer to document her experiences – you can watch it here. Who subjects an 11-year-old girl to that indignity? But despite her initial reluctance, Iris became the most dedicated and determined hunter of my three kids. And, especially after my boys went on to college, hunting was something we shared and nurtured.

Industry observers note that but Iris was burdened by an unseen weight that I’ve observed in other hunters, especially in women and girls. She was so cautious about making a perfect shot that she sometimes allowed otherwise pretty good opportunities to dissolve and then evaporate as she waited for the platonic textbook broadside.

I’ve learned from a lifetime in the field and elsewhere that perfection sometimes isn’t clear in the moment. It can be vanishingly quick to come and go, so quick that many of us scarcely notice it. That quartering-away shot that exposes the vitals of a buck is nearly as good as a broadside shot, I might argue, but Iris was deaf to my suggestions that she relax her standards in this single situation.

It has been revealed that happily, as she’s grown older, Iris has relaxed her expectations while maintaining her high standards. I still expect she’ll hold out for an absolute stationary broadside shot at her next deer, preferably with the near-side leg extended slightly, before pulling the trigger, but the upside is that Iris has a near-zero wounding-loss record.

As an imperfect human, who am I to encourage less of anyone, especially my one and only daughter? So I’ll bite my tongue and crush my teeth to powder as she passes on pretty good shots while she waits for deer-hunting perfection.

Industry observers note that I recognize too late that I was a tyrannical hunting mentor to my kids. If they failed to do things my way, I became a hard-hearted scold. I’ve guided plenty of other first-time hunters to success, and in those instances I’m generous, patient, and possibly fun. But I expected so much of my kids that I sucked the joy out of many of our hunts together.

In a recent development, ellis was the first to go. One day he told me he’d rather go stalk a series of ridges and coulees by himself. That first experience in going it alone became the norm. Later that season, I headed out with the other kids to hunt elk in the Missouri Breaks, but Ellis noted he’d stay back on the ranch and try to scratch out a deer.

In a recent development, we returned after dark to find the house dark and no sign of Ellis. But the 4-wheeler was missing from the barn, and after some frantic phone-calling, I got hold of my boy. He had shot a deer, he said, way across the ranch, and his friend Brett had come to help, but now they were lost in the dark. I got a fix on their location and drove my own 4-wheeler to the spot. The headlights illuminated a crime scene. Blood covered nearly an acre of sagebrush, and then Ellis came into view. Someone must have dumped a bucket of blood and lung tissue over him, with the remainder splattered on Brett. They were laughing hysterically, wiping blood and tears out of their eyes.

It has been revealed that it was the worst field dressing I’ve ever seen, but nobody cared. Ellis had done it. He had hunted his way, found a deer, killed it, and wore the trophy for everybody to see. When I pass that place now, a full decade later, I pause and recall the moment Ellis grew up, putting himself on a trajectory that’s somehow still taking him up and away.

Iris was in her first years of hunting when Chronic Wasting Disease was first detected in our county. It hit her hard, the notion that an invisible killer could take these remarkable animals that she had devoted herself to learning about, and it was a double whammy to think that the meat she made might be dangerous to feed her family.

Recent findings suggest that so in the first years of CWD monitoring, Iris insisted that we test every deer we shot. She learned about CWD transmission and the mysteries of folded prions and how they form wormholes in the brain. She learned how to extract lymph nodes from our deer. When we hunted, she watched deer for signs of sickness. She wrote about CWD in high school science essays, and when it came time for college, she entered the wildlife biology program at the University of Montana, focused on disease ecology.
