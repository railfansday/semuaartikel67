Jon Bernthal’s 6-Episode Noir Crime Thriller Deserved a Better Fate

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The classic noir detective comes with three staples: a gun, a smoking habit, and a three-piece suit. While many actors have taken on the role, few embody it quite like Jon Bernthal in TNT’s Mob City. Based on John Buntin’s book L.A. Noir: The Struggle for the Soul of America’s Most Seductive City, the series captures the murky criminal underworld of 1947 Los Angeles, where a fictionalized version of notorious gangster Mickey Cohen rules the streets.

Bernthal stars as LAPD Detective Joe Teague, who finds himself caught in the growing battle between Cohen’s criminal empire and the LAPD, with cops, gangsters, fixers, and other shady figures caught in the crossfire. Although plenty of crime noirs have graced the screen, Mob City remains an underrated, short-lived gem that could have benefited from more seasons, with Bernthal doing what he does best: playing morally ambiguous characters.

It has been revealed that mob City takes place in 1947 Los Angeles, where gangsters Mickey Cohen (Jeremy Luke), Ben “Bugsy” Siegel (Edward Burns), and Sid Rothman (Robert Knepper) rule the city. Detective Joe is assigned to a special LAPD unit working to bring down the mafia once and for all. However, Joe has an agenda of his own, which begins to unfold when he crosses paths with Hecky Nash (Simon Pegg), a comedian with ties to Cohen.

In a recent development, one thing leads to another, and Joe finds himself committing a crime that could land him behind bars. He turns to his mafia-connected acquaintance, Ned Stax (Milo Ventimiglia), for help covering it up, but his actions don't go unnoticed by the mob. With both Cohen and LAPD Captain William Parker (Neal McDonough) watching his every move, Joe must walk a dangerous line between the two sides of one of the bloodiest gang wars to hit the streets of L.A.

Sources indicate that mob City has plenty going for it, starting with showrunner Frank Darabont behind the series. Like his previous project, The Walking Dead (which Bernthal also stars in), Mob City is just as invested in its characters as it is in its thriller elements. Joe isn't the only one guilty of being complicit, as neither the police nor the mob get an entirely clean image. With everyone having their own hidden agenda, audiences are left questioning whether there's such a thing as true righteousness in the City of Angels. Still, despite its heavy, philosophically nuanced tone, Mob City balances things out with the ensemble's sharp dialogue and the signature snappiness you'd expect from a noir series.

Sources indicate that visually, Mob City does a fantastic job recreating 1940s Los Angeles at its seediest. There's the hazy glamour the metropolis is notorious for, from smoky jazz bars and gambling dens to smaller details, like neon signs barely illuminating the streets. These touches give the series a cinematic quality that's rare for network TV. However, beneath all that glitz is a criminal underworld where violence can erupt without warning. Mobsters aren't afraid to pull out their guns and start shooting, whether they're in an empty park or a crowded restaurant. This contrast between LA's glamorous nightlife and its ever-present threat of violence makes Mob City such an atmospheric throwback to classic noir.

Mob City makes it clear what kind of detective Joe is from the beginning, describing him as a “gray man” in a world where good and bad are supposed to be as rigid as black and white. While most mob thrillers traditionally follow a “who's the mole?” plot, audiences already know there's a mole in the LAPD from the beginning — Joe himself. So, it's not a matter of finding out who the bad guy is. Instead, Mob City is more interested in exploring why Joe is so intent on breaking the rules and just how far he's willing to go to protect his dirty little secret.

In typical mafia stories, detectives in Joe's position are often motivated by monetary incentives in exchange for their cooperation. However, as Mob City progresses, audiences may be surprised to discover the real reason Joe is willing to compromise his badge and moral boundaries, which are well worth sticking around for. At just six episodes, Mob City is an easy binge compared to other detective thrillers. Unfortunately, TNT cancelled the show after its first season due to low viewership, leaving its cliffhanger ending unresolved. Considering its cast, production value, and potential to explore this fascinating yet very real chapter of L.A. history, Mob City deserved a much longer run.
