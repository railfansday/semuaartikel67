Tech Report: A 5th-century boat burial held a carved alder chair, and its owner carried a leprosy branch new to northern Europe

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

DNA from a 5th-century boat burial at Fallward, Germany, points to a man of about 50 with Danube roots and a leprosy branch latest to northern Europe.

Between 1995 and 1998, the archaeology service of Germany’s Cuxhaven district excavated a boat burial at Fallward, near the North Sea coast. In grave 265, a man of about 50 lay in an oak boat more than 13 feet (4 meters) long, with a carved chair outside it.

Recent findings suggest that his skeleton then went into storage. Nearly three decades later, a genetics lab in Kiel sampled his heel bone and a molar. It found leprosy bacteria, plus DNA that points to the Danube region.

The strain sits on a branch first known from an Egyptian mummy and had not been found in central or northern Europe before. His exact homeland is still not pinned down, the researchers say.

Recent findings suggest that fallward was a village on a terp, an earth mound raised on a beach ridge to keep homes above storm floods. There were no dikes yet. The study lists it among eight mound villages between the Elbe and the Weser founded in the 1st century BCE.

It has been revealed that the village was probably abandoned by the end of the 5th century CE. Its cemetery was used from the late 3rd century onward and held about 200 cremations and 60 burials of bodies. Some pits went 6.5 feet (2 meters) down into old marsh clay.

Industry observers note that the wet clay kept wood, furs, cloth and plant mats from rotting. Several graves were lifted as whole blocks and frozen. A German Research Foundation project at the Lower Saxony Institute for Historical Coastal Research (NIhK) in Wilhelmshaven is now opening them in the lab.

The man died in the second quarter of the 5th century. Wooden panels closed his boat like a roof. Inside, he lay on a reed mat, and fish bones turned up in the boat with him.

In a recent development, the chair outside the boat was cut out of one alder trunk, with notched patterns, and was probably painted when upcoming. The NIhK calls it the Throne from the Marsch.

A maple footstool lay by his feet, with a runic inscription and a hunting scene on its underside. Rolf Kiepe of the NIhK photographed both pieces.

According to recent reports, diggers also found a game board, a bronze basin, a clay pot and a little box in the shape of a water bird. A late Roman military belt lay in the grave too.

Many of these things show wear and old repairs. The authors think they belonged to him or his family and were not made for the grave.

Hauke Jöns of the NIhK said the belt had already suggested that the man “had had connections across great distances.” The wood came from local trees, but the decoration indicates the influence of late Roman art.

In Kiel, the Ancient DNA Working Group of the Institute of Clinical Molecular Biology (IKMB) got 12 bone samples from 11 people buried at Fallward. It read the DNA on a high-throughput sequencing machine. Seven males and one female could be identified.

Three people had enough DNA to compare with other ancient groups. The adult man in grave 14 and a 3-year-old girl in grave 250 matched northwestern Europe, closest to Anglo-Saxons in England. Their relatives may have joined the North Sea crossings of the migrations of the first millennium AD.

Industry observers note that the man in grave 265 grouped elsewhere. His DNA fell among Roman-era people from the Danube frontier in present-day Croatia, Slovenia, Hungary and Serbia.

A mapping program called mobest placed the most likely location of genetic similarity more than 870 miles (1,400 kilometers) southeast of Fallward, near the old Roman border.

His Y chromosome, passed from father to son, belongs to a lineage called haplogroup G, which today is most common in the Caucasus.

It has been revealed that A scan for germs found Mycobacterium leprae, the bacterium that causes leprosy, in both the tooth and the heel bone. There was more bacterial DNA than human DNA. The team read over 99 percent of the bacterial genome at least four times.

Lead author Daniel Myburgh, a doctoral researcher at the IKMB, and his colleagues built a family tree of 44 ancient and 185 modern leprosy genomes.

The Fallward strain landed near the root of branch 4. Only one genome on that branch is older, from a mummy at Abusir in Egypt dated to the 4th to 2nd centuries BCE.

Myburgh noted the man “is the first individual from this group to have been identified in Europe.” Earlier work on medieval graves tracked other strains, such as one from central or western Asia in a pilgrim’s skeleton found by Winchester researchers.

Recent findings suggest that according to the World Health Organization, leprosy mainly harms the skin and the nerves. The Fallward man’s bones show no leprosy damage.

Either he had no symptoms or the infection had only reached soft tissue. Almut Nebel, a professor at the IKMB, mentioned, “He may have travelled long distances whilst carrying the disease.”.

The authors give more than one possible life story. He may have been a foederatus, a hired soldier who served in the Roman army.

Ben Krause-Kyora, who leads the study at the IKMB, remarked the man could have been a mercenary who later “settled between the Elbe and the Weser,” or a long-distance trader.

The paper adds that the belt does not necessarily mean army service. Such belts were also traded, inherited or taken as war booty. Roman soldiers carried habits and symbols across provinces, as a study on what held Roman soldiers together found.

In a recent development, his bones suggest a third option. His upper arm muscles were very well developed. Together with the boat, the reed mat and the fish bones, that could point to a boatman or a fisherman.

In a recent development, most of the human DNA was in bad shape. The three usable genomes covered only 32,000 to 67,000 of the roughly 1.24 million genetic markers that ancient DNA labs usually check. The man’s sample also showed less ancient damage than the girl’s.

Recent findings suggest that other statistical ancestry models could not set him apart from the two local people. The authors write that his origin needs caution and that three people do not speak for all of Fallward.

Sources indicate that two children in graves 224 and 282 read as boys in the DNA, though they were buried with brooches and beads. Their data were too weak to be sure.

In a recent development, the raw sequences sit in the European Nucleotide Archive. The ROOTS Cluster of Excellence paid for the work, Kiel University mentioned on November 3, 2026.

According to recent reports, many Fallward grave goods ended up in museum cases while the bones stayed in storage. A sample from one heel now links the man with the alder chair to the Danube frontier and to a leprosy branch whose earliest known member comes from Egypt.

The full study was published in the journal iScience.
