Astronauts could turn everyday trash and moon dust into something surprisingly useful

Researchers have combined paper fibers and simulated lunar dust to develop lightweight insulation that could be manufactured on the moon.

Trash on the International Space Station is bagged by astronauts and loaded onto a cargo ship.

The ship then either returns it to Earth or burns up in the atmosphere. On the moon, part of that waste might be put to use as insulation.

Paper fibers have now been ground together with imitation moon dust by a team of chemists.

The mixture was dried into an aerogel, an ultralight solid made mostly of empty space. Very little heat passes through such a material.

Recent findings suggest that according to the results, some insulation for a lunar base could be made on site. Two ingredients would be needed, and both should be plentiful there: waste paper and the ground outside.

In a recent development, the recipe was developed by Alexander Wei, a chemistry professor at Purdue University, and graduate researcher Jacob Staker. Their colleagues included researchers at NASA’s Glenn Research Center in Cleveland.

Wei mentioned a lunar settlement will generate waste rich in cellulose, the plant fiber found in paper and cardboard packaging.

“And I can guarantee that astronauts on the moon will never run out of moon dust,” Wei added.

For now, the aerogels exist only as samples made on Earth. A manufactured stand-in was used for lunar soil, and the paper was clean laboratory filter paper.

The final drying step relied on a method the team considers less practical on the moon than freeze-drying.

In a recent development, between 60% and 70% of wastepaper by weight is cellulose. When cellulose is torn into much finer threads, it can strengthen a material in ways the original fibers cannot.

It has been revealed that A stand-in for lunar soil, or regolith, has already been mixed with recycled plastic by other engineers. Wei’s team paired the dust with paper instead and used it as a grinding aid.

In a recent development, filter paper was first shredded in a food blender. The team then loaded it into a mill together with water, ceramic cylinders, and a powder called Lunar Highlands Simulant.

This powder is made from Earth rock to match soil from the moon’s highlands.

For two hours, the jars were spun at an acceleration about 60 times that of Earth’s gravity.

The team expects a mill of this kind to work in the moon’s weak gravity.

Industry observers note that the paper fibers were thinned by the ceramic cylinders alone. Once dust was added, they became much thinner still.

The most common fiber width fell to about 4 millionths of an inch (0.1 micrometers).

According to recent reports, fibers milled with dust were eight times thinner than those milled without it. They were also at least 30 times thinner than the original paper fibers.

The milled mixture was frozen in small molds, and its water was replaced with alcohol.

The team then removed the alcohol with pressurized carbon dioxide, which is a standard way of drying aerogels.

“Imagine an aerogel as a high-tech thermos for a lunar base,” Staker said. “Most of it consists of empty space that insulates the interior, helping it maintain the temperature of what’s inside.”.

Empty space accounted for 79.3% to 98.6% of the volume in these samples.

It has been revealed that in total, 18 batches were made, and the recipe was varied between them. Cellulose made up 1% to 9% of the wet mixture by weight. Dust made up 25% to 75% of the finished aerogel.

Insulation is rated by its thermal conductivity, which describes how easily heat passes through a material. A lower value is better.

The batches scored between 30 and 45 milliwatts per meter-kelvin when measured in air.

According to recent reports, an earlier attempt had produced a freeze-dried aerogel of imitation regolith and natural polymers. Its lowest reported figure was 70 in the same units.

The best fresh batches therefore had less than half the thermal conductivity of the earlier material.

The best insulation came from the batches with the least cellulose. Among the 18, the lightest batch was made from a mixture with 1% cellulose. Its conductivity of 30.9 was the lowest in that group.

When exposed to the moon’s near-vacuum environment, the pores would contain virtually no gas.

The team expects the aerogels to insulate better under those conditions. Several times less heat is conducted by other aerospace aerogels in a near-vacuum than in air.

Temperatures on the lunar surface can reach 266°F (130°C). That heat is a problem for many of the organic materials used in aerogels.

A sample heated in nitrogen did not start to break down until about 653°F (345°C).

Insulation and stiffness responded to the recipe in opposite ways. Heat was generally blocked best by the low-cellulose batches.

However, those batches were also soft, and they shrank the most during drying.

It has been revealed that A batch made with 1% cellulose came out 52% narrower than its mold. Only 3.2% shrinkage was seen in a batch made with 9% cellulose.

In a recent development, the stiffest batch was also made with 9% cellulose. Under compression, it was nearly 300 times stiffer than the softest batch.

“Rather than trying to make the world’s best aerogel, we’re looking for the sweet spot where we can get the properties we need with the limited resources we have available,” Wei said.

To look for that balance, the team fitted a statistical model to the 18 batches. Three recipes suggested by the model were then made and tested.

Dust would be far more plentiful on the moon than waste paper. For that reason, one recipe was required to be at least 60% dust by weight.

A figure of 61.2% was suggested by the model. The resulting aerogel had a conductivity of 30.5, which was the lowest in the study.

The same aerogel shrank by about half. It also did not meet the standards for the strength test.

Sources indicate that A second recipe was balanced across six measured properties. It insulated nearly as well, at 30.8, and shrank by 18.5%. This aerogel was stiff enough to be tested, but only 25% of it was dust.

The aerogels have not yet been made the way a lunar crew would make them. The team wrote that freeze-drying would be far more practical on the moon than the carbon dioxide method.

According to Wei, the lunar surface offers a natural vacuum and subfreezing temperatures at night. Wei remarked the water removed from the aerogel could also be recovered and reused.

In a recent development, however, this kind of material can be damaged by freezing. Growing ice crystals pack the fibers and dust together.

That packing is known to lower the quality of cellulose aerogels. To slow the growth of the crystals, the team proposed adding simple alcohols or sugars.

Recent findings suggest that only one kind of imitation dust was used, and the paper was not real trash. Every heat-flow measurement was taken at 73°F (23°C) in air.

For the weakest recipes, additives known to strengthen cellulose aerogels should be tested, the team suggested.

Sources indicate that freeze-drying is now being studied by the team for its next batches. Those tests should show whether a freeze-dried aerogel can insulate as well as the samples made so far.

The full study was published in the journal ACS Sustainable Chemistry & Engineering.
