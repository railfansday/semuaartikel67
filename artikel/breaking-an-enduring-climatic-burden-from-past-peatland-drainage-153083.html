Breaking: An enduring climatic burden from past peatland drainage

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Over millennia, peatlands have removed hundreds of gigatonnes of carbon dioxide (CO2) from the atmosphere but emitted a proportion back as methane (CH4), exerting a slow net cooling effect1. Artificial drainage of these ecosystems—a centuries-old practice2,3—causes decomposition and the re-release of CO2 but suppresses CH4 emissions4, with a net climatic effect that is not well quantified at the global scale5. Here we reconstructed the radiative forcing from worldwide peatland drainage over 1800–2020 by combining a new flux dataset, hydrologically driven models of CO2 and CH4 fluxes and a hindcast of drainage extent and intensity. We found that drainage has produced a net heating effect of +73 (48–99) mW m−2—equivalent to 2.7 (1.8–3.6)% of industrial-era radiative forcing—originating from 0.4–0.5% of global land area. The net heating results from the dominant forcing of drainage-induced CO2 emissions (+83 (52–110) mW m−2) over that of avoided CH4 emissions (−9 (−15–−2) mW m−2). Even without further expansion of drainage, maintaining right now drained areas could almost double the cumulative warming by 2100. Our reconstruction captures the opposing impacts of peatland drainage on global budgets of CO2 and CH4 and quantifies the strong legacy effects of historical drainage on present and possibly future climate.
