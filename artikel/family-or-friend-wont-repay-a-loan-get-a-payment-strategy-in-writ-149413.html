Family or Friend Won’t Repay a Loan? Get a Payment Strategy in Writing First

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

If a relative or friend has stopped repaying money you lent them, ask privately for a payment plan they can afford, with a set amount and start date. Confirm it in a text they answer. Save a demand letter or small claims court for a broken plan, or forgive the rest and say so.

Without a set payment or due date, repaying a loan is easy to put off, and every month of silence makes the subject harder to raise.

So skip the hints and make one specific request. Call or meet in private. A text is easy to ignore, and with others listening, embarrassment can turn into an argument. Propose an amount and a schedule they can actually meet. A sister owed $2,400 might ask her brother for $100 a month starting next payday, which clears the debt in two years.

In a recent development, afterward, text them a short summary of the plan and ask them to confirm it. Their reply is a written record that they owe the money and agreed to repay it, which matters if the plan falls apart.

In a recent development, if payments don’t start, or start and then stop, put the request in writing. Send a short letter or email that states what they owe, the payments they missed, a deadline to pay, and that you’ll file in small claims court if they don’t. Keep a copy. California’s courts require you to ask for the money before you file, and your copy reveals the judge you did.

Once the deadline passes, file in small claims court. It takes claims up to a cap your state sets, from $2,500 in Kentucky to $25,000 in Delaware and Tennessee. Filing is cheap. California’s fee for a $2,400 claim is $50. For the hearing, bring the bank or payment app record of the transfer and every message in which they agreed to repay you.

Recent findings suggest that suing a relative or friend can cost you the relationship, win or lose. When the relationship matters more than the money, forgive the rest on purpose and tell them it’s now a gift, which settles the debt for both of you. Then stop lending to that person. For anyone else, lend only money you could afford to give away.
