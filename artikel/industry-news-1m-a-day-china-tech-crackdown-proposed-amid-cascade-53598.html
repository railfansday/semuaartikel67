FIRST ON FOX — Rep. Pat Harrigan, R-N.C., wants to close a glaring national-security loophole that allows Chinese entities to cohabitate the same data centers where critical American services also run.

The North Carolina lawmaker is proposing the Digital Infrastructure Act of 2026 to address the potential risk with a $1 million-per-day fine for violators. Meanwhile, the U.S. has experienced a series of outages and incidents this week that have put a microscope on the potential for foreign cyber threats and have prompted skepticism regarding U.S. readiness to defend against digital weaknesses.

"Look, the federal government already determined that companies like China Telecom and China Unicom posed a threat to our national security and revoked their licenses to operate here," Harrigan stated in an interview with Fox News Digital.

According to recent reports, "But those companies were still able to maintain connections inside of American data centers. So, if we think about that, we determined that they were too dangerous to operate here, but we left them with a foothold in the very infrastructure that carries Americans' information.".

Recent findings suggest that despite the cascade of outages — including in U.S. air traffic control systems, Microsoft and banking services —Harrigan did not directly draw a line between Chinese companies and the disrupted services. There is also no proof that any of the incidents were the result of cyberattacks.

Harrigan, instead, argued that preventing cyber vulnerabilities in the future requires lawmakers to eliminate what they know to be weaknesses now.

"What we're talking about is the infrastructure that supports our banks, our hospitals, our businesses and our communication systems," Harrigan said.

He declined to elaborate on whether the existing loophole has already led to cyberattacks or stolen data but noted that he was made aware of the liability when American businesses approached him with concerns.

In a recent development, his bill would prohibit high-risk carriers from owning or operating a data center in the U.S. or offering cloud services to Americans. The measure would be enforced by a $1 million fine for every day of a continuing violation — a penalty that would extend to affiliates and even American venture partners doing business with Chinese entities.

The bill also includes a time-limited exception for companies approved by American communications officials.

"We talked to a lot of companies, frontier labs and data center owners, as well as operators that are concerned about this," Harrigan noted. "And it's also part of folks from our national security apparatus within government.".

It has been revealed that as part of its mission, the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) reviews foreign communication companies and green-lights their operation in the U.S. When identifying a security risk, the FCC issues a denial of application, a power granted to it under Section 214 of the Communications Act of 1934.

Recent findings suggest that in China’s case, companies like China Telecom Corporation, China Unicom Operations Limited and Pacific Networks Corp. have all failed to secure FCC authorization for security reasons.

"The Commission finds that due to several factors related to China Mobile USA’s ownership and control by the Chinese government, grant of the application would raise substantial and serious national security and law enforcement risks," the FCC wrote in its evaluation of China Mobile USA, a Delaware-based company.

Even so, the FCC order does not address what the company needs to do with its hardware.

"A Section 214 order is a license to sell regulated telecom service, not a physical eviction order," Harrigan’s office noted.

Asked if he believes Republicans will be able to address the legislation before the end of the 119th Congress, Harrigan remarked he believes the issue will likely have to be left to the next session, which begins in January.
