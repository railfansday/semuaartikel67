In a recent development, WWII RECORDS RECOVERED. Germany has agreed to restitute an archive documenting Nazi persecution of Jehovah’s Witnesses, after the latter sued to recover it from Dresden’s Military History Museum, reports The Newly introduced York Times. An amicable settlement finalized on November 20 concludes a years-long dispute about the rightful ownership of 31 documents and files related to the arrest and murder of the Kusserow family, who were Jehovah’s Witnesses. The archive in question “represents an invaluable part of the Witnesses’ cultural heritage and shines a light on the often overlooked abuses the group faced during World War II,” the Kusserow family stated. IN MEMORIAM. The artist and curator Tamlin Blake was killed in her home in Riebeek West, north of Cape Town, South Africa, and a suspect has been arrested, reported local media 2 Oceans Vibe. Blake’s body was found on November 22. “The attacker took several items before [he] fled,” remarked a spokesperson for the Western Cape regional police. The victim’s smartphone tracking device helped locate her stolen belongings, among items thought to have been worn by the attacker. The locally celebrated artist was also chief curator at Spier Arts Trust, a Cape Town-based organization that supports artists, helping them find opportunities to earn income, exhibit, and develop their practice, according to the website. Blake used textiles, found materials, and other mixed media, most recently consisting of tapestries woven from recycled hand-spun newspaper. “A prominent aspect of Blake’s work is her fascination with materials and manipulating new mediums in ways which take them past the merely decorative or prosaic. She was instrumental in developing beading techniques which are now used by various South African artists,” states her website.

FOOD FOR THOUGHT. About fifteen years ago, when Laila Gohar started making massive sculptures out of butter, she was something of an outlier, though the surrealists, Salvador Dalí in particular, had long looked at food as a playful window into the absurdity of daily life. In the Middle Ages, feasting royals were also known to host bacchanalian demonstrations of edible bounty turned into veritable sculptures. Now, according to The Guardian, Gohar is part of a trending wave of artists who use food as both main course and main medium. “I am interested in the rituals around eating and gathering, and in the way an ordinary thing can become something strange or theatrical when you change its scale, material or context,” said Gohar. “Food is one of the materials I work with, but I don’t really think of what I do as being separated into food, art, or design. They all feed into each other.”.
