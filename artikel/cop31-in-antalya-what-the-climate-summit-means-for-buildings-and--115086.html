Industry observers note that the 31st United Nations Climate Change Conference (COP31) concluded on November 22, 2026, in Antalya, Türkiye, following negotiations that extended beyond the scheduled closing date. Hosted by Türkiye with Australia leading the negotiations, the summit brought together representatives of nearly 200 Parties, alongside researchers, businesses, climate organizations, and civil society, to advance international climate action. While countries reached decisions on climate finance, adaptation, just transition, and other agenda items, they failed to agree on the proposed overarching Antalya Pact, with disagreements over fossil fuels, finance, and the level of ambition in the final text. For the built environment, COP31 advanced several initiatives addressing building energy performance, electrification, urban resilience, and climate investment, while leaving broader questions of implementation and international coordination unresolved.

The Antalya Compact for Energy Efficient Buildings.

One of COP31's most direct initiatives for the building sector was the Antalya Compact for Energy Efficient Buildings, debuted by the Turkish Presidency on October 29 in collaboration with the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP)-hosted Global Alliance for Buildings and Construction (GlobalABC). The voluntary framework supports two of the Presidency's 2035 objectives: pursuing a 25% reduction in the operational energy intensity of the global building stock compared with 2020 levels, and accelerating efficient electrification in buildings in support of the 35-by-35 target, which seeks to increase electricity's share of global final energy consumption to 35% by 2035. At the second ministerial meeting of the Intergovernmental Council for Buildings and Climate (ICBC), held during COP31, 24 countries, the European Commission, and 38 non-Party stakeholders expressed support for or endorsed the Compact.

Industry observers note that the framework identifies six areas for action, including integrating energy efficiency and electrification into national strategies, strengthening building energy and carbon performance, accelerating renovation of existing buildings, reducing energy demand, improving buildings' flexibility within energy systems, and mobilizing finance and technical capacity. Its proposed measures range from stronger building energy codes and renovation strategies to electrified heating and cooling, energy management, passive cooling, and investment in public buildings and social infrastructure. While the Compact does not establish binding targets for individual countries, it provides a shared framework for governments and industry to advance building decarbonization, linking operational energy performance with renovation, affordability, and long-term resilience.

Climate-Resilient Cities: Housing, Infrastructure, and Local Implementation.

Cities and urban resilience were also prominent at COP31, with the Turkish Presidency and UN-Habitat convening a High-Level Dialogue on Urban Resilience, Adaptation and Climate Action on November 12. The discussions addressed how national climate plans and adaptation strategies can be translated into investments at the local level, with housing, infrastructure, urban regeneration, informal settlement upgrading, and post-disaster reconstruction identified as areas requiring stronger implementation. Participants emphasized the importance of connecting national climate commitments with local development priorities while strengthening coordination between governments, municipalities, financial institutions, and development partners. The dialogue also highlighted the financing gap facing cities, with climate-resilient infrastructure and services requiring investment beyond current levels.

Financing Urban Resilience Through the Sustainable Urban Resilience Financing Facility.

According to recent reports, alongside discussions on climate adaptation, COP31 highlighted the Sustainable Urban Resilience Financing Facility (SURFF), an initiative developed through Türkiye's İLBANK in partnership with UN-Habitat and the World Bank. Introduced ahead of the summit during Newly introduced York Climate Week, the initiative aims to help cities and countries translate climate commitments into investment-ready urban resilience projects, addressing the gap between national climate strategies and implementation at the municipal level. It is structured around three pillars: country-led Resilient Cities Platforms, a Resilient Cities Centre of Excellence, and an international dialogue intended to facilitate cooperation, share experience, and improve access to climate finance.

Fossil Fuels and Electrification: An Unresolved Transition.

Recent findings suggest that the transition away from fossil fuels remained one of the most contested concerns at COP31. Countries were divided over whether the final political text should include an explicit commitment to move away from coal, oil, and gas. The proposed Antalya Pact did not include a clear reference to fossil fuels, prompting objections from the European Union, the United Kingdom, and several climate-vulnerable nations, which argued that the text did not adequately address the primary drivers of climate change. Disagreements over the language of the energy transition, climate finance, and the level of ambition required prevented the draft from reaching consensus. The outcome leaves the commitment made at COP28 to transition away from fossil fuels in energy systems without a new collective roadmap at COP31.

In a recent development, outside the formal negotiations, efforts to advance electrification continued through voluntary initiatives. Around 80 countries supported the Presidency's 35-by-35 Global Electrification Pledge, which seeks to increase electricity's share of global final energy consumption to 35% by 2035. For the built environment, electrification connects the transition to buildings through heating, cooling, cooking, and energy systems, while energy efficiency and passive design strategies can reduce demand. The Antalya Compact similarly addresses building energy use through renovation, efficient systems, and measures to reduce operational demand. However, electrification targets and building efficiency initiatives do not replace a negotiated global commitment to phase out fossil fuels, leaving the pace of transition dependent on national policies, energy infrastructure, investment, and further international cooperation.
