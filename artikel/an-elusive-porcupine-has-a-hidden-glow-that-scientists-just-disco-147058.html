An elusive porcupine has a hidden glow that scientists just discovered

Researchers discovered that Mexican hairy dwarf porcupines glow under ultraviolet light, making them easier to spot at night.

The Mexican hairy dwarf porcupine is difficult to see in a tree even during the day. When ultraviolet light is shone on one at night, however, its quills glow a blue-green color.

Recent findings suggest that this glow was observed on every live porcupine examined by a research team in southeastern Mexico. The researchers also used it to locate the animals in the treetops after dark.

Living animals also glowed far more brightly than old skins kept in a museum.

The team wrote that “no photoluminescence observations have ever been made in live individuals of the Mexican hairy dwarf porcupine.”.

The study was carried out at El Colegio de la Frontera Sur (ECOSUR) in Chetumal, Quintana Roo.

Since 2023 the institution has helped environmental authorities to rescue and examine porcupines living in the city.

Sources indicate that the Mexican hairy dwarf porcupine is a small spiny rodent that lives alone and is active at night. It spends most of its life in trees.

Recent findings suggest that the species belongs to the same family as the larger North American porcupine. It is found from central Mexico to Panama, but little is known about its basic habits.

It has been revealed that the first observation was made in June 2024 by senior author Jonathan Pérez-Flores. He was examining two females when he pointed a small ultraviolet flashlight at them and their quills lit up.

It has been revealed that ultraviolet light lies just beyond violet at the edge of human vision. Some materials absorb this light and release it again as a color that people can see.

Sources indicate that the effect is known as photoluminescence. A similar thing happens when a white T-shirt is placed under a blacklight.

The flashlight used by the team also produced some visible purple light. For that reason only other colors such as green, blue and yellow were treated as a true glow.

In a recent development, twenty adult porcupines were examined over the following three months. Seventeen of these animals were female and three were male.

It has been revealed that each porcupine was sedated for a health check. While it recovered in a quiet and dark place the light was held about a foot (30 centimeters) away.

Most of the photographs were taken with ordinary mobile phones.

According to recent reports, all 20 animals glowed and this was true for both males and females. The head was the brightest region and showed a greenish-blue color.

The head also carries more quills than any other part of the body. The back, the legs and the tail showed the same greenish-blue wherever quills were present.

On the belly the glow varied in strength, which the team linked to the wider spacing of its quills.

According to recent reports, freshly collected quills told a more detailed story. Each one highlighted green where it looks yellow in daylight and blue at its brown tip.

Patches of skin without hair or quills did nothing more than reflect the purple of the flashlight. Neither the hair nor the claws glowed in any of the 20 animals.

Recent findings suggest that the most unusual result came from the hands and feet of the porcupines. The palms and the soles of the feet produced a bright yellow glow that was visible from a distance.

Industry observers note that “To the best of our knowledge, this phenomenon has not been reported in any other species of mammal,” the authors wrote. A 2023 catalog of 125 glowing mammals contains no such pattern, they noted.

Glowing has been described in a growing number of mammals, including domestic cats, pocket gophers and North American bats.

The source of the yellow glow on the palms and soles has not been identified.

The researchers suspect that pigments or proteins in that skin are involved but these were not tested in the study.

Recent findings suggest that two preserved porcupines from the ECOSUR museum collection were examined in the same way for comparison. Both specimens had been prepared more than 10 years earlier without the use of fluorescent chemicals.

In ordinary light the quills of these specimens had a pale cream color. Under ultraviolet light they gave off a greenish glow so faint that it could barely be seen.

Industry observers note that no glow at all was observed on the skin or fur of the museum specimens. The palms and soles that had shone so brightly on the living animals also remained dark.

The authors suspect that the age of the specimens explains much of the gap. Keratin, the main material in quills as well as in human hair and nails, degrades slowly as the years pass.

Light could have made matters worse, since it destroys glowing molecules in keratin through a process called photobleaching.

This finding is relevant because the majority of studies on glowing mammals have relied on museum specimens.

Porcupines have been included in only a few of these studies and observations of live animals had been anecdotal.

The authors argued that “it is essential to conduct this type of research on both living and dead organisms to observe any differences.”.

The authors cited a 2022 study that found green-glowing quills on museum skins of African porcupines but not American ones.

The researchers next wanted to know whether the glow had practical value, since canopy animals are hard to census.

Industry observers note that for the test they chose trails on the ECOSUR campus where porcupines had been seen before. The route was walked twice a month from July to December 2024.

Industry observers note that every walk began no earlier than 7 p.m. and ended by midnight along roughly 0.6 mile (1 kilometer) of trail. At 10 stops spaced about 330 feet (100 meters) apart, the canopy was searched for 15 minutes.

Trees along the route stood 39 to 49 feet (12 to 15 meters) tall. Even so, the quills and soles stood out against leaves, bark and fungi.

Over the 12 walks, porcupines were detected 20 times, or roughly 1.7 sightings per route.

This porcupine is docile and can stay still for several minutes, so the animals found were photographed and filmed.

The authors wrote that “no previous study had used this method for locating arboreal mammals during nighttime surveys.”.

It has been revealed that the survey method does have an crucial limitation. Males and females of this species cannot easily be distinguished and individual animals lack any markings that would identify them.

For this reason the 20 sightings may not represent 20 different porcupines. The same animal could have been recorded on more than one night.

In a recent development, the photographs were also taken in a range of settings and mostly with mobile phones. Only three of the 20 sedated porcupines were male.

Industry observers note that the function of glowing in mammals remains a matter of debate among scientists. Some researchers consider it a simple by-product of the pigments or structure of fur and skin.

Sources indicate that others prefer a checklist of five criteria drawn up by earlier researchers. Under it the glowing material should sit somewhere visible.

The animal should also live in conditions where the glow would show.

Sources indicate that A third criterion asks for visual behaviors that might rely on the pattern. The remaining two deal with the wavelengths involved and with whether any onlooker has eyes sensitive to them.

In the porcupine’s case the authors believe the first three have been met. A related species, Coendou spinosus, presents its back and vibrates its quills when threatened. A glow could add to that display.

“Photoluminescent structures may facilitate perception and communication among individuals of this species during the night,” the authors wrote.

Industry observers note that for the other two criteria the evidence is circumstantial. Many rodents may see ultraviolet light. Nobody has checked this porcupine for the light-sensing proteins, called opsins, that this would need.

Future research could test whether porcupines use the glow to find one another in the dark.

The flashlight used in the study emitted light at 395 nanometers and this appears strongly violet.

The team recommends that surveys also test ultraviolet light at a shorter wavelength of 365 nanometers, since some animals glow differently under each wavelength.

The study reveals that a simple flashlight can help scientists find an animal that is otherwise very difficult to observe.

The full study was published in the journal Neotropical Biology and Conservation.
