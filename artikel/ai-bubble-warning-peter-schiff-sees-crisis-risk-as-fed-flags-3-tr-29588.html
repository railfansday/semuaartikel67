In a recent development, the AI bubble debate is getting harder to dismiss as the Federal Reserve highlights more than $3 trillion in potential financial commitments tied to the advanced-computing buildout. Peter Schiff believes the boom could end in a crisis worse than 2008. But the Fed’s findings reveal a more complicated risk beneath the technology rally.

It has been revealed that in a November 17 warning, Peter Schiff compared the artificial intelligence boom with the subprime mortgage market, arguing that easy monetary conditions have helped inflate asset valuations. His prediction is severe, but the financing figures now emerging from the Federal Reserve give investors a reason to examine the risks more closely.

The Federal Reserve’s November 13 Financial Stability Report demonstrates how rapidly financing for advanced computing has expanded.

Recent findings suggest that debt issuance linked to advanced computing infrastructure exceeded $680 billion by October 2026, roughly five times the $130 billion recorded in 2023. It also represented around 31% of nonfinancial investment-grade corporate debt issuance in 2026, compared with less than 6% three years earlier.

Outstanding public debt connected to advanced computing exceeded $1.1 trillion.

Those figures do not prove an AI bubble is about to burst. They do show how much capital is flowing into the infrastructure supporting the technology boom.

The more striking figure involves obligations beyond conventional borrowing.

Sources indicate that the Fed’s report describes more than $1 trillion in undiscounted values for leases that have not yet commenced. It also identifies more than $2 trillion in other obligations, including take-or-pay contracts and residual-value guarantees.

In a recent development, that distinction matters. These commitments should not be described as $3 trillion in outstanding loans or realized losses. They represent future financial obligations and guarantees that could become more difficult to manage if demand or expected returns disappoint.

Federal Reserve Governor Michael Barr also discussed the potential scale of AI-related leases, contracts and guarantees in a November 16 speech, putting their maximum nominal value above $3 trillion.

Another concern is the growing financial connection between major technology companies and AI startups.

Large technology firms and chipmakers have invested heavily in AI labs and cloud-computing businesses. Some recipients then spend money on computing services or hardware supplied by those same investors.

That creates a complicated relationship between investment, customer demand and reported business growth. It does not automatically mean the revenue is artificial, but it raises questions about how sustainable demand would be if outside funding slowed.

Recent findings suggest that the Fed also highlights guarantees tied to data-center equipment and computing infrastructure. If hardware depreciates swiftly or demand falls short, the value supporting some financing arrangements could weaken.

Schiff’s warning deserves attention, but it should not be mistaken for a Federal Reserve forecast.

The Fed identifies vulnerabilities involving elevated valuations, complex financing and the possibility of a sharp correction in AI-related equities. Its report also notes that market contacts are concerned about circular financing and heavy reliance on debt.

However, the report does not declare that a crisis is inevitable. It also describes the broader financial system’s resilience, including capital and liquidity buffers at large banks.

For investors, the real question is whether future AI earnings can justify the enormous investment now being made in infrastructure.

A sharp reversal in AI valuations could extend beyond technology stocks. Companies, lenders and investors connected through financing arrangements could face pressure if expected returns weaken.

Industry observers note that that could also affect other risk assets if investors reduce exposure, sell leveraged positions or become more cautious about growth investments. Crypto markets could feel that pressure through broader risk aversion and leveraged liquidations, although the Fed’s findings do not establish that an AI downturn would cause a specific Bitcoin price decline.

According to recent reports, the AI bubble warning is worth taking seriously, but a crash is not a foregone conclusion. Investors should watch earnings, infrastructure spending, debt issuance and the ability of AI businesses to turn enormous capital commitments into sustainable cash flows. That’s a more useful test than simply betting on Peter Schiff’s prediction.
