Latest Update: Burlwood Is Making a Comeback With a Modern Twist

Recent findings suggest that few materials make a visual statement quite like burlwood. Its swirls and naturally irregular patterns give it a depth and individuality that are difficult to replicate. That distinctive grain comes from burls, rounded growths that develop on tree trunks or branches and are often associated with injury, stress, or infection.

Burlwood gained popularity during the Art Deco era of the 1920s and ’30s before becoming a defining feature of furniture design in the 1970s. Today, it’s experiencing another moment. Yet its current appeal is less about recreating the past and more about rethinking how the material fits modern spaces.

Design Milk spoke with four designers to learn why burlwood continues to resonate and the fresh ways they’re using it.

Sources indicate that bookmatched burlwood creates a striking symmetrical pattern across this three-drawer chest, set against a sculptural curved wood bed frame. Photography by Emily Hart.

“Burlwood does what a great print does,” says Tiffany Brooks, principal designer of Tiffany Brooks Interiors. “It gives your eye somewhere to go. Up close, you see all these little swirls and knots, and from across the room, it reads as one rich surface. Even two pieces made from the same wood can have completely different personalities.”.

Abbe Fenimore, owner of Studio Ten 25, shares that appreciation for burlwood’s organic qualities, seeing the material as a way to introduce visual interest without relying on patterned textiles.

Industry observers note that the tightly patterned grain of this burlwood chest extends across its drawer fronts, while colorful glass candlesticks and art books add color to the display. Photography by Ellen Renee.

For Eddie Maestri, founder, principal, and creative director of Maestri Studio, burlwood’s appeal lies in its departure from the predictable. “To me, the imperfections are the beauty,” he says. “Every piece of burl is different, and you can’t fake it.”.

The designers we spoke to see burlwood as having staying power, particularly as homeowners move toward spaces that feel more personal and collected.

According to recent reports, “The material is timeless,” Brooks says. “What changes is how we use it. Right now, I think people are a little tired of rooms that look like they all came from the same inspiration board. Burl brings some individuality back.”.

A honey-toned burlwood table features a richly figured top and tapered legs, standing out against the darker wood cabinetry behind it. Photography by Ellen Renee.

Fenimore sees a similar appeal in burlwood’s ability to bridge different eras and styles. Its balance of modern and vintage sensibilities, she explains, can give even a brand-fresh piece the character of something collected over time.

Maestri points to its long history, from French cabinetmakers to the Art Deco era and the 1970s, as evidence of its enduring draw. “It keeps coming back because people want warmth and things made by hand,” he claims, noting that burlwood can bring a sense of soul to interiors that otherwise feel overly polished.

Walnut, maple, and mappa burl each bring a different quality to a piece, with variations in color, grain, and visual weight influencing how designers use them.

Brooks reaches for walnut burl when she wants to introduce depth and create a moodier atmosphere, while maple burl offers similar movement in a lighter, softer palette. For a bolder grain pattern, she favors mappa burl.

According to recent reports, A burlwood nightstand introduces warmth and a touch of traditional detail to this otherwise contemporary bedroom, adding a rich wood accent against the sculptural charcoal headboard and modern lines. Photography by Nick Sargent.

Maestri, meanwhile, has a clear go-to: mappa burl, also known as poplar burl. “It’s my favorite because it’s lighter and more golden than walnut, so you get all that movement without the room feeling heavy,” he claims.

Industry observers note that while Mimi Meacham, founder and principal designer of Marian Louise Designs, gravitates toward rich walnut burl and lighter mappa burl, the individual piece matters more to her than the wood species. “The color, scale of the figuring, and finish all affect how it will feel in a room,” she says. “I want the grain and the shape of the furniture to complement each other.”.

According to recent reports, A close-up reveals the distinctive natural markings of a burlwood table, with brass-and-acrylic drawer pulls adding a mix of warm and modern finishes. Photography by Emily Hart.

Because burlwood already has so much movement, surrounding colors and finishes can either complement its natural pattern or create an intriguing contrast. Brooks likes combining burl with warm brass, matte paint, and textiles such as nubby linen and velvet. “I’d put a honey-toned burl next to a deep aubergine or an inky blue in a heartbeat,” she claims. Fenimore also likes pairing burl with color and pattern, especially soft blues, olive greens, warm neutrals, and even unexpected pops of pink or red.

A burlwood tray with a gold-toned rim highlights the wood’s warm coloring and varied markings, with stacked books providing a colorful backdrop. Photography by Ellen Renee.

Meacham gravitates toward warm wood tones paired with blues and greens, from soft periwinkle to deep green, along with antique brass, linen, velvet, and plaster. She also considers the finish of the burl itself when choosing surrounding materials. A glossy burlwood surface, for example, can be balanced by a matte wall or softly textured fabric.

It has been revealed that for a warmer, earthier direction, Maestri recommends tobacco, ochre, olive, and soft plaster white, complemented by brass, stone, linen, and mohair. He also prefers finishes with a subtle luster to emphasize the grain’s richness. Fenimore likes the contrast burlwood creates with lacquered surfaces, brass, upholstery, and contemporary furniture silhouettes.

A curved burlwood console showcases the wood’s richly varied grain across its drawer fronts and rounded frame, with pale green stools and white fringe adding a touch of color and texture. Photography by Emily Hart.

Burlwood is finding its way into a wider range of applications throughout the home. Brooks likes seeing it incorporated into architectural details, such as the back of a kitchen island or as an inset on a painted door. “A little restraint makes the detail feel more special,” she says. For larger pieces, she points to Worlds Away for its clean-lined interpretations of burlwood, as well as Ambella Home, Chairish, and 1stDibs for finding distinctive designs.

Maestri also appreciates seeing burl used beyond traditional furniture, including on lamp bases, picture frames, and cabinet doors. At home, he has a burlwood bar cabinet from Soho Home, and he’s drawn to the brand’s Wallace collection, which uses mappa burl in sculptural, curved forms.

Recent findings suggest that A burlwood coffee table showcases the wood’s warm tones and naturally irregular markings, while art books and a decorative bowl give the tabletop a relaxed, collected feel. Photography by Ellen Renee.

Contemporary furniture designs also explore the interplay between burlwood, contrasting materials, and simpler silhouettes. Jonathan Adler’s Bond collection, for example, combines burl with acrylic on a dresser and metal on a dining table. Meacham points to Made Goods’ Bradley mirror, which lets the mappa burl frame take center stage. Fenimore recommends looking at brands like Modway and TOV Furniture, particularly for smaller items such as chairs, ottomans, and drink tables with burl accents.

It has been revealed that the rich, swirling grain of the half-moon burlwood console complements the delicate branches and floral motifs in the botanical artwork above. Photography by Emily Hart.

Recent findings suggest that for anyone hesitant to introduce such a distinctive material, the designers recommend beginning with one piece, such as a side table, mirror, or tray. Brooks suggests starting with something that serves an existing need, even if it’s as small as a drink table next to a chair. Maestri puts it simply: “Pick one piece and let it be the star. Keep what’s around it simpler so the grain can stand out.”.

According to recent reports, fenimore encourages mixing burlwood with other wood tones rather than trying to match everything. She also sees the material as a way to ground a room that already has plenty of color and pattern. Meacham recommends giving the grain some breathing room through intentional styling and a variety of finishes. “That doesn’t mean everything else needs to be neutral—I love burl with color—but you want your eye to have somewhere to rest.”.

Burlwood’s latest resurgence doesn’t require a revival of any particular decorating style. The same material that characterized Art Deco cabinetry and 1970s furniture feels equally at home alongside contemporary silhouettes, unexpected colors, and contrasting finishes. Its history is part of its appeal, but it needn’t dictate how we use it.
