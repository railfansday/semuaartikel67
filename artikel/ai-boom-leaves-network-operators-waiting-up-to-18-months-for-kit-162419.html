Optical equipment prices up around 50% this year as telcos compete with datacenters for components, notes RETN.

The AI building boom is driving up optical networking equipment prices and stretching delivery times to as much as 18 months, according to network operator RETN.

RETN states that network operators and telcos are now directly competing with AI datacenters for chips and other components.

As Reg readers will be aware, memory prices have soared by four or five times over the past year or so, but RETN notes optical networking equipment prices have risen by around 50 percent since the start of 2026. Worse are the extended delivery times, with optical network kit that used to arrive within three months now taking 12 to 15, and some orders taking up to 18 months.

This is as bad as, if not worse than, the shortages during the pandemic, when chip supply problems left network vendors unable to fulfill orders. The concern now is that because of the AI boom, demand is outpacing supplies.

In its report, "AI, Connected," RETN notes developers building outside established datacenter hubs may need to involve network carriers two to five years before opening.

The sudden demand for network kit from hyperscalers and AI campuses is only one factor, RETN claims. Raw material shortages, skilled labor shortages, energy constraints, geopolitical tensions, and logistical challenges are also compounding the issue.

It has been revealed that however, it concedes that many carriers didn't help themselves by previously postponing network upgrades to cut expenditure. That work has now become unavoidable at the worst possible time.

Network diversity is now seen as a must-have, the report says. Where customers were once satisfied with two routes to long-haul destinations, they expect operators to provide four or more redundant routes now.

Industry observers note that the aim is to prevent a single failure from taking down critical services. Multiple connections are only useful protection if they avoid shared physical failure points, such as the same ducts or cable routes. A diverse circuit might cost €1,800 ($1,975) a month instead of €600 ($658), it claims, but an outage could prove far more costly.

It has been revealed that RETN also says AI datacenters are moving away from populated areas to remote locations, where access to suitable land, power and water may be easier to come by. Hyperscalers are building in the Nordics, where land is affordable, the climate is cooler, and plenty of renewable energy is available.

Sources indicate that remote sites may lack the fiber infrastructure needed to connect them to other datacenters and customers. The Register reported on this issue earlier this year.

For sites close to cities, developers often fund the fiber themselves, which adds just a few percent to the total project cost. But over longer distances, the case becomes harder to make, and carriers are understandably reluctant to build a long-haul route to serve a single remote site.

Some developers are prepared to swallow the cost: RETN says a major cloud provider is exploring converting an abandoned factory in Central Europe into a large-scale server farm, despite little existing fiber in the area.

Industry observers note that however, RETN's warning is that connectivity must be planned alongside power and construction, not left until the datacenter is nearly ready to open. ®.
