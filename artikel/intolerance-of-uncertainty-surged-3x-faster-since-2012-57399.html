Source: Columbia University Mailman School of Public Health.

Sources indicate that over the past decade, rates of anxiety and mood disorders among adolescents and college-aged adults have climbed sharply across North America. Yet clinicians and epidemiologists have hotly debated a central question: does this statistical surge reflect a true rise in underlying psychological distress, or are young people simply more familiar with clinical terminology and more willing to report symptoms?

Newly introduced research led by Columbia University Mailman School of Public Health and Boston University School of Public Health provides compelling evidence that underlying cognitive vulnerability is genuinely intensifying.

Published in JAMA Psychiatry, the study tracks three decades of data on intolerance of uncertainty (IU)—a foundational psychological mechanism characterized by acute distress when facing ambiguous situations, missing information, or unpredictable outcomes.

Analyzing data from more than 35,000 college students in the United States and Canada between 1992 and 2023, the researchers found that intolerance of uncertainty increased steadily over 30 years—and accelerated dramatically around 2012, growing roughly three times faster over the past decade than during the 1990s and 2000s.

To track population-level shifts in how young adults tolerate ambiguity, first author Dr. Jeroen M. Van Baar, postdoctoral associate in epidemiology at Boston University School of Public Health, and senior author Dr. Katherine M. Keyes, professor of epidemiology at Columbia Mailman School of Public Health, conducted a systematic temporal meta-analysis using the Web of Science database.

After screening nearly 300 full-text peer-reviewed studies published between 2007 and 2025, the team synthesized data from 138 studies encompassing 35,296 college-aged participants across the U.S. and Canada who completed standardized 27-item or 12-item versions of the Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale.

Sources indicate that across the entire 31-year window from 1992 through 2023, average IU scores increased by approximately 0.65 points annually. Crucially, the trajectory bent sharply upward around 2012: between 2012 and 2023, intolerance of uncertainty rose at roughly triple the annual pace recorded between 1992 and 2011.

According to recent reports, “Our results provide updated insights into one of the core building blocks of anxiety: intolerance of uncertainty,” explained Keyes. “IU is not itself a health outcome or a root cause of disease, but an intermediary cognitive process that contributes to the development of anxiety.”.

For an individual with high intolerance of uncertainty, everyday unpredictability, such as attending a latest class without knowing every expectation or navigating an unstructured social event, can trigger disproportionate cognitive threat and emotional distress. Elevated IU is a well-established transdiagnostic risk factor linked to generalized anxiety disorder, panic disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and depression.

Crucially, because “intolerance of uncertainty” remains a specialized clinical metric largely unfamiliar outside psychology, it provides a vital safeguard against reporting bias.

Recent findings suggest that “Some researchers have argued that increases in anxiety and mood disorder symptoms among young people may partly reflect greater mental health awareness,” noted Van Baar. “Because young people are now more familiar with terms like ‘anxiety’, they may be more likely to report symptoms in surveys even if their underlying feelings have not changed. On the other hand, intolerance of uncertainty remains a relatively unfamiliar concept outside psychology, making this study less vulnerable to that criticism.”.

“The fact that we observed a consistent increase in intolerance of uncertainty that mirrored trends in anxiety and mood disorder symptoms suggests these patterns are unlikely to be explained solely by greater mental health awareness and may instead reflect genuine changes in underlying mental health,” Keyes added.

While the study did not isolate a single environmental trigger, the authors point to a convergence of mutually reinforcing societal shifts—including the rapid adoption of smartphones and social media around 2012, escalating academic competition, rising loneliness, economic instability, childhood adversity, and the COVID-19 pandemic.

According to recent reports, “It is likely that these factors are intertwined and mutually reinforcing, jointly contributing to population-level increases in intolerance of uncertainty and anxiety among young adults,” Van Baar said. “Any explanation will be strongest if it accounts for both the long-term upward trend since the 1990s and the inflection point observed around 2012.”.

Industry observers note that despite the sobering trajectory, the researchers emphasize a clear path forward: unlike broad macroeconomic forces, intolerance of uncertainty is a modifiable cognitive habit. Targeted cognitive-behavioral therapies specifically designed to build tolerance for ambiguity already exist, and scaling these interventions for young adults could help curb the youth anxiety crisis at its cognitive roots.

What is “intolerance of uncertainty,” and how does it relate to anxiety?

Recent findings suggest that intolerance of uncertainty is a psychological tendency to feel distressed, overwhelmed, or threatened when facing unpredictable outcomes or missing information. It acts as a core cognitive building block that significantly increases a person’s vulnerability to developing anxiety and mood disorders.

How much has intolerance of uncertainty increased among college students?

Sources indicate that analyzing data from 35,296 U.S. and Canadian college students between 1992 and 2023, researchers found that scores rose steadily by about 0.65 points per year, and accelerated three times faster between 2012 and 2023 compared to the previous two decades.

Does this prove rising youth anxiety isn’t just due to greater mental health awareness?

According to recent reports, yes, it provides strong evidence against the “awareness-only” theory. Because “intolerance of uncertainty” is a specialized psychological concept rather than an everyday term young people use to self-label, its steep rise mirrors anxiety trends without being skewed by changing vocabulary or awareness.

To estimate IU trends among US and Canadian young adults attending college 1992-2023 by extending the prior meta-analysis and including the short-form IUS.

Web of Science was used to track English-language articles from Canada or the US citing the source articles for the long-form or short-form IUS across 13 databases.

Eligible studies were peer-reviewed empirical articles with nonclinical undergraduate samples from the US or Canada that reported sample size and mean long-form or short-form IUS.

Following MOOSE guidelines, 2 investigators extracted study characteristics and mean IU, resolving discrepancies jointly. Studies were merged with the prior meta-analysis and analyzed using fixed-effect meta-regression. To assess IU across scale forms, mean IU was converted into SD units and regressed onto data collection year, weighted by sample size, and adjusted for mean age and sex or gender distribution. Study data were analyzed from September 2025 to July 2026.
