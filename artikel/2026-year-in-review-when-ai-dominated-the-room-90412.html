📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

I’ve been writing this column for nearly nine years, and over the past year, I’ve seen legal professionals adapt to upcoming technology faster than ever before. At the same time, technology has advanced exponentially, with artificial intelligence changing how information is obtained and how work gets done.

At record pace, established legal technology companies have embedded AI into their products, rolled out AI-enabled features, and connected consumer-grade AI to their platforms using model context protocol connectors. Meanwhile, AI startups proliferated across the legal technology landscape, with some disappearing overnight, while others were snapped up and acquired less than a year after launch.

It was a year of rapid change, making it challenging for lawyers to keep up. From tracking updated software features and emerging AI-powered technologies to learning how to use AI ethically, lawyers had a lot on their plates. That’s where my 2026 columns came in. Each one offered a summary of the latest legal technology news or provided roadmaps for legal software adoption in law firms.

Trusts and estates lawyers rely on drafting software to create complex planning documents and manage related estate work. These tools include features like guided interviews, automated forms and shared client information that support document creation and reduce drafting errors. Some software also helps lawyers customize prepares and handle tasks such as trust funding and estate administration. Understanding how the options differ can assist lawyers in choosing the software that fits their trusts and estates practice’s workflows.

AI systems reflect the assumptions and perspectives of the people who develop them, as well as the data used to train them. Those assumptions can shape AI outputs and reinforce bias, especially when women and other underrepresented groups have little input into how systems are designed. Drawing on the Women + AI Summit, including more perspectives when developing AI matters; it can impact the questions considered, improve outcomes, and determine how these systems affect users and the public at large.

Recent findings suggest that at Legalweek 2026, agentic AI and the competition to become law firms’ central AI platform were recurring themes. There were also plenty of updates from legal technology providers, from platforms that connected research, drafting and firm data to specialized tools for e-discovery, litigation, legal research and skills training. The conference announcements highlight how companies are building both integrated ecosystems and focused applications that help lawyers incorporate new AI-driven workflows into their firms.

Sources indicate that drawing on findings from the 8am 2026 Legal Industry Report, this column examines how the profession is responding to rapid AI adoption, access-to-justice hurdles and concerns about the rule of law. The report shows that individual AI use has grown faster than the implementation of firm policies and training, even as survey respondents see technology as one way of bridging the access-to-justice moat. Also notable was that lawyers were most concerned about the rule of law and its continued degradation.

The growing number of court filings with fabricated or inaccurate case citations has led to a new category of tools designed to check AI-generated legal documents. Citation checkers can help identify false citations, misquoted language and inaccurate descriptions of case holdings. There are citation-checking tools built into drafting platforms in addition to those found in stand-alone applications, with a focus on their capabilities and costs. Before investing in a new tool, consider whether the time saved through AI research, drafting and citation checking justifies the expense or whether traditional research and drafting may ultimately be more cost-effective.

As AI takes on routine work traditionally assigned to junior lawyers, it may also reduce opportunities to develop practical legal skills. Training platforms can allow law students and early-career lawyers to build experience through courtroom simulations, interactive exercises and AI- or VR-based practice. These tools allow people to rehearse tasks, such as deposition questioning, negotiation and courtroom advocacy, with some offering immediate performance feedback. Using these simulation-based learning platforms, recent law school graduates are better able to bridge the experiential gap and ensure that they have the building blocks needed to thrive in the AI era.

According to recent reports, partnerships and integrations dominated the news at ILTACON 2026, as legal technology companies worked to connect their AI tools with legal data, software and workflows. In addition to those developments, there were upcoming products that streamlined litigation, legal writing and law firm pricing. The conference takeaway was that legal AI is moving beyond stand-alone tools toward connected systems, for better or for worse.

Litigation teams often work with large, complex collections of digital evidence, which can be difficult to organize and analyze using traditional methods. AI-powered fact analysis tools can assist with the review of case materials by identifying people, events and inconsistencies and by creating timelines that link to supporting documents. These tools can help litigators analyze and understand the evidence and collaborate more efficiently with their teams.

As latest AI-powered platforms enter the market, solo and small-firm lawyers now have more affordable options for legal research. There are some tools priced at $100 or less per month that lawyers can use to quickly and easily search caselaw and statutes. Some also offer case analysis and citation-checking tools to confirm subsequent case treatment. These platforms provide smaller practices with more options, but lawyers should still weigh the cost and accuracy rates against existing research tools, along with the time required to verify AI-generated results.
