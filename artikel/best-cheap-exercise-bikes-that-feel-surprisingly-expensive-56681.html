There are plenty of reasons to start riding inside. Questionable weather, overly packed schedules, and less daylight can cause outdoor riders to head inside for some indoor saddle time. It’s also possible you’re someone who doesn’t ride outside and you just want a good low-impact workout in the comfort of your own home—without breaking the bank.

Recently we did extensive testing in the office on different exercise bikes. I found that some people who don’t ride bikes outside may actually prefer some of the less expensive exercise bike options. Some of the higher-end exercise bikes we tested feel like a real bike outside—and pair with specific apps like Zwift for when you want to get some group rides or racing in. If you aren’t used to a bike outside, then getting comfortable in that position can be a hard transition. More narrow saddles, a more aggressive position, and controls that simulate the shifters on a bike may not be what you want. Largely, these more inexpensive exercise bikes have more upright positions, wider and more padded saddles, and controls that would resemble the exercise bike you might see on the floor of a fitness center.

According to recent reports, keep in mind that inexpensive is relative—the bikes we have on this list range from about $200 up to $700. If you’re looking to boost your mood, stay consistent, or supplement your training with an affordable indoor cycling bike, these cheap exercise bikes deliver the best mix of value, performance, and reliability.

Recent findings suggest that just like with a regular bicycle, if you want the best experience on your indoor bike, it’s crucial to get the proper fit. Josh Taylor, a global brand ambassador for the online fitness site Spinning, recommends finding an indoor cycling bike that puts you in the same position as the bike you’re accustomed to riding outside.

For example, if your daily rider is a road bike, look for an indoor cycling bike with a handlebar option that incorporates a traditional bullhorn bar and mimics the hand-position options of drops and brake hoods. However, if you’re already used to taking studio classes, an indoor cycling bike with just the traditional bullhorn-style bar will work great, says Ben Sharp, three-time Order of Ikkos medal recipient and founder of Sharp Coaching.

If you don’t ride at all outside or in a spin class yet, then it will take a bit more playing around with the exercise bike to figure out sizing and position. Start with getting saddle height set up correctly, then if you can adjust reach to the handlebars that’s step two. For the reach shoot to have a slight bend in the elbows and remember to keep your shoulders relaxed and away from your ears. Matt Phillips, Senior Test Editor at Bicycling, recently published a story on dialing in saddle height. What applies to saddle height on an outdoor bike largely applies to exercise bikes as well. “A common starting point is a height that provides around 30 degrees of knee bend at the bottom of the pedal stroke (6 o’clock position). But that is a starting point only, not gospel,” states Phillips.

In a recent development, when you’re riding an outdoor bike, you’re mostly working against inertia as you pedal. In order to recreate that sensation and effort on a stationary bike, manufacturers have to simulate that resistance in some way. Most choose to use a fan or a flywheel (a weighted disc that spins as you pedal).

In a recent development, flywheels work by storing inertia as they spin, and smoothing out the pedal stroke to eliminate that choppy up and down motion you’d otherwise experience. For most budget spin bikes, a heavier flywheel means a smoother ride, though some bikes opt for a light (8-pound) flywheel that maintains its inertia by spinning four times as fast instead.

Since the pedals on spin bikes are fixed to the flywheel (no coasting), the smooth-moving mechanism helps keep your pedaling consistent. These bikes will usually have a knob or lever that allows you to adjust the resistance, making it harder or easier to pedal, independent of your cadence (how fast you’re spinning the pedals). If you’re used to riding a bike outside, then think about it like riding a fixed-gear bike or fixie.

Fan-operated resistance bikes are a bit simpler: As you pedal, the fan blades push against the air around the machine. Your cadence is directly related to the amount of effort required to push the pedals, meaning these bikes don’t allow you to spin at a fast cadence with low resistance, which can be nice for warming up or cooling down.
