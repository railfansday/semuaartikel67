Industry observers note that scattered sparsely across Earth’s surface, from Nebraska to Mali to the Philippines, drilling rigs are rising from platforms surrounded by sheds and tanks. Each contains a tower 10 to 15 stories high. Only they aren’t pumping fossil fuels. While they’re reminiscent of machinery used to extract oil and gas, these rigs are on the hunt for a different type of gas—one trapped in microscopic pores of rock—that some researchers believe could help solve humanity’s clean energy needs for hundreds of years. Or at least offset our ever-increasing demand.

Natural stores of hydrogen gas exist throughout our planet’s crust on every continent. It’s colorless and odorless, and it frequently escapes into the atmosphere through natural seeps or during mining for metals like copper and zinc. But finding it in commercially viable quantities has always been difficult. Now, researchers and entrepreneurs think they have reason to hope this challenge can be overcome.

“There are places where we believe we’re going to find natural hydrogen accumulations, and in those places it’s going to be a very competitive energy source,” says Pete Johnson, CEO and cofounder of hydrogen exploration tech giant Koloma. The company prepares to drill 20 wells in the next two years in some of “the best hydrogen systems” in the world, including in the U.S. and the Philippines.

It has been revealed that hydrogen is already a multibillion-dollar global industry. Fertilizer companies combine it with nitrogen to make ammonia. Oil refineries use it to lower the sulfur content in diesel. Metalworkers use it to prevent oxidation. Hydrogen is “an essential element for survival of humanity,” states Ukrainian geochemist Viacheslav Zgonnik, PhD. But nearly all of the hydrogen used in those processes is manufactured, and much of it from methane gas in an energy-intensive way. So energy intensive, in fact, that companies produce 9 to 11 tons of carbon dioxide for every 1 ton of hydrogen, according to the Environmental and Energy Study Institute.

Some companies are exploring ways to produce hydrogen with fewer carbon emissions. But even so-called green hydrogen still requires energy to produce and may never be economically viable at scale, notes Zgonnik. That’s where natural hydrogen deposits enter the picture. And scientists and prospectors believe natural hydrogen could serve far more industries than just fertilizer and petroleum production. Some even envision it could help power the world with near-zero carbon emissions in the process.

According to recent reports, exactly how much hydrogen exists, however, is still up for debate. A 2024 U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) paper modeled the amount of hydrogen likely to percolate under the surface and found enormous amounts. The bulk is likely “impractical to recover,” according to the paper published in Science Advances, but if even a small fraction of the total amount could be captured, it “would supply the projected hydrogen needed to reach net-zero emissions for [about] 200 years.”.

In a recent development, A 2025 report from The Royal Society was more cautious, tempering the optimism of the USGS model by using currently available hydrogen survey data. But even that suggested hydrogen “could be a game changer.”.

Hydrogen forms naturally through at least two processes: chemical reactions between water and iron-rich rocks, and the splitting of water molecules by radiation in rocks containing uranium, according to the Royal Society paper. The gas then saturates porous rock, akin to an underground sponge sealed on the top by a dense layer of caprock such as clay or other sedimentary rocks. Stick a straw—or in practical terms a drill—into the sponge, and out comes the hydrogen.

In a recent development, known hydrogen accumulations occur in the oldest and most stable rocks on Earth at the core of the continents. Researchers speculate it exists in places where Earth’s crust tried to separate, like the Midcontinent Rift, which stretches beneath portions of Kansas, Iowa, Minnesota, and Wisconsin, and the Great Rift Valley in eastern Africa. The gas has also been found in regions where tectonic plates collided, including parts of the Pacific Rim off the coasts of North America and Asia, says Canadian hydrogen researcher Barbara Sherwood Lollar, PhD.

But natural underground hydrogen, also called gold or white hydrogen, has been discovered in plenty of other locations, too. In Mali, a country in western Africa, drillers searching for fresh water discovered a natural hydrogen system. Decades later, the Bourakebougou field, as it is called, began to produce enough hydrogen to supply electricity to a nearby village.

In a recent development, local supplies of natural hydrogen could be among the gas’s first and most practical applications, claims Lollar, who has studied natural hydrogen for decades, one of the few early researchers investigating hydrogen as a potential source for clean energy. In May, she coauthored a paper in Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences detailing how more than 70 percent of the continental crust “has the potential for hydrogen generation.” That widespread distribution, she says, could make the gas a source of local and regional power.

Industry observers note that “Another one of the easy wins in all of this would be to use this hydrogen not just for a small community,” says Sherwood Lollar, a University of Toronto professor. “The same rocks that produce hydrogen and helium are the rocks that produce nickel, copper, gold, diamonds, critical minerals.” That means mining conglomerates could capture hydrogen already being vented and use it to power their facilities.

Johnson takes the idea a step further. Industries that require large amounts of power, such as data centers, or industries that require hydrogen, like fertilizer manufacturers, could relocate to places where natural hydrogen is abundant, reducing the need to transport the gas.

Companies like Koloma are already surveying for economically viable deposits around the world. Johnson says his company has identified several potential fields.

Industry observers note that assuming the hydrogen reservoirs are large enough to economically produce, the process would look much like drilling for natural gas, he says. The timeline for developing the industry could be similar, too.

“We’re in some early innings right now,” Johnson says. “I mean if you look at how long it took us to develop the concept of shale gas, that was a 20- to 30-year process.”.

Then a few years after companies began drilling successful wells, the shale gas industry boomed. Natural hydrogen could follow a similar trajectory. Some companies are also exploring ways to stimulate hydrogen production underground by injecting heat and water into rocks before extracting the resulting gas. Those efforts, however, remain in their infancy.

Natural hydrogen could do more than help satisfy humanity’s insatiable appetite for energy—made worse by the buildout of energy-hungry data centers. It could also reshape the global energy map.

It has been revealed that “If the Philippines, for example, has domestic energy, what it means is that its neighboring countries like Taiwan, Japan, all these energy-poor countries may also have it,” Johnson says. “Anytime you find a new energy source in new places, it sort of rewrites the rules of geopolitics.”.

In a recent development, he’s not certain exactly what role the gas will play in the world’s future energy portfolio. Will it render oil and gas obsolete, or will it become no more than a bit player? “That’s the question we’re trying to answer.”.

First, researchers and prospectors need to hunt down untapped hydrogen reservoirs and prove that they’re worth drilling.
