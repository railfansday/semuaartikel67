45°C inlets just killed the chiller. Most people haven't noticed yet

In my mind and in my car, we can't rewind, we've gone too far.

Sources indicate that SC25 in St. Louis looked like a plumbing convention cross booked the supercomputing hall. Pipes, pumps, manifolds, quick-disconnects, and a lot of people who clearly spend more time thinking about pressure drop than floating-point. I kept expecting to see a booth selling industrial fire hoses. Chicago is a much bigger building – McCormick has roughly four times the exhibit space St. Louis had – so the pre-show question was whether SC26 will still look like that or whether the hall is large enough to dilute the effect.

If anything, cooling was even more dominant in Chicago. The heat problem didn't get smaller just because the building got bigger.

We also saw a lot of newly introduced companies displaying their cooling wares at SC26. I follow this stuff closely and see two broad trends. First: consolidation with significant industrial suppliers buying up the liquid cooling pioneers. Second: anyone with an angle is heading into the market. Both trends were visible on the floor in Chicago, and everyone had something to say about higher inlet temperatures, whether they have a real story or not.

Industry observers note that the real news, though, is a number Nvidia dropped earlier this year: 45°C (113°F) liquid inlet temperature for their parts. That's not a small tweak. Most liquid-cooled systems have been living in the 30-35°C (86-95°F) world because that's what the chips would tolerate. Jump to 45°C (113°F) and a whole bunch of datacenters that currently need chillers suddenly don't.

If AMD, Intel, and the Arm folks can match it – and we’d be shocked if they aren't already working on exactly that – then free cooling stops being a cool climate thing and becomes the default for a lot of places.

Recent findings suggest that rooftop dry coolers can reject nearly any incoming fluid temperature and bring it below 45°C (113°F). There are few places in the world where ambient temperatures approach 113°F, and for the hours when outside temps spike, adiabatic assist is available. This is where water is trickled through a mesh surrounding the dry coolers, which drops the effective temperature another 10–20 percent.

So, in our newly introduced cooling world, there are two closed loops: one taking heat from the server gear and then passing it to a second, different loop that takes the hot water to the roof, cools it, then brings it back again.

What's missing here? It's the chillers. You won't need them anymore. These are essentially huge rooftop refrigerators that cool water even more after the dry cooling. They're expensive, need a lot of maintenance, gulp electricity and are thankfully unnecessary now for new datacenter builds.

The power and water savings are not trivial. Legacy designs that rely on chillers and open evaporative towers commonly put 20 percent to 40 percent of total facility energy into cooling. A large share of that is the chiller plant itself – compressors, pumps, and cooling-tower fans. Move to dry coolers running at 45°C (113°F) inlet and that slice collapses.

Published estimates and vendor modeling for high-temperature liquid loops put the reduction in cooling energy in the 50 percent to 70 percent range versus a conventional chiller-plus-tower plant, which translates into a 10 percent to 20 percent drop in total datacenter power  – depending on the starting PUE (Power Usage Effectiveness) and climate.

According to recent reports, water use falls even harder. Traditional evaporative towers can consume millions of gallons a year at a mid-size site. Dry coolers use essentially no outside water in normal operation; adiabatic assist only wets the mesh during the hottest hours, cutting annual water consumption by 80 percent to 95 percent in most climates. Those are the numbers that make the 45°C (113°F) inlet such a big deal.

In a recent development, the same shift also changes the economics of the mechanical plant itself. A chiller plant is not just an operating cost; it is capital equipment that has to be sized, installed, maintained, and eventually replaced. Dry coolers are simpler, cheaper to install, and have far fewer moving parts. When the chips will accept 45°C (113°F) fluid, the business case for putting a mechanical refrigeration plant on the roof of a new build gets hard to defend in most climates. That is the part that has not yet fully registered outside the cooling specialists.

Recent findings suggest that designing systems around these higher inlet temperatures still takes real engineering work – sizing the loops, choosing the dry coolers, and making sure the facility can run at the newly introduced set points without surprises. Existing buildings add another layer. Many were built with pipes, pumps, and heat exchangers sized air cooling or lower temperature liquid. Raising liquid inlet temperature means checking flow rates, pressure drops, and making sure the existing set-up delivers.

In a recent development, at SC26 I had meetings with a couple of companies that do this work and buttonholed more in their booths. My top-of-mind questions were: Are the 45°C (113°F) inlet temps for real? Yes, they are, and are being used as a design basis to an extent – they need to leave headroom, of course.

Any news on whether the other chipmakers are going to hit the Nvidia number on inlet temperature? Attendees were closed-lipped on this one. Nothing public from the other vendors (AMD/Intel/Arm). But on the cooling vendor side, they are happy to see the increase and will comply.

Recent findings suggest that my instincts are telling me that the other chipsters may have been caught flatfooted by Nvidia's 45°C stake in the ground, but those same instincts also tell me that handfuls of Peanut M&Ms healthy snacks. I’ll nose around more.

There's real money at stake here from a TCO standpoint. One cooling vendor reports that their initial testing indicates 3 percent to 4 percent cooling energy cost decrease for every additional 1°C inlet temperature increase. That's a significant number at scale.

We're approaching a point where industry advances are answering many of the objections (the real ones, not the hysterical ones) raised by the anti-data center movement.

Much lower energy use, water consumption down by 85 percent to 90 percent, higher efficiency and lower costs. Not a bad deal, right? ®.
