Air Barrier for an Elevated Home

According to recent reports, I’m about to build a house on the coast in Charleston, S.C. (Climate Zone 3A). It’s elevated on wood pilings and the space under the house is open to the outdoors, enclosed only with louvered panels between the pilings. I have most of the enclosure details nailed down, but I haven’t determined the best way to air-seal and insulate the open floor trusses above the pilings. My biggest concern is hot, humid air from below reaching the underside of the air-conditioned subfloor and condensing on the surface. What’s the best strategy to insulate and air-seal the underside of an elevated coastal home?

According to recent reports, it’s wise to consider inward vapor drive in humid climates like Charleston. When the outdoor dew point is higher than the air-conditioned indoor temperature, there’s going to be a strong moisture drive from the outside toward the home’s interior, which allows water to accumulate in the materials below the flooring. Prolonged wetting can lead to rotted framing, mold problems, and damaged flooring, along with damage to other materials in contact with the floor.

There are a few ways to prevent this. One option is to place a vapor-retarding insulation layer—spray foam—right below the subfloor. The outside (bottom) of the foam will be close enough to the outdoor temperature that water won’t condense on the surface, and the foam will substantially block moisture from moving through it. Provided no ductwork is run in the floor, it’s a way to get decent air-sealing, and the cost is reasonable as well.

That said, I don’t like to use spray foam across an entire floor—it’s risky to install and full of questionable chemicals. Another option is to create a conditioned space within the floor system. The challenge is air-sealing around the posts, beams, and structural hardware that hold up the house and the plumbing and electrical lines in this area. Plus, you’ll have to condition the space, which requires additional HVAC work. This can be simple (like adding a supply register and a path back to the house) or pretty involved (running ductwork down walls to reach the underfloor space).

Sources indicate that my third and preferred option is to create an airtight, vapor-retarding layer across the bottom of the floor trusses. A layer of XPS or foil-faced polyiso (don’t use EPS, it’s too vapor-open) across the bottom of the whole floor will help slow vapor drive into the floor system and bring practical benefits like easier air-sealing and a little additional insulation. In Climate Zone 3A, turn to the International Residential Code (IRC) Table 806.5 in the section on roof ventilation for guidance on the R-value needed, since the floor system will have insulation levels comparable to roofs. The table calls for 1/2 in. of rigid insulation. Using the calculation procedure in the table footnotes indicates a higher level (in the 11/2-in. range) might be less risky, assuming you are completely filling a 12-in. to 16-in. floor-truss cavity with blown-in cellulose or fiberglass, which will be more than the code-required R-38 when the table was developed. Cover the foam board with fiberglass-faced drywall sheathing or plywood. Air-­sealing will still be complex given the elevated construction, and attention will need to be paid to the rim joist/perimeter (spray foam works in this area, as shown in the drawing). But, this system would really slow vapor movement throughout the floor system.
