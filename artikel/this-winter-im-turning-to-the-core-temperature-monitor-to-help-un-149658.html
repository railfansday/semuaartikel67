I have been doing what I considered heat training for years—going on hard rides at the peak of summer heat and humidity. Riding in conditions that felt like I was riding into a hair dryer blowing hot and wet air into my face made me feel like I was checking off this heat training box. The reality is that I’ve been leaving performance on the table by not putting a little more structure around it.

This year I started to incorporate structured heat training into my routine—using the CORE body temperature sensor on rides outside as well as layered up rides inside on the trainer for active heat sessions. The CORE temperature monitor made it onto our gear of the year and has become an essential training tool for some of our team at Bicycling.

Like so many others, my time to ride has gotten more limited over the last few years—my schedule seems to be filled by work, family life, and more house projects than “free time.” Even with a packed schedule I was able to feel strong through multiple century gravel rides with more than 10,000 feet of climbing. I attribute a large piece of that base fitness to the weeks of heat training leading into those significant days in the saddle.

According to recent reports, next year I’m targeting my biggest days on the bike yet. I’ll be utilizing the CORE Temperature Monitor and fresh features in the app throughout my training to help get me there. Here we’ll go through the basics of heat training, differences between passive and active heat sessions, and why using the CORE sensor and app for your training this winter can give you the boost you need to hit your 2027 goals—whether those goals are staying strong, tackling your biggest ride, making it to the finish line, or stepping up the podium.

When done correctly, one of the major benefits of heat training is an increase in hemoglobin mass. I know, this isn’t a metric you’re used to seeing on your head unit or smartwatch. Consider this—when cyclists train at altitude camps one of their goals is to increase hemoglobin mass by the end of the camp. In short, increased hemoglobin mass can improve the blood’s ability to transport oxygen, which supports endurance performance, as well as potentially a higher VO₂ max.

Let’s be real—most of us aren’t going to be testing our hemoglobin mass at home to track progress. The good news is there are some easy ways you can see your progress throughout your heat training blocks. Aline Barre, Endurance Performance Lead at CORE, gave these three signals to look out for to watch your progress:.

Whatever your heat sessions look like, Barre noted to make sure you are staying appropriately hydrated for your fluid losses during your sessions and stop if you feel dizzy.

Industry observers note that the CORE temperature monitor is a small monitor that gets attached to your heart rate band. It can be put on an arm strap or chest strap, but I’ve only used it on my chest strap. When fitting the sensor on a chest strap, you need to set the sensor up so it lines up under your armpit against your torso. For me, this means I just have it set up on the side of the strap and I wear the heart monitor strap in the same place I did prior to adding the CORE monitor. The CORE temperature monitor gives people insight into their internal temperature during activity without being invasive—one previous tool was a rectal thermometer.

In a recent development, the team at CORE has developed an app that pairs to the temperature monitor. It allows you to track your heat sessions, monitor your progress, and watch your heat training load go up during an active heat training session. Simply download it to your phone and you can access all your heat data there. I had a little bit more of a challenge installing the screen on my head units. Eventually I was able to get it set up as a screen on my different head units to monitor heat levels while riding outside.

Active heat training is when you are doing an activity, typically overdressed, to raise your body temperature while you are training. For cyclists this typically looks like putting on a few extra layers while you hop on the trainer. Just keep the fan off and watch your body temperature rise while you pedal.

This is where the CORE sensor comes in. It indicates you how much your body temperature is rising to both keep it high enough for the target adaptations, and prevent it from tipping to an unsafe zone. “Athletes should spend about 45-60 minutes in CORE’s Heat Zone 3. This is the ideal zone for inducing physiological adaptations. More heat strain can overcook an athlete and less is not sufficient exposure,” says Barre. When I’m in heat zone three on the CORE app I typically sit around 101.5 degrees to 102.5 degrees.

Remember to bring plenty of fluids because you will more than likely be losing more sweat than you do on a typical trainer ride. During my heat sessions I typically sweat through all three layers and into my painter’s suit. You can weigh yourself before and after your ride to get an estimate of sweat loss. Typically, every pound of bodyweight lost equates to about 16 ounces of fluid loss. “Replace about 1.5 times [the lost] amount in total, before, during, and after the session,” says Barre. If you want to pick up another gadget to dial in your winter training, check out the Nix Biosensor to get a more accurate estimate of your fluid loss in a trainer session.

According to recent reports, “The ideal heat training set up is indoors where the climate can be controlled and there is no wind convection. The best protocol is to overdress with several non-permeable layers to trap heat against the skin, as heat strain is a product of both core and skin temps. Athletes should spend about 45-60 minutes in CORE’s Heat Zone 3. This is the ideal zone for inducing physiological adaptations. More heat strain can overcook an athlete and less is not sufficient exposure,” indicates Will Truettner, Head of Brand and Marketing at CORE.

There are still heat training benefits from passive heat training even though it does not appear to be as impactful as active heat training. Additionally, athletes like Reggie Miller swear by passive heat training, or sauna use, to support recovery. I’ve been using the Backyard Discovery Rylan Infrared Sauna for the last few months. While I can’t definitively say it’s a performance enhancer, I can say that I feel like I recover faster after building the sauna into my routine.

According to recent reports, just as with active heat training, start with shorter sessions and work your way up as you feel comfortable—and remember to hydrate. “Build up gradually—start with 10 to 15 minutes and add time each session,” says Barre. One tip Barre gave to get the most out of your heat sessions is something I’ve started to incorporate into my own heat training days. “Do it right after training. Your core temperature is already high, which makes the passive session more effective. Keep the break in between as short as possible,” says Barre.

In a recent development, if you decide to add sauna sessions to your heat training block, then be sure to take your CORE temperature monitor off before you get in. You can add the passive training session to the app after you get out of the sauna. In the app you can select between options like standard sauna versus infrared, time and temperature in the sauna, and body temperature when you first got into the sauna. The different options will give you a different heat load score at the end.

Industry observers note that my first proper heat session was in my basement on the trainer. I layered up like I was on a Fresh Year’s Eve ride in Michigan: thermal base layers, a warm midlayer, sweatpants, a hoodie, and a winter hat for a cherry on top. Here’s where it gets fun—I bought a painter’s suit on Amazon and cut it off at the knees so the suit legs didn’t get stuck in the cranks. I stuffed myself and all those layers into the painter’s suit and climbed on my trainer bike—without a fan. I pulled up Alp du Zwift, started pedaling, and I thought to myself, this will be interesting.

It’s no surprise this is not the way to start heat training if you haven’t tried it before. I thought I’d be fine because I had two water bottles and I dropped in an extra bit of electrolytes—I was used to riding in the humid New Jersey heat, so I thought it would be no problem. By the top of the Alp du Zwift I knew I had overdone it.

Now, there’s a newly introduced coaching tool in the CORE app that can help prevent you from making the same mistakes I made when getting started. I’ve been trying the new feature in the app, and it makes progress easy by having a clear plan for your goals. I chose to go with the building phase, which came out to five sessions per week where my target heat training load per session is five over the course of five weeks. I was able to put in a target date for when I want to have a proper base built up, and the coaching tool worked backward from there.
