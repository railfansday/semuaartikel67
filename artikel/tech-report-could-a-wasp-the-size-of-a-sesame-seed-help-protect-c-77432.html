Tech Report: Could a wasp the size of a sesame seed help protect carrots and canola?

Scientists discovered Gonatopus quebecensis, a tiny wasp in Québec that attacks leafhoppers and might help protect crops naturally.

Some leafhoppers in Québec farm fields have a small sac stuck to their bodies. These insects are known to spread disease to carrots and canola.

Each sac holds a wasp larva that feeds on the leafhopper as it grows.

Sources indicate that the wasps were raised to adulthood by researchers, who found a species nobody had named. It has now been called Gonatopus quebecensis.

It belongs to a group known as pincer wasps and is about as long as a sesame seed.

Recent findings suggest that the wasp is a natural enemy of a crop pest and was already living in the fields. The researchers do not yet know whether it kills enough leafhoppers to help a farmer.

Leafhoppers on the province’s farms have been studied by scientists at Université Laval in Québec City since 2021.

Study supervisor Edel Pérez-López said the sightings “sparked our curiosity about the natural enemies of these important agricultural insects.”.

In a recent development, the study itself is a description. Lead author Thomas Vinatier and five colleagues described the wasp’s body and read a stretch of its DNA. The insect it grows on was also identified. The wasp was not tested as pest control.

Recent findings suggest that in July 2025 the team collected leafhoppers carrying wasp larvae. The insects came from highbush blueberry fields in the Mauricie region of Québec.

They were moved into a university greenhouse where the wasps bred on leafhoppers kept on potted barley. The room was held at 73°F (23°C) and the lights stayed on 18 hours a day.

Adult wasps emerged and the colony kept going. One female now serves as the reference specimen for the species. She was reared there in January 2026.

For close study the researchers randomly picked 11 adults. Six were females and five were males. Seven more were dissected so the mouthparts could be examined under a microscope.

Industry observers note that the reference female and one male are now stored in Ottawa. They are kept in the Canadian National Collection of Insects, Arachnids and Nematodes. The other specimens stayed at the university.

The newly introduced species is named after Québec. A total of 55 Gonatopus species are now known from the Nearctic region. That region covers most of North America.

According to recent reports, the female has no wings. She is mostly black with brownish-yellow on parts of her legs and antennae. At 0.12 inches (2.97 millimeters) long she could pass for a small ant.

Each of her front legs ends in a pincer. It is made of an enlarged claw and the last segment of the foot. A row of tiny plates is carried on that segment.

It has been revealed that pincers like hers give the family its common name. Females grab their prey with them. The family has 1,928 known species that attack leafhoppers and their relatives.

The male is a little smaller at just under a tenth of an inch (2.41 millimeters). He is almost entirely black and has clear wings.

In a recent development, males and females are hard to match in these parasitic wasps. The two sexes of one species can look very unlike each other. Matching them by eye is therefore unreliable.

Rearing helped because both sexes came out of the same colony on the same host. The match was supported by the DNA data.

In a recent development, A short stretch of a gene was read from four wasps. Two were females and two were males. Biologists use that stretch as a DNA barcode because it differs from one species to the next.

No named species in the public databases matched the four wasps. Two unnamed records did.

Recent findings suggest that one was a larva from Québec that the same lab had raised earlier from the same leafhopper species. It was a 99.91% match.

The other was an unidentified larva from Ontario. It was filed in a reference library of 1.5 million barcodes from Canadian invertebrates.

The Ontario larva’s barcode matched the females’ exactly and the males’ at 99.80%.

The wasp had therefore been collected and sequenced before in two provinces. It may have gone unnamed because no adult specimens were available.

Among the named species compared, Gonatopus pedestris had the most similar DNA barcode, at 95.74%.

Only one G. pedestris barcode was available for the comparison. It came from a specimen collected in Norway. The authors wrote that more samples are needed to show how the two species are related.

The wasp’s host is the aster leafhopper. This insect is about an eighth of an inch (3 to 4 millimeters) long.

The wasp’s larvae develop on the aster leafhopper and feed on it.

In a recent development, an infected aster leafhopper can pass along phytoplasmas as it feeds on a plant. Phytoplasmas are bacteria-like organisms that have no cell wall.

One result is aster yellows, a disease capable of heavy losses in canola and carrots.

The aster leafhopper is abundant on Canadian farms. In Québec strawberry fields it was found more often than any other leafhopper in the team’s earlier surveys.

In a recent development, spraying has not been a sure answer for these crop pests. Strawberry fields in eastern Canada were examined in a 2024 study by members of the same lab.

They reported that growers’ sprays may not have been keeping leafhopper numbers down.

In a recent development, the search for the leafhopper’s natural enemies had a second motive. Pérez-López mentioned it was the province’s push to use less insecticide.

In a recent development, the farming question was left open by the researchers. They do not yet know what share of leafhoppers the wasp attacks or at what time of year. It is also unclear whether other leafhopper species are used.

Sources indicate that wingless females are poorly sampled by the traps entomologists set for flying insects. Wasps like this one may therefore be undercounted across North America, the authors wrote.

It has been revealed that the team suggested repeating its own method in more places. Leafhoppers that carry wasp larvae would be collected and kept alive until the wasps emerge. The adults can then be matched by body and by DNA.

The authors wrote that such studies must come first. Only then can the wasp’s contribution to pest control be judged.

According to recent reports, three jobs are planned by Pérez-López’s lab. They are a full genome for the wasp, a closer look at its breeding and trials with other leafhopper species.

The full study was published in the journal ZooKeys.
