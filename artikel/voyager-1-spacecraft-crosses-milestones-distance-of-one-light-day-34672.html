Voyager 1 spacecraft crosses milestones distance of one-light day from Earth 49 years after launch

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

NASA has confirmed that Voyager 1 is the first human-made object to reach one light-day's distance from Earth, passing the milestone today. A countdown at Orbital Radar confirmed that Voyager cruised past the threshold at a leisurely 37,844 mph just after 5 am ET on Wednesday, November 18. It means that Voyager is now more than 16 billion miles away, and that a signal from the spacecraft now takes fully 24 hours to travel the distance.

According to recent reports, there are many astronomical numbers behind what is an extraordinary feat. Sci-fi reveals casually mention light-years as if they are like a trip to the local convenience store. One light-day isn’t a distance to be derided, though. Over the last half century, hurtling through space at a cruising speed of 38,000 mph, the Voyager spacecraft has clocked about 16 billion miles (about 26 billion kilometers).

These are probably bigger distances than you are used to fathoming. So NASA has kindly provided some context with the distances of various heavenly bodies in miles and light-speeds. For example, the Moon is 238,855 miles distant, or about 1.3 light seconds away from Earth. Due to Mars’ independent orbit around the Sun, it can take between three minutes and 22 minutes for light to travel between the red and the blue planet. Meanwhile, the Sun’s rays reach Earth, about 93 million miles away, in 20 light seconds. Now let us remind you that Voyager 1 is on the brink of being a whole light-day away.

It has been revealed that on Wednesday, with this major milestone passed, Voyager 1’s journey will be far from over. NASA says that it is on course for an obscure star called Gliese 445, now in the constellation Ursa Minor. We’ll have to keep the champagne on ice for another 40,000 years to celebrate the fly-past of that star, though.

Voyager 1 carries a copy of the Golden Record. Like sending a message in a bottle, NASA scientists released Voyager with the hope that someone, somewhere will eventually read the message. Moreover, the receiving aliens will need to source tech to play the record, which contains sounds, music, and the data needed to reconstruct photos that showcase the diversity of life and culture on our planet.

If an alien got hold of the record on Wednesday and managed to rustle up a gramophone player to listen to it, a message sent to Earth via radio waves would take a full 24 hours to be received.

Recent findings suggest that space enthusiasts are also invited to participate in a global event “by creating a wave of golden light.” It sounds rather pointless, but enthusiasts are asked to flash the three-letter call sign for NASA's Voyager mission, VGR, into the night sky - Morse Code style - using their phone flashlights. There’s a website you can open to flash your screen appropriately.
