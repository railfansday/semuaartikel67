Does culture change people’s personalities?

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that personality is often misunderstood. For instance, people tend to think they are way more unpredictable and complex than they are according to others, to the point of mistakenly assuming that their “personality changes from one situation to the next”. In fact, everybody has a preferred pattern of reactions to different situations, and if you hang out with people long enough you will have no trouble predicting how they are likely to act in any given situation, or indeed understanding what makes them tick. And yet, even if humans are predictable, that doesn’t mean that they are rigid or unable to flex according to the demands of the situation. One of the earliest findings in psychology is that behavior is always a function of both the person and the situation, which means that in order to predict how people will act you must take into account not just who they are but also what the situational requirements are. Micro vs. Macro At the micro-level, this explains why personality may be “suppressed” or inhibited in strong situations, that is contexts where there is a great deal of pressure to behave in a specific way or form: from a church (quiet, respectful, pensative) to a nightclub (loud, extraverted, energetic), to a lecture (focused, pensative, attentive), to a soccer game (depending on where you are, anywhere from excited and energized to aggressive and stressed). That is not to say that people stop being who they are when they are required to inhibit their personality; but rather, that personality becomes a weaker predictor of behavior when the strength of the situation temporarily eclipses it. At the macro-level, this begs the question of how much our personality may change when we make substantial long-term changes in our chosen environments or contexts. Most notably, people changing jobs or employers will often switch from one organizational culture to another; and people switching cities or countries will also be faced with different cultural demands to adapt or adjust their behaviors. Let me use myself as an example: moving from Argentina to Britain first, then from Britain to the US, required shaping my main adaptations to meet the cultural demands of each environment: I first learned to become less excitable, emotional, and direct (all of which work far less in Britain than Argentina), and then learned to suppress the sarcasm, nuances and indirectness in my communication (as these are less effective in the US than Britain). But, what does the research show about how culture, either at the societal or organizational level, impacts people’s behavior? And can this impact be significant or consequential enough to translate into actual personality changes? Adaptations Let’s start with the second question, which is actually simpler. Since personality can mostly be understood as “what you typically or habitually do,” enduring changes to how you behave—fresh adaptations—will indeed be perceived as personality changes. So, for instance, after living in NYC for over a decade the fast-paced nature of the city can make you more impatient, to a point that may not make you stand out in NYC (where type-A impatience is the norm) but will certainly stand out if you transfer that impactience to slower tempo cultures. As the famous line suggests, “The problem with city people in the countryside is that they expect even nature to meet deadlines.” Note, however, that city people often come from the countryside in the first place, but unless they adapt to their new fast environments and adopt the appropriate speed, they will not last very long. As for the first question, there’s plenty of research evidence—especially recent scientific papers—showing that while the essence of personality may remain largely unchanged even after people switch cultures, there are still logical changes to their personality at the more granular level, which correspond to the adaptational requirements of their adoptive cultures. In a way, we could say that our personality evolves when we go outside our comfort zone and challenge ourselves with new contexts that require new adaptations and broadening the behavioral repertoire in our tool kit. For example, a recent longitudinal study comparing 300 Japanese students studying in the United States with 108 students who remained in Japan found that international experiences were associated with changes in personality-related behaviors, particularly greater openness to new experiences and more socially adaptive behavior. Interestingly, the researchers also found that students who chose to study abroad already differed from those who stayed home, suggesting that personality not only influences how we adapt to new environments, but also which environments we choose to enter in the first place. Likewise, a recent meta-analysis examining 25 studies, comprising 40 samples and over 5,700 participants, found that living and studying abroad was associated with measurable personality changes, including increased openness to experience, greater self-confidence and agency, and lower levels of neuroticism (that is, greater emotional stability). Although the effects were generally small, the findings suggest that personality is not simply something we carry with us wherever we go, but also something that is shaped by the environments and cultures we inhabit. In another longitudinal study tracking 180 British university students, some of whom spent a year abroad across 32 different destinations, those living overseas became somewhat more agreeable, curious, and less anxious than their peers who remained at home. Interestingly, although the overall personality changes were relatively small, the researchers found that people’s everyday behavioral tendencies predicted longer-term personality change, suggesting that adapting how we behave in unfamiliar environments may eventually influence who we become. Perhaps even more intriguingly, a series of four studies examining Spanish-English bilinguals found that people’s reported personality characteristics varied depending on the language they were using. Specifically, participants tended to report higher levels of extraversion, agreeableness, and conscientiousness when responding in English rather than Spanish, consistent with the cultural norms associated with each language. This suggests that our personality is not always expressed in the same way across situations, and that simply switching languages may activate different aspects of our identity, even without changing countries. Workplace influence Even career or employment switches can shape people’s personality, as a growing body of research suggests that our jobs are not just an expression of who we are, but can also influence who we become. For example, a longitudinal study tracking Australian workers over five years found that moving into more senior managerial and professional roles was associated with increases in openness to experience, while another study examining nearly 7,700 German workers found that occupational demands were associated with subsequent personality development, particularly among those who remained in their jobs long enough to be shaped by them. Likewise, a longitudinal study following people from age 18 to 26 found that work experiences and personality influenced each other, such that people tended to select jobs compatible with their existing traits, but those same jobs subsequently reinforced or modified their personalities. In other words, we may choose our careers based on our personalities, but our careers can also end up choosing the personalities we develop. Of all these patterns of change, the most salient is the positive impact that environmental and cultural switches have on openness to experience, the academic term for intellectual and experiential curiosity. Interestingly, the effect is a form of “snowballing” whereby cultures operate in a way that accentuate who people are in the first place. In other words, our choices are a function of our inherent or a priori preferences and dispositions, and they contribute to augmenting them. This is why when people change, the most common outcome is that they become a more exaggerated version of their earlier selves. Psychologists have long documented the phenomenon of niche-picking, whereby people gravitate towards environments, relationships, and experiences that are compatible with their existing personalities. Importantly, this is not just a matter of selecting environments that suit us, but also being shaped by them, as illustrated by a longitudinal study showing that the personality traits that predispose people to certain work experiences tend to be reinforced by those same experiences over time. In other words, curious people may seek intellectually stimulating environments that make them even more curious, while competitive individuals may gravitate towards demanding, high-pressure cultures that further amplify their competitiveness. Thus, we don’t just choose environments that reflect who we are; we also become more like the environments we choose. Paradoxically, then, the more we change, the more we become like ourselves.
