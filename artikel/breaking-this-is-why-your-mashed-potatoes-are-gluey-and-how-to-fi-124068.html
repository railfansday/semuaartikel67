Consider this your mashed potato primer, complete with tips on how to fix gluey mashed potatoes and how to prevent them from the start.

It has been revealed that my mom’s potato masher is one of my favorite kitchen tools, from its retro design to the thick, grid-patterned bottom that hasn’t bent or warped over the years, despite heavy use. I attributed her consistently perfect fluffy mashed potatoes to this simple tool, even when she was experimenting with new mashed potato ideas. And it turns out there is some truth to it because it created smooth mashed potatoes rapidly yet thoroughly, preventing her from pummeling the spuds into a sticky, gummy mess.

Sources indicate that you see, overworked potatoes are the biggest reason for gluey mashed potatoes, so using the right tool is the best way to prevent them in the first place. And if you came here because your potatoes are already gummy, we have some helpful tips for how to fix gluey mashed potatoes after the fact.

The biggest culprit for gluey mashed potatoes is overworked potatoes. Potatoes are naturally starchy, and a little bit of starch is a good thing. Just as aquafaba (starchy cooked chickpea liquid) can be whipped into a rich vegan mayonnaise, potato starch thickens mashed potatoes and contributes to a creamy texture. But there is a limit.

Sources indicate that as you mash chunks of potatoes into a mostly smooth mixture, the spud’s cell walls collapse, freeing the starch within those cells. The more you mash, the more cells are broken down, and the thicker, starchier and gummier the potatoes become. This can happen when you mash potatoes too long by hand, but it happens more quickly when using electric tools.

Recent findings suggest that food processors and immersion blenders have incredibly fast, sharp blades. As they whip around, they cut through the potatoes’ cells so rapidly that the once silken mash becomes gluey in a flash. I can’t urge you strongly enough to pass on these tools when mashing potatoes.

There are three components to avoiding gluey mashed potatoes: Choosing the right potato, using the right tool and considering temperature.

Recent findings suggest that for starters, choose a starchy or all-purpose potato, like a russet or Yukon Gold. These potatoes are relatively low in moisture, helping the potato break down into a creamy mixture. They’ll give you more wiggle room as you mash than waxy potatoes, so the potatoes will turn out delicious even if you lose yourself in exuberant mashing.

Next, use the right kitchen tool for the job and reach for a potato masher, food mill or ricer. These handheld tools give you more control over speed and power as you mash. I prefer a potato masher with a crisscross grid pattern over those with a wavy design because there’s less open space, making each mash more productive.

Industry observers note that finally, warm your butter and milk before adding them to the potatoes. This step may sound finicky, but it prevents the warm potato starches from seizing against cold ingredients and becoming dense. Switch to a spatula and stir them in to keep a gentle touch and retain a fluffy texture.

Recent findings suggest that you’ll never be able to totally remove the excess starch and get rid of the gluey texture without starting over, but you may be able to improve it. Adding more fat or liquid can improve the mouthfeel, and stirring in other ingredients can alter the overall texture.

Sources indicate that knowing what types of potatoes are best for which recipes can save you a lot of grief—and this knowledge will go a long way to preventing gluey potatoes. For the creamiest, smoothest, lightest-as-air mashed potatoes, go with a starchy russet potato. Some people like using all-purpose Yukon Gold potatoes for their naturally buttery flavor, but I prefer to use all russets (or a blend of russets and Yukon Golds for flavor).

According to recent reports, it’s best to avoid waxy potatoes, like red or fingerling potatoes, for mashed potato recipes. They keep their shape when they’re cooked, which is why they’re better for potato salads and soups than creamy mashes. They do make an excellent smashed potato, though, if you’re looking to do something a little bit different with your side dish.

As with all cooking rules, this one is made to be broken. If you only have red potatoes in the pantry, you can still use them to make mashed red potatoes. Just keep the potatoes a little bit chunkier so they don’t get gummy. And make sure you refer to them as a “rustic” mash when you bring them to the table to properly temper expectations.
