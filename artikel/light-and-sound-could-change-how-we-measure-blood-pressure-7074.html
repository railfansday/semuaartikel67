Light and sound could change how we measure blood pressure

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A wrist sensor read blood pressure beat by beat from laser-made sound in an artery, matching a cuff within a few mmHg in two volunteers.

In a clinic, a blood pressure check usually means a cuff squeezing an arm to produce a reading for that moment.

A sensor pressed to the wrist has now read blood pressure beat by beat, with no cuff at all.

Recent findings suggest that the sensor flashes pulses of light into the skin and picks up the faint sound an artery gives off in response. That sound tells it how wide the artery is with every heartbeat.

In two volunteers tested over three months, its readings differed from a clinical finger-cuff monitor by only a few points on average.

Two people make a proof of concept rather than a finished product, and the researchers wrote that a full clinical trial is still to come.

Amir Rosenthal and Daniel Lange of the Technion in Haifa, Israel, led the work with doctoral student Gil Gelbert.

Rosenthal runs a lab for biomedical imaging and sensing at the university’s Viterbi Faculty of Electrical and Computer Engineering.

Blood pressure is one of the most important indicators of heart health. Yet it is still usually measured with an inflatable cuff, which gives a reading at a single point in time.

The alternatives have been a catheter inside an artery or repeated cuff readings, and neither suits daily life.

Recent findings suggest that wrist sensors have tried to fill the gap with stand-in signals, such as the pulse that light-based sensors read through the skin. Those signals shift when the device moves and need frequent recalibration.

A more direct route is the artery itself. Each heartbeat widens an artery slightly, and the amount it widens tracks the pressure inside.

Ultrasound patches can follow that widening, but they need rows of transmitting elements and careful alignment over the artery.

The wrist’s radial artery is only about 0.08 inches (2 millimeters) across and sits about 0.16 inches (4 millimeters) deep, which makes that alignment hard to hold in a wearable.

The Technion team turned to an effect in which light makes sound. Near-infrared laser light pulses through the skin 100 times a second.

Blood absorbs that light far more than the tissue around it, so the artery warms and expands by a tiny amount and sends out an ultrasound wave.

A detector on the skin records the wave. The gap between the sound from the artery’s near wall and its far wall gives the artery’s inner width, and that width changes with every beat.

In a recent development, what makes the device unusual is its detector. It is a channel for light etched into a silicon chip and coated in a soft silicone layer.

The channel is about a thousandth of a millimeter wide, far thinner than a human hair.

Pressure from the sound wave changes how light moves through the channel, and a readout system measures the change.

Sources indicate that because the detector is so small, it picks up sound from almost any direction, so it doesn’t have to be aimed at the artery. A single element does the job that patches need arrays for.

An established formula then converts the artery’s width into blood pressure, calibrated once per person.

The team first tested the sensor on artificial arteries built to mimic the wrist.

Shifting the sensor up to 0.4 inches (10 millimeters) off center, or rotating it up to 30 degrees, changed the measured width by only about a hundredth of a millimeter.

A conventional piezoelectric detector element, tested the same way, worked only within half that range and lost the artery’s edges when turned by just 5 degrees.

On a 29-year-old man’s wrist, finding the artery took less than 10 seconds. The team then slid the sensor across his arm while it recorded, and the readings held steady.

A standard pulse-echo ultrasound element, by contrast, produced an error of roughly a fifth of a millimeter when moved just 0.3 millimeters.

The blood pressure test used two volunteers. One was the 29-year-old man, whose pressure ran slightly high. The other was a 42-year-old woman, whose pressure ran slightly low.

Over three months they sat for 120 measurements of 10 seconds each, some at rest and some while tensing their core muscles, which raises blood pressure.

A finger-cuff system approved for clinical use recorded the reference pressure at the same time.

The sensor was calibrated once per person at the start. For every later session it was taken off and put back by hand, with no markers to guide its position, the way a watch goes back on a wrist.

Averaged across all 120 measurements, the sensor’s readings differed from the cuff system by 2.6 mmHg on the lower, diastolic number. On the upper, systolic number, the gap was 4.7 mmHg.

The authors wrote that this matches the accuracy reported for the leading wearable ultrasound patches in studies of similar size.

In one session, the sensor tracked the man’s pressure continuously for more than 30 minutes, several days after it had been calibrated.

According to recent reports, two people are enough to show the idea works, not to show it works for everyone. The authors wrote that a full validation study would need more than 85 participants.

Only a study that size, they wrote, could tell whether the sensor performs differently in men and women.

According to recent reports, every reading was also taken sitting still. The authors wrote that sliding the sensor didn’t disturb the signal, but blood pressure during walking or exercise has not been tested.

Both lasers, the one that fires the pulses and the one that reads the detector, still sit on a lab bench.

The team wrote that compact laser diodes could replace them, and that the readout electronics could eventually be built into the chip itself.

It has been revealed that the sensor also records the full shape of each pressure pulse, beyond the high and low numbers. The authors wrote that this information was not used in the study.

The researchers believe the technology could eventually go into smartwatches and medical patches.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Communications.
