Bizarre deep-sea fish with a see-through head captured in stunning 4K

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A rare encounter nearly 2,000 feet beneath the ocean's surface gave scientists an extraordinary opportunity to film one of the deep sea's most unusual creatures. Researchers from the Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute (MBARI) spotted a barreleye fish (Macropinna microstoma), a mysterious species known for its transparent head and remarkable eyes that can rotate to track prey.

The discovery occurred during an expedition aboard the research vessel David Packard. It marked only the eleventh time MBARI scientists have encountered this elusive fish during nearly four decades of ocean exploration.

It has been revealed that members of MBARI's Biodiversity and Biooptics Team were investigating marine life approximately 90 kilometers (55 miles) offshore when they spotted the fish about 600 meters (1,970 feet) below the surface. Using the remotely operated vehicle (ROV) Doc Ricketts, the researchers were filming and collecting deep-sea organisms when the unexpected visitor appeared.

Sources indicate that "Seeing a barreleye fish is a rare treat. Crew from across the ship joined the science team in the control room, mesmerized by this remarkable animal. Deep-sea animals capture the imagination, creating wonder and awe that we hope will inspire stewardship of the largest living space on Earth," said Senior Scientist Steven Haddock.

A Surprise Encounter Deep in the Pacific Ocean.

The discovery was an unexpected highlight of the eight-day Diving Deep for Delicate Drifters expedition. Haddock and his colleagues had primarily been investigating gelatinous marine animals, including jellyfish, siphonophores, and ctenophores, also known as comb jellies. Their research seeks to understand how these delicate organisms have evolved to survive in the open ocean at great depths.

Sources indicate that to investigate life throughout the water column, the team combined blue-water scuba dives near the surface with remotely operated vehicle surveys hundreds of meters below. These complementary approaches allowed researchers to observe and collect animals living in very different parts of the ocean.

Industry observers note that the barreleye appeared just as the day's underwater operations were coming to an end. The ROV pilots were "flying" Doc Ricketts toward the surface for recovery when Research Specialist Shannon Johnson noticed the unusual fish and immediately asked them to stop.

According to recent reports, most of the scientists had already moved to the ship's wet lab, where they were preparing to catalog specimens collected during the dive. When word spread about the sighting, they hurried back to the control room, accompanied by crew members eager to see the extraordinary animal. The rare encounter provided an exciting conclusion to a busy and productive week of exploration.

How the Barreleye Fish Uses Its Transparent Head to Hunt.

Recent findings suggest that the barreleye's strange appearance is more than a biological curiosity. Its unusual anatomy helps it find food in the ocean's twilight zone, a dimly lit region where sunlight is faint and spotting prey can be difficult.

Earlier observations by MBARI Senior Scientist Bruce Robison and Research Specialist Kim Reisenbichler helped scientists understand how this remarkable fish hunts.

Recent findings suggest that rather than constantly swimming in search of food, Macropinna can remain nearly motionless in the water, supported by its large fins, which provide both stability and precise control over its movements. Its tubular eyes normally point upward, allowing it to watch for the faint silhouettes of animals passing overhead.

Industry observers note that once the fish detects something worth eating, its eyes can rotate within a transparent dome of tissue covering the top of its head. This unusual ability allows the barreleye to maintain visual contact with prey as it changes position to capture it.

In a recent development, the clear, gelatinous covering serves another vital purpose. Barreleyes can feed on small crustaceans trapped among the stinging tentacles of deep-sea siphonophores, which are colonial animals related to jellyfish. The protective dome helps shield the fish's sensitive eyes while it retrieves its meal.

Rare 4K Footage Reveals Extraordinary Details.

Scientists were able to spend approximately 20 minutes watching the barreleye during the encounter. Using the high-resolution 4K camera aboard Doc Ricketts, they recorded unusually detailed views of its anatomy and movements.

The team also adjusted the vehicle's lighting to make the fish's transparent head shield easier to observe. These changes helped reveal the unusual structures that make this species so distinctive.

Industry observers note that ROV Pilot/Technician Cameron Law brought Doc Ricketts close enough to capture additional footage with the vehicle's virtual reality camera. The resulting 3D stereo imagery provides another perspective on the fish and could eventually be used in educational programs and public outreach.

It has been revealed that "Macropinna reminds us of the surprising ways that animals have adapted to thrive in the ocean's midwater," said Haddock. "So many of them survive by looking for subtle optical cues, like a shadow passing overhead, and this sets off evolutionary competition, pitting bioluminescent camouflage against increasingly sophisticated visual capabilities.".

An Extraordinary Sighting After Nearly 7,000 Deep-Sea Dives.

The rarity of the encounter becomes even more striking when considering MBARI's extensive exploration history. Over nearly 40 years, the institute has conducted approximately 7,000 remotely operated vehicle dives and accumulated more than 41,000 hours of deep-sea video. Despite that enormous effort, researchers have recorded just 11 encounters with Macropinna microstoma.

Sources indicate that MBARI's previous sighting of Macropinna occurred in 2021. The latest encounter represents two notable firsts for the institute: its first 4K footage of this species and its first observation of the fish from the new flagship research vessel David Packard.

Recent findings suggest that MBARI scientists have also encountered other members of the barreleye family during their ocean expeditions. This summer, researchers working with Schmidt Ocean Institute in the mid-Atlantic Ocean recorded another unusual deep-sea species, the binocular fish (Winteria telescopa).

Together, these encounters provide rare opportunities to observe the remarkable adaptations that allow animals to survive far beneath the ocean's surface, where light is scarce and much of the marine environment remains unexplored.

The Diving Deep for Delicate Drifters expedition was supported through the David and Lucile Packard Foundation's longstanding commitment to MBARI's scientific research.
