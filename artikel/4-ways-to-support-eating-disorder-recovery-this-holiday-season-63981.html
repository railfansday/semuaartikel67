4 Ways to Support Eating Disorder Recovery This Holiday Season

Industry observers note that the holidays are about spending time with friends and family, traditions, and meals. For someone in recovery from an eating disorder, though, the changes in routine, latest foods, and comments about eating and weight can make what is supposed to be an enjoyable time feel overwhelming.

Even someone who's made considerable progress in their eating disorder recovery can find themselves plagued by eating disorder thoughts or anxious about situations which previously would have been quite manageable. Family can be a source of this stress, especially when the subject of dieting, exercise, and appearance comes up.

It has been revealed that in my work as a psychotherapist specializing in eating disorders, I've found that most family members and friends genuinely want to be supportive. But even with the best intentions, they may not realize how their words or actions affect someone in recovery. Here are four ways loved ones can offer support during the holidays.

Industry observers note that how many times do you have conversations about food and weight over the holidays? Someone will be saying they're going to skip breakfast so they've got room for dinner. Someone else will be talking about their New Year’s resolution to lose weight. A relative will comment on someone's looks or joke about having to go to the gym after dessert.

Recent findings suggest that these comments are so ubiquitous that we tend to just ignore them. For someone in the eating disorder recovery process, though, those same comments can be a reinforcement of the very beliefs they're trying to dismantle (that certain foods are "bad," that you have to "earn" your food, that your body size is a measure of your worth, etc.). Try to avoid commenting on what other people are eating, how much they're eating, or whether their bodies have changed. This includes compliments about weight loss. You may not know why someone's weight has changed, and what you think is "just being nice" may actually reinforce eating disorder thoughts and behaviors. These comments also contribute to weight bias and stigma.

It has been revealed that pay attention to how you talk about your own eating, too. Describing yourself as "bad" for having dessert, or saying you're going to have to make it up, sends the message that eating is something you should feel guilty about.

It has been revealed that if another family member is starting to go down the diet-calories-and-body line of thinking, consider changing the subject. The person in eating disorder recovery doesn't necessarily have to be the one to speak up and set that boundary.

Holiday meals don't always happen at predictable times. Dinner may be served several hours later than usual, people may skip meals in anticipation of a large gathering, or there may be an expectation that everyone should eat at the same time.

For someone in the recovery phase from an eating disorder, eating regularly throughout the day may be an essential part of the treatment. They may need a snack just before the main holiday meal, or may need to eat when the rest of the family are skipping a meal because they want to leave room for dinner.

It has been revealed that comments like "You'll spoil your appetite" or "Can't you just wait until dinner?" can make an already stressful situation more difficult. What may be a very minor change in aims to someone without an eating disorder may be much more complicated for someone working to establish regular eating patterns.

If you're hosting, consider asking ahead of time whether there's anything you can do to help. You don't need to make an announcement or draw attention to someone's eating. Simply having food available in case they need to eat when others aren't can be helpful.

Unless you're directly involved in supporting your loved one's meal plan as part of their treatment, avoid monitoring their portions or questioning what they're eating. Even when it comes from a place of concern, having someone watch what you eat can increase anxiety and self-consciousness, making eating even more difficult.

Recent findings suggest that when someone you love has an eating disorder, it's understandable that you might worry about how they're doing. You may wonder whether they're eating enough, how treatment is going, or whether they're struggling more than they're letting on.

According to recent reports, when you're together for the holidays, though, try not to let concerns about your loved one's eating disorder dominate your interactions. Eating disorders can take up so much time and mental energy that people may lose interest in activities they once enjoyed, withdraw from relationships, or have difficulty engaging in everyday life. But that doesn't mean every conversation needs to revolve around their eating disorder or how they're doing in treatment.

Talk about things other than food and recovery. Ask about a show they're watching, a book they've read, a movie they've enjoyed, or something else that might interest them. Include them in family conversations and traditions, even if they seem less engaged than they used to be. The idea is to give them opportunities to connect with others without feeling pressured to participate or having every conversation come back to their eating disorder.

There's a lot of pressure to enjoy the holidays. We're supposed to look forward to family gatherings, appreciate time together, and participate in traditions that are meant to be fun. But the holidays can also bring up anxiety, grief, loneliness, and difficult family dynamics.

According to recent reports, for someone in eating disorder recovery, these feelings may be accompanied by anxiety around food and body image or guilt after eating. They may want to be part of the celebration while also feeling desperate to avoid certain aspects of it.

Recent findings suggest that if they say they're having a hard time, you may want to offer reassurance and try to convince them there's nothing to worry about. "It's only one meal," "Just relax," "Don't let that ruin your holiday," etc., are all things people say with the best of intentions but can quite often leave the person feeling misunderstood or frustrated with themselves for having the difficulty they do.

Instead of trying to convince them they shouldn't feel anxious, acknowledge what they're experiencing. You might say, "I know these gatherings can be stressful" or "I'm glad you're here, even though I know this isn't easy." You can also ask whether they'd like some company, want to talk, or need a little space.

Feeling anxious or uncomfortable doesn't necessarily mean someone isn't making progress in their eating disorder recovery. They may be working hard to participate in a holiday meal or eat foods they've previously avoided, even when eating disorder thoughts are especially loud. These experiences can take a lot of effort, and being able to face that discomfort without turning to familiar eating disorder behaviors is an important part of recovery.

According to recent reports, the holidays can be especially challenging for someone with an eating disorder. Being mindful of your words and behaviors, supporting their treatment, and continuing to include them in family traditions can help them feel supported and connected to the people and experiences they care about.
