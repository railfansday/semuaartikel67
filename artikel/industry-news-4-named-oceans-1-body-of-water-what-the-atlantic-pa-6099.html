Industry News: 4 named oceans, 1 body of water: what the Atlantic, Pacific, Indian and Arctic stand for

Sources indicate that NOAA claims Earth has one global ocean covering 71 percent of the planet. See what 4 of its named regions stand for.

Most people learned the oceans from a classroom globe, where names such as Pacific and Atlantic sit in different places as if each were its own body of water. The water on that globe is one connected ocean. The familiar names mark regions of that single ocean. They do not mark separate oceans. The count of oceans therefore depends on where people decide one region stops and the next begins.

Industry observers note that even the number of oceans is not fixed, and it moves between four and five depending on a single decision about borders. NOAA counts the Southern Ocean as a basin of its own when it compares the oceans by size. One common definition places it everywhere south of 60° S, according to Britannica. The fifth ocean is a line of latitude that mapmakers drew around Antarctica, and the water inside it is the same water as before. If drawing one line can add an ocean, then drawing other lines can make the oceans already named larger or smaller. That happens region by region, and by very different amounts.

The NOAA National Ocean Service set out to count how many named oceans exist and what they are, including the Pacific, the Atlantic and the Arctic. The agency that set out to count the oceans concluded that there is really only one to count. "There is only one global ocean," the NOAA National Ocean Service noted. Of Earth's entire surface, 71 percent lies under that single ocean. Each named ocean is a convention people laid over that water, so the count is a count of names. Yet the names are not interchangeable. The regions they mark differ enormously in size and depth, in how well people know them, and in what they mean for people living on land. Each name stands for something different. One is a region whose size depends on where its borders fall. Another is the largest region, and earthquakes and volcanoes ring it. A third is a region tied to the rains of South and Southeast Asia. The last is a frozen region full of life.

The Atlantic is one of the oceans people know best, but it is not the biggest. The NOAA National Ocean Service counts it among the four historically named oceans and among the three best known. The Atlantic is still second in both area and volume, according to Britannica. Second place is a vast expanse, and the 85.133 million square kilometers most people would accept as the Atlantic's size looks exact and fixed. That figure holds only as long as everyone agrees on where the Atlantic ends.

Industry observers note that the Atlantic has the least settled borders of the significant oceans, even though its name is one of the oldest and most familiar on any map. "The Atlantic exhibits the largest change in surface area and volume when its marginal seas are subtracted," Alyn C. Duxbury, a research associate professor emeritus of oceanography at the University of Washington, Seattle, remarked, as reported by Britannica. The question of how big the Atlantic is therefore has no single answer. The answer depends on whether the bays and seas along its edges count as part of it. Even an ocean whose existence no one disputes has a size that comes partly from where people draw its boundaries.

The Atlantic's edges affect its depth as well as its area. Its surface is larger than the Indian Ocean's 70.560 million square kilometers, yet the Atlantic is on average 3,646 meters deep, compared with 3,741 meters for the Indian Ocean, according to Britannica. An ocean that is large but relatively shallow fits a size that rests partly on shallow bordering seas, though the depth figures alone don't prove it.

The Atlantic outranks the Indian Ocean in surface area, but its water does not reach as far down on average.

Recent findings suggest that the Pacific Ocean is one of the four historically named oceans and among the three best known, according to the NOAA National Ocean Service. Calling it familiar undersells it, because the Pacific covers 168.723 million square kilometers of the planet's surface, according to Britannica. A globe that prints each ocean's name in the same type suggests a set of rough equals. Anyone who pictures the oceans that way is picturing the labels on the map, not the water beneath them.

Industry observers note that the Pacific is as deep as it is wide. NOAA reports that it is the deepest ocean basin, and that depth does not rest on one famous trench. Its floor lies an average of 3,970 meters down, according to Britannica, so great depth is the ordinary condition across the whole basin rather than a record set in a single spot. The Pacific's lead comes from depth as well as surface, and it amounts to more than the biggest patch of blue on a globe.

The Pacific's edges deserve as much attention as its size. The Pacific basin is known as the Ring of Fire, NOAA reports, and the name comes from the volcanoes and earthquakes clustered around its edges. People who live along the Pacific's borders have a reason to care about them that goes beyond distance or trade. For them, the edge of this ocean is a source of danger.

The largest region of the one ocean is also the one with a ring of volcanoes along its border, and its size tells only half of its story.

People know the Indian Ocean for its history and its weather, not for its size. On a globe it sits among the big familiar names as if it were their equal. It is one of the four historically named oceans and among the three best known, according to the NOAA National Ocean Service. NOAA ranks it as only the third-largest ocean basin, which makes it the smallest of the three oceans most people can name. Its place among them must rest on something other than how much water it holds, and that something is what people have done on it and what it does for people on land.

People learned the Indian Ocean's name early because they sailed it early. It was the first ocean that people navigated extensively, according to Encyclopedia.com. It became a working sea rather than an empty space at the edge of a chart, and its name stands for a sea route as much as for a basin.

According to recent reports, people who live far from the Indian Ocean's coasts also depend on its weather. Its monsoons are the source of heavy rainfall in western India and Southeast Asia, according to Encyclopedia.com, so the wet season in those regions has its origin over this ocean. The connection also runs from land to sea. The Ganges, the Indus and the Brahmaputra are among the sources of the ocean's sediment, which ties the Indian Ocean to the great river systems of South Asia.

Of the familiar ocean names, this one says the least about size and the most about how closely people on shore live with a stretch of water.

Sources indicate that the Arctic shares the old title of ocean with three far better-known regions, yet it is the smallest and least familiar of them all. It is less commonly known than the Atlantic, Pacific and Indian oceans, according to the NOAA National Ocean Service, and NOAA ranks it the smallest of the five ocean basins. That ranking puts the Arctic below even the Southern Ocean, the basin that mapmakers created by drawing a line around Antarctica. The least-known ocean still lies at the top of very familiar maps, with Alaska, Canada, Greenland, Norway and Russia all facing it along their northern coasts, according to NOAA. Its small size and low profile say little about how much life the region holds.

The Arctic's sea ice is a home for living things, not a bare white surface over empty water. Many people picture the top of the globe as frozen and lifeless. Bacteria and algae live inside the brine channels that run through the sea ice, NOAA reports, which places life inside the ice at the bottom of the food chain. Polar bears live on that same ice, so the frozen surface supports animals as large as bears as well as organisms too small to see. That makes the region a working ecosystem rather than an empty stretch of white.

In this smallest region of the one ocean, the frozen surface is itself the habitat, and any loss of ice would leave less room for the algae inside it and the bears on top of it.
