When to Arrive at (and Leave) a Party, According to the Pros

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that once again, we find ourselves on the verge of party season, that most wonderful (and exhausting) time of year. The nights are long and busy; the days are short and tired—and it’s all totally worth it. This season also ushers in the usual quandaries: What to bring, what to wear, and, perhaps the most confusing of all, when to arrive.

It has been revealed that everyone agrees that the best dinner party guests know to give their hosts a bit of time past the official start, but how much is right? And how does that change for a cocktail party? Fear not; we’ve gathered the best and brightest party-goers to answer all those questions: how to be late enough to be fashionable and early enough to be polite, courtesy of those who know best.

Sources indicate that ELLE Decor Rising Star Romilly Newman, the chef and food stylist who towers her chocolate cakes as high as her well-decked greenery, thinks “30 minutes is the sweet spot,” she indicates. “As a frequent host, I'm always running behind and secretly wishing everyone would arrive late. Whether it's running to buy bags of ice, ironing the tablecloth, or an extra braise in the oven.” She warns, however, “An hour late is when it starts to get into dangerous territory—as the host is most likely waiting to serve a hot meal.” But, when it comes to dinner, when it’s time to sit down, it’s also time to stay down: “Cocktail parties are come and go, dinner parties are a nightlong commitment.”.

According to recent reports, ivy Getty, a stalwart of guest lists from Milan to Manhattan, suggests arriving a bit earlier: “The best window is 15-20 minutes, so as not to rush the host. I don’t really think of these as rules: they feel like common sense.” She adds: “Obviously, if you are going to a funeral or graduation you arrive early—or on time at the latest.”.

Ryan Nordheimer, a Latest Yorker who aims elaborate monthly party potlucks, suggests that “the sweet spot” for those headed to dinner parties is 5 to 15 minutes after the host tells you to arrive. He adds, “please never be early. Those 5 extra minutes always come in handy as the host.” If it’s a cocktail party, however, particularly one on the longer side, “you can arrive right on time to 30 minutes late.” Plus, he says, “You can always text the host the day before and ask—just don’t ask them the night of.”.

In a recent development, then, when every interesting topic has been exhausted, and any talk about the weather has completely dried up, there’s the question of departures. Newman says, “I like to think of a dinner party like a play in three acts. There's the mingling, the meal, and the lingering. When the host starts clearing the table and blowing out candles, it's time to pack up.”.

Sources indicate that nordheimer has a more scientific approach to closing time: on “a weeknight, I like to have people leave around 10pm,” he says. He’ll go an hour later on weekends. “But every host is different. The host reserves the right to kick people out; just start yawning a lot to make it obvious,” he notes. Clear signals—and choosing guests who can pick up on them—is key.

It has been revealed that as for how a host should plan, “You should have most of your food done by the time guests arrive,” states Newman. At the end of the day, though, as long as a guest arrives ready to keep the energy high and be gracious to their host, they can’t go wrong.
