In 2021, Kwabena Boahen Asare found himself in at the deep end when he joined a skin cancer laboratory to gain hands-on research experience.

Sources indicate that for Asare, who was a first-year undergraduate student at Dartmouth College in Hanover, New Hampshire, at the time, it was an exciting but daunting prospect. Not only was he the sole bachelor’s programme student working alongside senior researchers with years of experience under their belts, he was also the only Black person in the lab. Although Asare’s mentor and colleagues were supportive and encouraging, he felt overwhelmed and out of his depth. “There was a steep learning curve,” he says.

Recent findings suggest that at the time, Asare viewed his struggles as a sign that he didn’t belong in research. This feeling was compounded by the fact that he was surrounded by colleagues who didn’t share his background as a first-generation student from Ghana.

His experience, and those of many like him, is commonly labelled as impostor syndrome — the fear of not being as competent or knowledgeable as other people think, despite objective accomplishments. In a world that often prizes unflinching self-confidence and boldness, impostor syndrome is usually cast as a psychological flaw that the individual needs to ‘fix’. But it can be a natural response to working in a challenging environment or striving towards an ambitious goal, organizational psychologists say.

Industry observers note that impostor syndrome can also be a reaction to systemic bias and exclusion in the workplace, particularly for people from under-represented groups, including women, claims Ruchika Malhotra, an inclusion strategist and founder of Candour, a workplace consultancy in Seattle, Washington. The company advises organizations on strategies to create diverse teams and inclusive cultures. One study1 conducted at universities in the United States found that women who work in scientific fields that particularly value intellectual brilliance are more likely to feel like impostors than are their male colleagues.

Much of the discussion about impostor syndrome pathologizes healthy self-doubt and shifts the responsibility onto individuals instead of the environments that exclude and marginalize them in the first place, adds Malhotra. “A lot of the ways that it’s been talked about haven’t been constructive,” she says.

Although it comes with drawbacks, in some situations, impostor syndrome can be beneficial. For instance, it can help people be better prepared and more focused, particularly when they have a long to-do list.

“Impostor thoughts are almost like your superpower when things get pretty overwhelming,” says Basima Tewfik, an organizational psychologist who studies impostor syndrome at Massachusetts Institute of Technology in Cambridge.

Looking back, Asare indicates that his impostor syndrome prompted him to reflect on why he wanted to become a researcher in the first place. It has also made him more conscious of his resilience, how he needs to improve and when to seek help, as well as giving him more empathy. This has made him better able to support younger students and researchers who are feeling out of place. “It became my strength,” says Asare, who is now an oncology researcher and consultant at Dartmouth Hitchcock Medical Center in Lebanon, New Hampshire.

Impostor syndrome was first described as ‘impostor phenomenon’ in a 1978 paper on high-achieving women2 co-authored by psychologists Pauline Clance and Suzanne Imes, both then at Georgia State University in Atlanta. They wrote that people who experience this phenomenon “maintain a strong belief that they are not intelligent; in fact they are convinced that they have fooled anyone who thinks otherwise”.

Industry observers note that in the decades since, impostor phenomenon has morphed into a syndrome, even though it isn’t a medical diagnosis, nor is it classified as a psychiatric disorder. A review published in 20233 suggests that around 70% of people will feel unworthy of their accomplishments at some point in their lives. These feelings are even more prevalent in academia, with a 2012 study4 finding that as many as 82% of graduate students experience impostor syndrome to at least some degree. This pattern of thinking often affects high achievers and is associated with lower job satisfaction, hindered performance and burnout. Although some studies, including the 2012 paper, suggest that impostor syndrome affects mostly women and people from other groups under-represented in academia, others argue that it arises in specific situations rather than among particular groups. Relatively few studies have investigated whether commonly cited strategies, such as listing one’s accomplishments and accepting compliments from others, are actually effective.

According to recent reports, impostor syndrome has become a catchphrase in popular culture, with dozens of articles, books and courses describing what it is and how people can overcome it. But in the scientific literature, at least, the exact definition of impostor syndrome, or phenomenon, varies widely. This has created a murky picture of what it really is and how it affects people, says Tewfik. “There are a lot of different ways people describe this phenomenon,” she says. The loose definition has made it easy to confuse impostor syndrome, which causes people to make inferences about what other people think of them, with regular self-doubt, which is more of a personal reflection of your own capabilities, according to Tewfik.

In a recent development, much of the research on impostor syndrome has also used measures that have made it difficult to distinguish from other psychological patterns, such as negative affectivity, a personality trait that is linked to a tendency to experience negative emotions such as fear, anxiety and insecurity, adds Tewfik. This crossover might explain why most studies on impostor syndrome have shown only negative outcomes, such as a lack of career planning and low motivation to lead5. “It’s possible that what we’ve been studying is actually something much more akin to fear,” says Tewfik.

According to recent reports, this overly negative slant is exacerbated by the fact that many workplaces, including academic ones, place too much emphasis on self-confidence, says Tomas Chamorro-Premuzic, an organizational psychologist at University College London. One 2024 study6 found that workers who display confidence are perceived as more competent and dominant, irrespective of their actual ability.

Recent findings suggest that if impostor syndrome hinders a person’s ability to work or form relationships, it’s usually a symptom of a deeper issue or clinical condition, such as an anxiety disorder. But feeling like you’re about to be ‘found out’ before delivering a major presentation, for example, is usually a sign that you’re being challenged or working outside your comfort zone, says Chamorro-Premuzic. “There is a very healthy degree somewhere that we’re not appreciating,” he adds (see ‘A toolkit for working with impostor syndrome’).

Recent findings suggest that in scientists, impostor thoughts can simply be a by-product of thinking critically and approaching things with a healthy dose of scepticism, claims Samantha Sutton, an independent career coach for scientists in Boston, Massachusetts. It’s also often a natural response to working in a high-achieving, competitive environment or towards an ambitious goal and should be normalized instead of treated as a problem to solve, she adds. “If you’re up against something significant, you should be scared,” says Sutton. When she communicates this to her clients, they are often relieved to hear that they don’t need to strive for total self-confidence before taking steps towards their goals. “They realize that nothing is wrong,” adds Sutton.
