Munich startup RHISE AI raises angel round for personal AI assistant Lucie

In January 2021, Andy Loy’s Munich startup KONUX raised $80 million in a Series C round, and around the same time, its founder was in hospital, exhausted and burned out at 29.

Loy eventually stepped away from the chief executive role, but when he returned to building technology, he had a different idea of what it should do. Rather than create another product competing for people’s attention, he wanted to build one that could give it back.

That idea became RHISE AI, the Munich tech giant behind Lucie, a personal AI assistant that connects information from different parts of a user’s life, including health data, calendars, and email.

The company announced an angel funding round today, raising €2.66 million backed by more than 40 investors. Among them are Andy Bechtolsheim, co-founder of Arista Networks and an early investor in Google, and Prince Max von und zu Liechtenstein, chairman of LGT. Former Siemens and Alcoa chief Klaus Kleinfeld, Splunk founder Michael Baum, and Bayern Munich footballer Leon Goretzka are also investors.

Industry observers note that michael Baum says he has spent much of his career backing technology designed to capture people’s time. Lucie, he believes, takes a different approach.

“I’ve spent 35 years building and backing technology that wanted more of people’s time. Lucie gives it back. I invested, and I use it daily,” he said in a statement.

Recent findings suggest that loy founded RHISE AI with chief technology officer Danil Gontovnik, Samy Samandjeu, and Chris Seybold. Their product is built around a relatively straightforward premise: an assistant should be able to recognise when something in your life needs attention, without requiring you to ask first.

According to recent reports, lucie brings together information from connected services, including wearables, calendars, and inboxes. Its multi-agent system, Heartbeat, monitors that information in the background. If it spots something unusual, such as a suspiciously high bill, it can suggest what to do next. The user must approve an action before the assistant carries it out, according to the company.

“Personal AI will soon know more about us than any technology before it. The question is who it works for,” Loy said.

Sources indicate that that question is becoming harder to avoid as technology companies develop assistants with access to increasingly personal information. RHISE AI notes it has designed Lucie to comply with GDPR from the outset, stores personal data in Germany, and does not use advertising or sell user data.

More than half of its active users have connected wearables that share real-time health readings with the assistant, the tech giant noted.

For a European startup, the timing is interesting, as some of the biggest consumer AI rolls out of the year have left European people waiting. Apple’s AI-powered Siri features are missing from EU iPhones, Google’s Gemini Spark does not include the European Economic Area, and Meta’s Muse is currently available only in the US and Canada.

That leaves room for European companies to make their case, particularly as concerns about data protection and dependence on American technology grow. Meta’s decision to use conversations with its AI assistant to inform advertising recommendations, introduced in December, has added to those concerns. The policy excludes the EU, UK, and South Korea.

In Germany, 72% of respondents consider the country too dependent on the US for AI, according to a Bitkom survey.

RHISE AI is clearly hoping that its European roots will count with users who are reluctant to give another Substantial Tech company access to their personal lives. But data hosting and privacy commitments are only part of the equation. People will also need to trust the assistant’s decisions, particularly when it can access health information, private correspondence, and financial details.

The assistant at the moment supports English and German and is available only on Apple devices. An Android version is planned.

Recent findings suggest that for Loy, the project is a considerable departure from the industrial AI business he built with KONUX. The underlying ambition, however, is still to make technology useful. This time, he wants to measure that usefulness by what people no longer have to spend their time doing.
