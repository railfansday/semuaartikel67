The 2026 documentary You Can See Everything is enjoying acclaim, earning very high scores from professional critics (97 percent) and fans (95 percent). In the film, director Nathan Fielder interviews infamous biotech fraudster Elizabeth Holmes and her partner Billy Evans, having been granted close access to the couple, as the title indicates.

At the same time, there are reports of Fielder’s status as something of a surprise celebrity crush. This isn’t the first time Fielder’s been upheld as an example of an unconventional celebrity crush. He appeared shirtless on the cover of Latest York magazine in 2022 (2) with the caption “Hot cat dad alert.” The cover story discussed his show The Rehearsal.

Celebrity crushes have been debated as potentially questionable. New York magazine referred to crushes on Fielder, questioning his “inexplicable attractiveness” (1,2).

Sources indicate that psychologists call celebrity crushes parasocial romantic relationships, or PSRR for short. Parasocial refers to the idea of the unreality of the relationship because most people who have a crush on a celebrity will never interact with the person, much less date them. If this is the case, why do people have celebrity crushes, including the less conventional ones?

Recent findings suggest that parasocial relationships of various sorts (friendships and romantic attachments) are mostly carried out in the imagination of the person with the feelings. Often journalists and members of the public do not understand their value. A good way to think about these common feelings is that our fantasies are part of our imagination. Imagining social attachments means playing with the idea of friendship or romance in our own hearts and minds. It has benefits, like being a way to rehearse real relationships and to avoid the downsides of those relationships. There are potential costs such as expecting too much of relationships that don’t fit the ideal of the Hollywood romance or being relatively unimpressed by the looks of regular people.

According to recent reports, it’s important to keep in mind that our real relationships also use our imaginations. We fantasize about the crush we have on an office mate, and we imagine telling off the school bully, for example. It may be strange to realize that we probably spend less time actually interacting with real-life romantic partners than we spend daydreaming about interacting with that person. (Daydreaming is very common.).

According to recent reports, what about that so-called offbeat crush? Let’s take the example of Nathan Fielder. Spoiler alert: The following contains references to events in his documentary. First, Nathan Fielder may not look like Chris Hemsworth, but he is still a reasonably attractive man. We may have lost perspective given that Hollywood beauty is extreme. If we saw him in a café in our hometown, we would probably find him attractive. [Note: if you don’t believe me, imagine a picture of an average Walmart shopper in your hometown, and put that next to a picture of Nathan Fielder. Compare and contrast.].

In a recent development, but there are particular aspects of the documentary You Can See Everything that likely increase his appeal. The film is very emotional and has a very noticeable soundtrack designed to heighten feelings. But more than this, there are extreme cues for intimacy and self-disclosure, both of which build and increase attraction.

If you have seen this documentary or other footage of Elizabeth Holmes, you may have noticed her very unusual eye contact. An expert on lying suggests that this very low blink rate and intense stare is one of her manipulation techniques (3). Looking someone directly in the eye is something people interpret as a sign of honesty. So you can imagine that this kind of unusually intense eye contact is something people fall for. In You Can See Everything, both Elizabeth and Nathan present with this intensity.

Why draw attention to eye contact? Well, in social interactions, eye contact is also a sign of intimacy. What’s more, maintaining eye contact and disclosing highly personal information are indicators of social closeness. Friends tell us their secrets. Intimates look deeply into our eyes. This combination of intimacy and telling secrets might well make us feel close to Nathan, regardless of his motives for using it.

Recent findings suggest that isn’t it a well-known trope to say that men are bad at sharing their emotions and making themselves vulnerable? So, it follows that someone doing that on a kind of epic scale might be attractive. Taking the idea of vulnerability further, Nathan appears to be charmed by Elizabeth’s manipulation techniques. The film (again, spoilers!) leaves us with the idea that perhaps he’s even fallen in love with her. This is surely an ironic end to a documentary where an interviewer falls for the manipulations of a woman who is known as one of the most epic liars in recent history.

Nathan indicates a host of other attractive characteristics that are classically crushworthy. Some of these include his intelligence and social status, his bravery in confronting Elizabeth with very difficult questions, and his ability to show both empathy and vulnerability.

In a recent development, we naturally want to attach to desirable individuals. Part of this is based on cultural stories about what makes a desirable mate. The documentary itself plays with some of these cultural stories, for example, in presenting the unconventional love story between Elizabeth Holmes and partner Billy Evans.

To sum up, crushes on public figures like actors and filmmakers are very normal. Many of us have probably spent time imagining we might meet them and that they would like us. Sometimes a celebrity crush might seem unconventional, but we should keep in mind the crazy high standards of Hollywood beauty and the host of other characteristics we find appealing. Things like an ability to be vulnerable and open, to be brave, and to demonstrate your ability to stare unblinkingly into another person’s eyes are things that will catch and hold our attention.

Recent findings suggest that whether or not you are crushing on Nathan Fielder, Elizabeth Holmes, or any celebrity, the documentary You Can See Everything is a (fever?) dream of sorts for anyone interested in the psychology of human social interactions, especially at the extremes. You’ve probably never seen anything quite like it. If you have a chance to catch it, I hope it inspires some interesting conversations for you.
