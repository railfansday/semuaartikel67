How to Become Change-Curious

We are swimming in a sea of content about change: social media, health and wellness reporting, and advertising (including those little videos at the gas station). Messages about change are inescapable and part of the background noise of everyday life. Often we ignore the onslaught of “be better, do better” messaging, and other times we eagerly try to change ourselves because we so passionately want our lives to be different.

What if we paused for a moment, and instead of forcing ourselves to just do it, we stepped back and shifted toward exploring our relationship with change—to befriend it—so change can increasingly be about wonder and learning and less about stress and avoidance?

Like any troubling relationship, it can be useful to explore some of the dynamics that are not working for us. If we are fortunate to find ourselves changing with ease and little friction, that is a cause to celebrate. For most of us, at least some of the time, we have some difficult experiences engaging in change. We might tell ourselves that change is good; that we should want it. Such a message is difficult to accept when we are not certain change is what we want or need. We might also believe that if we don’t want change, there must be something wrong with us; we are resistant or self-sabotaging or anxious or overwhelmed. Some of these things might be true, but labeling ourselves brings up shame, and shame can be crippling, as it can bring to mind past failures or negative messaging.

Change can also be hard because it takes work. Even if we are completely inspired and motivated to change, developing habits and learning fresh ways to do things takes time; a recent study found it takes an average of 66 days for a habit to form. For any individual, it can take more or less time for a behavior change to become automatic.

Change is work; it takes time and disrupts our lives. We have cognitive, emotional, and behavioral friction that makes it more difficult to start and complete. Yet, despite the difficulty, we seek change. We want our lives to improve, so we purchase lots of self-help, coaching, therapy, gym memberships, food plans, and surgeries to get started. Across cultures throughout the lifespan, some aspects of change and growth are constantly occurring as we seek to change ourselves and our circumstances.

Given that change is both hard and something we desire, what if we considered approaching the change process with a learner’s mind? Instead of forcing ourselves to endure change or shaming ourselves into it, we can befriend change through cultivating curiosity about all it has to offer. Wondering about change and being open to all the ways we show up in the change process has the potential to make change less daunting.

We have many options to be curious about change.

Industry observers note that noticing change in our environment, and then noticing change in ourselves. We can start by spending some time noticing change in the weather or change in plants. Maybe we notice how construction or demolition is changing a neighborhood, or how our neighbor's families are changing and growing. We can then consider changes in our own experience over the years—in our living situations, job, or social life. We can notice the ones that have been the most meaningful. How did these changes happen? Some changes occur because of luck or circumstances, and it is likely that we were able to take advantage of our good fortune. Some changes occur because we worked hard at making change. How did we do it? What characteristics helped us become a “changer,” and how could we tap into those characteristics if we wanted to change again?

Envisioning what change might look like. This is a common approach to increasing motivation for change. What if we decided we could envision something for ourselves in the same way we read a book or watch a movie? We could envision being in a few different situations and view our story with wonder instead of expectation.

It has been revealed that looking at the process more than the outcome. We tend to be outcome-oriented. We see products that promise to help us build muscle, learn a language, or see younger, more beautiful skin in a few weeks or a couple of months. The promise of relatively quick fixes is so compelling that even when we logically understand there is no way a product can live up to our expectations, we might find ourselves literally buying it. Purchasing a service or product to help us change is not necessarily a problem, and it can be a good thing. Expecting the product to have specific outcomes is where we can become discouraged. So many things impact the actual outcome of our attempts to change: time, environment, our body limits and composition, other demands taking our time.

According to recent reports, shifting our attention to the process requires that we step away from holding the outcome as our top priority. Instead, it’s about going along for the ride and adopting a perspective that we are learning and improving our lives by noticing how we feel after engaging in exercise, making the effort to practice a new instrument, or consuming nutritious foods.

According to recent reports, sampling change. We can check it out and see what it does for us. Events such as Sober October or Dry January were created to help drinkers wanting to explore, but not commit to, changing their drinking behaviors—to test out life without (or with less) alcohol. Virtually any change can be tested out and explored. Sampling a upcoming behavior is not the same as engaging in a dedicated lifestyle change, but it allows for some action. It also provides an opportunity to approach a new way of being in a manner that reduces overwhelm; a chance to wonder about what might be possible.

Sources indicate that curiosity is a powerful frame of mind. It has the potential to tame the intensity of change and open up possibilities for transformation that come from the power of exploration and wonder.
