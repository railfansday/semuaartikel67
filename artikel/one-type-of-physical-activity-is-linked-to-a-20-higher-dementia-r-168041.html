One type of physical activity is linked to a 20% higher dementia risk

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that regular physical activity has long been recognized as a way to help protect against dementia. However, upcoming research from the University of Southern California (USC) suggests that the circumstances in which people are physically active may be just as key as the amount of activity they get.

In a recent development, an analysis involving more than 4.2 million people found that recreational activities such as walking, cycling, running, swimming, and playing sports were associated with a significantly lower risk of dementia. Surprisingly, people who engaged in higher levels of physical activity at work appeared to face an increased risk of developing the condition.

Published in The Lancet Public Health, the study is the first systematic investigation into whether a phenomenon known as the "physical activity paradox" also applies to dementia. Already well established in cardiovascular research, this paradox refers to the observation that physical labor does not necessarily offer the same health advantages as recreational exercise and may even be associated with a greater risk of disease.

Led by David Raichlen, professor of biological sciences and anthropology at the USC Dornsife College of Letters, Arts and Sciences, the research team examined data from 74 cohort and case-control studies. Together, the studies included more than 4.2 million people between the ages of 45 and 93 from 30 countries. Over a median follow-up period of 10 years, researchers monitored diagnoses of all-cause dementia, Alzheimer's disease, and vascular dementia.

Industry observers note that participants who engaged in higher levels of recreational physical activity had a 24% lower risk of dementia compared with the least active individuals. Physical activity associated with household chores also appeared to offer benefits, although this finding was supported by only one study. In contrast, higher levels of physical activity at work were linked to a 20% greater risk of dementia. Physically active commuting was also associated with a modest increase in risk, but evidence for this relationship came from just two studies.

"The findings challenge the long-held assumption that 'every move counts' for brain health," mentioned Natan Feter, the study's first author, a postdoctoral fellow in Raichlen's Evolutionary Biology of Physical Activity Lab at USC and a researcher in the Graduate Program of Epidemiology at Universidade Federal do Rio Grande do Sul (Brazil).

Why Exercise and Physical Labor May Affect the Brain Differently.

Researchers often group recreational exercise, manual labor, household chores, and active commuting together when measuring "Physical activity." However, the authors suggest that the circumstances surrounding these activities vary considerably and could influence their long-term health effects.

According to recent reports, people who exercise during their free time generally have greater control over the intensity and nature of their activities. Recreational exercise can also provide opportunities to socialize and relieve stress. In comparison, physically demanding occupations may require repeated movements, extended periods of standing, heavy lifting, and insufficient time to recover, along with exposure to potentially harmful workplace conditions.

According to recent reports, the researchers do not believe that physical movement itself is likely to explain the elevated dementia risk associated with occupational activity. Instead, the relationship may arise from the social and environmental factors commonly associated with manual labor.

It has been revealed that physically demanding occupations are more likely to expose workers to psychological and social stress, limited control over their work, unsafe conditions, and higher levels of air and noise pollution. Each of these factors has independently been associated with dementia risk. Workers may also struggle to participate in recreational exercise because of exhaustion, limited free time, or a lack of access to safe spaces for physical activity.

Opposing Patterns in Dementia Risk.

The researchers also discovered that the relationship between physical activity and dementia risk was not linear and followed opposite patterns depending on the type of activity.

Industry observers note that as recreational exercise increased, dementia risk gradually declined until the apparent benefits leveled off. Occupational physical activity revealed the reverse pattern, with dementia risk increasing at higher activity levels before eventually reaching a plateau.

Important Limitations and Unanswered Questions.

It has been revealed that the authors emphasize that there is still too little research on physical activity related to household chores and commuting to draw definitive conclusions about either. Most of the available evidence also comes from high-income countries, despite projections that low- and middle-income nations will experience the greatest increases in dementia cases in the coming decades.

Another limitation is that almost all the studies depended on participants reporting their own physical activity. This approach can introduce errors if people do not accurately remember their activity levels. Nevertheless, Feter mentioned the consistency of the findings across several additional statistical analyses suggests that the observed relationships warrant serious attention.

It has been revealed that the researchers stress that their findings should not discourage people from pursuing physically active occupations. Since the analysis relied on observational research, it cannot establish that occupational physical activity causes dementia.

According to recent reports, instead, the results underscore the importance of examining the conditions under which people are physically active. Recreational exercise continued to be associated with a lower risk of dementia, while occupational activity remained linked to a higher risk even after researchers accounted for differences in age, education, and chronic health conditions.

How Sedentary Activities May Also Influence Brain Health.

Recent findings suggest that the importance of context may extend beyond physical activity. Separate research by Raichlen, Feter, and their colleagues, published recently in the journal Alzheimer's & Dementia, suggests that different types of sedentary behavior may also be associated with differences in brain structure.

In that study, mentally stimulating activities performed while sitting, such as working at a desk, were associated with greater volume in certain regions of the cerebral cortex than more passive activities, such as watching television. These differences included brain regions involved in executive function.

Together, the findings could have notable implications for public health strategies aimed at reducing dementia risk. The authors suggest that encouraging people to "move more" may not be sufficient without also addressing the circumstances in which physical activity takes place.

Recent findings suggest that "In other words, when it comes to protecting the brain, the context of physical activity may matter just as much as movement itself," Raichlen stated. "Improving access to safe parks, walking trails and recreational facilities, ensuring people have adequate leisure time for exercise, and reducing harmful workplace exposures may ultimately prove as important as promoting physical activity itself.".
