Machina Aims to Democratize Car Customization with Innovative Robots

It has been revealed that automotive manufacturing is a laborious, resource-intensive process. While the complex metropolises that are modern car factories work well for high-volume products, it is often prohibitively expensive for automakers to retool these plants to create distinct body panels for limited-production models. Now a California-based organization called Machina Labs aims to plug that gap with an innovative metal manufacturing process that promises speed, flexibility, and cost-savings.

According to recent reports, machina’s business is based around its RoboCraftsman platform, which it basically pitches as a factory in a box. The system uses a pair of large robotic arms to perform a variety of tasks on metal panels, from striking designs into pre-stamped panels to fully forming new pieces. The robotic arms are controlled by software that can switch between different projects as promptly as Machina can upload new designs, and the system also scans the completed panel to see if any additional work needs to be done, and to trim, finish, and heat treat the pieces. The entire setup can be folded into two shipping containers that can then be easily transported.

Machina Labs was founded in 2019 and so far has mostly served clients in the aerospace and defense industries. But now Machina wants to enter the automotive space, launching a new division called Machina Rebellis. With its RoboCraftsman platform, Machina aims to give automakers a way to skip the costly retooling process, with its technology providing a more efficient method to rapidly craft customizable body panels, allowing OEMs to efficiently build more distinctive limited-edition cars.

Machina’s interest in the automotive world started a few years back when it built a truck called the Anvil, based on the Ford F-150 Lightning, to demonstrate the unique body panels its metalworking machines could produce. While still fairly crude, this early prototype got Toyota’s attention, and the Japanese automotive giant is now an investor in Machina Labs via Woven Capital, Toyota’s venture capital division.

It has been revealed that the process has improved vastly since the Anvil was built, and to demonstrate this, Machina Labs created a latest show vehicle, the Lassen, which is being shown at the Los Angeles auto show. Based on a Toyota Tacoma, the Lassen wears a unique hood, tailgate, and fenders, showing off the sort of customization that Machina’s process is capable of. Machina worked with Callum, the design house formed by Ian Callum, to pen the look of these panels before manufacturing them using the RoboCraftsman platform.

Recent findings suggest that while the Lassen isn’t a drastic departure from a standard Tacoma, the changes that Machina made would typically take around four to six months to produce via traditional methods, the tech giant remarked at a media event at its second factory in Chatsworth, California. Using its RoboCraftsman platform, Machina was able to take the Lassen from sketches to a finished product in just two and a half weeks.

Industry observers note that the Lassen shows off Machina’s two main capabilities. The hood and tailgate demonstrate the RoboCraftsman’s ability to re-strike pre-stamped panels with custom designs. The Lassen’s tailgate has a bold embossed Machina script, while the hood also says Machina and has a topographical map of its namesake, Mount Lassen in California. The RoboCraftsman platform can put just about any design on a metal panel, and Machina also had tailgates on display featuring a retro Toyota design and a Los Angeles Rams logo.

Recent findings suggest that the fenders, meanwhile, are entirely fresh panels formed by the RoboCraftsman, and they have a bulging, powerful look. These fenders started as a sheet of metal—Machina works in both aluminum and steel—before being shaped by the robotic arms. This process is a bit slower than the re-striking process, which can take minutes to complete, but a fully formed panel can be finished within hours.

Sources indicate that machina believes its RoboCraftsman platform is best suited to production runs in the hundreds and thousands. This could include special-edition models for large OEMs, or working with aftermarket companies to create body kits and other visual customizations. OEMs can also use Machina’s process to swiftly create body panels for early pre-production vehicles, typically an expensive and time-consuming part of the development cycle. In the future, Machina also envisions sending its RoboCraftsman units directly to OEM manufacturing sites to speed up the process even further.

In a recent development, over the past decade, high-end carmakers realized there is a lot of money in personalization, leading to a surge of one-off and limited-production supercars, designed to the exacting specification desired by their wealthy buyers. These cars, often hand-built, are incredibly expensive. Machina Labs says that its RoboCraftsman platform could democratize customization, with the speed and flexibility at which it can work allowing automakers to save money and offer personalized details on more ordinary, everyday vehicles.

In a recent development, the RoboCraftsman platform provides a way to quickly manufacture custom body panels, and the flexibility to switch among a large number of different projects in a short amount of time. The Lassen serves as a proof of concept as Machina courts automotive companies, and seeing the RoboCraftsman operate in person, we certainly see a space in the automotive world for Machina’s quick and flexible manufacturing innovation.
