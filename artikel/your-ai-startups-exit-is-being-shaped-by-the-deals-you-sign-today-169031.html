Your AI Startup’s Exit Is Being Shaped By The Deals You Sign Today

By Aaron Rubin and Greg Volkmar.

Valuations for AI companies have soared, and the companies are moving faster than ever. In the financings our firm has worked on so far in 2026, AI companies raised at median pre-money valuations up to 2.5 times those of non-AI companies from seed through Series C.

Across the broader private market, Crunchbase’s Current Valuation Model estimates that the more than 1,900 startups on its Unicorn Board are worth about $2 trillion more collectively than their last reported funding-round valuations.

When everything moves that fast, the terms of your financings and of your contracts for compute, data and distribution get set early and in a hurry. Some of those terms will later slow a sale, add cost and erode value. The key is making sure you take long enough to consider what a deal does to your exit before you sign.

Sources indicate that take license-and-hire deals, where a buyer licenses your technology and hires your team instead of acquiring the company. They’ve become more common in AI, and the National Venture Capital Association has responded by updating its model charter. In 2025, it added an optional investor approval right over these deals, and sign-off by the board, including the investors’ directors, was enough to satisfy it.

The November 2026 update requires a vote of the preferred stockholders instead. Investors are often reluctant to use an approval right to block a deal. But it puts them at the negotiating table when an offer arrives, with a voice in how the proceeds are split between retention packages for the team and the cap table.

The stakes are higher when an investor is also a strategic partner. Nvidia and its NVentures investment arm alone have invested in more than 260 startups since the beginning of 2024, including 121 this year alone, Crunchbase data indicates.

Sources indicate that strategic capital often comes tied to compute, cloud or distribution agreements. Those agreements can carry exclusivity, change-of-control and right-of-first-negotiation terms, and a right of first negotiation can force you to put a competing bidder on ice while you talk to your strategic investor first.

Be realistic about your leverage. Terms from the hottest AI rounds get cited as “market,” but what you can get depends on how much investors want your deal. Spend your negotiating capital on the few provisions that will matter most in a sale instead of trying to optimize on marginal topics.

An AI company’s most valuable assets are often the hardest to diligence.

Start with data. A buyer will want to know where your training data came from and whether your contractual rights cover how you use it and how the buyer plans to use it. Publicly available data isn’t necessarily licensed data. And permission to use a customer’s data to serve that customer doesn’t always cover training models for everyone else. If you fine-tuned on customer data under contracts that limit its use to serving those customers, a buyer may not be able to fold your models into its own products. The buyer may ask for a special indemnity, a larger escrow or a lower purchase price.

According to recent reports, your code gets similar scrutiny. In our deals, investors, and especially acquirers, now ask how much of the codebase was written with AI tools and whether those tools pulled in copyleft or commercially licensed code. We tell clients to audit their code regularly and fix challenges as they surface. It makes that diligence conversation far easier.

According to recent reports, compute and model-provider contracts raise a different problem: a buyer may inherit a commitment it doesn’t want. Minimum-spend commitments, reserved capacity and exclusivity terms generally stay with the manufacturer in a sale. A buyer with its own GPUs may have no use for three more years of your reserved capacity, and it will factor the remaining payments into its offer.

All of this is easier to fix at the negotiating table than in the middle of a buyer’s diligence.

• Map who decides on a sale. Know which board and investor approvals it would trigger, including any approval right over a license-and-hire deal.

• Track your data and code rights. Record data sources and use restrictions and audit AI-generated code periodically.

• Stress-test your infrastructure commitments. Run cloud, GPU and model-provider obligations against slower growth and a sale.

In a recent development, • Keep strategic rights narrow. Limit the scope, duration and triggers of any right of first offer or negotiation you give a strategic investor.

Recent findings suggest that • Read commercial contracts with an exit in mind. Check the change-of-control and termination rights before you sign.

In a recent development, few founders are thinking about a sale when they close a seed round or sign their first GPU contract. You don’t have to predict the exit. You do have to know what you’re giving up each time you sign, so the speed that got you funded doesn’t cost you flexibility when the right acquisition offer arrives.
