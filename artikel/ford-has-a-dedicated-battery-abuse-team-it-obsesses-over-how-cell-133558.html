Ford Has A Dedicated Battery Abuse Team. It Obsesses Over How Cells Fail

Electric vehicle batteries live a harder life than the ones in your phone or laptop. They spend most of their time outdoors, deal with extreme heat and cold, and can take a beating from road debris and potholes.

Industry observers note that that means for battery makers, engineering for durability is a priority right up there with energy density and charging speed. And to make sure its cells can handle the abuse of daily life as an EV, Ford has a team dedicated to understanding how batteries fail—and how to stop it.

“That team is 100% focused on battery abuse, design, and testing as a function,” Wesley Thibault, the global chief engineer of battery product development at Ford mentioned last week during a media roundtable at the automaker’s upcoming battery plant in Marshall, Michigan.

It has been revealed that ford is new to the EV battery business. Last week, it started cranking out lithium iron phosphate (LFP) battery packs at BlueOval Battery Park Michigan, using technology licensed from China’s CATL, the world’s largest battery maker. The batteries will power the all-vital Ford Fathom pickup, which the automaker says will enter production toward the end of next year and kick off a new generation of vastly better and more profitable EVs.

Despite the licensing deal, Ford officials stated they were involved in developing the batteries from the outset, with a focus on durability.

According to recent reports, the job is not as simple as it sounds. The battery abuse team isn’t tasked with destroying fully made cells and then finding ways to make them indestructible. Instead, Thibault said, it is involved in the design process from the start.

“They want to understand down to the physics level, how a cell fails and why, and when it does fail, how we can prevent it from creating harm to the occupants,” he said.

Quality operator at Ford's BlueOval Battery Park Michigan.

For example, the team measures how cells vent, a crucial process that keeps batteries from catching fire or damaging themselves when they overheat. When temperatures inside a cell get too high, the electrolyte, the liquid chemical that shuttles electrons between the anode and cathode, gets hot and releases gases.

It has been revealed that if those gases stay trapped, they can cause short circuits or thermal runaway, so they need to escape in a controlled way. Thibault noted his team can measure all of this analytically—using computer models—before the cells are validated.

Production adds another layer of scrutiny. At various stages, the materials inside the cell, as well as the finished cell itself, go through thousands of quality checks, Ford engineers noted. Those include CT scans, X-ray machines, lasers, camera inspections, and AI systems.

For example, when the anode slurry is coated onto a copper foil, every millimeter is scanned by lasers for wrinkles and other anomalies. All employees have the ability to pause production if they spot any problems.In case of any issues, they quarantine the cells affected during a specific time period. Ford also claimed that impacted components can be traced all the way from production to the vehicle they end up in.

Sources indicate that to be sure, not all of this is unique to Ford. Strict quality checks like these are common among major battery makers, who all aim to minimize scrap rates for costly batteries and maximize the amount of material that actually gets sold.

Ford engineer operating a winding machine, which brings together the anode and cathode, adding a separator in between.

Ford continuously tests the manufacturing process in other ways, too. Engineers noted they intentionally feed damaged components and cells—which they call “red rabbits”—into the production machinery to confirm the quality systems catch them and are working as intended.

When cells are almost ready, they’re blasted with helium gas to check for leaks. Once a pack is assembled, it’s mounted on a hydraulic shaker that mimics thousands of miles and years of real-world road use. The shaker can twist, dive, squat, contort, yaw, and replicate the other movements of a car, putting the pack through everything it would see on the road.

Ford’s EV batteries have fared well, by and large. High-mileage Mach-Es with 100,000 or even 200,000 miles on the odometer show little range loss compared to their original capacity, according to data the company released this month.

Sources indicate that on the other hand, Ford’s emphasis on battery quality isn’t exactly surprising. The tech giant has grappled with high warranty costs in recent years. And both the Mustang Mach-E and the F-150 Lightning have had issues linked to their high-voltage batteries. Some early Mach-Es were recalled because their battery contactors would overheat during DC fast-charging. A small number of Lightnings were recalled in 2023 due to defective battery cells.

Ford will want to avoid any similar quality complications as it rolls out the high-stakes Fathom, which it hopes will outsell its previous EVs and set the stage for an electric comeback. It will be years before we know how the Fathom's batteries hold up in the real world. For now, it looks like the manufacturer is pulling out all the stops to make sure things go off without a hitch.
