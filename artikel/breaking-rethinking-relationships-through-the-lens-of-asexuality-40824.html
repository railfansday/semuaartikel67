Breaking: Rethinking Relationships Through the Lens of Asexuality

🌐 Source: Psychologytoday • 📅 18 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 6 min read (1272 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

Updated November 18, 2026 | Reviewed by Davia Sills.

This post was co-written with Paula Bange and Laura Botzet, Ph.D.

The belief that sexuality is a universal and essential part of the human experience is deeply ingrained in our society. In pop culture, this assumption is especially visible. Reality TV reveals like Too Hot To Handle are entirely built around the premise that people cannot resist sexual temptation. At the same time, these shows reflect the common idea that sexual attraction is closely tied to the beginning of romantic relationships. Accordingly, much of relationship science has been anchored in the idea that sexual attraction is a key mechanism guiding partner choice with the ultimate goal of having and raising children (1).

Industry observers note that asexuality, the lack of (or limited presence of) sexual attraction, challenges this long-held assumption. Looking at relationships through the lens of asexuality raises notable questions: What do we really need for intimacy and connection? And does a fulfilling life necessarily require sexual attraction or a romantic partner?

Sources indicate that if sexual attraction were a necessary condition for romantic love and relationships, asexual people must not want relationships. Right? This couldn’t be further from the truth. Research clearly shows that many asexual people seek emotionally intimate, close relationships but prefer not to engage in sexual intimacy (1, 2, 3, 4).

In a recent development, this highlights that romantic and sexual attraction don’t always go hand in hand. The split attraction model (5) distinguishes between different types of attraction, particularly sexual and romantic attraction, which do not always align. While many people experience both together, asexual people may experience romantic attraction without sexual desire. In other words, they might not desire sexual intercourse, but they fall in love, seek companionship, and build committed relationships.

It has been revealed that at the same time, asexual people are less likely to be in romantic relationships (6) compared to allosexual people (individuals who experience sexual attraction). In addition, in our own work, we found that asexual women are more interested in being single in the future compared to heterosexual women (2). Given that decades of research have shown that being in a romantic relationship, especially a satisfying one, is linked to greater happiness, better health, and reduced loneliness (7), this seems like bad news.

Contradicting this assumption, being in a romantic relationship does not seem to protect against loneliness for asexual people in the same way it does for heterosexual, bisexual, and homosexual people (6). Even when asexual people are in a good relationship, it doesn’t reduce their loneliness as much as it does for other groups. Yet, being in a bad relationship doesn’t increase their loneliness as much either. In short, asexual people seem to be less affected—positively or negatively—by relationship quality compared to others. Dovetailing with these results, asexual singles are most satisfied with being single compared to other sexual orientations (8).

Sources indicate that together, these findings challenge the common belief that everyone wants and benefits from a romantic relationship. For asexual people, there may be even more good reasons to stay single. While some asexual people may prefer being single because they anticipate difficulties in finding a partner and maintaining a relationship that fits their needs, other asexual people do not experience romantic attraction and may not want any romantic relationship at all. Yet other forms of connection, such as friendships, may offer an key source of intimacy.

Indeed, emerging research demonstrates that asexual people are more interested in, for example, alternative committed relationships than heterosexual people (2, 3, 4). These could be close friendships but may also take the form of emotionally close, committed, yet platonic partnerships. These so-called queer platonic partnerships are often described as deeply meaningful bonds that go beyond conventional friendship, while not necessarily involving romantic or sexual attraction (4). For asexual people in particular, queer platonic relationships may offer a way to experience closeness and commitment without the expectations tied to romance and sex.

Overall, asexual people seem to prefer constructing their ideal relationships outside of “traditional” scripts, yet more closely around their identity and needs. For example, asexual people may often date someone who experiences sexual attraction and wants to engage in sexual activities (3, 4). The couple will have to negotiate ways to meet both partners’ needs. Sometimes this may lead to consensually opening up relationships so that sexual needs can be met outside the relationship (2, 3). At the same time, research suggests that asexual people are less interested in becoming parents than allosexual people, indicating that having children may be less central for them or perceived as less normative (2). This further challenges key assumptions of research on partner selection that typically see reproduction as the ultimate goal.

Together, these findings highlight that asexual people are open to a variety of relationship structures and life paths.

Human experiences are diverse and so are asexual experiences. Differences in romantic and sexual attraction are just one piece of a much more complex picture of how people form relationships and define intimacy. Asexuality includes many different identities. For example, some asexual people do experience sexual attraction but only after they have formed an emotional bond with another person (demisexual), and others fall somewhere in between asexual and allosexual (graysexual) (9). All these nuances of (a)sexual experiences likely play a role in shaping what kind of relationship we are searching for.

Industry observers note that looking through the lens of asexuality reveals that there are different pathways to emotional connection and well-being. Relationships do not need to include sexual intimacy, not even romantic feelings, and can take on a multitude of forms. What asexuality teaches us is to rethink what intimacy and partnerships can look like and to recognize that a fulfilling life may not necessarily include a (romantic) partner altogether.
