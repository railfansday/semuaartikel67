Tech Report: Yale’s Sonnenfeld: Nvidia is a ‘detriment’ to U.S. industrial might — not a champion

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that investors cheered Jensen Huang’s return as Nvidia’s CEO on Monday, but renowned business academic Jeffrey Sonnenfeld of Yale thinks they’re celebrating a mistake. He sees a tech giant that is largely responsible for putting sophisticated technology in the hands of the country’s adversaries, at a moment when the risks couldn’t be higher. Far from a champion of American industry, he indicates, Nvidia is “a detriment to U.S. global industrial might.”.

As evidence, Sonnenfeld points to research suggesting Nvidia’s most advanced chips have reached China despite U.S. export controls, and to sinking shares of Anthropic, which he links to the realization that China’s seemingly miraculous open-source AI models are, in a sense, copies of top U.S. labs’ work, even as Nvidia has insisted that smuggling is a “non-factor.”.

According to recent reports, indeed, Huang’s return was greeted with just as much enthusiasm in Asia, The Washington Post reported. “The king is back!” read one headline from China’s state-affiliated Star Market Daily.

Industry observers note that the reinstatement itself followed heavy pressure from Washington. Nvidia’s chairman had effectively ruled out Huang’s return just last week. Then, in a torrent of private weekend calls led by Vice President JD Vance, top administration officials leaned on the board to welcome Huang back, with President Trump calling the board directly to vouch for him. “I made the deal,” Trump told CNBC on Monday.

Sonnenfeld says the directors who went along, and who returned Huang to the board even as it investigates whether foreign intelligence efforts targeted him, now face a “historic level of individual liability.”.

Sources indicate that the legal deluge has already begun. As we noted a couple of weeks ago, a potential class-action lawsuit was filed almost immediately. Days later, a shareholder derivative suit followed. Filed on Nvidia’s behalf against the directors themselves, the suit alleges the board ignored red flags about Huang’s relationship with suspected Chinese spy Alice Huang.

The class action is very much aimed at making Nvidia and Huang compensate investors for their losses, while the derivative suit puts the directors’ own behavior on trial, with any recovery going to Nvidia. Three more suits followed on Nov. 18, the day after Nvidia’s most recent earnings call; all five were filed in Northern California, where Nvidia is based.

Huang and Nvidia are also juggling a Justice Department antitrust investigation, a reported SEC probe into Nvidia’s accounting, a possible FBI review, a California subpoena, an export-control case that landed partner Megaspeed on the U.S. trade blacklist on Monday, and congressional inquiries, including a Senate Banking hearing next week at which Huang has agreed to testify.

Here’s more of what Sonnenfeld had to say, in a Q&A that’s been edited for length and clarity.

TC: So much has happened since we last talked. Huang was ousted, he’s come back. Do you think the board’s reliance on government assurances here is defensible or an abdication of its own duties?

JS: They failed to rely on experts. That’s one of the reasons this board is going to be sued and lose, and these directors are likely to face a historic level of individual liability. They have a very inadequate directors and officers liability policy. They have also just failed in their diligence. They hired the Wachtell [law] firm. The Wachtell firm barely got off the blocks to do an investigation. And then they have already reversed themselves, so they didn’t use the expertise needed.

It’s wild that they also put Huang right back on the board of directors.

The board showed, in addition to incompetence and collaboration, just cowardice in their capitulation because Trump has nothing to wave over them. If this was SpaceX, where 46% of its business comes from the government [you could maybe see the case for Trump’s involvement].For Nvidia, it’s not even 1% of its business that comes from the U.S. government, so [the board] didn’t have that threat. [Trump] can call off [federal] investigations, but he can’t call off the shareholder litigation. Some are already filed, and the estimates are there’ll probably be 30 of them, and they’re going to be extremely costly.

And this is not, by the way, just a blue or red state matter. We already have 14 GOP senators who’ve already come out against this, and very prominent conservatives, like Pete Ricketts and Josh Hawley and Tom Cotton and John Cornyn. Those are some issues that may help explain why Nvidia’s stock is tumbling today.Might the board say, ‘We saw the classified intelligence findings, and they assure us Jensen will be absolved of wrongdoing’?

They haven’t seen those findings, and we also haven’t seen what will be the result of the Senate Intelligence Committee, the Senate Special Committee on Investigations, the Senate Banking Committee, and all their House counterparts. The board hasn’t seen any of that, and even if they saw it, which they have not yet, that still wouldn’t matter.

Do you think the Commerce Department will continue to tighten its chip export controls to keep China from accessing so much compute or walk back some of those recent moves?

Recent findings suggest that we already see that Megaspeed has finally been put on the sanctions list, as it should have been before. And we have documentation now that Nvidia, rather than a champion of U.S. global industrial might, is actually a detriment to U.S. global industrial might, because their chips have now turned out to be in the hands and in the systems of not just the Chinese military, as they got channeled to Northwestern Polytechnical University, which is a military institution — this is going into weapons that will, of course, be funneled to Iran and Russia, and they didn’t have access to that a year ago. [Editor’s note: China has long denied supplying weapons to either country. Nvidia chips have been found in Russian drones, however, and the U.S. ambassador to China remarked in September that President Trump had discussed selling arms to China, an account Trump has denied.].

The open-source models that people have celebrated and marveled at — that China somehow has this ingenuity to be creating their own chips, so that Kimi and DeepSeek and these other celebrated open-source models were producing a miracle of Anthropic-like results — turns out we know why. They are pirating it. They are “distilling it” from Anthropic using high-end Nvidia chips.

Nvidia has denied reports that its most advanced chips have been smuggled into China.

There’s an Israeli tech giant, Lava, that just three weeks ago showed [dozens of] Blackwell chips are in China. We’re seeing, with the return of Jensen Huang, Anthropic plummet. Why would that be? Of course, this is because it’s hurting OpenAI. It’s hurting Grok. It’s hurting Perplexity. It’s hurting Gemini. And you’ve seen none of these tech titans celebrate this. We have the misogynist tech bros holding locker-room-type rallies about Jensen Huang’s return, but nobody else has spoken out in favor of this. [You’re not hearing from] the CEOs of Microsoft or IBM or J.P. Morgan or Walmart or General Motors or Caterpillar. Nobody thinks that this is a good move.

You don’t think Nvidia needed Huang back?

He was not the guy designing the chips. It’s important to point out that that’s a guy named Chris Malachowsky, a co-founder who’s still there who designed these GPUs and has a whole team of next-generation people like Jonah Alben doing the chip design. As for the great software that has leveraged these chips, that’s also not Jensen Huang. That’s a guy named Ian Buck, who’s been very much celebrated in the last two weeks. He’s the one who designed the CUDA software that leverages the chips. Those people weren’t going anywhere, and wherever Jensen Huang is has nothing to do with them.

Some are trying to make Huang’s forced resignation about the extramarital affairs to which he has admitted, calling it HR policy gone wild.

A CEO who doesn’t stand by his own code of conduct makes it completely unenforceable.

Wired quoted an Nvidia employee who remarked they’d never take an HR training course again if Huang was reinstated.

Industry observers note that this thing is a sexual affair with a subordinate — whether it’s coercion, or even if it truly was somehow consensual, it’s not fair. Every decision on resource allocation, on staffing, on product decisions, is not objective. It’s either favoritism, or, if the relationship fails, retribution. All the other people on that team rise and fall with a sexual relationship; it’s something they have no control over.
