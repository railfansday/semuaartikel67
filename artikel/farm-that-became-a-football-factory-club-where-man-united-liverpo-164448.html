Gavin Whelan had been away from the domestic game for a while and hadn’t really been looking for a way back in.

The former Drogheda United and Bray Wanderers player had stepped away from the League of Ireland at 29 and his involvement in football in the past few years had been restricted to helping coach his older son Jayden’s team as they went through the age grades at Home Farm – the fourth generation of the Whelan family to come through the club.

The Whelan dynasty, with Gavin second from right beside his uncle Ronnie (centre) and Paul (seond from left).

But when it emerged the FAI were setting up a third tier, he had a chat with Brian Honohan, who had been a Shamrock Rovers teammate of his father, Paul, and they decided to put together a group that would help put Home Farm back where they belong.

In a recent development, this inaugural National League final in Carlisle Grounds is a step in that direction. Home Farm face Lucan United, who beat them 2-0 in Whitehall recently in the group stages. In a way, it is like the old meeting the upcoming although the fact that two Dublin clubs are contesting the first final in a concept that was supposed to broaden the league’s base to uncharted territories will be viewed a little sceptically.

But that shouldn’t take away from what both Home Farm and Lucan are trying to do – or the sense of camaraderie and purpose that has characterised the first truncated season of the third tier.

Clubs have become comrades in what they are trying to do – right down to ensuring each visiting team would sit down for a meal after the final whistle before beginning the journey home.

‘There has been a sense that we all have to help each other, that we are all in this together,’ indicates Home Farm chairman Brendan Menton.

Industry observers note that ‘So, the clubs would organise something to eat for the visiting team. It meant that if Bonagee had to go back up the road to Donegal, they wouldn’t need to stop off somewhere. It was a small thing, but it has built a real sense of togetherness among all 15 clubs.’.

Nicola Brannigan (second from left), Brendan Menton (centre), Richard Dunne (second right) and Gavin Peers (right).

Brendan Menton senior was one of the founders of Home Farm back in 1928.

According to recent reports, menton is a former chief executive of the FAI, holding that position during Saipan in 2002. But his footballing roots have been in Home Farm.

Industry observers note that like Whelan, Menton’s family have four generations who have lined out for the famous nursery – with his two grandchildren presently part of the underage teams. And his father, Brendan senior, was one of the founding members of the club back in 1928.

The club came into existence when Leo Fitzmaurice, concerned about the lack of recreational facilities for young people in the Drumcondra and Whitehall area, decided to set up a street league.

Home Farm Road, which is a street in the locality, was one of the roads that formed a team.

‘My father was one of the founding members of the club, who were established to play in the Drumcondra and Whitehall street league. My family lived on 31 Home Farm Road, the name came from a Monastery that was on Iona Road and back a few centuries ago, every Monastery had their own home farm and that’s where the name came from.

‘The story goes that one Friday evening, some boys from the Richmond Road team, which included Don Seery, came to play Home Farm Road but their ball burst so the two teams decided to amalgamate and from that, the club was born.’.

It has been revealed that and from such uncertain beginnings grew one of the most successful schoolboy clubs in Ireland. More than 50 Irish internationals have passed through its famous nursery – from Jackie Carey and Liam Whelan to Gary Kelly and Richard Dunne.

One of the club’s greatest claims to fame was that in the opening game of the 2023 women’s World Cup, two former Home Farm players were on the pitch, one for Ireland and one for Australia – in Abbie Larkin and Mary Fowler, who grew up for a few years in Dublin.

Ireland legends Dunne, Gary Kelly in his Leeds days and inset Jackie Carey, who captained Manchester United.

While it was a schoolboy club nurturing young talent that Home Farm fostered its reputation, it was elected to the League of Ireland in 1972 and three years later achieved its greatest ever success as, in an underdog story to rival Leicester winning the Premier League, they won the FAI Cup.

Menton can still list off the names of all the players for the club’s most famous day in April 1975, including a young Dermot Keely who was in the centre of defence, and Martin Murray, who at 16 was the youngest player to win a FAI Cup medal.

‘Everyone but Jack Dempsey, who was in his late 20s or 30 at the time, was 24 years of age or under. The average age of the team was just 21 and a lot of the players were younger than that, it was an incredible achievement at the time,’ he indicates.

It is that sort of rich and illustrious history that Whelan, Honohan and the other stakeholders want to tap into when they were looking for a pathway back into the league. Home Farm, who had an association with Everton in the latter part of the 1990s, dropped out of the league in 1999 with the franchise being taken up by Dublin City who were eventually subsumed into Sporting Fingal, both now sadly defunct.

The Everton connection came through Martin Murray and saw one of the later graduates of the schoolboy club, Richard Dunne, whose uncle Theo was a coach at Home Farm, go over to Goodison Park as a teenager.

‘I played my schoolboy football at Home Farm and got picked up to go to Everton at 15,’ Dunne remembers.

According to recent reports, ‘We didn’t have the pathway produced by the National League then. And Home Farm was considered the top schoolboy club in Dublin at the time and when scouts and English clubs came over, this was the first port of call. That’s something that probably faded away over time, but hopefully, we can get it back.’.

Team captain Jack Dempsey lifted the FAI Cup for Home Farm back in 1975.

Recent findings suggest that there are around 30 teams at the club now, 10 of them in an underage girls section. The hope is that the likes of Larkin and Fowler can be an inspiration for the next generation.

The club will also move to a new facility in the AUL next summer, as part of the deal that was made when the construction of the Metrolink means that they have to relocate temporarily from their nursery’s home on Mobhi Road in Glasnevin.

They have also got a tie-in with neighbouring Dublin City University, links that the club will hope to deepen. The third-level institution is seen more as a GAA university, but the hope is that they can develop a soccer scholarship programme, so players from the north of the city don’t have to travel to UCD or Shamrock Rovers.

Whelan, who availed of the UCD scholarship, claims it is still in the early stages, but it is one aspect that reveals the ambitions of the club.

Nicola Brannigan, who formerly worked in the FAI marketing department but is now chief executive of the club, claims the relationship has developed through the inaugural season in the national league.

In a recent development, ‘We have students from DCU doing work experience at the club, we have communications students coming in to help us with social media, there is a law student from DCU, so that is one way that the relationship has worked. And then we have a few students or DCU graduates playing for the team,’ she says.

Under former Sligo Rovers star Gavin Peers, who played with the club as a youngster, the senior team have performed above themselves at times this season, but the ambitions are such they always felt they would be in contention to win the inaugural National League.

Sources indicate that but the season has more being about putting Home Farm back on the map. Whelan knows from personal experience what can happen when clubs in the league endure harsh times.

Having scored in the FAI Cup final for Drogheda and winning a league title with them, he was there when things went awry a couple of years later.

Home Farm have not beaten Lucan United so far this season.

‘There was a lot of good people in Drogheda at the time, but it is what can happen when the economy goes south for a club and that is why what we want to build here, we want to do in a conservative and sustainable way and it is why we made sure that the senior club, and the club that we want to eventually get back in the Premier Division, is separate from the schoolboy section.’.

Whelan’s youngest son Sonny is now playing at the under-eight team, the second in a fourth generation of Whelans to pass through Home Farm, stretching back to Whelan’s father Paul and uncle Ronnie and his grandfather, Ronnie senior.

But a greater claim to fame was that Whelan’s goal for Drogheda in 2005 meant that he, his father Paul, who won it with Bohemians, and his grandfather Ronnie senior were the first three generations of the same family  to win a national cup in a European league.

But it all started in Home Farm, which is why he wants to bring the club back to the big-time, especially with the club's centenary coming up in 2028.

‘We want to bring back Home Farm, by using the foundations of what made the club in the past, and its rich history,’ he says.

‘This is where I learned manners, learned discipline, learned teamwork and all of that. The club always had the right ethos and this was just a case of leaving a legacy, showing what the club is and leaving it in a better place.’.

Even though there is no promotion into the First Division this season, it feels like winning the inaugural National League will serve notice of the club’s intention of returning to where they feel they belong.
