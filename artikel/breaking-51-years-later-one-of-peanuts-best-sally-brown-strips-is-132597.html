Breaking: 51 Years Later, One of Peanuts’ Best Sally Brown Strips Is Still a Perfect School Joke

For years, one of the problems with reading Charles Schulz's Peanuts was the inconsistency among its collected editions. You'd think that Peanuts’ publishers, as the stewards of one of the most influential comic strips​​​​​​​ in the Western world, would simply reprint the comics in chronological order. It was rarely that simple.​​​​​​​.

There is a broad assortment​​​​​​​ of Peanuts comic collections on store and library shelves, of course. However, it's more common to find Peanuts books that feature themed selections, like 1985's baseball collection Significant League Peanuts. When you do find a straightforward collected edition of Peanuts, usually in secondhand stores, it often has a few conspicuous omissions. (They’re also usually 40- to 50-year-old books printed like dimestore novels, with yellowed paper and ink that’s started to flake off the pages​​​​​​​.).

In a recent development, some of this was reportedly due to Schulz himself, who exercised a lot of editorial control​​​​​​​ over which Peanuts strips were collected and where. Specifically, Schulz is the reason why it was so difficult for so long to find official reprints of Peanuts' first few years, as he apparently regarded the strip's early days as something of an old shame.

That didn't change until 2004, when the Seattle indie comics publisher Fantagraphics began to release The Complete Peanuts, a 26-volume series that features the comic's full run in chronological order. Even if you're familiar with Schulz's work, there are likely a few surprises waiting for you somewhere in The Complete Peanuts, as quite a bit of Peanuts was voluntarily thrown down the memory hole. Unless you were lucky enough to grow up right alongside the comic’s initial publication, there are a few Peanuts strips in the full collection that even a superfan may not have seen.

It can also highlight how some characters entered the strip almost fully formed. Charlie Brown's little sister Sally doesn't have quite the same evolutionary arc as the rest of the Peanuts gang. Once she was old enough to go to school, it rapidly became both her literal friend and her worst enemy.

It has been revealed that sally was first introduced in May 1959 and spent​​​​​​​ her first few appearances as an infant before being allowed to accelerate into childhood. Sally started kindergarten in 1962 and remained roughly the same age for the rest of the strip’s run.​​​​​​​.

As you read through The Complete Peanuts, many of Sally's running jokes and character tics appear surprisingly early.​​​​​​​ Her one-sided crush on Linus goes back to 1960, and her ambivalence towards both school and life more broadly is visible by the 1970s. Charlie Brown may rage against the unfairnesses of the world and Linus may be stoic about them, but Sally looks at them as if through the eyes of a nihilistic French philosopher. They don't matter, because nothing matters.

According to recent reports, that helps to explain Sally's general attitude towards school, which is one of the most consistent elements of her character over the course of 40 years​​​​​​​. Sally would rather tell a story than learn a fact, which applies whether she's practicing basic skills or making a presentation in front of the class. She simply inhabits a different, stranger world than most of the people around her.

In the Sunday strip for Jan. 19, 1975, Sally gets up in front of her class to deliver a report on rock snakes, which she presents with a cadence suitable for network television. The look on her face in the fourth panel is what really sold this for me, as it's one of the most manic grins Schulz ever drew​​​​​​​.

Recent findings suggest that "What, you may ask, is a rock snake? That is a good question! A rock snake is a snake that sneaks up behind you, and throws a rock at you!" Sally tells her class. "Now, here is my exclusive... it used to be thought that rock snakes were dangerous, but my authority says this is not so...".

This is, of course, all nonsense, although it's fun to think of how a snake would pick up and throw a rock. I imagine it works something like a slingshot.

The punchline is that Sally has apparently based her entire report on something Linus told her, which is a little out of character for him, but maybe he was simply trying to scare Sally at the time. However it shook out​​​​​​​, Sally's rock snake report is going to be on my mind whenever I see someone else trying to fake their way through an assignment.
