Cycling Taught Me How to Suffer—a Lesson That Helped Me Lose 140 Pounds and Find Recovery

In a recent development, SEPTEMBER 2026, THE week of the UCI Road World Championships in Montreal and the city was lousy with bicycles. On most corners, a latest pack of amateur cyclists gathered. Bixi—Montreal’s bike share program—was represented, but as a garnish to the inundation of mountain, beach, fixie, and road bikes, along with their riders from around the world, taking over the city. I watched all this in awe. Cycling changed me because it taught me how to suffer. The sport familiarized me with the type of suffering that wrecks you, but you put yourself through it on purpose. You suffer to emerge resilient and better.

It has been revealed that I'd heard elite cyclists—guys like Toms Skujins, Sean Quinn, and Mike Woods—talk about this pain in passing, but I was in Montreal to learn more. I wanted to know if they saw the power of suffering in the same way I did.

IN 2001, I was an obese drug addict. Checking into rehab that year put a number to my weight: 536 pounds. I’d been diagnosed with congestive heart failure, drugs were quickly killing me, and I was motivated to change. Once I’d been sober for a year, I was able to see that my weight was causing me suffering.

Industry observers note that standing was a herculean effort, placing my feet in the right spot to support me, calculating the angle my knees would be in at the apex of my shifting mass, deep breath, locked core, and a hope and a prayer that I didn’t topple. Tying my shoes was done in bursts of held breath and near-blackouts. I slept sitting up, because the pressure of my chest caused me to choke and gasp. I could once soothe that suffering with food and drugs and kick that discomfort down the road a bit, but in sobriety I needed healthy outlets.

Sources indicate that I’d successfully managed lose 100 pounds before my wife tricked me into going to the gym. When she’d ask that I go, I’d tell her that the gym was a place for fit people. So she bought me a package with a personal trainer, stated I could flake on the appointments, but I’d be flushing money down the toilet if I did. I went, but I was geared towards avoidance, and whenever something in the gym got hard, I’d just tell the trainer I couldn’t do it.

According to recent reports, riding a bike was something I did during outings with my wife and little kids maybe a few times a year. We’d cram our minivan full of bikes, find the flattest part of the Venice bike path, and slowly cruise for ten minutes at a time. Getting them back into the car was the hardest thing I had to work through.

Little did I know that someday cycling would be the key to changing my life.

ROUND AND ROUND I went losing and regaining weight. In 2009, after getting down to 300 pounds and ballooning back up to over 400, faced with having to start over once again, I turned my sights to running.

Industry observers note that I tried running on a desolate stretch of the LA river near my house in Studio City. Not only was I unbearably slow, running hurt. My flat feet sent electric jolts of white-hot pain cascading up and through my body. That first day, I’d wrapped my ankles so tightly with ace bandages that my toes went numb. My feet were no better off and my nipples bled. The next day I could barely stand. The suffering didn’t stop when I stopped running. My feet, ankles, knees, and hips hurt long after—and I had no control over it.

With running out, but I turned to an old beach cruiser in our shed. The street we lived on was relatively flat, but every street branching off it was a steep climb. After a few rounds on my street, I decided to be ballsy and see how long I could last going uphill.

My legs were screaming, but there was no electric jolt of pain in my pedal stroke. My heart thundered, my mind told me I had to stop. But without the jarring jolt I got when my feet hit the ground while running, I knew I could go further.

Despite never riding my bike uphill as a kid, despite weighing 400 pounds, despite every physical and mental signal in me telling me to quit, I made it up to the top of that hill, up to Mulholland and Laurel Canyon, and it was glorious. Fighting through that urge to stop was incredible; getting to the top even more so. The pain while running was a warning that stayed with me after I stopped. The pain of cycling uphill was legs, lungs, and heart. I could push through it, and it stopped when I stopped.

I asked Tour de France cyclist Mike Woods about this distinction of suffering when I was in Montreal. “Suffering is a bit of a misnomer for endurance events. Are you really suffering? It’s elected, you know?” he told me. “You choose the pain, you choose when to stop, you choose when to keep going. I don’t truly suffer. Suffering is when you don’t choose for that pain to be inflicted on you”.

Industry observers note that and then he stated something that really resonated with me: “But it does prepare you for real suffering, when you have something bad happen to you in life. Doing endurance events, I find—it’s fake suffering, but when you have those bad moments in your life, you’re better prepared to manage them.”.

Industry observers note that AFTER THAT HILL climb, I started riding my bike regularly, eventually graduated to a mountain bike, and then to a road bike. I rode to the top of every mountain in Los Angeles. I discovered I was capable of so much more than my mind or body allowed me to believe. Legs screaming, heart pounding, mind numbing, this was suffering, and I found that I loved it. And, like Woods said, I found that the more I suffered on a bicycle, the less I suffered off it.

Industry observers note that “One day at a time, one pedal stroke at a time. Just move forward,” Toms Skujins, a two-time Latvian road race champion, told me in Montreal. “When you’re racing, you can stop at any point. You’re the one doing the damage, you know? That’s very voluntary suffering, because there’s, obviously, things in the world that happen where you don’t choose to suffer, and you have no choice there. Here… if your legs are screaming too much, you can just stop. For me, that kind of suffering is definitely easier to take.”.

Industry observers note that within my first year of cycling, I was riding every day. There was a loop that took me from Studio City over the hill to West Hollywood. I’d cross Los Angeles, ride up PCH, cross the Santa Monica Mountains, and ride back to Studio City along Ventura Boulevard. It got me 100 miles and 8,000 feet of climbing, and I did it a few times a week. I’d do shorter 30- to 50-mile rides on the remaining days.

According to recent reports, sean Quinn, a US pro cyclist, gets it: “It sounds easy when you’re sitting on the couch, but when you’re actually in that moment suffering, your body’s basically sending you all the signals it can to make you just do anything else, just stop, stop the suffering,” he told me. “There’s no instant fix to a lot of your suffering. You have to be patient, accept that it’s gonna be shitty for a while, and wait it out, and keep a cool head, and eventually it does get better. Of course, you have to take action.”.

When I was riding every day, I got down to 213 pounds. I’d like to say I stayed there. But I went back to work, stopped riding, my diet unraveled, and over the next few years, I gained nearly all of it back.

In 2016, 400 pounds again, I ruptured the biceps tendon in my right arm and had to have surgery to repair it. As a sober guy, I turned down the post-op painkillers. I was up all night in pain and had to work the following day. On set, with the motion of production buzzing around me, the pain was dull, but it was unrelenting, just like a ride up Angeles Crest Highway, more than 120 miles out-and-back, with over 13,000 feet of elevation gain. I hadn’t chosen the injury, but I had chosen to go without the pills, and that reminded me of the pain on the bike, something I picked and something I knew would come to an end. I’d remind myself of that and go back to learning my lines and preparing for the scene.

It has been revealed that that day reminded me that I knew how to do this. So I got back on a diet, and by 2018 I was down to 260, and I’ve held steady there ever since.

Recent findings suggest that riding a bike taught me that I could suffer now in benefit of tomorrow. It’s been the most powerful lesson I’ve learned about myself.

ON THE DAY of the elite men’s road race, we fans lined the streets. There was dedicated bike parking near the finish line—Fans Village Bonjour Montreal—with thousands of bikes in neat rows. We all lined up to watch the attacks, riders going solo for that long and treacherous circuit. An American, Brandon McNulty, went off the front with about three laps to go and won the World Championship this year. It was thrilling to see. At the press conference afterward he said, “I was just dying, but I kept pushing.”.

Industry observers note that leaving Canada, I couldn’t help but think that I shared something with everyone else there for the races. A secret membership, dues paid in sweat, and a code, to respect and revel in my voluntary suffering.

I’d gone to Montreal to ask the riders if they saw suffering the way I did, and in different words, all three told me that what you do on the bike carries over into the rest of your life. I’d thought cycling taught me how to suffer, but what it did was give me a place where I was in control of it, where I could make it a practice, so that when the suffering of life came along, the suffering I had no control over, I was better equipped to deal with it.
