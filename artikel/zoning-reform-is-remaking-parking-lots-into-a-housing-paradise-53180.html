Zoning reform is remaking parking lots into a housing paradise

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Developers across the country paved over paradise to put up a parking lot. But changes to zoning laws to increase housing supply now have developers paving over parking lots to create a housing paradise.

It has been revealed that in California, a series of state laws has made that easier. Florida lawmakers have gone after the same land. San Diego suburb National City took matters into its own hands. Because of its high housing costs, California has been the leading model for how reforms can work. But the Golden State is also a model for how neighborhood advocates and cities fight the reforms, often rendering them worth less than the paper they’re written on.

According to recent reports, much of the zoning reform push across the country focuses on where ground-up developers could build residential density, particularly infill in urban areas. The pressure is on land.

According to recent reports, underused parking lots are one option. The median share of downtown land used only for parking is about 26% in U.S. cities with more than 500,000 people, according to the U.S. Department of Transportation.

California’s Assembly Bill 2011 created a ministerial approval path, exempt from environmental review, for apartments on sites where office, retail or parking is a principal use. AB 2243 expanded those tools for commercially zoned property when it took effect last year.

Florida’s Live Local Act requires cities and counties to allow multifamily rentals by right on commercial, industrial and mixed-use land when a share of the units is affordable. Lawmakers have amended the law multiple times, including this year, since enactment in 2023.

According to recent reports, national City reveals how the local action can work. The city loosened height limits in its mixed-use zones last year, making underused lots more workable for housing.

It has been revealed that national City Investment Group has broken ground on Market View, a 120-unit workforce housing project that will replace a parking lot beside Manolo Farmers Market in National City. This small grocery chain has locations in San Diego, where leaders have instituted reforms that have faced neighborhood challenges and lawsuits that new state law quashes.

Opponents still turn to the courts, even though reforms were meant to curtail or preclude lawsuits. And they are getting more inventive.

In San Francisco, neighbors are suing over an 848-unit project at 15 Marina Blvd. The towers would replace the Safeway’s parking lot and rise above a newly built store. They say the city wrongly ruled it eligible for AB 2011 and denied them a hearing.

To get the case into federal court, local opponents invoked an 1871 law, commonly known as the Ku Klux Klan Act. The developer wants the federal court to dismiss the case.

Recent findings suggest that in Los Angeles, the fight is over habitat. Planners rejected 398 homes at the Woodland Hills Country Club in August, citing mountain lions. The state Department of Housing and Community Development remarked the city violated AB 2011. Pro-housing groups have sued the city while the developer threatens its own lawsuit for damages that could exceed $20 million.

According to recent reports, under AB 2011, a qualifying project gets ministerial approval, which means municipal staff checks it against objective standards and no hearing is required. Residents lose the chance to comment on the project or its environmental effects, said Sheri Bonstelle, a land-use attorney at Greenberg Lusker who represents developers, told HousingWire TBD.

“Neighbors are really upset that they no longer have a voice at all,” Bonstelle mentioned.

Developers will also pay for the faster path. AB 2011 projects must meet labor standards, including prevailing wage. Bonstelle said that can add roughly 30% to construction costs, which developers weigh against discretionary review and the risk of a lawsuit under the California Environmental Quality Act.

“It’s literally a monetary analysis,” Bonstelle noted.

The math has worked for some of her clients, she remarked. They’ll trade off higher costs for the elimination of entitlement risk and confidence that a project can be built. Bonstelle said lenders also favor the ministerial path. That’s especially crucial now, given the tight lending environment.

How much access neighbors get depends on the city. Los Angeles has a detailed AB 2011 application, Bonstelle said, so developers must file substantial information that neighbors can request and review. The city knew these projects were coming and was thorough about what had to be filed. Smaller cities are the bigger problem, she said.

“It’s more of an glitch with the smaller cities that haven’t had the projects come through yet,” Bonstelle remarked.

In a recent development, some cities hand developers a checklist meant for a building permit, she mentioned. Her firm then goes through item by item with city attorneys to explain that most of those materials are not due at the planning stage of ministerial review.

Other cities believe state law should not apply to them and decline to process applications, Bonstelle remarked. In one case, her firm is working with a small city that has been “incredibly difficult.” The firm is asking the state Department of Housing and Community Development for a technical assistance letter explaining what the law requires.

It has been revealed that florida’s Live Local Act addresses similar commercial land. Cities and counties must approve multifamily rentals administratively on commercial, industrial and mixed-use land, with no rezoning or public hearing, if at least 40% of units are affordable for 30 years. Lawmakers have amended the law three times since 2023, most recently in 2026.

According to recent reports, the law covers parking lots zoned for those uses, Patrick Chopson, an Atlanta architect and co-founder of Cove, told HousingWire TBD. “That’s a substantial deal,” Chopson remarked. Cove, which has a proprietary AI platform, recently acquired Deerfield Beach, Fla., architectural firm Gallo Herbert Architects. The firm has done a lot of work statewide in affordable housing.

It has been revealed that the act also funds projects. In St. Petersburg, Nashville-based Elmington Capital Group intends Skyway Flats, a 174-unit affordable apartment building on surface parking next to an assisted living facility in the city’s Skyway Marina District.

Industry observers note that the five-story building will have 28 one-bedroom, 100 two-bedroom and 46 three-bedroom apartments. All will target households earning 30% to 80% of the area median income, and 18 will go to young adults aging out of foster care.

Pinellas County commissioners unanimously approved $6 million in March to buy the land. The county will require affordability for 99 years. The $77 million project also draws on federal tax credit equity, tax-exempt bonds and a state loan through the Live Local Act.

Skyway Flats is in predevelopment. Elmington expects financial closing and a construction start in early 2027.

Similar to Pinellas County officials, National City planners acted on their own. They changed zoning to allow more density on parking lots. The change fits within the California law context. San Diego County alone needs more than 171,000 homes, regional officials say.

It has been revealed that the city’s planning commission began reforming the code in July 2025 to allow four stories. Previously, the code capped buildings at three.

That cap limited how many units a project could hold, Andrew Regenberg of Gafcon, the firm working with the owner, told the commission in a public hearing. Chris Dallo, who heads the investment group, backed the change.

“We believe that more housing means more opportunity not just for our market, but for local businesses,” Dallo told the commission.

Market View is scheduled for completion in the first quarter of 2028. It will have three buildings, mostly studios, one-bedrooms and two-bedrooms, linked by open space and the market. RDC designed it, and R.D. Olson Construction is the general contractor.

Councilmember Ditas Yamane, who represents the district, said the groundbreaking followed a long review.

“This milestone is the result of a long, thoughtful process and extensive due diligence to bring this vision to life,” Yamane said.
