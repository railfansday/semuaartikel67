Tech Report: A newly introduced discovery could give the world 12 months to prepare for extreme El Niño and La Niña weather events

Researchers have found the conditions needed to predict some El Niño and La Niña events 12 months ahead of time.

Most El Niño forecasts are only reliable about six months ahead of time. Predicting a full year in advance has long been considered nearly impossible.

That is because the Pacific Ocean becomes so sensitive to tiny shifts in the spring that forecast models easily lose track of what comes next.

Now, researchers have found that certain 12-month El Niño forecasts really do hold up, depending on the state of the Pacific on the day the prediction is made.

Forecasts issued during a La Niña held up a year in advance in all nine climate models an Australian team tested. Forecasts issued when the Pacific was in neither phase did worse than a forecast built from nothing but the historical odds.

For farmers and water managers, that could mean months of extra warning before some El Niños.

Nandini Ramesh of CSIRO, Australia’s national science agency, and UNSW Sydney led the work. Her co-author Mandy Freund is at the University of Melbourne and the ARC Centre of Excellence for the Weather of the 21st Century.

According to recent reports, the team graded about 7,500 past forecasts rather than building a fresh forecasting system. Ramesh mentioned the researchers are still a few steps from applying the findings to agency models.

In an interview with Earth.com, Freund stated the accuracy of forecasts that started during a La Niña surprised the team most. Routine checks of forecast performance, she said, often don’t sort forecasts by their starting state.

El Niño, La Niña and the neutral years between them make up a cycle scientists call the El Niño-Southern Oscillation, or ENSO.

“Separating forecasts according to their starting state, we were able to uncover a much clearer picture of when long-range ENSO predictions can be trusted,” she noted.

Industry observers note that the warm phase of that cycle is El Niño, when the surface of the central and eastern tropical Pacific runs warmer than usual. La Niña is the cool phase. Both tend to peak around December, and both change weather patterns around the world.

Recent findings suggest that A declared El Niño or La Niña can set off drought warnings for farmers and water managers, the authors wrote. It can move the prices of farm commodities and start public health planning.

By one estimate they cite, seasonal forecasts influence companies worth a combined $13 trillion.

The trouble is the spring. From March to May the tropical Pacific is unusually sensitive to small disturbances. A forecast made before those months and aimed at the winter after them loses much of its skill.

Forecasters call this the spring predictability barrier. It is a big part of why predictions beyond about nine months have stayed so difficult.

In a recent development, the team worked from an archive of decade-long climate simulations run by modeling centers in nine countries. Those include the United States, the United Kingdom, Japan and Canada.

These are physics-based models, which simulate the ocean and atmosphere from physical laws rather than past statistics.

The centers started a fresh batch every year from 1961 to around 2020, and the team used the runs that began between October and January.

Rather than judging the simulations on exact temperatures, the researchers asked one question of each: would the following winter land in La Niña, neutral or El Niño?

Sources indicate that they sorted every year into one of those three bins using sea surface temperatures in a patch of the central Pacific. The coolest third of years counted as La Niña and the warmest third as El Niño.

Sorting the years that way left about 7,500 one-year forecasts to grade against NOAA‘s record of what the ocean actually did. Then the researchers split the results by which of the three states the Pacific was in when each forecast began.

In every one of the nine models, forecasts that began during a La Niña scored higher than forecasts that began during an El Niño or a neutral year.

Their skill a year out matched what seasonal forecast models manage three to four months ahead, a range forecasters already rely on.

Forecasts that began from a neutral Pacific did the opposite. In every model, they scored below a forecast built from nothing but the historical odds of one state following another.

Inside a single model, the gap between starting points was larger than the gap between the best and worst models.

“The finding that one-year forecasts starting from La Niña perform well in all nine of the models tested suggests there is something about La Niña conditions that makes the tropical Pacific system more predictable over the following year,” Freund said.

In a recent development, stronger La Niña events were predicted better than weaker ones. For El Niño the pattern was less clear, though badly missed forecasts of strong El Niños were rare.

The team then asked the question a farmer might ask of any forecast: when a model predicts a particular switch, how often does it actually happen?

In one American model, a forecast of El Niño issued during a La Niña came true 82 percent of the time, a year ahead of the event. The same model’s forecast of a La Niña from neutral conditions came true only 9 percent of the time.

In a recent development, three of the nine models were right more than 70 percent of the time when they predicted a La Niña would give way to an El Niño. Eight of the nine were at their worst predicting a La Niña from a neutral year, a switch that is rare in the real ocean.

And when a model called for an El Niño from neutral conditions, not one beat the historical odds. Those calls are the likeliest false alarms.

Across all starts from neutral, the models were right 23 to 43 percent of the time. A forecast built from the historical odds alone would have been right 61 percent of the time.

Sources indicate that freund told Earth.com that the weakness from neutral starts remains an important caveat. “Unfortunately, starting from a neutral Pacific, the physics-based models often make the wrong predictions,” she stated.

“The system can be very sensitive: it is an example of what is known as the ‘butterfly effect’ where very small disturbances can be amplified over time into huge changes in a system’s behavior.”.

Asked by Earth.com what farmers and water managers should take from the result, Freund stated the study is encouraging.

Those are people, she said, who rely on knowing about an El Niño much further ahead than forecasts now allow. When a La Niña is under way, a forecast for the following year can be trusted more.

“This gives farmers, water managers and other decision-makers valuable extra time to prepare, including decisions about which crops and seed varieties to plant or how to manage water resources,” she noted.

The method itself is simple, Freund said, and would not take much for forecasting agencies to consider. “It could provide this extra layer of confidence we can lay on certain predictions of ENSO,” she said.

The researchers also tested two-year forecasts, and for almost every kind of switch those were wrong more often than right.

Industry observers note that the one exception came after an El Niño, when every model did better than the historical odds at saying there would be no El Niño for the next two years. Long stretches without an El Niño have been linked to multi-year droughts across the subtropics.

Why a La Niña makes the following year easier to predict is the open question. The team offered three possibilities.

Sources indicate that A La Niña’s cool water tends to linger into spring instead of fading in February or March the way an El Niño’s warmth does. Earlier studies also found fewer random atmospheric disturbances during a La Niña.

And a La Niña is when the upper tropical Pacific refills with warm water, so the state of that reservoir may carry a lot of information about what comes next.

The authors set aside long-term warming trends, slow decade-scale swings in the Pacific and the differences between one El Niño and another.

They also didn’t test the statistical and machine learning methods they suspect would do better from a neutral start. Those methods work from past patterns instead of from today’s conditions. Whether the La Niña advantage holds in the models agencies actually run each season hasn’t been tested.

The team’s next step is to work out which of the three explanations is doing the work. The confidence check it proposes can be run on any model from the forecasts it has already made.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.
