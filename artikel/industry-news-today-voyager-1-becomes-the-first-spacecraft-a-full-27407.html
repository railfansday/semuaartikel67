Industry News: Today, Voyager 1 Becomes the First Spacecraft a Full Light-Day From Home

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

NASA’s Voyager 1 left Earth on Sept. 5, 1977, carrying a camera, a set of science instruments and a gold-plated record of sounds and images from home. Forty-nine years later, it’s about to reach a distance no spacecraft has reached before: one light-day, or roughly 16.1 billion miles, from Earth. At that range, light needs a full 24 hours to make the trip between Earth and the probe.

Industry observers note that at 5:16:07 a.m. ET today on Wednesday, November 18, Voyager 1 became the first human-made object to reach one light-day from Earth, about 16.1 billion miles away. From that point on, a radio signal between the spacecraft and NASA’s Deep Space Network takes more than a day to arrive in either direction.

Industry observers note that NASA puts the journey at 49 years, two months and 11 days, counting from Voyager 1’s launch in September 1977. The probe still sends data home, though at a rate slower than dial-up, and NASA looks a live discussion with the Voyager team on Thursday to mark the occasion.

It’s quite an accomplishment for a spacecraft built in the mid-1970s. One light-day is the distance that light travels in a single day at the speed of light, which is approximately 186,282 miles per second. That translates to 25.9 billion kilometers or about 16.1 billion miles in a single day. At an average human walking speed of 3 mph, it would take a human 612,015 years to walk that distance.

Fortunately, Voyager 1 has been traveling a bit faster than humans can walk. It currently zips along the cosmos at a steady 37,763 mph, a speed it achieved in 1980 after a gravity-assisted Saturn flyby. It has been flying at that speed ever since.

The milestone comes 14 years after its previous one, when Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause, the point where the sun’s heliosphere ends, and entered interstellar space. It’s also 28 years after passing Pioneer 10 to become the human-made object that has traveled the farthest into space.

It has been revealed that NASA is marking the milestone with a global Shine Bright for Voyager’s Light-Day event on Tuesday evening. Just after sunset, the agency wants people to head outside and point their smartphone or tablet lights skyward, sparking a planet-wide wave of light as Earth rotates, much like fans doing the wave at a stadium.

NASA also made a website for the occasion. The website is simple. It flashes Voyager’s VGR call sign in Morse code. If you want to try it, click the link, hit begin, and then point your phone or tablet screen at the sky. The website does all the work for you.

NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory will follow up on Thursday at noon ET with a moderated discussion on the milestone, featuring status updates on both Voyager probes and a live Q&A with reporters and audience members.

According to recent reports, the historic milestone isn’t without its drama. Voyager 1 has been in space for nearly half a century, and in that time, NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory has done everything it can to extend the life of Voyager 1. That includes troubleshooting concerns, performing maintenance, and shutting down various systems to save power and extend the spacecraft’s lifespan.

Sources indicate that several of those efforts took place in 2026. The team running Voyager 1 spent weeks shutting down three devices and powering on three low-power alternatives to reduce overall power usage. This initiative, which was internally called The Massive Bang, was also performed on Voyager 2. The transition was completed in early September, giving Voyager 1 more time with its dwindling power supply while also keeping it warm enough to continue operating in the frigid conditions of interstellar space.

It was a tricky maneuver that required the team to simultaneously turn multiple instruments on and off. The difficulty of the maneuver was amplified by the fact that it took almost a full day for any signal to reach Voyager and another full day for Voyager to send a signal back, with hours of downtime while the spacecraft performed the operations.

All of this took place several months after Voyager 1 experienced a sudden drop in power, which necessitated shutting off one of its three remaining instruments still in operation. Voyager 1 now has only two instruments still in operation.

Sources indicate that making it a whole light-day away from Earth is likely going to be Voyager 1’s final milestone while it still has enough power to communicate with Earth. Voyager 1 is powered by three radioisotope thermoelectric generators. They are essentially nuclear-powered batteries that generate energy by converting the steady heat from decaying plutonium-238.

Industry observers note that when Voyager 1 first debuted, its radioisotope thermoelectric generators produced about 470 watts of power. But plutonium-238 doesn’t last forever, and after 50 years in space, the RTGs now produce about 215 watts of power, with an average loss of about 4 watts per year. Most of that power is used to keep Voyager 1 up and running, which is why NASA has shut down eight of the 10 instruments that Voyager had when it launched.

At its current rate of usage and decay, Voyager 1 is expected to shut down its final science instrument in the 2030s and will eventually lose communication with Earth in 2036 due to insufficient power to send back a radio signal.

It has been revealed that the same fate is expected for Voyager 2, Voyager 1’s twin, which has also spent half a century in space. While it is labeled the sequel, Voyager 2 actually launched 16 days before Voyager 1, and the two have spent nearly identical amounts of time traversing the cosmos. Thanks to its slower speed and trajectory, it likely won’t reach the one light-day distance until after its instruments shut down.

Recent findings suggest that thanks to the vastness of space, Voyager 1 will lose all of its power before it hits its next milestone, which is the beginning of the Oort Cloud, a vast, icy shell of comets at the edge of our solar system. It’ll take about 300 years for Voyager 1 to make it there.

It has been revealed that thanks to the near-perfect vacuum of outer space, Voyager 1 will continue to fly at its current speed even after it shuts down entirely. So it’ll still hit that milestone; it just won’t be able to phone home to tell us about it.
