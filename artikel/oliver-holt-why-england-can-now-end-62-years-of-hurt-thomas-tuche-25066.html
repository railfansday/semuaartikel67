By OLIVER HOLT, CHIEF SPORTS WRITER.

Published: 12:00 EST, 16 November 2026 | Updated: 13:04 EST, 16 November 2026.

The glum face of Lamine Yamal, head in hands, stared out from the front page of the Spanish sports paper AS as it sat on newspaper stands in Madrid on Monday morning. ‘Fin a la racha,’ the headline remarked. The end of the run.

For Spain football fans, England’s 3-2 dramatic victory over the world champions in the Spanish capital on Sunday evening made this akin to a day of mourning, the first time their team had tasted defeat in 44 matches, an end to a gilded period of invincibility.

According to recent reports, for England, the feeling was very different. Because Sunday night in the cauldron of the Metropolitano felt very much like a beginning. Thomas Tuchel has been the head coach for almost two years but this was the moment of his true arrival.

This was the victory that freed us from looking back to the Groundhog Day pain of that World Cup semi-final defeat by Argentina in Atlanta last summer and allowed us to look forward to a future that seems luminous and full of hope.

It has been revealed that this was the victory that allowed us to stop blaming Tuchel for what happened four months ago and start looking forwards again, dreaming about the glories that he might yet deliver. This was the victory that validated Tuchel and why the FA hired him in the first place. This was the victory that allowed us to believe he might be the Messiah after all.

This felt like the moment when he embraced us, with all our quirks and football idiosyncrasies, and we embraced him. He had promised ‘proof of concept’ on the eve of the game and he delivered it with bells on. Tuchel has harnessed the best of the English game and welded it to his principles and the result is intoxicating.

Thomas Tuchel has harnessed the best of the English game to down world champions Spain.

Industry observers note that once you have seen England play the way they did for an hour against Spain, you are lost. You are lost to the belief that the 62 years of hurt might really be coming to an end at Wembley in the final of the European Championship in the summer of 2028. You are lost to the idea that, finally, everything is falling into place.

For the 63 minutes up until the sending off of Elliot Anderson, I have never seen an England performance as good as the one that unfolded in the Metropolitano. Spain are the best team in the world and England outclassed them and stupefied them. They were better than them with the ball and better than them without the ball. They were magnificent.

Because of the context, because of the wonder of the stage, because of the odds they faced, England’s victory over Mexico in the grandeur and the mayhem of the mighty Azteca Stadium during the World Cup might edge Sunday’s performance but for pure football, what England delivered in Madrid demands comparison with the best they have played for many a year.

It has been revealed that this year’s World Cup came just too soon but now Tuchel and England understand each other perfectly. And now England are purring. They have a manager who commands the players’ respect and they have a group of players that commands the respect of the manager.

In a recent development, yes, it was just a Nations League group game but it was against one of the best national sides the world has ever seen. England worked so ferociously and attacked so fluently and defended so devotedly that they reached a level that drew admiration even from their opponents.

Many, many things will live long in the memory. James Trafford’s miraculous double save in the dying minutes gets better every time I watch it. Ben White’s goal channels the beauty of Carlos Alberto’s strike for Brazil against Italy in the 1970 World Cup Final, too. Let’s hope that strike, and his superb defensive performance, bring an end to the boos that have been his companion of late.

Jude Bellingham was relentless, tireless and glided through the game against Spain.

Tuchel had promised ‘proof of concept’ on the eve of the game and he delivered it.

And then there was Jude Bellingham. Just when you think you can’t admire him any more than you do, he delivers a performance like the one he produced against Spain, a performance that lifted him above even the exalted organization of the other players on two fine teams.

Bellingham was tireless. He was relentless. He glided past challenges. He thundered into tackles. He dominated midfield. He has the heart and the sheer brilliant indomitability that Bryan Robson possessed.

Bellingham has it in him to be England’s greatest ever player. He needs trophies to fulfil that destiny but maybe, finally, trophies are in reach again.

He has the team around him to do it, which Robson never quite had. Jordan Pickford is a good goalkeeper but Trafford might just be better. Ezri Konsa had his best game for England in Spain. White and Lewis Hall were superb at full back. Little more needs to be remarked about England’s front six. They are as good as any unit in world football.

Harry Kane, of course, was the best player in the world last year. Some seem to assume that the Ballon d’Or will inevitably pass to Yamal or Kylian Mbappe next. I think it is more likely to be Bellingham.

The man is a colossus. He is a footballer who has everything. He does not know how to be beaten.

Bellingham saw off Barcelona rival Lamine Yamal - and could beat him to the Ballon d'Or.

Industry observers note that with them together, the sky is the limit. And Bellingham has also proved to be a player of immense mental strength. It was not so long ago that people worried about his discipline but the fortitude he revealed when he was being targeted by Argentina at the World Cup was the best answer to that.

Recent findings suggest that he and Kane are England’s axis of ambition on the pitch. Tuchel is lucky to have them and they are lucky to have him, too. The coach has reached the point where his tactics and his team-building are bearing fruit.

Recent findings suggest that now England have a team-manager package that genuinely need fear nobody. The victory against Spain was a rehearsal for the bigger stage of major tournaments. Going down to 10 men after 63 minutes against the best team in the world might not have been in the script but that is what rehearsals are for.

After Sunday night in the Metropolitano, England are ready for their close-up.
