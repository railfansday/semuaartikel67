Microbiome home tests are taking off. Can they be trusted?

First, the good news: I have 133 microbial species living in my gut. That’s a collection that ranks as ‘excellent’ according to NirvanaBiome, the organization in Germantown, Maryland, that analysed my stool sample. The firm based its rating on the overall diversity of my gut’s residents plus my levels of specific beneficial and undesirable species.

Sources indicate that but then came a second, less promising assessment: Viome in Bellevue, Washington, identified only 61 kinds of active microorganism in my gut and suggested that some of them were not performing optimally. Viome recommended dietary changes and offered customized supplements to improve my health.

Industry observers note that I was intrigued by the results, but also flummoxed by the different messages. That’s not an uncommon response, states microbiologist Rob Knight, director of the Center for Microbiome Innovation at the University of California, San Diego. When scientists tested a handful of microbiome testing kits alongside one another in formal studies, they found wide discrepancies in the results. These differences make it hard to understand or really trust the findings from any given provider, says Diane Hoffmann, director of the Law and Health Care programme at the University of Maryland in Baltimore, who co-authored one of those studies.

The companies stand by their products. “Of course there’s variability,” says Kelly Moffat, chief operations officer for Cmbio in Germantown, which does the laboratory work and genetic sequencing for NirvanaBiome. “There has to be because this is complex testing.”.

According to recent reports, momo Vuyisich, chief science officer at Viome’s lab in Bothwell, Washington, also says he was unsurprised by the degree of variation found by formal studies. Labs use different methods to extract, sequence and analyse microbial genetic material. Viome, for example, sequences RNA rather than DNA, allowing it to uncover microbial gene activity, rather than only the presence of genes. Vuyisich argues that this is more relevant to human health. And he says that although every method will have biases, as long as a consumer sticks with the same provider, it should produce results that are comparable over time.

It has been revealed that interest in these kinds of test is booming. “There’s so much public enthusiasm and excitement about the microbiome,” states Knight. Some customers are simply curious about the microscopic beings that live inside them and potentially affect their health; others have health conditions such as irritable bowel syndrome and hope that their gut microbes will reveal the underlying cause or a path to better health.

Recent findings suggest that although people can order the tests and follow their recommendations without the help of a medical professional, some people are bringing the findings to their physicians. “I groan when I see someone come in with a microbiome test,” claims Fergus Shanahan, a gastroenterologist and immunologist at University College Cork in Ireland, who has spent much of his career studying the microbiome. For someone struggling with gut health, a consumer microbiome profile isn’t usually specific enough to help. And if there’s a concern about an infection, such as Clostridioides difficile, it’s more straightforward to test for known pathogens than to assess the entire microbial community.

It has been revealed that microbiologists and gasteroenterologists who spoke to Nature generally agreed that microbiome testing has the potential to both reflect and promote health, but many mentioned that the science isn’t yet mature enough to support microbiome testing for most consumers. In their view, the limited clinical usefulness of today’s consumer tests can make them poor value for money (costing around US$100–300). The specialists warn that acting on advice that doesn’t come from clinicians could lead customers to adopt unnecessarily restrictive diets or delay appropriate medical assessment. Critics say that consumer microbiome testing needs more regulatory scrutiny and oversight.

It has been revealed that “This is an industry that’s running wild,” states genomicist Jacques Ravel, director of the Center for Advanced Microbiome Research and Innovation at the University of Maryland School of Medicine in Baltimore and a co-author on one of the studies that tested several providers. With regards to testing methods, he says, “there’s no standard”.

Moffat says, “I think the jury’s out on exactly what it is that we can actionably do today based on this data.” But she says that microbiome profiles, combined with other testing such as gut metabolomics, could yield interesting and useful health information.

It has been revealed that the gut microbiome has emerged as an incredible correlate of overall health. From immune-system function to metabolism, skin disorders and mental health, study after study has found links between a person’s well-being and the trillions of microbes that live in their digestive tract. The premise of a head count of these species and assessment of their actions, to monitor and improve gut health in a personalized way, certainly sounds appealing. Viome’s website, for example, promises: “No more trial and error … Follow a strategy built precisely for you.”.

It has been revealed that companies can identify species and, on the basis of those lists, infer what the microbes are probably doing, such as manufacturing metabolites or inciting inflammation. But running even the simplest microbiome testing is expensive, says Alessandra Cervino, research director at PiLeJe, a science-based food-supplement manufacturer in Paris. She says her microbiome testing company, Luxia Scientific, closed in 2024 for financial reasons.

Some microbiome testing firms recommend fermented foods, such as kombucha, for gut health.Credit: Deb Lindsey for The Washington Post via Getty.

In a recent development, many testing companies also offer lifestyle and nutritional advice or sell supplements. For example, Viome analysed diverse functions performed by the microbes in my gut, such as digestive efficiency, gas production and serotonin manufacture. I then received a detailed eating proposal — which told me to eschew broccoli and load up on pecans, among other suggestions — along with an offer for precision supplements and probiotics with a price tag of more than $150 a month. Although it’s hard to change the composition of microbes in one’s gut, alterations in diet or even simply ageing can shift their activities, indicates Vuyisich. The manufacturer recommends re-testing every four to six months to check for shifts and upgrade the plan.

Industry observers note that he points to 19 peer-reviewed publications by Viome as evidence of the tech giant’s rigour (see go.nature.com/4actdhq). For example, the firm’s analysis for consumers is supported, in part, by published data on how gut microbes influence individuals’ responses to glucose in various foods, based on 550 people in the United States who collectively ate more than 30,000 meals while wearing glucose monitors1. The company validated those findings in a further cohort in Japan, Vuyisich says, but those data are not published.

Recent findings suggest that microbiome tests are not created equal, notes Tim Spector, a physician-scientist at King’s College London and co-founder of London-based ZOE, which offers microbiome testing and a supplement in addition to a gut-health app that I’ve been playing with. ZOE’s products, Spector says, are backed by research on thousands of people. “There definitely are some not-so-good players out there that give this field a bad name,” he says. “It’s not easy to do this well.”.

Sources indicate that there are also several ways to do microbe testing; companies take varying approaches that yield disparate results, as at least two studies have shown. Scientists at the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) in Gaithersburg, Maryland, shipped homogenized stool samples from one person to seven companies2. NIST’s remit is researching and applying standards for measurement. This experiment was a step towards understanding the diversity of testing options out there, states one of the study authors, NIST microbiologist Stephanie Servetas, who is based in Sheridan, Wyoming. The researchers didn’t name the companies in their report because they wanted to focus attention on the comparison between tests rather than perceived accuracy of any individual provider.

Recent findings suggest that the result: “Companies produce strikingly different results,” indicates co-author Scott Jackson, a molecular geneticist who used to direct the NIST microbiology program and is now a consultant in Saint Michaels, Maryland. “They couldn’t even agree on what’s healthy and unhealthy,” Jackson says. The microbe lists ranged from 34 to 906 identified genera; only 3 of which indicated up in all reports. In fact, the results were as varied from company to company as they would be for different people, despite all tests being done on samples from the same individual.

A European and Canadian team obtained similar outcomes when they sent homogenized samples from one person’s stool to six companies, also anonymized. Three companies rated the microbial diversity as “excellent” or “good”; two called it “average”; one remarked it was “unfavourable”3. The results were so variable, that at least some of them must be lacking in validation or standardization, claims co-author Céline Druart, executive director of the Pharmabiotic Research Institute (PRI) in Brussels, a non-profit organization specializing in regulation of microbiome innovations. “It’s very difficult to make a conclusion,” she says.

According to recent reports, this broad divergence in results is not surprising, says Jack Gilbert, director of the Microbiome and Metagenomics Center at the University of California, San Diego. The variation starts at sampling: scoop a bit of faecal matter into a tube, stab it with a coring device or swipe the sampler across some used toilet paper; ship it off as-is, or in some kind of preservative (as both Viome’s and NirvanaBiome’s kits asked me to). Each option could influence the balance of the microbes that are collected and what survives to be sequenced.

Industry observers note that in the lab, Servetas states the method by which the researchers extract genetic material from a sample is a big source of divergence. Gentler protocols will bias a sample towards microbes that are easy to crack open, whereas harsher methods will capture a higher share of species with tougher exteriors.

It has been revealed that after sequencing, another major source of difference, claims Knight, is the bioinformatics pipeline that labs use. What length or number of genetic sequences is required to consider a microbe present? What list of sequences and microbes are they using as a reference? “All bets are off when you then computationally analyse it with your favourite method,” says Knight.

In a recent development, would companies support having standards to validate their tests? “We are really big advocates on really having a standard here,” says Kimberley Sukhum, chief science officer at Tiny Health in Austin, Texas, which offers gut microbiome tests as well as vaginal microbiome tests.

But it’s hard to set those standards, in part because there is no known ‘ground truth’ for a natural microbial sample. Compare that with, say, a blood-glucose test; a lab can get a known value from a standard sample either right or wrong. But the microbiome, in its glorious complexity, does not allow for such simple validation. NIST has used mixed human donor samples to develop a standard4. And Tiny Health has experimented with generating a reference for benchmarking from synthetic communities. It was built from individual microbial species rather than sampled from faeces5. But neither of those standards is exactly like the microbiome of an individual person.

Industry observers note that even if labs could assess a microbiome perfectly, they’d still have a problem: there’s no defined healthy microbiome like there is one range of healthy blood-glucose levels. Each person’s microbiome is unique, like a fingerprint. There are infinitely many ways to have a healthy microbiome, and infinitely many ways to have an unhealthy one, indicates Shanahan; it’s not entirely clear how to draw a line between the two.

In a recent development, companies must compare customer microbiomes with some standard of health, but these vary too. They might be based on past customers or large, public databases. But microbiomes differ considerably depending on factors such as ethnicity, sex, age, recent antibiotic use or even whether someone was born by caesarean section.

It has been revealed that using the wrong comparator population could skew results and recommendations. Ravel, for example, studies the vaginal microbiota, which the scientific literature notes should be dominated by a few species of Lactobacillus. But that pattern is less likely to hold true for Black people6. Most Black women with low levels of vaginal Lactobacillus “are not sick, and to tell them that they are not healthy is literally wrong and it can be hurtful”, says Ravel.

Despite the many critics of such testing, Sukhum thinks that her family has benefited from it. She joined Tiny Health in 2021, soon after giving birth by caesarean section to her daughter, who underwent four rounds of antibiotics for ear infections in her first year of life. Sukhum, who lives in St. Louis, Missouri, came to dislike what she was seeing in her daughter’s microbial profile. She followed tips such as feeding her fermented foods, and slowly, that profile changed. “Now,” Sukhum says, “she has the nicest-looking gut microbiome in the entire family.”.

It has been revealed that that’s why Sukhum thinks it’s good for people to be able to order microbiome tests. “Having information in hand, today, can still have an impact on individuals and the choices they make,” she states. And Tiny Health is eager to show this. In 2025, it published results from a randomized study of 54 infants born by caesarean section, showing that babies who received microbiome analysis and personalized tips for improving gut health had more of the beneficial microbe genus Bifidobacterium and lower rates of eczema than did babies who received no such interventions7.

Few microbiome studies are designed to show a microbial intervention causes a health benefit, however. “The large problem we have in the microbiome field is a lot of the studies that have been done are highly correlative,” notes Ravel. Linking a bacterial species to a condition, often with a fairly small sample, isn’t sufficient to power recommendations for a change in diet, probiotics or supplements.

It has been revealed that but more data, fuelled by thousands of people and their microbiomes, are coming, says Spector. In September, his team declared results from more than 200,000 people that linked hundreds of microbe species to type 2 diabetes and prediabetes, suggesting that the microbiome might also help to assess diabetes risk8.

According to recent reports, for most curious customers, microbiome testing is probably harmless, says Gilbert, who sits on the scientific advisory board for several microbiome companies. But other scientists say that there are reasons for buyers to beware. One is the cost. Siew Ng, a gastroenterologist and director of the Microbiota-I Center at the Chinese University of Hong Kong, recalls one person who arrived at an appointment with her after taking a stool test. He was toting a year’s worth of supplements, uncertain what to do with the products that she estimates cost him a few thousand US dollars.

It has been revealed that hoffmann ran focus groups with physicians about microbiome testing, and they raised further concerns9. One paediatric gastroenterologist reported seeing people who adopted highly restrictive diets after receiving test results and ultimately developed eating disorders. Hoffmann’s panellists also reported accounts of direct-to-consumer test customers who performed their own faecal microbiota transplants (FMTs). Such transplants could be dangerous without medical professionals to screen the donor material for safety. Vuyisich indicates he’s never seen a microbiome organization recommend FMT.

“There really is a gap between what the science is able to say and what the consumer and patient is expecting,” says Druart.

It has been revealed that that’s why she, Hoffmann and others are calling for oversight10 — which, today, Hoffmann calls “minimal”. Some nations have two regulatory routes, one for wellness tests and one for medical diagnostics, and a lot of microbiome tests take the wellness route.

Recent findings suggest that druart and Gianluca Ianiro, a gastroenterologist at the Gemelli University Hospital in Rome, have both led coalitions that suggested regulating microbial tests on a two-tiered system3,11. One test, for curious consumers, would offer limited results. The other, more-regulated test, would be prescribed by health-care professionals who would also analyse the results for their patients. Ianiro says he does occasionally order microbiome tests for certain patients.

Recent findings suggest that microbiome research is still moving forwards. “I’m optimistic,” claims Shanahan. “When the science catches up and it becomes more sophisticated, some day we will be able to do a lot better with a microbiome test.”.

It has been revealed that already, microbiome analysis is making inroads in clinical science. Gastroenterologists are excited about a microbial pattern that can identify colon cancer12. Ng expects a colon cancer screening test that she’s involved in developing to be available in China soon. And others are making microbiome-based predictors for how people with cancer will respond to certain treatments13.

It has been revealed that as for me and the 61, or 133, or however many microbial species that populate my gut, I’m glad to know they’re in there, contributing to my health. I’ll keep feeding them nutrients and fibre, but I don’t think I’ll be ordering another census any time soon.
