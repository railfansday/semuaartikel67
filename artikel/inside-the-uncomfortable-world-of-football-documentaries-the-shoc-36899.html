Paul Scholes and Phil Neville took charge of the Salford City team for one afternoon only, celebrity juxtaposed with the surroundings of a Northern Premier League Division One North fixture in a bracing January cold, played out before a few hundred supporters.

It has been revealed that the club’s new managers, a double act from nearby Ramsbottom United, stood behind the goal studying the Salford squad. Neville approached them at full time, asking if they’d like to say a few words inside the dressing room – or more accurately, portacabin – by way of introduction.

‘It was dark at full time,’ co-manager Anthony Johnson recalls. ‘I’m not exaggerating the point, I opened the door and there were these cameras, these lights on. Nobody told us about a documentary.

According to recent reports, 'The lights literally came on and I went, “S***, I’m under the spotlight here”. There was no training, nobody sits you down to tell you how it works.’.

First airing in a primetime slot on BBC One, Salford’s Class of ’92 docuseries was hooked on the Manchester United legends owning the club but quickly became a show about the shouty personalities of Johnson and his partner, Bernard Morley, charting three promotions through non-League from 2015.

Salford are the smallest club ever to be the focus of this form of storytelling. Now, such documentaries are a popular vehicle for the country's elite.

Co-managers Anthony Johnson (left) and Bernard Morley became the accidental stars of a BBC documentary about the Class of 92's ownership of Salford City.

The celebrity of (from left) Paul Scholes, Nicky Butt, Ryan Giggs and Phil Neville was juxtaposed with Salford's humble facilities on the series.

The Amazon cameras were in for Manchester City’s title-winning campaign in 2018 and also collaborated with the club to document the end of Pep Guardiola’s reign.

It has been revealed that they are across town this year, right in the middle of United’s worst start to a season since the 1980s. Amid the infighting, public rows, Michael Carrick’s sacking and Mauricio Pochettino’s appointment, the streaming service should emerge as the real victors of what has been an inconceivably dismal three months at Old Trafford.

The sight of a camera crew scuttling out of a press conference with Carrick weeks before he was relieved of his duties, boom mic hovering, painted a neat picture of the additional stress that can be placed upon managers when the pound signs prove too large for executives to ignore.

‘You can’t be yourself,’ notes Simon Grayson, Sunderland manager at the start of their infamous Netflix drama Sunderland 'Til I Die, with part one culminating in relegation from the Championship in the 2017-18 season. ‘You never forgot the cameras are there.’.

Like Johnson, Grayson had been unaware of the planned documentary. David Moyes rejected the idea the year before when he was Sunderland boss, just as Ruben Amorim did at United in 2025.

Recent findings suggest that grayson didn’t grant Netflix access to ‘the inner sanctum’ and provided them footage of training remotely. Wrexham boss Phil Parkinson has had the power to dictate where and when crews can film across their 57 episodes of Welcome to Wrexham.

Industry observers note that ‘I’m a private person,’ Grayson adds. ‘I don’t like people seeing what I’m doing. There were times when they actually tried to overstep the mark... you’d limit them to where the cameras would be.

It has been revealed that 'Even the players would say they were apprehensive and felt it was a bit intrusive. Aiden McGeady said that he felt really uncomfortable doing it, as the rest of the dressing room did.’.

Vincent Kompany didn't like the way he was portrayed as a hot-head in a documentary during his time at Burnley.

Pep Guardiola relaxed his attitude towards the film-makers covering Man City when they learned where to position themselves in his dressing room without acting as a distraction.

Intriguingly, Guardiola embraced the cameras – but only eventually, initially insisting that filming could not resemble a circus. There was a scarcity of dressing room footage in Amazon’s 2018 edition. Only the notorious tirade at his players during half-time of a tempestuous FA Cup defeat at Wigan made it but that changed when City embedded their own production staff.

Sources say a relaxing of rules took time, around 12 months, as camera operators learned where to stand without acting as a distraction or finding themselves physically in the way.

Recent findings suggest that city have been part of five documentaries in six years now, with Guardiola so enamoured by the producers that they became part of the backroom staff – even acting as auxiliary kit men on away days. He sent personal audio messages of thanks when he bid farewell last May.

He utilised them to create montages for pre-match speeches and leant on them to film players in slow motion to watch the gait of a player recovering from injury. That comes from the cameras being part of the furniture.

Critics will argue an in-house production is to the detriment of the eventual sanitised product, although City will point to Guardiola’s emotional breakdowns in A Beautiful Obsession – the story of his final season at City – as being raw and uncut.

Recent findings suggest that the format is now moving towards in-season docs (regular episodes being available during a season rather than the whole show coming out at the end). This mirrors success stories in NFL and A Beautiful Obsession co-director John De Caux believes full-season pieces are ‘slightly on the wane’.

It has been revealed that ‘There were changes (in camera staff) all the time,’ Johnson says, reflecting on Salford’s annual series while he was in charge. ‘We were losing at half-time in one game… I make no apologies for this but I came in and went, “Move out of the f***ing way”. In previous seasons, they’d know not to be in that position.

According to recent reports, ‘You're having the same conversations again. It was like, "Hang on a minute. I've had this conversation before". The previous people knew about it. They have the footage from earlier seasons – so do your homework, like we all do with our jobs. It almost felt like a bit of a lack of respect at times.’.

The Spurs All or Nothing is memorable for an eye-popping scene between Danny Rose and Jose Mourinho, when the left back didn’t realise cameras were rolling.

Former Sunderland boss Simon Grayson was unaware of their planned Netflix documentary and didn't grant the producers access to his inner sanctum.

These are the challenges that Amazon face with United, somehow attempting to earn trust or forging relationships in a matter of months before they pack up and leave. The film crew will be seen as outsiders.

There is intrigue within the industry as to exactly how granular Amazon can go amid the United chaos and whether they use fly-on-the-wall cameras, as was the case with Arsenal (in 2021–22) and Tottenham (in 2019-20).

The Spurs All or Nothing is memorable for that eye-popping scene between Danny Rose and Jose Mourinho, when the left back didn’t realise the cameras were rolling.

Industry observers note that unlike Mourinho, Pochettino – his predecessor, sacked in November 2019 – had banned them from his office. He was never keen on the idea of the documentary and hardly features in the series, his dismissal coming early in Episode One – a sign perhaps that the film-makers got little out of him.

It will be fascinating to see if they gain access to his inner sanctum at United.

The club have had to double their media commitments this season as a result of the streaming deal, although they have been reluctant to organise sitdown interviews with players, telling Amazon that they must establish their own rapport for those.

Industry observers note that some clubs have been known to give their content away for free, with Sky Sports taking two seasons of Mission to Burnley without paying. The driving force behind those moves is to promote brands. Under Alan Pace’s premiership, Burnley are staring at potential relegation to League One.

Recent findings suggest that vincent Kompany, then the Burnley manager, is understood to have been vexed at his portrayal as a hot-headed screamer. Brendan Rodgers cannot have been overly pleased with Being: Liverpool, which ended in public ridicule and comparisons to David Brent. Players at other clubs have confided that they hid from cameras.

‘You can’t be yourself, you never forget the cameras are there,’ indicates Grayson of his season alongside the makers of Sunderland 'Til I Die.

Salford's co-manager Anthony Johnson gives his players a dressing down in the BBC documentary.

Joey Barton, meanwhile, is still waiting for his moment – and may be for some time. Fleetwood Town commissioned their own team to chart his start in management and streaming giants Dazn purchased the rights.

It was so close to being aired that Fleetwood held a premiere at Highbury Stadium for 100 supporters including the local mayor, only for one of Barton’s brushes with law enforcement in the intervening days to suspend the broadcast. Prolonged legal proceedings meant the idea was canned and Dazn’s licencing period expired.

Industry observers note that it was later resurrected, focusing in part on then Fleetwood owner Andy Pilley’s fraud trial – a crew followed him into court on day one – but was then shelved when a guilty verdict was handed down. Cameras are following clubs up and down the EFL from Birmingham to Cambridge via Sheffield Wednesday.

Grayson, for his part, refused to share his drive home from training with the crew and now admits that the ultimately unfavourable edit may have been a result of him not giving the production tech giant what they wanted.

‘I did a presentation with the players with a flip chart and everybody thought it was an hour before kick-off of a league game,’ he claims. ‘It was actually done at 11:30am before a pre-season game!

'That’s why I let them film it, because it was a pre-season game, and it had no real meaning. But they presented it in the doc as if it was before the first game of the season.

Sources indicate that 'Part of the severance when I was sacked was that I still needed to do some work for them. I did three different (choreographed) interviews in a London hotel with different shirts on after I’d been sacked, done as if they were filmed at the time.

‘People said, “You didn’t come across that passionate about the club when you were talking about it”. Well, why would you when you were filming in November after you’d already been sacked? We were talking about deadline day in one of them. I’d already done something on that in August.

Recent findings suggest that ‘I think they had sympathy with me, but also if they’d had more sympathy, they wouldn’t have even asked me to do it. I’ve spoken to managers since and they’ve gone, “F***ing hell, you did well to put up with that".'.

Grayson only ever watched four episodes back and has heard his successor, Chris Coleman, was also dealt a raw deal.

Fabian Delph leads a Manchester City inquest during an FA Cup defeat at Wigan - the only scene from the dressing room that made it into Amazon's documentary on the 2017-18 season.

Industry observers note that mauricio Pochettino banned cameras from his office in his final days at Tottenham. Will he do the same at Manchester United?

In a recent development, by his own admission, Johnson enjoyed the process more than Grayson did. One ex-Salford player claimed that nothing was really cut from the show other than a fight on a team-bonding night out. It was authentic, no-holds barred.

Until the end of Johnson and Morley, that is.

Industry observers note that ‘We won the league and we ended up getting sacked anyway,’ Johnson claims. ‘We went to meet Gary (Neville) and the board. We went upstairs in Hotel Football and a cameraman followed us. Obviously they knew what was going on. When we got to the room for the meeting, they wouldn't let the camera in.

Recent findings suggest that ‘I'm actually smiling thinking about it. The documentary followed me and Bern everywhere, eating food or whatever, and then when the day comes to sack us, they didn't want the camera in the room!

‘The conversation was fine in the room itself but I think the perception from the outside world looking in would've been: "What the f*** is that all about?”’.
