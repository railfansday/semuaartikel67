This tutorial covers simulation training, evaluation, export, and real-robot deployment.

Recent findings suggest that A walking policy can keep Microduck balanced, but it does not tell the robot how to steer around an obstacle in its path. In this workflow, we reuse the walking policy from our first Microduck tutorial and keep its weights fixed. This follow-up trains a separate avoidance controller using time-of-flight (ToF) sensing. You choose a direction with a gamepad; the controller adjusts the velocity command before passing it to the walking policy.

According to recent reports, the IsaacContrib-ObstacleAvoidance-Flat-MicroDuck task separates obstacle avoidance from balance. A navigation policy uses ToF sensing to adjust forward, lateral, and yaw commands at 50 Hz. The frozen walking policy then converts those commands into joint actions, allowing the robot to learn obstacle avoidance without retraining its walking behavior.

During training, the navigation policy’s actor receives ToF history and body and controller state available on the real robot. Its critic has access to additional simulation state to guide learning, but is not needed for deployment.

Sources indicate that rewards encourage progress toward the goal, safe arrivals, and movement around obstacles. Following a movement command earns credit only when the robot makes progress with sufficient clearance; risky approaches reduce that credit. Penalties discourage fast approaches, abrupt command changes, collisions, and falls.

Sources indicate that the obstacle-avoidance task lives in isaaclab_tasks.contrib.microduck_obstacle. Its configuration is in obstacle_avoidance_env_cfg.py, ToF sensing is in sensors.py, and actions, commands, observations and rewards are organized under mdp/.

Recent findings suggest that microDuckToFCamera extends Newton’s LegacyMultiMeshRayCasterCamera to model the VL53L5CX—a compact time-of-flight (ToF) sensor on the microduck that measures distance across an 8×8 grid at 15Hz. The simulated sensor has a 45° field of view and a 0.12–4 m range. It follows the duck’s head, with placement similar to the sensor on the real duck.

Real recordings informed the sensor placement and floor readings in simulation. Training adds noise, delays and missing measurements—including near the grid’s edges—to expose the policy to imperfect data. Train the navigation policy with PPO across 16,384 parallel environments for 1,500 training iterations. Its actor, critic, normalizers, and optimizer start fresh. The task configuration loads the fixed walking checkpoint, robot model, and obstacle assets.

The 16,384 environments shown here are configurable, not a minimum requirement. Lower --num_envs to fit your GPU’s available memory. With fewer environments collecting experience in parallel, reaching comparable policy performance may require more training iterations and longer training time. We also trained this policy on NVIDIA DGX Spark in approximately 6 hours using 4,096 parallel environments for 3,000 PPO updates, demonstrating a smaller-scale configuration for the same workflow.
