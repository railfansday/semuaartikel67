Golshifteh Farahani (Alpha, Pirates of the Caribbean: Dead Men Tell No Tales) is Leila, a young widow seeking a fresh start in Kabul, Afghanistan, in writer-director Chabname Zariab’s debut feature When She Hears the Bells (Les Clochettes de Kaboul).

Industry observers note that the France-based Iranian actress is attending the 30th edition of Estonia’s Tallinn Black Nights Film Festival (PÖFF), where the film had its world premiere on Wednesday night in the First Feature Competition. On Thursday, she talked to The Hollywood Reporter about the film and its call to stand up for human rights. It has been revealed that after all, Leila soon finds out that the kitchen she works in is a so-called bacha bazi house. These are establishments in which boys and young men are forced to dress in women’s clothes and dance for entertainment — regularly followed by sexual exploitation. Set in 2021, the film also sees the Taliban moving ever closer to Kabul. Leila tries to flee while helping a group of boys escape before the city falls. Sayed Hashimi (13 Days, 13 Nights) co-stars in the thriller, which includes images actually shot in Afghanistan. Le Pacte is handling sales.

Farahani talked to THR in Tallinn about When She Hears the Bells, why the film can speak to the Western world and why she isn’t spending time thinking about her next project.

How did you find out about this film and decide to star in it?

It has been revealed that chabname wanted me for some time, but I wasn’t reading the script because I was thinking I’d done The Patience Stone [set in war-torn Afghanistan]. So, it’s a space for other actresses to do that. But she insisted and insisted and insisted, and at the end she said, “We’re not going to do the movie [without you].” So, I ended up accepting the part without reading the script. I called her and said, “Let’s do it.”.

I couldn’t believe that this was Chabname’s first feature script and her directorial debut.

When I read the script, I was also really surprised. It was amazing. The script is as good as the movie.

According to recent reports, when She Hears the Bells combines thriller elements with the humane and emotional elements. How hard is that combination for you as the star?

In a recent development, I’m happy that I could see that it works because I’m very objective when I watch my movies. I know when it’s bad and it doesn’t work, and I know when it’s good. Last night [during the premiere], I could feel this works because I constantly had goosebumps over goosebumps over goosebumps. I felt, “Yeah, the emotion works.” For one of my friends, it is impossible to watch a movie without checking his phone. It was the first time I watched him not touching his phone, and at one point he just [leaned] forward. He was really engaged, and that was the best sign. The movie works. It really works.

It has been revealed that there are all sorts of dark concerns and threats in When She Hears the Bells. There is the bacha bazi, the situation of women, the encroaching Taliban. Do you see these as sharing any common themes?

According to recent reports, the way I always look at it is that you’re telling a story. It’s just that the story is about horrible things. I think about the character who is actually in that situation, which is terrible. From the outside, when you watch it, it is unbearable. But the character is in it, so, as the character, I don’t judge it as horrible. It’s just that I’m living in that horror.

Recent findings suggest that as an audience, you say, “This is awful, this is horrible!” But as a character inside, you cannot say this is horrible. You just act on it.

Do you have any hopes for what audiences will take away from the film?

If the audience is moved by it and asks questions, if the movie is seen and appreciated, it’s enough because so many of these movies can be so boring and so much.

According to recent reports, these subjects are too much for the white man to even comprehend. Because sometimes the horror is too much for the civilized world. They can’t even handle it. So they zap [it out] subconsciously.

But I think this movie works because it doesn’t show the actual horror. If you saw the horror [committed against] a little boy in the film, the audience would zap. But because it’s all left to the imagination, it keeps the white audience on a fine line of sitting and watching and feeling it, not blocking out the feeling.

In a recent development, this is the third year in a row that you have a film here at Tallinn. How much do you like the city and the festival?I love Tallinn, and I think the festival is great. I think people come here to watch movies, and the fact that this festival exists is a blessing because we all know the situation of the world and the budgets, and there are always challenges for these little festivals. But I come back to Tallinn anytime they ask me because I think it’s a great festival. I have had three movies here: Reading Lolita in Tehran, Oh, What Happy Days!, and this one.

Is there anything you can share about any potential next projects?

I’m a bit pessimistic, and I feel there’s a big war coming. So, in my head, I feel we just need to prepare for a war. My headspace is not much in the artistic world, because I feel the situation of the world is so bad, and I feel that humanity needs to be prepared for war. Especially in Europe, people have been so spoiled, and I think the first place that is going to be completely smashed is Europe. So, in my headspace, I’m in preparation for the end of the world, the end of our world. I think we’re going to enter a newly introduced order.

Recent findings suggest that who would start the war, or where?I just feel the world is going to go upside down. The world has become a minefield. No matter where you go, something is going to explode everywhere — every region you think of: America, Los Angeles, Florida, Africa, Europe, China, Taiwan. Everywhere you go, you might step on a mine, and it might explode. You just need to prepare.
