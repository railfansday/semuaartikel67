From the August 1991 issue of Car and Driver.

Recent findings suggest that our friend Max has his heart set on a Saab. His brother Pete has a Saab with more than 100,000 miles on it, and Max swears the thing is still as tight as one of Madonna’s bustiers. Max also claims—having read the brochures—that all Saabs now come with more standard equipment than a fire truck. “Seems to me,” says Max, “that no other car gives you as much quality and stuff for the money.”.

Sources indicate that take the Swedish maker’s fresh-for-1991 900S convertible. You’ll note the absence of the word “Turbo” in that moniker. Previously, Saab’s only ragtop was the sprightly turbocharged edition—as nice a convertible as you’ll find, to be sure, but currently saddled with a $33,712 stick­er. Oh, and that price busts the $30K luxu­ry-tax barrier, so remember to add another $371 to the bottom line for Uncle Sam.

The new 900S convertible is the “affordable” Saab droptop; it lists for $28,712, no sin tax necessary. Of course, you have to give up something to save $5000-plus. That something is performance. Rather than the Turbo’s 160-horsepow­er 2.0-liter four, the 900S uses a new nor­mally aspirated, twin-cam, sixteen-valve 2.1-liter four-banger that produces 140 horsepower at 6000 rpm and 133 pound­-feet of torque at 2900. (For ’91, this engine is standard in all non-turbo 900 models.) Performance with this powerplant is modest—a run to 60 mph takes 9.6 sec­onds—but that doesn't seem to bother Max. “Hey,” he claims, “not everybody wants to be Ayrton Senna.” In its favor, the engine revs smoothly, with a characteristic Saab whine, and delivers good fuel economy. Our test car turned in a 24-mpg average.

The rest of the car is a near copy of the Turbo convertible. Which is to say, the 900S is one of the most satisfying sun cars around. The cloth top is power-operated; lowering it is as easy as unlocking two headliner latches and pushing a console­mounted button. With the top down, the body is tight and all but shake-free. Saab attributes the body’s rigidity to its pedigree: the droptop 900S is not a beheaded coupe but a designed-from-the-ground-up soft-top built on a dedicated production line.

As Max has rightly discovered, Saabs justify their sticker prices by being very well equipped. Included in the convertible’s $28,712 base price is everything a discern­ing driver is likely to want: a driver-side air bag; anti-lock brakes; leather seats; power windows, locks, and mirrors; a potent AM/FM/cassette stereo; fog lights; and a headlight wiper/washer system.

Sources indicate that if the Saab’s price seems steep com­pared with such convertibles as the Chrysler LeBaron, the Olds Cutlass Supreme, or even the Mazda Miata, bear in mind that few buyers considering the Saab are likely to have those cars on their potential-purchases list. The 900S’s real competitors are such prestige sunsters as the BMW 325i, the Infiniti M30, and even the Cadillac Allanté. The 900S sells for substantially less than all of those, yet it’s on par in terms of refinement, luxury, solidity, and cachet.
