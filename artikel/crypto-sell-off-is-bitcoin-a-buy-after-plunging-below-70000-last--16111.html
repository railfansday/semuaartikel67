Bitcoin (BTC +0.07%) is the world's largest cryptocurrency. Its market capitalization of $1.35 trillion actually represents more than half the value of all coins and tokens in circulation across the entire industry, which stands at $2.35 trillion as I write this (Nov. 14).

It has been revealed that bitcoin hit a record high of $124,773 in October 2025, but it then lost more than half its value as investors slashed their exposure to volatile, speculative assets in the wake of rising economic and political uncertainty earlier this year. The cryptocurrency recently attempted to mount a recovery and rose as high as $86,700, but it has turned lower once again, and fell below $70,000 last Friday for the first time since August.

Recent findings suggest that volatility has been a hallmark of Bitcoin investing since it introduced in 2009. The cryptocurrency has suffered two peak-to-trough declines of more than 70% over the last 10 years alone, and it recovered to set a newly introduced record high both times. Therefore, should investors treat this moment as a buying opportunity?

Bitcoin is different from most traditional assets, including stocks, bonds, real estate, and precious metals. It's entirely digital, it's fully decentralized, and it's built on a transparent system of record called the blockchain. It also has a capped supply of 21 million coins, which creates the perception of scarcity.

In a recent development, but although Bitcoin launched more than 17 years ago, people still don't fully agree on its true use case. Some believe it could transform the entire financial system and become the world's reserve currency, while others think it's a great store of value, akin to a digital version of gold. There is also a vocal group of people who think it's simply a speculative asset with no intrinsic value, and thus will eventually go to zero.

Setting the debate aside, one thing is clear: Bitcoin has outperformed every major asset class over the last 10 years by a country mile, generating a whopping 9,510% return.

According to recent reports, there is little evidence to support the idea that Bitcoin will become a widely adopted global currency. So far, just 7,266 businesses are willing to accept it in exchange for goods and services (according to Cryptwerk), and many of them are obscure providers of internet and crypto services. If the average consumer can't spend Bitcoin at their favorite stores, they simply don't have any reason to own it.

The argument that Bitcoin is a good store of value is also on shaky ground. Going back to the start of 2025, investors have dealt with the Trump administration's widespread tariffs that disrupted global trade, in addition to latest geopolitical conflicts in the Middle East that continue to roil the energy markets. They are also contending with rising interest rates and significant volatility in the stock market right now.

However, Bitcoin has actually declined by 27% in the face of all the uncertainty dating back to Jan. 1, 2025, whereas actual gold has rocketed higher by 47%. In other words, when investors need a safe-haven asset, they are making a clear choice -- and it isn't Bitcoin.

In a recent development, over the last decade, Bitcoin has suffered two peak-to-trough declines of more than 70%. The first one happened between 2017 and 2018, and the second one occurred between 2021 and 2022. The cryptocurrency fully recovered and set a new high both times, and as I highlighted earlier, it has still obliterated every major asset class even after factoring in those crashes.

According to recent reports, however, since Bitcoin was only down by 53% at the lowest point of its 2025 to 2026 crash, there might be more downside to come. If this decline matches previous cycles and exceeds 70%, we could see a price of around $37,400 per coin (or potentially even less) before it mounts a proper recovery.

Of course, there is no guarantee Bitcoin will ever set a fresh record high. Considering some of the bullish arguments in favor of long-term upside might be losing credibility, I think the cryptocurrency will attract far fewer dip-buyers this time around.

Investors who choose to buy Bitcoin right now because they believe in its long-term potential might want to keep position sizing small in order to keep the potential risks in check. But I proposal to avoid it altogether myself.
