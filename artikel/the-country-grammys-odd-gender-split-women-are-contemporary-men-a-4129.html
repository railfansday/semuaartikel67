The Country Grammys’ Odd Gender Split: Women Are ‘Contemporary,’ Men Are ‘Traditional’

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

First, the exciting news: It’s going to be a record-setting year for female country artists at the 2027 Grammy Awards. For the first time in history, the entire Best Contemporary Country Album category is dominated by solo women artists. The last run to come close was 2021’s pool, but that also included the mixed-gender band Little Major Town, when the category was known as Best Country Album.

At the 69th Grammy Awards, Kelsea Ballerini’s Mount Pleasant, Carter Faith’s Cherry Valley, Megan Moroney’s Cloud 9, Kacey Musgraves’ Middle of Nowhere, and Ella Langley’s Dandelion are the contenders for Contemporary Country album, making for an incredible group of nominees where, no matter who wins, a woman will take top honors. But there’s a clear division when the Contemporary field is compared with that of Best Traditional Country Album.

It has been revealed that country’s women have often found recognition from Grammy voters when country-radio playlists fail. Despite Langley’s success this year, women still are played on the airwaves a small proportion of the time compared with their male counterparts. From Musgraves’ 2019 win for Golden Hour to the Chicks in 2007, the awards are often a corrective for the industry’s deeply endemic gender imbalance and reluctance to embrace left-of-center artists of any gender. It’s hard to say if these nominations will ever push programmers to shift their thinking: Musgraves’ sweep wasn’t enough to put the now-classic “Rainbow” into heavy rotation. But it does provide a boost that can help level the playing field when Music Row won’t come to bat.

According to recent reports, that remarked, there’s something strange afoot among this year’s country album Grammy nominations. While the Contemporary Country Album category is entirely women, the Best Traditional Country Album category is … all men. Last year marked the first awards for the Contemporary-versus-Traditional split, and it resulted in a win for Jelly Roll in Contemporary and Zach Top in Traditional (where Margo Price was the only female nominee). Allowing for more opportunities to recognize great music is always welcome, and the albums here are excellent — we are particularly ecstatic to see Charlie Worsham’s Once Upon a Second Time Around in contention in a year where he’s also up for a CMA Award and is about to become a Grand Ole Opry member. Charley Crockett’s Clovis, Vince Gill’s 50 Years From Home: Down at the Borderline, Cody Johnson’s Banks of the Trinity, and Willie Nelson’s Workin’ Man: Willie Sings Merle round out the group. But the gender split illuminates an problem many feared would happen when the Best Traditional Country Album category was introduced — that a “traditional” label (as opposed to “alternative” or similar) would go on to reinforce the patriarchal, and white, ideal of what “real” country music sounds and looks like. Carly Pearce’s truly excellent Honest Woman, for example, was submitted in the Traditional category but didn’t make the cut. Despite the sonics of the record (a lot of classic-country and bluegrass influences), would she have had a better shot in Contemporary? It’s hard to say, but based on the past two years of nominations, women can barely get in contention for Traditional album, while Nelson and Crockett are up for the second year in a row. None of this should diminish any of the artists’ achievements, but it’s concerning to see women, who are often policed for not being “country enough,” and non-white artists of any gender not make the “Traditional” cut at all.

Make no mistake, it will be a great year for country music at this year’s Grammys: women breaking records, Langley and Lambert snagging seven nominations each and in the main categories (including a much-deserved Producer nod for Lambert), and artists like Worsham and Faith getting recognized for truly superb albums. We just hope that siloing by gender isn’t a trend that continues in years to come, because the last thing the genre needs is an award that reinforces that division even further.
