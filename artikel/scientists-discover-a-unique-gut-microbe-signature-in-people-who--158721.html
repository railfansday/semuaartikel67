Scientists Discover a Unique Gut Microbe Signature in People Who Live to 100

🌐 Source: Scitechdaily • 📅 27 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 4 min read (766 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

People who lived to 100 carried a distinctive chemical fingerprint in their guts, and boosting the bacterial enzyme linked to it helped older mice respond better to insulin.

A single dose of engineered gut bacteria made older mice more sensitive to insulin about five months later. The UC San Diego team also analyzed data from young adults, older adults, and centenarians to see how the same gut bacterial activity varies with age in people.

Sources indicate that the study focused on bile acids, which the liver makes to help digest fats. Bile acids also carry signals that help regulate metabolism, inflammation, and the body’s internal clock. Gut bacteria make enzymes called bile salt hydrolases (BSH) that change the chemical makeup of bile acids and can alter the signals they send.

Older mice showed more microbial activity linked to BSH and more extensive changes to their bile acids than younger mice. The researchers then looked for a similar pattern in existing data from 48 young adults, 107 older adults, and 125 centenarians.

Centenarians had higher ratios of certain modified bile acids than either of the younger groups. Their gut microbiomes also contained more copies of a gene linked to BSH activity than those of older adults.

“Many studies of aging focus on what goes wrong as we get older, but our findings suggest that some changes in the gut microbiome may actually represent the body’s attempt to preserve metabolic health,” said senior author Amir Zarrinpar, MD, PhD, Stuart and Barbara L. Brody Endowed Chair in Circadian Biology and Medicine and professor of medicine at UC San Diego School of Medicine. “If we can identify those beneficial functions and safely strengthen them, we may eventually be able to develop fresh ways to promote healthier aging.”.

To test whether boosting BSH activity could change metabolism, the team gave 82-week-old mice a strain of gut bacteria engineered to keep making the enzyme. The bacteria took hold after one treatment and continued producing BSH over time.

About 20 weeks later, the mice responded significantly better to insulin and were modestly better at clearing glucose from their blood than mice given a matched control bacterium. By week 32, the treated mice also showed changes consistent with lower body weight, less body fat, and more lean mass.

Sources indicate that other measurements suggested that the mice used more fat for energy and that their mitochondria worked better. The researchers also saw shifts in the activity of genes involved in inflammation and the body’s internal clock.

Sources indicate that centenarians and aged mice represent different stages of life, and the mouse experiment did not establish whether the treatment extends lifespan. Studies in people will need to determine whether higher BSH activity helps preserve health with age, reflects healthier aging, or both.

In a recent development, the researchers plan to test how different forms of the enzyme affect bile acid metabolism, inflammation, and energy use. They also want to see whether female mice respond similarly, given known differences in how males and females age.

“The goal isn’t simply to live longer—it’s to understand the biology that allows some people to remain resilient as they age,” stated Zarrinpar. “The microbiome gives us an intriguing opportunity because, unlike many aspects of aging, it may be something we can modify. This study gives us a starting point for asking whether beneficial microbial functions can be amplified to help preserve metabolic health later in life.”.
