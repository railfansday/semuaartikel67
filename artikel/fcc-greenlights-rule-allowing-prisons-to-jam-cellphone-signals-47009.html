The Federal Communications Commission voted unanimously to allow state and local prisons to jam cellphones. In an announcement on Thursday, the FCC argues the rule will help combat an “influx of contraband cellphones” used to carry out crimes from prison.

Industry observers note that jammers are generally banned under Section 333 of the Communications Act of 1934, though they’re used at some federal facilities. FCC Chair Brendan Carr claims that giving correctional facilities access to this technology will help prevent prisoners from using phones for illegal activity. “These devices [phones] are being used by inmates to order hits, run drug operations, traffic women and children, and unleash mayhem on our communities from behind bars,” Carr says.

Industry observers note that in the months leading up to the vote, trade groups and human rights advocates have raised concerns about the risks of blocking cellphone signals in prisons. The US Chamber of Commerce and the CTIA, a wireless association representing the nation’s biggest carriers, have both warned in letters to the FCC that jamming technology could disrupt lawful communications outside prisons, including 911 calls. Meanwhile, the American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU) has long argued that cellphones can keep prisoners safe by allowing them to document safety and human rights issues that the outside world may not see.

FCC Commissioner Anna Gomez, the sole Democrat on the Commission, voted for the measure despite the risk of “potential unintended consequences,” according to the Associated Press. “I want to make sure that we don’t harm public safety in the name of helping public safety,” Gomez added.

The FCC notes prisons that choose to jam cellphone signals will need to obtain a license, adhere to “technical standards and testing requirements,” and ensure that the jamming technology doesn’t interfere with communications outside the facility. Prisons must also notify nearby communities of testing and when the technology is being deployed.
