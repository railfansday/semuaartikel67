Breaking: The 3 Types of Running Workouts You Need to Train for Your First Half Marathon

Sources indicate that nowhere in any running rulebook does it say you have to train for a 5K or 10K before signing up for a half marathon. As a beginner, 13.1 miles is an awesome way to approach long-distance running. You just need a initiative that meets you where you’re at right now and progresses gradually to get you to the starting line feeling strong.

Cut to Runner’s World’s 20-week beginner half marathon plan created by Meg Takacs, CPT, a UESCA-certified running coach and founder of the Movement and Miles app.

In a recent development, it balances three key workouts designed to help you build endurance safely, recover well, and get comfortable with longer distances. You’ll spread these workouts throughout 20 weeks, which gives you plenty of time to build rather than your typical 10- or 14-week plan.

Each workout progresses as you move through the plan, so it’s a good idea to familiarize yourself with their basic structure and purpose before you jump into training. Here’s how to get the most out of each workout.

Recent findings suggest that run/walks help you build aerobic fitness and endurance gradually. You’ll start with a one-minute run followed by a one-minute walk for 20 minutes total, adding one minute to the run interval every session. With run/walks starting at three times a week, you’ll work up to a 10-mile run/walk in week 16, where you run for 20-minute intervals and walk for two minutes (this is your weekend long run).

Do the run intervals at a comfortable, low-intensity pace, meaning you should be able to hold a conversation with a friend the whole time. On a scale of 1 to 10 for rate of perceived exertion (RPE), aim for a 2 to 4 for effort level, with 1 being easy like a walk and 10 meaning all-out effort.

Why it works: This is the backbone of the beginner half marathon plan. Run/walk sessions help you build the endurance you need to run farther.

10 x 1 minute walk, 1 minute easy-effort run.

Recent findings suggest that in week four of this plan, you’ll start incorporating continuous easy runs, beginning with 25 minutes at a truly easy effort. The longest continuous run you will complete is 8 miles with as few walk breaks as possible by week 17.

Throughout the plan, there’s more of a focus on time-based easy runs rather than distance. “For beginners, time on your feet is more crucial than mileage in order to get your body gradually acclimated to running,” Takacs previously told Runner’s World.

Why it works: Continuous easy runs help boost endurance and give you the confidence you need to run farther.

Run 25 minutes at an easy effort (RPE 2 to 4), limiting walk breaks as much as possible.

The first of your weekly speed workouts begins in week 3. You’ll complete 400-meter repeats at a moderate effort with two-minute walks in between. Moderate effort is slightly above your easy effort, reaching a 4 to 6 out of 10 RPE. “The goal is to try and run each repeat faster than the last,” Takacs adds.

You should still be able to speak during the work intervals, but you’re breathing heavier and can only talk in phrases. Over the course of the training plan, you’ll work your way up to longer intervals, like 800-meter, 1200-meter, and mile repeats.

Why it works: Speed workouts aren’t just there to help you get faster; they also boost your overall fitness and running economy so easy runs feel easier.

Recent findings suggest that short postrun strides help build speed and leg turnover, and you’ll find them sprinkled throughout your plan. These accelerations will help you build more speed, improve your running form by getting your legs to turn over quicker, and boost your confidence.

According to recent reports, the goal of strides is to run at a hard effort for 10 to 15 seconds, but it doesn’t have to be an all-out sprint. Aim for a 7 to 10 RPE; you should only be able to say a word or three at a time.

Sources indicate that in any training proposal, strength workouts are crucial to prevent injury, support your running economy and durability, and get stronger. In this plan, you’ll complete strength workouts once or twice a week before or after your runs. Depending on your preference, you can decide to run in the morning, then follow with strength in the afternoon or evening (or vice versa).

Takacs recommends starting with bodyweight exercises if you’re new to strength training and then adding in weights as you get stronger.

There’s a reason coaches tell runners to work hard and recover harder. Your recovery days are meant to feel like a true recovery where you can come back to your next run feeling refreshed, strong, and excited to get back out there.

Sources indicate that it’s a good idea to loosen stiff muscles with foam rolling, stretching, and some mobility drills. If you need a complete day off, it’s also okay to not do anything at all except watch TV, read a book, or hang out with friends. A day off is good for the mind and the body. This is also a good opportunity to make sure you’re getting to bed early so you can show up to each run as your best, well-rested self.
