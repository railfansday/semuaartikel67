Diabetes and Alzheimer’s share a link that scientists are still trying to explain

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A review of 40 studies examines the link between type 2 diabetes and Alzheimer’s, offering fresh estimates of risk and unanswered questions.

Industry observers note that how many people with type 2 diabetes go on to develop Alzheimer’s each year? Five in every 1,000, according to a latest estimate.

The estimate comes from a review of 40 studies. In it, people with type 2 diabetes were about 53% more likely to develop Alzheimer’s than people without it.

Diabetes has been tied to dementia for years. Trouble is, most earlier reviews threw several kinds of diabetes or dementia into one pot.

This one was limited to type 2 diabetes and Alzheimer’s disease, and a link between the two was still found. Yearly case counts, rarely reported before, were worked out as well.

Emmanuel Adewuyi is a researcher at the Curtin School of Population Health at Curtin University in Australia.

Adewuyi worked on the review with lead author Thi Thu Huong Nguyen and colleagues in Australia, the UK, and Nigeria.

Sources indicate that the 53% figure describes the relative increase in risk. The rate of upcoming cases over time is called incidence.

“Relative risk tells us how two groups compare, while incidence tells us how often new cases occur: putting those numbers together gives us a clearer picture of the burden of Alzheimer’s disease among people living with type 2 diabetes,” Adewuyi said.

According to recent reports, every study in the review was observational. People’s health was recorded, and no treatment was assigned.

It has been revealed that research like that can show that two conditions go together. It can’t show that one causes the other.

Sources indicate that dementia affects more than 57 million people worldwide. Alzheimer’s disease accounts for about 60% to 70% of those cases.

Type 2 is by far the most common form of diabetes, accounting for more than 90% of cases.

Sources indicate that both conditions become more common with age. Many of the same heart and metabolic risk factors are shared by the two.

According to recent reports, type 2 diabetes has a stronger link to vascular dementia than to Alzheimer’s. Vascular dementia follows damage to blood vessels in the brain.

Industry observers note that mixing the two, the authors wrote, blurs how much of the risk belongs to Alzheimer’s. Adewuyi’s team therefore kept only studies in which both type 2 diabetes and Alzheimer’s were clearly identified.

According to recent reports, studies that mixed type 2 with type 1 or pregnancy-related diabetes were dropped. Studies that could not separate Alzheimer’s from other dementias were dropped as well.

Recent findings suggest that the researchers searched four medical databases for studies published through April 2026. They found 9,431 articles, but only 40 met their criteria.

Industry observers note that those 40 studies were published between 1997 and 2026 and covered 12 countries. South Korea contributed 10 studies, the United States eight, and the United Kingdom seven.

It has been revealed that in 38 studies, researchers followed participants over time, with average follow-up periods ranging from two to 15 years. In most studies, the average participant age was between 55 and 75.

The combined study samples totaled more than 27 million people, though some people may have appeared in more than one study. For example, all seven UK studies used data from the same source, the UK Biobank.

Industry observers note that in 26 of the studies, people with type 2 diabetes were compared with people without it. New Alzheimer’s cases were counted in each group.

The results had already been adjusted for other factors, most often age and sex, the authors wrote.

When the 26 were pooled, Alzheimer’s risk was 53% higher for people with type 2 diabetes. With any single study taken out, the increase stayed between 50% and 57%, the authors wrote.

Across subgroups such as region, age, and study date, the increase ranged from 40% to 66%.

Industry observers note that separate results for men and women were given in only a handful of studies. Similar increases were seen there: 76% for men and 73% for women.

The authors wrote that the sex-specific figures should be treated with caution.

Industry observers note that the International Diabetes Federation’s Diabetes Atlas has its own, lower estimate. In the atlas, the increase for Alzheimer’s was put at 34%.

According to recent reports, the authors wrote that the two estimates are compatible with each other. Different sets of studies in the two reviews may explain the gap, they wrote.

Across 23 of the 40 studies, covering about 9 million people, the pooled rate among people with type 2 diabetes was about 4.7 fresh Alzheimer’s cases per 1,000 people a year.

Recent findings suggest that from study to study, it swung from under 1 case per 1,000 people a year to almost 14. Much depended on how a study went looking for Alzheimer’s.

Where hospital or national health records were combed, about 4 cases per 1,000 people a year were found.

Sources indicate that where participants were diagnosed using clinical criteria or memory tests, the figure was about 10. Health records probably miss milder cases, the authors wrote.

The yearly rate per 1,000 people varied by region. It was about 7.5 in North America and 5.8 in East Asia and the Pacific. In Europe and Central Asia, it was 1.2.

Recent findings suggest that health, access to care, and diagnosis may all differ by region, the authors wrote. Those explanations still need to be tested.

It has been revealed that only a few studies split their results by sex. In that small set, the yearly rate was 3.6 per 1,000 women and 2.8 per 1,000 men.

In a recent development, the authors used a standard scale to assess the evidence behind both main estimates. They rated its certainty as very low.

None of the 40 studies tested a treatment, and their results varied widely.

The team rated 38 studies as having a low risk of bias and two as having a moderate risk. But those ratings did not account for how often participants visited a doctor.

According to recent reports, people with type 2 diabetes may have more medical appointments. That could give doctors more chances to notice and diagnose Alzheimer’s.

Earlier research by Adewuyi and colleagues found that the two diseases share some genetic influences. However, it found limited evidence that genes linked to type 2 diabetes directly increase Alzheimer’s risk.

According to recent reports, insulin resistance, high blood sugar, and inflammation could also help explain the connection, the authors stated. But the 53% higher risk highlights a pattern across groups of people, not proof that diabetes directly causes Alzheimer’s.

Sources indicate that in 11 of the 40 studies, researchers relied on participants to report whether they had diabetes. Most studies also took place in high-income countries.

The review could not determine whether better diabetes management changes a person’s risk of Alzheimer’s.

Studies inconsistently reported how long participants had lived with diabetes, how well their blood sugar was controlled, and which medications they used.

Metformin, pioglitazone, and acarbose have each been tied to lower dementia risk in earlier research. So have other diabetes drugs, GLP-1 drugs among them, in people with type 2 diabetes. Cause is as hard to pin down there as here.

Adewuyi’s team described the fresh figures as a benchmark for future studies testing whether diabetes treatment changes Alzheimer’s risk.

In the meantime, the team sees a case for watching memory and thinking as part of long-term diabetes care.

The authors mentioned that future studies would be better if researchers combined individual patient data from multiple studies instead of relying on published summaries.

This would help them account for other risk factors more accurately.

The full study was published in the journal Alzheimer’s Research & Therapy.
