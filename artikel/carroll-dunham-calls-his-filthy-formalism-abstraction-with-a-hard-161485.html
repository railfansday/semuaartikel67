Industry observers note that I thought it best to confess up front that I was still forming an opinion about his work. Carroll Dunham took that like a gentleman. He is familiar, he made clear to me during ourranginginterview, with the two main camps of interpretation about his potty-mouthed paintings and drawings: 1) that he is a high formalist with an ear for the unconscious seldom rivaled since Surrealism, and 2) that he is a simple vulgarian.

“I’m lucky people even want to write about my work,” Dunham remarked. “I made the paintings. They can say whatever they want.” At 76, Dunham is thin, likable, and frank. He has a sharp, deadpan face framed by transition lenses and Beckettian white hair. When we spoke in his studio in Connecticut, he wore a silk suit in the shade of gray-green the French call caca d’oie, the shirt buttoned only in the top half, cholo style, with chunky sneakers in the same shade. The spacious upstairs had been given over to small abstract sculptures he was making out of aluminum sheeting with assistants. It was July, and Dunham was between two capstone surveys of a long career. A show of his drawings spanning 1974 to 2024 had recently come down at the Art Institute of Chicago, while curators at the Institute of Contemporary Art, Miami, were finalizing a survey of his paintings from 1983 to the present for December. It was the kind of summary moment an artist is lucky to be alive for.

Sources indicate that since the ’90s, Dunham’s best-known pictures have starred carefully framed cartoon penises, vaginas, and anuses in varying states of specificity, usually attached to the bodies or the faces of humanoids engaged in sex or violence. This scenery belongs to a tradition of ridiculous bodily extremes, from Greek vases to Thomas Rowlandson to Gustave Courbet to Nicole Eisenman. His style, meanwhile, belongs to the black outlines and dropped-in colors of cartoons. That graphic clarity seems to ask us to read his bodily subjects loud and clear, without any of the technical seduction of peers like Lisa Yuskavage or John Currin.

In a recent development, although his loud and lusty scenarios committed no real transgression (Peter Saul took care of that in the ’60s, then Robert Mapplethorpe in the ’80s), what may genuinely surprise the uninitiated is the academic remove that characterizes the writing about Dunham. His work is discussed—and discussed primarily by those born before 1975—almost exclusively in the specialist language of form or space or paint application, with little reference to the subject matter. It seems that the writers in Camp 1 understand the shock factor that attends men dildoing their rectums with pistols, or angrily ejaculating on each other from enormous penis-noses, as ammunition against the claim that subject should dictate one’s opinion of a painting. As if to deny the charge of prudery, some of them describe moments of rapture. On a Dunham painting made between 2006 and 2012, of a giant woman spreading her anus and vagina above a landscape like a parade float, the artist Paul Chan wrote of feeling transported: “When I entered the room where it hung [at the Whitney], I gasped. The painting was at once exuberant, provocative, and entrancing. It literally took my breath. I quickly glanced at the other people in the room. Was anyone else having an asthma attack looking at this?”.

In a recent development, representing Camp 2 are John Yau (The Brooklyn Rail, 2007: “Saul is an iconoclast and Dunham is a wannabe.”) and a young man in a basketball jersey I met at the Chicago show. This man was examining a pencil scribble from 2020 of a fist clasping an erection, the balls parted and meatus shaped like a teardrop. I asked him what he thought, and he said, “I feel like I’m being pranked.”.

Sources indicate that dunham walked me to the “other studio,” a smaller room reserved for painting, where acrylic tubes lay neatly on a table in gradients, labels down. A canvas he had just finishedstood on sawhorses. It showed a man with long black hair down on all fours in a sculpture studio. The painting is called Looking for Something, and its protagonist’s pants are on the floor. His skin is white, and a woman, who is purple, kneels behind him, her head thrown back in a wail or moan. With her hand, she does something to the man’s ass that is not visible to us. In the foreground, another purple person, a Peeping Tom, watches them through a window. Nine of these most recent “Sculptor” paintings, from about Covid to now, were to go up at the Matthew Brown gallery in Manhattan in September as a sort of appetizer for the Miami show.

I asked where these recent purple/white folks come from. “They come from my work,” Dunham explained. “And I don’t mean that to be glib. They really do. So to even answer that question, I would have to go, ‘Before I was doing these paintings, I was doing these paintings, then these paintings,’” he continued, miming with his hands successive leaps into the past.

Industry observers note that BORN THE FIFTH IN A line of Carrolls to a Connecticut poultry farmer who turned to real estate, Dunham went to Phillips Academy (Andover), class of 1967. The sculptor Mel Kendrick was his roommate. “Tip was doing Rapidograph drawings,” Kendrick recalled as we spoke by phone, using Dunham’s nickname. “Almost hippie drawings, very fine sketches.” (Of “Tip,” Dunham mentioned, “It’s a WASP thing.”) Kendrick also recalls Dunham’s appetite for science fiction. (Face-down on his studio table, I spotted James L. Cambias’s Darkling Sea.) Dunham took darkroom classes. In the school’s literary magazine, his finely exposed photograph of a pouting and rotund lady in a playground swing illustrates a poem by Anthony Grafton, the historian-to-be. “Anything he did, he always had a touch,” Kendrick said. “Tip is very specific. And that’s something I don’t know if people know about his paintings, even now.”.

It has been revealed that after a year at the University of Rochester, Dunham followed Kendrick to Trinity College, in Hartford, where they worked in a small studio art department. “That meant smoking a lot of dope and reading Artforum,” mentioned Dunham. On weekends, Dunham and Kendrick would drive down from Connecticut in Dad’s (Carroll IV’s) pickup truck, Kendrick recalled, to sample the upcoming galleries in Soho—Bykert, Paula Cooper, Sonnabend, OK Harris—and to feast on spaghetti and jug wine at the apartment of their Trinity teacher Terence LaNoue.

LaNoue made almost three-dimensional combine paintings with poured latex and molds. He “was a great teacher, but I always had a kind of resistance to what he was making,” Dunham remarked. “And I’ve been that guy. I know that I’ve had good experiences between students and me where it really didn’t have to do with them being that turned on about my work.” Dunham never followed, either, in the footsteps of the artist with whom LaNoue installed him for a formative internship. Dorothea Rockburne produced—and still does, at 97—large, clean panes of paper or vellum, lined geometrically or coated in graphite. But the two got on, and still do. “Brighter than almost anybody around,” Rockburne said of him by phone.

What attracted Dunham was the planning implied by Rockburne’s planes—she taught him and Kendrick “how to handle a T-square and other basic things,” she stated. He was also drawn to the repetitious text paintings of Mel Bochner (who lived with Rockburne), and to the heaps of raw material arranged by Barry Le Va and by Richard Serra. On graduating, he moved into a loft on Thomas Street shared with Kendrick and Peter Wheelwright, the architect. His 1970s and ’80s in Latest York were “isolation lite,” he told me. Dunham was part of no scene, he said, but befriended the photographers of the so-called Pictures Generation, and married one: Laurie Simmons. For seven years he worked in a frame shop, then for another seven at Time magazine, as a layout artist, all the while plotting his entrance into the art world.

DUNHAM’S ’70s ART BEGAN with single-line squiggles on canvas or paper. Some are elegantly coiled springs, while others resemble long strings dropped to the floor, their loops filled with ground colors of fast-drying casein or acrylic. He debuted with them at Artists Space in 1981, the Chicago survey was their first time on view since. Rockburne sees little of herself in Dunham’s famous later work. “It had that kind of [Sol] LeWittian, instructional aspect,” said the artist John Newman, another Dunham confederate from those days, by phone. “In the ’70s, drawing was sort of raised to a upcoming place,” Newman explained, “as a much more immediate and idea-oriented kind of territory that wasn’t so laden with the history of painting and sculpture.”.

Recent findings suggest that the string-loops soon became blobs. When Dunham found the lid of a shipping crate on the street, around 1980, and used that for his surface, the almost sculptural grains and knots of the plywood veneer became guidelines for even more specific and conflicting blobs all mashed together—amoebas, geysers, wilting donuts, kinked tubes, beans, ripples, Froot Loops—in palettes ranging from medical textbook to clown paint. It is with these plywood paintings that the Miami survey begins. From 1984 to ’88, they appeared in three shows at the Baskerville + Watson gallery, with the middle show limited to monochromes.

“When the veneer things came out,” Newman recalled, it was amazing. “Think about the first time you ever read James Joyce. You really had to step up to it, participate in it. It was new information.” The art dealer David Nolan “hated them” at first, he remarked by phone: “I thought these garish, psychedelic colors were just there to shock me, and I just wasn’t interested.” But after a while, he began “to peel away the layers and see how they’re put together. And once I understood that, I thought, Oh God.”.

The third show sold out before even opening, scoring acquisitions by the Museum of Modern Art, the Whitney, and the Brooklyn Museum. Dunham quit his magazine job. Gestural painting had been deemed safe again by young galleries, and although Dunham used a brand of biomorphism that didn’t quite fit with the headliners—Basquiat, Baselitz, Schnabel—he was made. Instead of buying up pallets of plywood and sticking safely to what worked, however, Dunham pivoted again. By the ’90s, his veneer paintings migrated to stretched linen, this time as single forms. They began to elongate into screaming fish-lipped mouths, nugget bodies, wincing faces, and undeniable penises. “They weren’t until they were,” Kendrick said of these organs. Nolan noticed them, too. But since they were untitled, Nolan said, “I thought he was avoiding what was right in front of our noses. They are bodily explosions. Come on.”.

DUNHAM HAD BEEN PLENTY aware. He described the ’90s work to me as “abstraction with a hard-on.” But he seemed as bewildered as I about its origin. He spoke with plainness about sex and aggression as if he were a social worker, treating his own work as a sort of passive conduit for those realities. This tone did not seem to me like an act. I asked if he had tried to be provocative with his art, then or now. He remarked no: “It doesn’t interest me very much … The entire enterprise was provocative.” He spoke of Duchamp’s urinal.

According to recent reports, into the fresh century and his large survey at the New Museum in New York from 2002 to 2003, which was his last show of that scale until this year, his canvases begin to carry entire buildings and shiploads, colonies and planets full of hateful little nugget people with their penises, guns, and hideous gourd breasts. The exceptions from sex—lumpy tree scenes and landscapes—became the transgression. From there, he went more humanoid. In the past 20 years, his nuggets have grown up, first into suited G-men with fedoras and angular suits, then into cleanly clarified cartoon nudes, their globe buttocks outlined in elegant black and set in plain landscapes or rooms. They wrestle, fight, bathe, masturbate, copulate, and blind each other. They examine their gaping anuses, their big labia like bike tubes, and their nipples like air-hockey pushers.

These characters occupy about half the Miami paintings. In one, Horse and Rider (My X), 2013–15, a woman holds her arms up in a “Why me?” pose on the back of a horse. The lines of her reach continue south into a stand of flowers and a broken tree branch, dividing the canvas into a perfect X, which Dunham has left visible in pencil beneath the paint. At the geometric center is the woman’s anus, as if to say “Look here.” Just beneath, the horse’s anus gapes, too. He turns back at us with a look like “Excuse you.” (No human or humanoid, however, has eyes in a Dunham work.).

Dunham remarked of this X composition, “I was very excited when I figured out that that would work.” He explained: “It’s two layers of truth. There’s the subject and whatever that requires to be plausibly rendered.” (He counted “breasts, genitalia, asshole, some notion of limbs, and hair.”) “Then the geometry of the canvas has this other kind of truth, and the two things have to become one thing.” And the woman? “I honestly do not remember why I thought it would be interesting to put a woman on a horse in the paintings.” He thought. “It’s nice to have a black dot in the center of a painting. And it’s even nicer when that black dot is a woman’s ass, you know?”.

Nolan was the only one I spoke to who saw strategy in Dunham’s turn to hyper-figuration. He saw the artist “dealing with his own demons or faults about the world.” But he described the work as Dunham did, composition-first. “If you try to forget there’s a face in there, the compositions are very strong.” He advised me to view the paintings upside down, pointing out convincing similarities to Aztecan decoration and adding that recently he, Nolan, had refused to sell one—for a lot of money—until the potential buyer flew to Upcoming York to see the shapes in person. “That is a problem with the younger generation. They will lose all of the subtleties.”.

In a recent development, WHAT DOES THE YOUNGER generation think? The Miami show this winter (and the Chicago catalog forever) will give them the chance to see Dunham’s career as a single entity and to decide if the evolution of blob to aggression to anus stands up to the hindsight of a more literally minded era. In the purple alien characters on view at Matthew Brown this fall, from the series Dunham calls “Studio on Another Planet” (2022–26), one was hard-pressed not to read a narrative about artists at work in a culture that will misunderstand them: a solitary white male surrounded by voyeurs (critics? dealers? posterity?) as he quietly molds his clay on their land, incurring their sinister attentions. In the last of those canvases, Finished (2019–2025), the aliens have had enough. They’ve dragged the artist outside and are now quartering him to death.

In a recent development, “You’re not supposed to get it,” Newman noted of Dunham’s pictures. “Art is about having an experience.” Newman speaks, Darwin-like, of Dunham’s characters emerging from the primordial soup of the plywood paintings. “Younger people probably don’t know the early work. And I actually think if they did, it would help them understand his later work better.” In my conversations with Camp 1, I began to wonder if there has ever been a generation more permissive, more game, than the one that had Dunham as a sort of living symbol of egress: out of the hermetic tasks of Minimalism, through pure shape, into a future where human fictions of any extreme can live without excuse or embarrassment. And I began to envy them.

Industry observers note that I asked Dunham if he believed pure abstraction existed. He said yes, adding, “And I think that when I started my work, I was doing it. And then I think at a certain point I realized that it fascinated me to try to add some really literal aspects of just being a human being incarnated, or incarcerated, however you think about it, in a body.”.

According to recent reports, but I didn’t fully buy Dunham’s hermetic account of his own work as “always in some way self-referential.” There seems to be plenty of lived Artist v. World in his pictures and posture, and plenty of visual breadcrumbs leading to the culture at large. I asked, for instance, which cartoons he watched with his two children, who were born in 1986 and ’92. His eyes widened. “Ren & Stimpy was good,” he noted of John Kricfalusi’s unhinged and influential program. “But the masterpiece of ’90s cartooning as far as I’m concerned was Angry Beavers,” which he watched with his son. I was fishing. The affinities between Dunham’s ’90s work and the prevailing depravities of Nickelodeon and Cartoon Network are inarguable; and in those affinities, a latest generation may see homage to the last golden age of a truly transgressive child-absurd.

According to recent reports, speaking of his younger child, Dunham flashed back: “He came into my studio a lot. He would pick up on things I was drawing and then he would draw stuff of his own that was kind of like that. So I got a lot of, you know, 6- and 7-year-old energy coming back at me about my own work.” Dunham smiled. “It definitely encouraged a certain kind of energy. It’s very sweet to think about actually.”.
