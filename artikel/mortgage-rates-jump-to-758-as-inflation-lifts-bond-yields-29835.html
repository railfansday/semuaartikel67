Mortgage rates jump to 7.58% as inflation lifts bond yields

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Mortgage rates skyrocketed last week on the back of rising inflation and bond yields, erasing some of the recent affordability gains for potential homebuyers.

It has been revealed that at HousingWire‘s Mortgage Rates Center on Tuesday, rates for 30-year conforming loans averaged 7.58%, up 23 basis points from one week ago. Rates for 30-year jumbo loans jumped 31 bps to average 7.87%, while rates for 30-year loans through the Federal Housing Administration (FHA) rose 15 bps to 7.37%.

HousingWire Lead Analyst Logan Mohtashami wrote over the weekend that “last week wasn’t good for mortgage rates or the bond market” due to hawkish statements from Federal Reserve officials and hot inflation data from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS). Additionally, the U.S. conflict with Iran and potential tariff increases against Mexico propelled long-term Treasury rates and mortgage rates upward.

It has been revealed that interest rate traders took notice. According to the CME Group‘s FedWatch tool, more than 93% expect benchmark rates to increase by 25 bps at next month’s Fed meeting. That’s up from 76% one month ago. It would be the second hike of the year by the Fed and put the federal funds rate at a range of 4% to 4.25%.

Kyle Bass, business production manager at Refi.com, pointed to Mortgage Bankers Association application data showing that recent downward moves in rates had positive impacts on loan demand. But with visible volatility in rates on a day-to-day basis, he stated, preparation between loan officers and borrowers is needed to lock in the lowest possible rate and get lower monthly payments.

In a recent development, “Borrowers who benefit are the ones who prepared before the number moved. A shadow application, with income documents, statements and credit in order, lets a homeowner ask what their current mortgage could do for them the day rates cooperate. Waiting for a better number only works if you are ready when it arrives,” Bass said.

In a recent development, A report released Tuesday by Realtor.com demonstrates that the new-home segment is giving the typical buyer lower rates and better affordability than existing homes. Homebuilders, who are also under financial pressure, continue to offer rate buydowns and other incentives as they seek to offload their inventory.

Recent findings suggest that realtor.com reported that the average mortgage rate for a fresh-home buyer in the third quarter was 5.01%, more than a full percentage point lower than the 6.15% rate for an existing-home buyer. That translates to a difference of $258 in monthly payments for someone buying a $400,000 home with a 10% down payment.

Sources indicate that “Builders have adjusted to a market where affordability is still a major hurdle, but that adjustment isn’t always visible in the listing price,” Joel Berner, senior economist at Realtor.com, noted in a statement. “Reduced mortgage rates can help make a new home more affordable for buyers while allowing builders to hold asking prices relatively steady.”.

Recent findings suggest that the report noted that reduced rates are often temporary and only last for the first few years after origination. But even when comparing 30-year fixed rates, those who bought a new home in the third quarter saved, with average rates of 6.19% compared to 6.68% for existing home-buyers. On the same $400,000 purchase with 10% down, the new-home buyer saves about $117 per month in interest.

“Mortgage-rate incentives can make a meaningful difference in what a buyer pays each month, but it’s important to understand how long the lower rate lasts,” Berner said. “A temporary buydown can provide relief early on, while a lower rate fixed for the life of the loan offers a different kind of savings.”.

Recent findings suggest that selma Hepp, chief economist at Cotality, remarked in a statement that tariff-driven increases to building materials are putting pressure on the upcoming-home segment. In turn, this winds up being felt by consumers through higher home purchase prices. Regardless of the type of home being purchased, rising insurance premiums and property taxes are also straining budgets.

“For housing, the key question is not simply whether mortgage rates decline, but whether they fall enough to meaningfully improve purchasing power and bring more buyers and sellers back to the market,” Hepp mentioned.” Until then, I expect activity to remain relatively subdued, making the spring homebuying season an increasingly important test of whether the market can finally turn a corner.”.

While housing market professionals continue to grapple with fewer home sales and lower mortgage origination volumes, the U.S. economy as a whole continues to show resilience to inflation, tariffs and geopolitical uncertainty.

Recent findings suggest that melissa Cohn, regional vice president at William Raveis Mortgage, pointed to last week’s Producer Price Index (PPI), which demonstrated 6.5% inflation on an annualized basis in October. It was the largest year-over-year increase since November 2022. That came only days after the Consumer Price Index showed monthly inflation of 0.4%.

Sources indicate that “Unfortunately, the CPI number that we got earlier this week has set off bond yields, and bond yields were probably up almost 25 basis points from where they were at the end of the previous week,” Cohn mentioned Friday in a statement. “It’s not just because of the CPI number, but because of expectations about the Fed, where members are saying, ‘Well, we’re definitely going to have to hike rates.’ We know that the market’s expecting it.

Recent findings suggest that “All of the benefit that we got in mortgage rates over the course of the past three weeks was basically just thrown out the window again, because mortgage rates are really tied to the rate of inflation. We’re back on the inflation wagon.”.

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh offered some forward-looking remarks on Monday during a jobs and productivity conference in San Francisco, saying that the central bank continues to look closely at the development of “super intelligence” and its impacts on the global and national economies.

Sources indicate that “At the Fed, our role in all this is neither advocacy nor opposition. Our responsibility is to recognize that the widespread adoption of this technology will have significant impact on the economy, with key implications for monetary policy down the road,” Warsh said.
