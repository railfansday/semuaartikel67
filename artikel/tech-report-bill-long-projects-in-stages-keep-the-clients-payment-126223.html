Recent findings suggest that don’t wait until a months-long project ends to bill it. Tie each payment to something the client can see, like an approved design or a framed house, and write those triggers into the contract. Keep the last payment small, so most of the price is paid before the job ends.

In a recent development, waiting means you pay for the whole job before the client pays for any of it. Months of wages, subcontractor bills, and materials go out first, and a client who disputes your one invoice at the end can hold back the entire price.

According to recent reports, A milestone the client can see leaves no doubt about when a payment is due. A first draft or a framed house is either finished or not, while a trigger like “half done” invites an argument over what half means.

Recent findings suggest that then set the amounts so the money you’ve collected stays ahead of what you’ve spent. That way the client’s payments, not your own cash, cover each stage as you work through it.

A web design studio building a $24,000 website could bill in four stages and collect $19,200 by the time the build is done, instead of waiting three months to send one invoice.

The contract should name what triggers each invoice, when it’s due, and what happens when a payment is late. Two clauses keep a client from stalling your payments. One states work on each stage starts only after the previous stage’s invoice is paid. The other says a milestone counts as approved if the client hasn’t responded within five business days.

Extra requests need the same paperwork. Write each one up as a change order, a signed add-on to the contract that sets its price, before you start the work. Add that price to the next stage’s invoice instead of doing the extra work for free.

Cap the final payment at 20%, and a client who drags out the last invoice can hold back no more than a fifth of the price.
