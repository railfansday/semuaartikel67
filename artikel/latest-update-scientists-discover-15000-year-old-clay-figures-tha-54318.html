Latest Update: Scientists Discover 15,000-Year-Old Clay Figures That Rewrite the History of Human Art

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Clay figurines from the Middle East suggest that artistic traditions were already being shared across generations before farming, possibly with children taking part in their creation.

It has been revealed that long before agriculture transformed life in the Middle East, people were already shaping clay into figures that reflected the world around them. Some resembled birds, mammals, and humans, while others carried traces of red or orange coloring. One fragment may even depict the facial markings of a mountain gazelle.

It has been revealed that these small artifacts, originally excavated from three sites in present-day Israel, date back roughly 15,000 to 11,500 years. Researchers believe they provide the earliest known evidence of a clay figurine-making tradition in the region, suggesting that people were using the material for symbolic expression much earlier than previously thought.

It has been revealed that at the time, the region was home to Natufian communities, hunter-gatherers who had begun living in permanent settlements before the development of agriculture. Archaeologists already knew that Natufian people made figurines, although most previously discovered examples were fashioned from bone or stone.

In a recent development, clay figurines, by contrast, were generally believed to have become common during the Neolithic period, when the agricultural revolution began around 11,500 years ago. The newly identified objects suggest that the practice had much deeper roots, revealing a wider range of artistic expression among these early communities.

The findings also raise questions about how cultural traditions developed before farming. The researchers suggest that Natufian artistic practices were more varied and complex than previously recognized, and that some aspects of the region’s later Neolithic culture may have originated thousands of years earlier.

According to recent reports, the research, published November 11, 2026, in the open-access journal PLOS One, was conducted by Dr. Laurent Davin of the Hebrew University of Jerusalem in Israel and colleagues. Beyond revealing an earlier history of clay figurines, the study offers clues about how creative practices and cultural knowledge may have passed between generations.

“These discoveries change how we understand the beginnings of artistic expression in the first villages of humanity. Thousands of years before agriculture and pottery emerged, people were already using clay to create figures, as part of their beliefs and knowledge of the natural world. What is particularly fascinating is the evidence that children were involved in making these objects, suggesting that creativity and cultural knowledge were shared across generations,” stated Davin.

Reference: “Modelling symbols among the first-sedentary communities: Emergence of clay figurines in Epipaleolithic Southwest Asia” by Laurent Davin, Marion Sindel, Mina Weinstein-Evron, Reuven Yeshurun, Daniel Kaufman, Leore Grosman, Anna Belfer-Cohen, Hamoudi Khalaily and François R. Valla, 11 November 2026, PLOS ONE. DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0360388.
