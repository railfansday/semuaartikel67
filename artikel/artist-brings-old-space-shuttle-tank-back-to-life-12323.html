Artist brings old Space Shuttle tank back to life

Natalie Rosen is not the type of specialist you immediately associate with being one of the key people behind the newly introduced exhibit of the space shuttle Endeavour.

She is not a former NASA technician or engineer, as many of her colleagues have been for the past several years, nor is she a curator or conservator. Before this job, she had no experience working on any aircraft or spacecraft, as the new Samuel Oschin Air and Space Center, an extension of the California Science Center, opened in Los Angeles on November 13.

Without her expertise and artistry, however, the largest component of Endeavour‘s “stack” would look nothing like it did when the shuttle was flying.

According to recent reports, “My background is in the fine arts. I have my bachelor’s and my master’s in fine arts from Cal State Long Beach,” Rosen told collectSPACE.com. “When I came in on this project, they were looking for a sculptor, and I had been sculpting.”.

According to recent reports, rosen had been working with mixed media for her thesis project and had experience sculpting foam. As it happened, the California Science Center had a lot of foam that needed shaping.

According to recent reports, for those unfamiliar, the space shuttle was assembled from three primary launch components. The orbiter and its solid rocket boosters were reusable, given the time needed to inspect, service, and, in the case of the solid-fuel motors, refurbish and refill between flights.

The 154-foot-tall (47-meter) external tank was the only major part of the stack that was disposed of with each launch. After providing propellant for the orbiter’s three main engines, the tank was jettisoned and disintegrated while reentering the atmosphere.

To protect the orbiter from the extreme cold of the cryogenic liquid oxygen and liquid hydrogen in the external tank and to keep the propellant and oxidizer from boiling off too promptly, the ET was covered in insulating foam. (Some of that foam became liberated during ascent and was what caused the loss of the space shuttle Columbia and its crew in 2003.).

The only flight-worthy tank to remain at the end of the program was ET-94. A so-called lightweight tank, it was built at a time when NASA was transitioning to a super-lightweight model and was therefore set aside. Originally slated to support a science mission on Columbia, it instead became a testbed after the tragedy, having its foam stripped off and tested to better understand what had happened.

It has been revealed that after the program ended, NASA donated ET-94 to the California Science Center. It arrived by way of a transoceanic barge and a road trip, then sat at Exposition Park for nearly a decade.

“So much of the foam on the ET had been dissected and removed during its time as a test article that they [the California Science Center] were trying to figure out how to put it back on and blend it in to the original foam in a convincing way,” remarked Rosen.

There has been a longstanding debate among shuttle enthusiasts and modelers about exactly what color the external tank is, and the answer resides in the foam.

In a recent development, “It’s orange. It’s cream. It’s burnt sienna. It just depends on the original foam, when you’re seeing it and how it’s aging. Because it starts as a kind of very ice cream-white almost color, and then depending on how long it sits out in the Sun, it turns orange,” remarked Rosen.

Sources indicate that by the time she had come on staff, the decision for the most part had already been made. Dennis Jenkins, the leader of the Endeavour project, had blended a special paint called “ET Orange” that was used to cover the existing acreage “bumpy” foam. Separately, a mocha-like color was picked out for the intertank.

Sources indicate that “And then the latest foam to be installed, which would have looked the newest on all of the closeout hardware—the feed lines, the ice frost ramps, the flanges around the intertank and the intertank rings—those were all the lightest color that we custom mixed. That was what we called the ice cream color,” Rosen noted.

Recent findings suggest that it was not only about color matching, though. For multiple reasons, the original aerospace-grade foam could not be used, so a commercial substitute had to be found that could match what Rosen described as the “orange peel.”.

“If you look at the tank very closely, there’s this bumpy texture, and there is a pattern from the original manufacturing and how it was sprayed on. There’s this overlapping line, a subtle-like stripe to it,” she stated.

Replicating that pattern was not easy.

Recent findings suggest that “There was a lot of trial and error in the beginning of what we could do to try and get it to match what was already there,” Rosen told collectSPACE. “We tried taking impressions off of the real foam and making molds and casting, but that didn’t really work. I then suggested we put in a dense urethane, like plain urethane foam, and I hand-sculpt the top of it to match what was around it. That ended up becoming the best solution for fixing the acreage foam that had an orange peel-like texture.”.

Jenkins, who had worked as a space shuttle engineer and led NASA’s delivery of the orbiters to their museum homes, had high praise for Rosen and her work.

“She had to come up with a lot of new processes because we couldn’t apply foam the same way they did at Michoud. It’s not flightworthy by any means, but just coming up with how to make it look real took a lot of imagination on her part, and she was pretty much a one-woman show for most of it,” he noted.

Sources indicate that some of the repair work to the external tank was finished before it was lifted by crane into the Samuel Oschin Air and Space Center and mated with the boosters and Endeavour. From then on, Rosen had to climb scaffolding to get to her worksites.

Sources indicate that “It’s definitely the largest art project I’ve ever worked on, and, I mean, I touched every inch of it,” Rosen said. “Once it went vertical, I was going up and down stairs. Probably the scariest was the point in the scaffolding where it just became ladders. So I had to be very, very careful and calculated in what I was doing.”.

Even after the scaffolding was removed in preparation for the museum opening, Rosen’s ascents were not over. A couple of weeks ago, she had to install a final component on the aft dome of the liquid hydrogen tank. To reach that, she needed a scissor lift.

Now that the entire Endeavour stack has made its public debut, Rosen is not parting ways with the California Science Center. Her work on ET-94 has released her career into a newly introduced frontier.

Industry observers note that “In this last year, I have been working on different artifacts with the team. I have been helping leading up to the opening. I’ve helped in ways with the different aircraft, with their assembly, their disassembly, and reassembly. I’ve done some repairs in certain areas. I’ve worked with our satellite and probe collection to do repairs and also contacted specialists outside of my realm to clean and repair thermal blankets,” remarked Rosen.

“So I’m planning on staying with the museum for now. They’ve offered me another position, and I’m happy to stay,” she said.

The California Science Center’s Samuel Oschin Air and Space Center is now open to the public. Admission is free, but timed entry tickets are needed due to the high demand. Reservations can be made online or at 213-744-2019.
