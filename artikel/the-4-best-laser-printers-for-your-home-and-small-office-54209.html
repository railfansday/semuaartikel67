Laser printers, known for printing crisp, professional-looking documents swiftly, are generally considered big, expensive machines designed for large offices. While some larger models live up to that reputation, plenty of compact options fit into your home office if you print files often.

It has been revealed that while more expensive than an inkjet printer, most laser printers are a better long-term investment, with lower operating costs and designs built for heavy-duty printing. That noted, choosing the right one at a glance can be tricky, and may vary based on your needs.

Industry observers note that printers aren’t the most exciting tech products, and many folks see them as frustrating, unreliable machines that require constant repair or replacement and are increasingly unnecessary for the average person in the digital age. While that may be true for some, they remain essential for workers who need to print contracts or create physical records, and students who need hard copies of their homework. With a little research, you can find a good laser printer that makes life easier and lasts a long time.

The recommended printers are based on my personal experience and knowledge of printers, as well as research from reputable publications, including PCMag and The Wirecutter. I looked for printers with performance and features that suit both home and small office users, with price and value in mind. I also spent months testing the best overall pick and using it as my everyday printer to ensure it’s a worthy recommendation.

There’s no perfect laser printer, but if you’re looking for a reliable, easy-to-use unit that produces high-quality prints, the HP LaserJet Pro MFP 3101sdw fits the bill. I’ve been using this printer for several months and found that it’s an ideal fit for my home office. It also has plenty of advanced features that would make it a good fit for a small business.

With a 35ppm print speed, the 3101sdw is fast for its intended audience. When connected to a typical home Wi-Fi network, it takes about 5 seconds from pressing print to outputting the first page. Printed text looks good even at lower-quality settings, with small fonts staying sharp and legible. I found the scan quality more than adequate for everyday use, but 300 dpi is too low for designers, photographers, and other creative users. It also runs quiet, which is always a nice bonus.

It has been revealed that anybody who needs to make copies will find the ADF-equipped scanner handy, while the 2.7-inch touchscreen makes setup easy. It took me less than 10 minutes to get it up and running. Whether you connect via USB, Ethernet, or Wi-Fi, the printer responds swiftly. It also has a USB port for quick prints or saving scans to a flash drive.

While the 3101sdw is small enough to fit on a large desk, it’s more practical to put it on its own stand. I wouldn’t call it “lightweight,” but I could carry it down five floors without much effort. It won’t win any design awards, either, which is too bad because HP generally knows how to make great-looking products.

Recent findings suggest that the paper tray has an average 250-page capacity, but a notable drawback is that it has no manual feed tray. I didn’t find this an concern, but if you need to, say, print on envelopes, it could be problematic because you'd have to switch out the paper in the main tray. Another expert reviewer dinged the 3101sdw for lacking a fax function, but my workaround is to use the HP Smart companion app to scan and send (but not receive) faxes.

The 3101sdw offers stronger print quality, a better build, and ease of use. It’s hard to say how long mechanical devices like this will last, but HP’s printers are known for their reliability—I have a LaserJet from the 1990s that’s still going strong.

In a recent development, in my experience, Brother makes affordable laser printers that consistently deliver solid performance and text quality. At 36ppm and under $200, the HL-L2460DW is a strong basic option for a home or small office. Despite its barebones design, it has useful features like double-sided printing, Ethernet connectivity, wireless printing, a generous 250-page paper capacity, and a mobile app. Plus, it’s lightweight and doesn’t take up too much space.

Industry observers note that the HL-L2460DW supports a 1,200-page standard-yield toner cartridge or 3,000-page high-yield toner cartridge, but comes with a starter cartridge that only yields 700 pages out of the box. (This is typical of printer manufacturers.) If you plan to print a lot, I’d recommend buying a high-yield cartridge right when you buy the printer.

This is a “budget” option, though, so this printer comes with a few caveats. I don’t love the small LCD display, which has a basic interface that doesn’t display much info and doesn’t feel as well-made. The basic operation means there’s no scanning, copying, faxing, ADF, or other multi-function capabilities. Plus, black-and-white graphics and images don’t look as good as text. User reviews have also complained about its reliability. Still, given its balance of price, speed, and print quality, the HL-L2460DW is a good value regardless of budget.

If it’s for personal use and you only blueprint to use it every so often, I’d steer you toward a different model, the HL-L2420DW, which has a 30ppm speed and every aforementioned feature except Ethernet. It’s perfect for casual printing, and it costs $50 less.

It’s a strong, if pricey, complement to any office.

Despite its compact footprint, the 3301fdw has a 50-page duplexing ADF and a 250-page paper tray. It’s not the fastest color laser printer, but its 26ppm speed is more than adequate for home or small business use.

In a recent development, print quality is where HP’s laser printers excel. You can expect documents with crisp text and well-saturated, natural-looking color graphics and photos. Unlike most laser printers, the 3301fdw is compatible with select HP glossy paper, so you can make photo prints. They don’t really compare to inkjet photo printers, but it’s fine in most cases.

Sources indicate that like our overall pick, the 3301fdw is a breeze to set up via its 4.3-inch color touchscreen. For connectivity, it supports USB, Ethernet, and Wi-Fi, which covers most home and office networks.

Sources indicate that you may have guessed the printer’s substantial con is that it’s pricey to operate. Whether you’re using the standard or high-yield toner options, the cost per page is high. A true small-business laser printer needs to be a workhorse. It should offer a robust, office-centric range of features, print fast, support multiple owners, hold enough paper, and have a high duty cycle. Experts agree that Brother makes some of the best laser printers for offices, and the monochrome MFC-L2900DW multi-function model is a particularly good option.

A large 3.5-inch touchscreen makes setup and on-device operation easy without a computer, while USB, Wi-Fi, and Ethernet give you options for connecting. Combined with a fast 36ppm speed, sharp text print quality, low operating cost, and the option to use high-yield cartridges, you’ve got a very capable all-in-one laser printer.

It has been revealed that graphics don’t look as sharp as text, but that shouldn’t matter if your office prints mainly documents. In a recent development, the ADF has a scanner resolution of just 600 dpi, but that is plenty for text documents; the flatbed scanner has 1,200 dpi, which is fine for color images. If you need to print impactful pages with photos and charts, a color laser printer can print higher-quality graphics. Keep in mind, though, that even the cheapest color laser printer will be expensive, and its print quality may not rival that of higher-end models. They also do not support specialty media like glossy photo paper.

In a recent development, A basic laser printer’s sole function is to print documents, and that’s enough for most people. It’s generally compact and fits on a desk. But if you run a small business or work remotely in a home office, you may want a multi-function, “all-in-one” printer that scans and copies, too, among other tasks. Multi-function printers are far more useful, but they’re larger and cost more due to the extra hardware inside.

Recent findings suggest that “Yield” refers to the estimated number of pages you can get out of a single toner cartridge. Most laser printers should get around 1,500 pages from a toner cartridge, while high-yield cartridges should last 3,000 pages or more.

Toner cartridges are expensive, especially for color printers, but they offer a better cost per page than ink. My rule of thumb is that high-yield toners will save you money in the long run.

According to recent reports, similarly, a laser printer’s “duty cycle” rating refers to the maximum number of prints per month that the manufacturer recommends to keep it in proper working order. Basic laser printers have a duty cycle of around 5,000 pages or less, while heavy-duty models have ratings of 10,000 or higher. Printing beyond the rated duty cycle can lead to breakdowns, more maintenance, or a shorter lifespan.

For personal use or small businesses that print occasionally, 25 to 30 pages per minute (ppm) should be fine. Small offices with a few employees that print often may want a faster printer with a speed of 35 to 40 ppm. In a larger office, I’d look for at least 40 ppm, if not more. Keep in mind that manufacturers measure speeds under ideal conditions, and real-world results may not always match. Expect a small discrepancy of a few pages.

Recent findings suggest that most printers rely primarily on a USB cable to connect to your computer, but a hard-wired connection alone isn’t practical in today’s world of wireless laptops, phones, and tablets. I recommend choosing a printer with a wireless connection compatible with your most-used devices, such as Wi-Fi or Apple AirPrint. Some laser printers also support Ethernet for a high-speed hard-wired connection. Office-focused machines may include a front USB port so you can plug in a flash drive with the files you need to print.

According to recent reports, most laser printers have a paper tray that holds 250 pages, which is enough for personal and small business use. Some compact printers may have a smaller capacity–around 150 pages. If you expect to print a lot, consider a model with a higher-capacity tray or support for multiple trays, and look for an output tray large enough to hold everything you print out. All printers also have a multi-purpose tray for manually feeding non-standard paper like envelopes.

Laser printers use toner, a powder heated onto paper, while inkjet printers spray ink droplets. Laser printers are considered superior for their speed and print quality, but they are expensive to purchase and maintain. Inkjet printers offer color printing and affordability, but prints need time to dry, and the cost per page can be higher with heavy use.

However, the best modern inkjet printers today are fast and deliver great-looking prints, especially photos. They also support different paper types and sizes. Laser printers can also print in color, but they're pricey. Beyond speed, their advantages include consistent print quality and long-term reliability.

Sources indicate that which technology is better ultimately depends on your needs. Most people will find an inkjet printer sufficient and more versatile, but those who print often may find a laser printer more economical in the long run. In an ideal setup, consider an affordable laser printer for frequent print jobs and a good inkjet printer for color documents and photos.

In a recent development, color laser printers can print photos and graphics on standard paper, but if you want to print on photo paper, an inkjet is the better choice, especially one that supports multi-color ink tanks. Laser printers aren’t designed for photo-quality reproduction, and generally don’t work with specialty paper. (As we discussed, some HP models are compatible with select glossy paper.).
