Breaking: Does the Nvidia-led rip mark the market bottom? Maybe not

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The key accelerant to Monday's ripping rebound was a shocker. But the level that halted the rally was right where you'd expect.

Sources indicate that the S&P 500's 4.7% surge on Monday lifted it out of a steep-sided hole following last week's washout selling storm. Its high for the day was just under 7300, a level last seen on Thursday, corresponding to the index's summertime low and representing the floor of the former multi-month trading range. In other words, the pop stopped at the point from which the tape had broken down; the index slipped three-quarters of a percent from there by the close.

More than a third of the S&P's gain was provided by the 31% jump in Nvidia shares alone following Jensen Huang's stunning reinstatement as Nvidia CEO. Here, too, the stock closed right near the Iran-selloff low, which it cracked through some 12 days ago. It remains under all the relevant moving averages and trend lines.

According to recent reports, this isn't to minimize or denigrate these moves of rare magnitude, but simply to place them in context. The market was going to attempt a solid bounce no matter the news. S&P 500 futures were indicated higher by more than 2% even before the Huang bombshell struck before Monday's open.

By near-universal agreement, last week's flush had generated the kind of indiscriminate liquidation, oversold conditions and urgent hedging that typically set up at least a snapback rip. Some of these readings were as extreme as near the April 2025 low following the tariff panic, though not all.

According to recent reports, the reinstallation of Huang at the apparent behest of the White House triggered an emotional, spring-loaded ramp in the AI trade, a collective panic by the under-invested. When the government jolts the market with a policy shift like this, it not only assuages a prevailing fear in the market, but it grants psychic permission to investors who were frantically selling one day to ravenously buy back the same stocks the next.

It's slightly ironic, given that a common line last week on the recent market setback was that – unlike in the tariff panic or the Iran war corrections – there was no single policy reversal that could short-circuit the selloff and trigger a ferocious recovery.

And maybe there still isn't. Such one-day comebacks from a deeply oversold state are to be respected but not fully trusted. Looking back at prior one-day gains of such amplitude – or prior sequences of a daily drop and gain of more than 5% in the Nasdaq-100 – generate a fair number of episodes inside of nasty bear markets, along with a few consequential market bottoms.

A few further observations and questions at a potentially tenuous tactical moment for the markets:.

According to recent reports, -The standard playbook is to assume that we've put in "a low, not the final low." Oversold rallies can carry on a while – say, to the 20-day moving average, which for the S&P 500 is up another 3% from here – without getting escape velocity. Graybeards argue for keeping the market on a short leash, expecting there will at least be a retest sequence. Prudent to assume so, though it's inescapable that V-shaped bottoms have become a bit more common in recent years, as recently as at the end of March, on the merest hint that President Trump would consider ceasing Iran hostilities without forcing open the Strait of Hormuz.

In a recent development, -Index corrections of at least 10% that happen swiftly have generally been more associated with temporary bull-market dips than the start of nastier downturns. This latest S&P 500 correction happened over 33 trading days, among the dozen quickest. That would be reassuring, except that places it right between the declines of November 2007 and February 2022, which indeed were the start of bear markets.

It has been revealed that -Remember that the pain of this setback was inflicted after months in which Wall Street was constantly extolling the virtues of a "broadening" market (equal-weight S&P 500 outperforming) and marveling at the elegant rotation into "quality" and defensive stocks that was supporting the S&P 500. Those mechanics can work fine in phases, but never allow for a proper market-wide reset and, clearly, they don't inoculate the tape from eventual washouts.

It has been revealed that -We might have a fresh binary trading relationship to watch: It's early in the game, but with the Nvidia news jolting most AI plays higher, Anthropic in its third trading session since its IPO fell 1.4%. If Nvidia is an advocate for and backer of global open-weight models, then its revival could dim the view of Anthropic's closed frontier AI platform, while diminishing the advantage provided by Anthropic's $200 billion cash capex war chest.

According to recent reports, john Kolovos of Macro Risk Advisors on the latest reading: "The growing evidence of capitulation is encouraging. Bearish sentiment was arguably the market's best attribute coming into last week and still is. Sentiment became even more extreme over the past week, pushing our model to its lowest reading since the 2025 bottom, and reaching levels comparable to what we saw during the 2022 bear market. The six-month outlook has improved, but whether this is a V bottom thanks to the return of Jensen Huang, or the market undergoes basing and a possible retest, remains unclear.".

Industry observers note that -I'm making my way through "The AGI Chronicles: The Inside Story of the Race to Create an Artificial Superintelligence," the latest book by former New York Times technology writer Kevin Roose, a smart, engaging and brisk history of AI development and the personalities driving it. For a shortcut through the material, Roose's discussion on the New Yorker Radio Hour podcast is worth a listen.

-In returning to Nvidia headquarters Monday without his signature leather jacket, Jensen Huang was executing a Reverse Fonzie.

In the first season of "Happy Days," the mega-hit sitcom of the '70s and early '80s, ABC forced the Fonz character to wear a cloth windbreaker, fearing he'd alienate viewers who'd take him for a "hoodlum." Creator Garry Marshall argued to the network that a leather jacket was a safety requirement for motorcycle riders and pushed to let him wear. When the show took off, the leather became standard.

Huang is now perhaps trying to seem a bit less edgy, humble and relatable absent the biker attire.

President Trump's direct intervention to press Nvidia's board to reinstate Huang, juicing the Nasdaq for at least a day, reintroduced investors to the notion that stocks can only fall so far without the administration trying to punish the sellers.

But can Wall Street enjoy both a Trump put and a Fed put? If conviction about the AI trade and its aggressive spending is revived, and the economy runs hotter, and private-sector borrowing rises, will that not invite a more hawkish Federal Reserve – at just the time that many were handicapping a dovish pivot given the unsettled markets of late?

It's a live question. More than a month ago, as a fourth-quarter post-election rally was becoming consensus, I granted the setup but with a key caveat:.

Sources indicate that "Still, against most probabilities, 2018 happened. By which I mean a 19% S&P 500 peak-to-trough decline in the fourth quarter of a midterm-election year, one that saw total S&P 500 earnings grow by more than 20%. The 2018 tailspin had followed a massive momentum crescendo and reversal several months earlier, which gave way to an erratic tape that couldn't absorb what seemed at the time relatively unthreatening Federal Reserve tightening actions.".

Industry observers note that since then, after the market retrenchment, the 2018 analogy has grown so popular that I doubt it will unfold exactly that way. Still, as Renaissance Macro noted after Monday's rally, Nvidia's gain, which helped the S&P 500 recoup half of last week's loss, "may have fixed the S&P 500 loss but not the Fed, the [Treasury] curve or cash credit.".

Higher real yields remain a potential constraint on equity valuations. This chart from Wellington Management highlights the yield on 30-year Treasury Inflation Protected Securities – the long-term real yield – is now above the S&P 500's free-cash-flow yield for the first time in half a generation.

In a related vein, JPMorgan economist Bruce Kasman in his 2027 outlook describes a certain narrative dissonance: "Financial markets have projected strong earnings expectations, in part, because central banks have preached immaculate disinflation and been supportive of easy access to credit. The 2027 landscape — of above-potential growth, a sixth year of elevated inflation, and a renewed labor market tightening — poses a meaningful challenge to the magical thinking held by markets and central banks.".
