The S&P 500 (^GSPC +0.33%) fell 4.4% on Nov. 5, its worst day since April 2025. It was another reminder of how swiftly stocks can tumble.

It has been revealed that however, when stocks fall, not all tumble as much. Research from Hartford Funds, using Ned Davis Research data, shows that since 1973 the stocks that held up better in big declines have been dividend payers. In market drawdowns of 10% or more, dividend-paying stocks fell 14.4%, versus 28.2% for non-dividend-paying stocks. That's about half as much.

Here is what to look for in a dividend stock to help cushion the blow of a future stock market slump.

It has been revealed that hartford Fund's analysis covers every S&P 500 drawdown of 10% or more from 1973 through 2025. In those periods, dividend-paying stocks fell an average of 14.4%. The equal-weighted S&P 500, a good proxy for the average stock, fell 19%. Meanwhile, stocks that didn't pay dividends tumbled 28.2%.

Industry observers note that in other words, dividend payers lost about half as much as non-payers during a stock market correction. While they still lost money, they helped preserve more wealth during a market downdraft.

Hartford suggested two reasons why dividend stocks tend to hold up much better than non-payers during a market downturn. Many dividend stocks tend to be concentrated in industries less sensitive to the economic cycle. For example, most consumer staples and utility stocks pay dividends because companies in these industries generate stable cash flow due to resilient demand.

Sources indicate that additionally, a tech giant typically needs to have a track record of profitability and relatively predictable cash flow before it initiates a dividend. That business maturity would also make a company's stock price less volatile.

While dividend payers have historically delivered higher total returns with less volatility than non-payers, one group of dividend stocks stood out from the pack:.

Sources indicate that companies that grew their dividends outperformed the average dividend stock and had lower volatility. Companies that cut or eliminated theirs lost money on average, with the most volatility of any group.

Industry observers note that hartford also found that the highest yields haven't been the best bet. Since 1930, stocks with the highest payout ratios (which typically have the highest yields) outperformed the market about half the time, similar to companies with much lower payout ratios. However, the sweet spot was companies with an average dividend payout ratio of 49%. These companies prioritized dividend payments while maintaining plenty of flexibility to continue growing their dividends even during more challenging times.

Those results point to a few simple ways to put the data to work before the next pullback.

Sources indicate that while dividend stocks have historically helped cushion the blow during downturns, that doesn't guarantee they will outperform during the next one. For example, S&P 500 Dividend Aristocrats® (the term Dividend Aristocrats® is a registered trademark of Standard & Poor's Financial Services LLC) -- S&P 500 members with 25 or more years of annual dividend increases -- outperformed the market during the dot-com crash and global financial crisis. However, they underperformed during the COVID crash.

In a recent development, further, weaker companies tend to cut their dividends during periods of market stress. As we saw before, dividend cutters and eliminators have historically delivered negative total returns. That's why it's crucial to check a company's dividend payout ratio to ensure it has plenty of cushion to weather a market downturn.

When stocks tumble, history highlights dividend payers tend to fall about half as far as stocks that don't pay dividends, and dividend growers have done even better over time. It's a cushion, not a guarantee. If you're worried about a potential pullback, lean toward investing in companies with growing, well-covered dividends rather than non-payers and those with the highest yields.
