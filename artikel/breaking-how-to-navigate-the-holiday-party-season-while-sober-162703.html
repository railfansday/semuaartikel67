Breaking: How to navigate the holiday party season while sober

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that when Tat Read decided to give up alcohol four years ago to support a family member who was struggling with addiction, she knew that her career would present some obstacles. “Being in the industry that I’m in, I’m always at parties; I’m always at dinners,” notes the 45-year-old communications professional. “My job is about bringing people together. And when people are brought together, there’s always alcohol on the table.” Read, who says she was never much of a drinker to begin with, started limiting her consumption to a drink or two at work-related events about five years ago. As a mother of four, however, she found that even a small amount was more trouble than it was worth. “My kids are really young. I have to be up early in the morning, and it was starting to affect my sleep,” she says. “It just had downstream impacts that I really wasn’t appreciating.” Read says her colleagues supported her decision to give up alcohol completely, but says it’s still hard to navigate professional situations with upcoming people, like networking events. “I had trepidation about being fully sober because I knew it was an awkward conversation to have,” she says. “Sometimes I will hold a glass of sparkling water instead of champagne to not feel like I need to explain myself.” Read isn’t the only one who occasionally finds strategies to avoid conversations about her sobriety. According to a recent survey by the Boca Recovery Center, which operates addiction treatment centers in Florida, Indiana, Massachusetts, and New Jersey, 39% of Americans who abstain from alcohol hide their sobriety at work and 30% fake it to blend in—like drinking sparkling water from a champagne flute. And 13% have skipped a work event, celebration, or date to avoid the topic altogether. Given that more than half of sober Americans have been questioned, pressured, or teased about their decision not to consume alcohol, according to the survey, it is perhaps no surprise that 58% hide it from at least some people in their lives, and that 13% haven’t told anyone. “The holidays make this harder, as events tend to be based around drinking,” says Alison Tarlow, a licensed clinical psychologist who is the chief clinical officer at Boca Recovery Center. “Those hiding their sobriety are managing a drink, an excuse and a conversation all at once—which is socially exhausting in a setting that is supposed to be fun and relaxed.” Stigma remains even as more Americans go sober More Americans are choosing to go alcohol-free for a wide variety of reasons, though taboos related to addiction and mental health remain. According to the survey, one in four Americans have experimented with alcohol-free or reduced-drinking challenges; half of these cite physical or mental health-related reasons, compared with 10% who were recovering from addiction. Against this backdrop, younger generations appear less likely than others to hide their sobriety. According to the survey, only 5% of Gen Z keep it a secret, compared with 12% of millennials and 20% of Gen Xers. But these younger Americans also face the most outside stigma, as 68% of sober Gen Zs have faced questioning, pressure, or teasing compared with 49% of older sober Americans. “Telling people tends to involve fielding questions, which is generally off-putting to those who made such a personal decision,” says Tarlow. “Sobriety is a complex and sensitive decision, and people want to choose who hears about it and when.” Navigating the holiday party season without alcohol The holidays can be an especially challenging time for those who are looking to stay sober. Between personal and professional events, family and financial obligations ramping up, and work responsibilities starting to dwindle, many are left without some of the support systems, practical reasons, or reminders that help them maintain sobriety. Though they may feel judged for ordering an alcohol-free alternative, 93% of Americans say they would not think less of a coworker who skips alcohol at a work event, according to the survey. “The judgment sober people anticipate is usually harsher than what their peers tend to think,” says Tarlow. While colleagues may ask questions, she emphasizes that those who choose sobriety don’t need to explain themselves. “’I’m not drinking tonight’ is perfectly fine,” Tarlow says. “These events are meant to be fun, so focus on enjoying yourself, not others’ expectations. But, if pressure builds, there’s no shame in taking a break or leaving.” Coming up with excuses for not drinking can be exhausting and dishonest, says Phillip Barbb, an executive coach who specializes in sobriety. “You don’t want to feel like you have to have 25 conversations where you’re lying at the holiday party,” he says. “Creating this false narrative for yourself can weigh on you, because you’re building a gap between yourself and others.” Instead, Barbb, who has been sober for almost 19 years himself, suggests preparing a short explanation in advance that feels authentic and genuine, like, “I’m doing it for health reasons,” or “I decided I wanted to try not drinking this holiday season.” Some may also find it helpful to create a practical reason for staying sober to help them through workplace holiday events, like being a designated driver or making an early-morning commitment for the following day. Those trying to stick to their sobriety goals can also enlist the support of a colleague, date, or friend, though Barbb warns that these people shouldn’t be made responsible for someone else’s choices. “It is no one else’s responsibility to keep me sober or to keep me out of bad situations,” he says. “What I do think is cool is saying, ‘I want to go to the event, I don’t want to drink, so I may have a moment or two where I need to pull you aside to just talk or vent.’” How employers can offer more inclusive holiday parties for sober staff With so many Americans experimenting with sobriety—and so many hiding it from their colleagues—organizations will inevitably blueprint holiday parties for attendees who are abstaining from alcohol, whether the organization knows it or not. So it’s important for employers to consider ways they can make the environment more inclusive for workers who don’t want to drink. When a holiday party’s primary activity is consumption, it can make those who are going sober stand out. That is why employers are encouraged to include other activities, like bowling, an escape room, or mini golf. Choice of venue can also play a role, as an event held at a bar or club may create more anxiety—and discourage more from attending—than one held at an activity venue or restaurant. “If you’re hosting an event, you do have a responsibility to offer more than just beer and wine so that people can feel comfortable,” Barbb adds. “If you’re offering signature drinks, do a version without alcohol.” Workplace cultures are often set at the top, meaning it’s up to leaders to model responsible behavior and signal inclusivity for sober attendees. “It’s not anybody’s job—nor is it their right—to pry into the lives of their employees. But you should still make sure that you’re creating environments that are safe for people to be in,” Barbb says. “For executives and leaders, your responsibility for your people continues even outside of work hours.”.
