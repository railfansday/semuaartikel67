📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The Trump administration proposed a rule on Monday that officials said they expect to severely cripple so-called ESG investing in workplace retirement plans such as 401(k)s.

The proposed regulation, if finalized, would amend provisions under the Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974, or ERISA, to clarify how employers and other initiative "fiduciaries" can apply their duty of loyalty to 401(k) investors.

The rule affirms that the people who choose investments for their company retirement plan must make decisions "solely in the financial interests of workers and retirees, not to advance political or social agendas," U.S. Secretary of Labor Keith Sonderling remarked Monday in a written statement.

ESG — short for environmental, social and governance — funds let people invest according to certain values such as curbing climate change or improving corporate diversity. They're also known as socially responsible, sustainable, impact or values-based funds.

"We make clear in this rule that when you are selecting investments for a retirement proposal ... you may not have any ulterior motives or collateral interests," a Labor Department official said during a press briefing Monday morning.

Sources indicate that "We believe that will inform all fiduciaries as to whether an impact fund or ESG fund is appropriate for retirement funds," the official noted. "We believe the answer will be very rarely.".

Already, few 401(k) prepares offer ESG funds — partly due to whipsawing federal policy around their use.

In a recent development, about 5% of 401(k) looks offered an ESG fund in 2025, according to a Plan Sponsor Council of America report issued earlier this month. On average, less than 1% of overall investor assets in 401(k) plans are held in ESG funds, the report said.

The Labor Department proposal, which is subject to a 60-day public comment period, comes as the Trump administration has tried to make it easier for employers to add alternative investments such as private equity funds to workplace retirement prepares.

It also comes amid a broader Trump administration backlash against ESG and policies it views as "woke.".

Industry observers note that within days of his inauguration in 2025, President Donald Trump declared the U.S. would withdraw from the Paris Agreement, blocked subsidies for electric vehicles, pushed for more fossil-fuel production, and started a "huge pushback" against diversity, equity and inclusion policies, Diana Iovanel, a senior markets economist at Capital Economics, wrote in a research note last year.

"Our country will be woke no longer," Trump said during a speech to a joint session of Congress in March 2025.

More recently, Trump and the Republican-led Congress repealed key elements of the Inflation Reduction Act, former President Joe Biden's signature law to combat climate change. In April, a White House report remarked ESG investing has a "significant cost to investors and the broader economy.".

According to recent reports, monday's proposal undoes elements of a Biden administration rule in 2022, Labor Department officials remarked. That Biden-era regulation sought to loosen ESG investing rules put in place in 2020 by the first Trump administration. The Biden rule, for example, clarified that employers could weigh the economic effects of climate change and other ESG considerations when making investment choices, officials said at the time.

Among the ways the latest proposal seeks to curb ESG uptake in 401(k) plans is by eliminating the "tiebreaker test" and the "participant preference provision," the Labor Department said.

The tiebreaker test claims, for example, that if an ESG fund is "tied" with another fund after comparing various financial factors, a 401(k) strategy sponsor can choose the ESG fund because of its social benefits, according to legal experts.

Current rules also allow employers to select funds based partly on their workers' preferences, provided they still meet certain financial criteria.

Data demonstrates that ESG funds may provide stronger financial performance than that of more traditional funds, however.

For example, sustainable funds returned investors a median 4.9% in the first half of 2026, versus 4% for traditional funds, according to a Morgan Stanley analysis in September.

"Sustainable funds also continue to outperform over the long term: A hypothetical $100 investment into a sustainable fund in December 2018 — the start date for Morningstar's sustainable fund classification — would have grown to $171 today, while investing $100 into a traditional fund over the same period of time would have become $159 today, assuming median returns were achieved in each period," according to the report.
