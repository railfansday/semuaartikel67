Medieval Settings Are Back In These Mysteries, Thrillers, and Horror Novels

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

There’s a whole newly introduced wave of excellent new genre fiction set in medieval Europe, and since my mother was a medievalist, and I love to mention that fact, I figured I’d do a quick roundup of some new and upcoming releases. Before I get to the recommendations, you may be wondering: what time period exactly are we talking? For the purposes of this article, the medieval era is roughly defined as between 1000-1500, depending on where the characters live. For example: 15th century Italy = Renaissance. 15th century Estonia = Medieval. Burgundy, I suppose, is half and half. Periodization is a continuum much more than a series of hard stops and new beginnings, and the Renaissance is just the Middle Ages with better marketing (see Inventing the Renaissance for more on the debate).

Note: This list is organized in chronological order of setting. Most are new releases from this year or next year, but there are two older titles that are still recent enough to count for this trend. Books that are not yet published have their publication dates next to their publisher info. If you’ve got any Dark Ages-set mysteries to recommend to me, please comment, because I would very much like to read them.

Industry observers note that M. T. Anderson, Nicked (Pantheon Books) Setting: Mediterranean Medieval World, 11th Century.

This came out in 2024, which I consider recent enough to include as part of this trend piece—especially when considering a medieval time scale. Also it’s awesome. In Nicked, a group of disparate travelers sets off on an odyssey of the medieval world with a common 11th century mission: they’ve been hired to steal the Anatolian relics of a powerful saint and transport them to an Italian city state in hopes of sparking more commerce from pilgrims (the medieval equivalent of taking the Dodgers from Brooklyn to LA). Along the way, they form deep bonds, some blossoming into romance, and take in the sights and sounds of the entire Mediterranean world. M. T. Anderson does that rare feat of capturing the spirit of the time, granting readers a lively, Rabelaisian interpretation of an unfairly maligned era (see history book The Bright Ages for further appreciation).

Paul Brous, The Undying Lamb (Rising Action) Setting: Italian states, 1230.

When a book starts with a glossary featuring words from several now-dead languages, you just know that it’s going to be impeccably researched. In Paul Brous’ medieval-set thriller, an Albigensian assassin and an English knight must protect a child with an inconvenient lineage, as a vast array of forces attempt to eliminate their vulnerable charge. Perfect for those who enjoyed Conclave, but wish it had been set waaaay further back in history.

James Bennett, Mother of Night (Evil Twin, March 2027) Setting: Holy Roman Empire, 1291.

According to recent reports, in this intense work of historical horror, Saladin’s victories have sent a ragged group of crusaders fleeing from the Holy Land, their only option to board a rickety vessel soon enough broken by stormy seas. Shipwrecked and far from home, the few survivors find succor and strangeness amongst a group of miserable swamp-dwellers who warn of danger ahead; the crusaders, determined to forge ahead, do not listen, with predictably dire consequences and a surprising amount of singing.

Ferdia Lennon, Brother Nothing (Holt, June 2027) Setting: France, 1369.

In a recent development, set just a decade before the events of Doom Painting, Brother Nothing follows a newly appointed provost and his precociously intelligent daughter as they flee plague-ridden Paris for a town in the North of France. While their new abode does appear safe from pestilence, other dangers are many, including the perennial threat of English soldiers, an epidemic of children gone missing, and a murder pinned on a prodigal son who confesses to every possible crime— save the crime of which he is accused. As the provost uncovers layer after layer of shocking crimes and terrifying cover-ups, he and his few allies begin to suspect the entire town of conspiracy, for what reads a bit like Red Harvest, medieval style.

Sources indicate that A.K. Blakemore, Doom Painting (Scribner, April 2027) Setting: England, 1381.

I have complete trust in A.K. Blakemore’s power to render historical fiction as vivid and complex as depictions of the present, and her new novel of the English Peasant’s Revolt does not disappoint. In 1381, England’s most oppressed rose up against the conditions of their serfdom, driven to rebellion by the devastation of the plague and the endless war with France. For 16 days, they challenged the notion that some should rule while others serve, embodied by Wat Tyler’s epic call-to-arms: “When Adam delved and Eve span, who was then a gentleman?” As a side note, I’ve been planning to get a tattoo of that slogan in Ye Olde Englishe font for a while now, so maybe it’s time to bite the bullet. Also the book confirms what I have always known: Wat Tyler had major sex appeal.

Indrek Hargla, The Secret of Saint Olaf’s Church Translated by Adam Cullen (Pushkin Press) Setting: 15th Century Tallinn.

Recent findings suggest that indrek Hargla’s tale of a crime-solving apothecary is based in obscure, and very real, history; the mystery unraveled by the amateur sleuth takes its jumping off point from a mysterious note in Latvian historical records, for a richly detailed and vividly imagined take on a high-water mark in Latvia’s regional influence. In The Secret of Saint Olaf’s Church, the murder of an aging knight leads a city luminary towards a horrifying secret with deep implications for the city’s fragile economic status and unstable power structure.

Indrek Hargla, The Ghost of Rataskaevu Street (Pushkin Press) Setting: Estonia, 1419.

Sources indicate that hargla’s second novel to feature apothecary Melchior, released for the first time in the US this month, takes his hero on a dark journey stretching far into the city’s past. When three different townspeople die just after seeing a ghost, the pharmacist and amateur sleuth decides to dig into the matter. In between brewing tinctures, infusing remedies, and proscribing his usual treatments, Melchior keeps circling back to a house in the center of town, once the setting of a terrible crime, and key to understanding the more recent killings.

Jo Harkin, The Pretender Setting: England, Ireland, Burgundy, 15th Century.

According to recent reports, at the tail end of the Wars of the Roses, an ordinary farm boy is plucked from his simple village life and trained up to become a king. He’s been swapped for a commoner at birth by his dead Earl of a father and forgotten about in the midst of internecine warfare; now, he’s the last hope for the house of York to triumph over its Tudor rivals. But does he have the stuff it takes to fight through his own doubts and become king? And will he survive the endless sacrifices demanded in his ascent to the throne? This is an excellent book to help you understand the inspirations behind Game of Thrones, or the power politics of Elaine’s kingdom in Wheel of Time.

Katherine Arden, The Unicorn Hunter (Del Rey) Setting: Brittany, Late 15th Century.

Sources indicate that in 15th century Brittany, a noblewoman facing an unwanted union with a far-away lord puts in motion a clever initiative to hold off the wedding: her husband-to-be must join her in a unicorn hunt in the dense, dark forest near to her castle. Soon, they find themselves transported into a magical realm beset by an ancient curse that only the lady and her wooing courtiers can break. The Unicorn Hunter takes its inspiration from the real Anne of Brittany, whose marriage to the King of France was just another method of preserving what little sovereignty her province could hold onto under the steady creep of French imperial influence, and who brought two beds to her wedding as a symbol of her independence. I think I would have quite liked Anne of Brittany—Katharine Arden certainly feels a sprightly kinship with the impish aristocrat.
