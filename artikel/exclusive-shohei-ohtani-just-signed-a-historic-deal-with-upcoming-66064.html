Exclusive: Shohei Ohtani Just Signed a Historic Deal With Upcoming Balance

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Shohei Ohtani is used to making history. In addition to his four MLB MVP awards and two World Series rings, the Los Angeles Dodgers two-way great is the first member of the 50/50 club (having racked up 54 home runs and 59 stolen bases during the 2024 season), became the first player to hit three homers and record 10 strikeouts in a single game (which he managed in a 2025 NLCS contest against the Brewers), and earlier this year maintained simultaneous streaks in games on base (48) and innings without an earned run (32 and two-thirds).

Sources indicate that now, the 32-year-old Japanese icon is adding to those accolades with a pioneering endorsement deal, officially extending his relationship with his longtime partner Newly introduced Balance through to the end of his baseball career. (While a rarefied group of athletes have signed lifetime sponsorship deals—including LeBron James and Cristiano Ronaldo at Nike, and Damian Lillard at Adidas—Ohtani’s new contract appears to be both the first to exclusively cover a star’s playing days and the first of its kind in MLB.).

In a recent development, “There’s a lot of trust,” Ohtani tells me through an interpreter during a sit-down in Boston of his decision to commit to the Newly introduced England sportswear stalwart for the long haul. “We’ve obviously built friendships, as well. They’ve been able to accommodate my requests, the things I’ve asked for, and, like I said, that takes the involvement of a lot of trust. That relationship has been very, very good and smooth. I had no reservations about extending our relationship.”.

Earlier in the day, I shadowed Ohtani at Fresh Balance’s headquarters, where, as part of the agreement, the company is renaming its on-site think tank as the Shohei Ohtani Sports Research Lab. A team of specialists led the Dodgers superstar through a slew of shoes—including his on-field Ohtani 1 model, which will receive new editions in 2027—and then a rack of branded apparel. All of it falls under his Shohei Ohtani Signature Collection, which New Balance launched in 2024 (about a year and a half after the athlete first signed with the company). The collection has grown deliberately and unhurriedly ever since—mirroring his patience in the batter’s box; Ohtani is methodical when it comes to product development and uninterested in rolling out rushed goods with his name slapped on them.

Recent findings suggest that while conducting a kind of footwear postmortem on his 2026 season—which started well, but ended with a rare slump (and left knee and right biceps injuries that kept him from pitching after July 3)—Ohtani noted there’d been some moments of slippage on the heel of his landing foot when pitching. On a printed-out diagram, he used a pencil to sketch ways in which the shoe’s spikes might be slightly reconfigured to find a sweet spot for stability on the mound and speed around the bases. The question of switching footwear between pitching and batting comes up, but Ohtani has not yet done this (and it seems the consensus is to stick with one shoe only).

Sources indicate that “I have a lot of direct input into the footwear and apparel,” Ohtani claims. “That includes my style, which I like to keep simple, as well as performance-based requests, [like with my] spikes. Some years we make minor changes, and sometimes we’ll make a big change. They understand what I want.”.

Sources indicate that ohtani’s apparel line runs across three categories: performance, lifestyle, and fanwear. His custom logo—a striking ink drawing of his figure, designed by the Japanese manga artist Takehiko Inoue—is featured subtly on the bulk of the lineup. “Shohei doesn’t see himself as a glamorous player,” Chris Davis, New Balance brand president and chief marketing officer, tells me. “He sees himself as a gritty, hardworking athlete who wants to get better every day, and his logo reflects this—it has this kind of quiet intensity.”.

It has been revealed that while some of the future apparel designs boast cool technical features—like detachable, two-in-one, and reversible elements inspired by Ohtani’s two-way play—the man himself is only concerned that his products “fit comfortably and to be easy to wear.” When asked if there’s anyone whose style he likes or is influenced by, Ohtani shrugs. “Honestly, not really. I just like to wear what I like to wear.”.

“[Shohei] is very quick and clear with his input,” says Masahiro Uchida, Upcoming Balance’s general manager of athlete signature product, who has worked alongside Ohtani for years. “On the performance side, he always prefers softness and a soft hand feel. For the lifestyle side, he prefers clean and simple. He also understands the occasion. He always asks us, ‘Where would I wear this?’” One major goal for Ohtani with his apparel, Uchida adds, is to “support the expansion of baseball culture and show his respect for its heritage. He is a super geek about MLB. He knows so much.”.

Recent findings suggest that at 32, the duration of Ohtani’s career—and, now, his Fresh Balance deal—remains an open question. That age can be considered as getting toward the older end in pro baseball, but elite pitchers have a habit of playing well into their 40s—Justin Verlander, for example, just pitched his final season at 43.

Industry observers note that ohtani still looks on being a full two-way player in 2027, with hopes for a Cy Young Award—MLB’s top pitching honor, one of the few laurels he has yet to win—still alive. When the Dodgers’ season ended on October 15, Ohtani told the press that he was “not quite satisfied with how things shaped up on the pitching side.”.

What words of advice or motivation does he have, I ask, for the legion of kids hoping to follow in his footsteps? His answer feels like something he might be saying to himself as the next season approaches.

“If you want to become successful, baseball is a sport that takes a lot of repetitive practice,” he notes. “You always have to work on your fundamentals. Once you reach a certain level, the sport becomes very, very hard. At that point, you need to remember why you’re playing. Are you playing because you love the sport? If you have that drive, that love for it, then even when it’s tough, you can overcome it.”.
