Recent findings suggest that dr. Ayman Elhalwagy is Co-Founder and CEO of RootCause.ai, with a background in computer engineering, machine condition monitoring, and applied AI. His PhD at Brunel University focused on combining datasets and monitoring machinery. He later founded AI consultancy Numvio, where his work with enterprise data and forecasting systems helped shape his interest in causal models that explain business outcomes and support decisions.

Recent findings suggest that rootCause.ai develops a causal inference and simulation platform for business and operational decisions. It brings data from different systems into a shared ontology, builds causal models, and lets organizations test the potential effects of decisions before acting. In this interview, Elhalwagy discusses predictive versus causal AI, business digital twins, forecast validation, hidden variables, and the human oversight needed when simulations inform automated actions.

It has been revealed that your PhD at Brunel focused on combining datasets and monitoring the condition of machines, before you went on to found AI consultancy Numvio. What did those experiences reveal about the limitations of predictive AI, and how did they shape your decision to found RootCause.ai?

My PhD in computer engineering at Brunel University focused on combining datasets and monitoring the condition of machines, with the dataset work validated by NASA and the monitoring system used for mining equipment around the world.

Recent findings suggest that after that, I started Numvio, an AI consultancy, where we repeatedly saw the same problem: companies had years of dashboards and forecasts but still didn’t know what was causing the results. Predictions could tell them what was likely to happen, but not what to change, so finding answers often took months, by which time the decision had already been made. We founded RootCause.ai to make that work possible in days rather than months, and repeatable rather than a one off study.

Industry observers note that how would you explain the difference between predictive AI and causal AI to a business leader? Can you share an example where accurately predicting an outcome still fails to reveal how to improve it?

Put simply, predictive AI answers “what will happen” whereas causal AI answers “what happens if I do this”. For example, a global logistics company we worked with had 12,000 accident reports spread across six separate datasets, and dashboards on hundreds of variables. They could only see that accidents were happening, but they didn’t know what was causing them, therefore their only way to test ideas was to change a policy and wait months to see if the accident rate moved.

To solve this, we built a causal model across all six datasets, identifying that the main cause was driving more than 4.5 hours without a break, not the driving style or vehicle. Once the company knew the cause, they could effectively test in the model what enforcing breaks would do to accidents, and what it would cost in driving time.

Sources indicate that what research underpins RootCause.ai’s ability to identify cause and effect from existing business data? How do you account for hidden variables and distinguish causal relationships from misleading correlations?

According to recent reports, our causal discovery algorithm, SPARC-fast, identifies which variables influence others and runs around 1,000 times faster than standard methods on ordinary compute. It uses the tech giant’s ontology, or structured map of its data, to rule out implausible relationships and accounts for time delays to help establish whether a relationship is causal, since a cause must happen before its effect.

In a recent development, the algorithm has been benchmarked against public datasets with known causal relationships and can also detect hidden variables, or factors outside the available data that influence results. While it can’t identify what those hidden variables represent, customers can often recognise them. One customer, for example, identified a hidden variable as a promotion they had run but never recorded in their data. The result is a causal graph that people can read, edit and version, rather than a black-box result.

Recent findings suggest that what does a digital twin of a business actually represent? Could you walk us through how operational data becomes a model that can simulate decisions and their consequences across products, markets, and time?

Industry observers note that for us, a digital twin is a model of a business built from its own data, able to understand how different parts of the business affect one another, allowing you to change one variable and see the consequences across the rest of the business. We connect to the manufacturer’s existing data systems, automatically build an ontology to bring information from different sources into a consistent structure, and use SPARC-fast to identify the relationships between variables, including time delays and hidden influences.

Industry observers note that we then fit the model to the company’s data, accounting for differences across products and regions, and keep it updated as newly introduced data comes in. Companies can then simulate decisions such as changing prices, discounts, policies or tariffs to see their effects across products, regions and time horizons, or identify which single change would have the biggest impact on a particular business outcome. Results can be delivered through apps, APIs (Application Programming Interface), SDKs (Software Development Kit) or MCP (Model Context Protocol), allowing teams to use simulations within their existing systems.

It has been revealed that your team reports reducing forecast error at a publicly traded telecommunications company from 20% to 5%. How was that improvement measured, what baseline did you compare against, and how did it translate into better business decisions?

Industry observers note that one of our customers, a publicly traded telecom equipment manufacturer that builds equipment up to 18 months before resellers need it, makes accurate forecasting critical to avoiding over or understocking. Its existing in-house model, built by its own data team, had a weighted MAPE (mean absolute percentage error) of around 20%. We measured the improvement through backtesting against the same historical data, training the model on information available at different points and comparing forecasts with what actually happened. Across 2,086 product-region combinations and eight forecast horizons, covering 16,688 forecasts, weighted MAPE fell from around 20% to 5%.

The model also indicates which inputs are driving changes, translating into better business decisions by allowing the company to test pricing, discounts, bookings and reseller changes before setting its manufacturing plan. It is transparent about its limitations too: for brand latest products with no sales history, the company’s existing product family average still performs better.

In a recent development, the logistics example in your briefing identified prolonged driving without a break as a key factor in truck accidents. How did you validate that finding, and did changing break policies lead to a measurable reduction in incidents?

We analysed 12,000 incident reports and 10GB of telematics data across six datasets, covering tachograph violations, collisions and driving behaviour. We found that time since the driver’s last rest break was the strongest factor associated with accidents, with risk rising sharply after 4.5 hours of driving. Interestingly, it wasn’t the expected factors of speed, weather or driving style.

Sources indicate that the model highlights every relationship and its strength, allowing the customer to check how we reached that finding. We then simulated enforcing rest breaks, accounting for the additional driving time, and estimated savings of around $1 million per 2,000 trucks. This is a simulated estimate rather than a measured reduction in accidents following a policy change.

How reliable are simulations when businesses encounter conditions with little historical precedent? How does your platform communicate uncertainty and recognize when the evidence is insufficient to recommend an action?

Causal inference exists to answer questions history doesn’t contain, allowing companies to ask “what if something different happens”, rather than “what happened last time”. Companies such as Netflix, Amazon, Uber and Walmart use it, and it has only been accessible to those that can afford to build the capability.

Our digital twins are built on causal models. A pattern matching forecast can only repeat what it has seen, whereas a causal model captures the links between variables, allowing businesses to test values they have never encountered, such as a latest tariff level or inflation rate. The models are explainable, showing how each variable is calculated and how accurate it is. They can also identify hidden variables influencing results, so users know when vital information is missing rather than being handed a confident number.

We also automatically bring in external data such as inflation, interest rates, tariffs and weather to reflect conditions outside the business’s control. Customers can backtest the model against historical data, seeing how forecast accuracy changes across different time horizons. Forecasts are provided as a range rather than a single number, and the model retrains if predictions drift from reality over a sustained period. If the data is too limited or flat for a reliable forecast, we tell customers that too.

Which advances in AI are making causal modelling more accessible to smaller organizations? Where do large language models fit into your system, and what data and human expertise do customers still need?

Instead of using LLMs, our platform is hard statistics and applied mathematics, with LLMs not good at hypothetical scenarios, even when you give them good data. That is a limit of how they are built, as they learn patterns from what has happened. A “what if” question is about something that has not happened.

There is a view in AI research, Yann LeCun’s work among it, that feeding models more and more data will not on its own get them to reliable answers on this kind of question. We agree. That all noted, customers want to use their own agents to orchestrate our technology, so we have made the platform open to them. An agent can connect through MCP or API, run a simulation, and use the result as it goes about its work. The agent does the asking and the statistics do the answering.

It has been revealed that as AI moves from recommending decisions to executing them, what should remain under human control? What validation and safeguards would you require before allowing a causal AI system to act autonomously?

Today, a person decides which policy or action to take, while the system simulates the likely consequences. Once approved, that decision can be carried out through an API, SDK or MCP, but the technology does not independently decide what to do. Before enabling autonomous actions, we believe the model must be readable, editable and versioned, with backtested accuracy established for the specific decision and time horizon. Hidden variables must also be identified and understood, while ongoing monitoring should pause automated actions if predictions drift from reality. Results from actions taken should then feed back into the model, which is the current level of automation we are working towards, but at the moment most customers keep a person in the loop.

Sources indicate that what is the hardest unresolved research problem in causal AI today? What progress would most expand the range of business decisions these systems can reliably support?

Hidden variables are the biggest challenge. Most methods assume every relevant variable is in the data, but in business data that is never true. We can find hidden variables, but using them inside simulations, and mapping them back to things the business can measure, is unsolved. This is where much of our research team’s time goes.

Direction is another challenge. With time series data, time gives direction. Without it, direction is a guess, and a wrong direction breaks every simulation downstream. This is a major area of research amongst our PhD team, with RootCause able to right now tell you when relevant variables are missing. Our research breakthrough in this area allows us to significantly expand the cases, especially for many cases represented in real world scenarios, where we can detect these variables and model them accurately. We are also the only organization that we are aware of to have commercialized this capability, and combined it with an ontology system to try to “match” external data that we have curated to the patterns we established in customer data.
