Latest Update: Buying a Home Soon? This Overlooked Open Enrollment Perk Could Save You Thousands in Legal Fees

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that buying a home comes with enough expenses already. Between inspections, title insurance, closing costs, and a stack of paperwork that seems to grow every day, it's easy to lose track of what you're paying for.

But what if your employer could help cover some of the legal bills?

It's a possibility many homebuyers may overlook during open enrollment, when employees are typically comparing health insurance premiums and retirement benefits rather than thinking about hiring a lawyer.

Employer-sponsored legal prepares, offered through companies such as LegalShield, MetLife and ARAG, can provide access to attorneys for a modest monthly fee.

A real estate attorney can review a purchase agreement, make sure inspection and financing contingencies actually protect the buyer, check the deed and title, examine mortgage documents, and represent a buyer at closing. Depending on the proposal, some or all of that may be covered.

Jago Gavin, a LegalShield representative who works with employer groups, tells Realtor.com® that the real value is having someone to consult before signing something you might not fully understand.

"The law is not logical; it's legal," Gavin says.

That's relevant when buying a house, where an unfamiliar contract provision can have expensive consequences.

Gavin recalls a LegalShield member whose attorney spotted a potentially unfavorable mediation clause in real estate paperwork and recommended changing it before the buyer signed.

The potential savings can run from hundreds to thousands of dollars. But there's a catch: Not all legal prepares offer the same services, and buyers need to know what they're getting before signing up.

In a recent development, group legal prepares work much like other voluntary workplace benefits. Employees pay a monthly premium, usually through payroll deductions, in exchange for access to specified legal services.

The price can be surprisingly affordable.

In a review of publicly available employee benefits documents, Realtor.com found a proposal offered to employees of financial technology manufacturer Fiserv through MetLife for $15.50 monthly. LegalShield's published employer plans generally cost about $19 to $25 a month, depending on the coverage and employer.

That's roughly $186 to $300 annually, potentially less than the cost of a single consultation with a private attorney.

Industry observers note that the intends typically provide access to participating law firms that handle matters ranging from contract reviews and consumer disputes to estate planning. Some cover specific services in full, while others offer discounted rates for legal work outside the blueprint.

ContractsCounsel, an online marketplace connecting clients with attorneys, reports that lawyers on its platform charged an average flat fee of $620 to review a real estate purchase agreement, based on completed projects.

Compare that with LegalShield's published Choice strategy, which costs $21.95 monthly, or $263.40 over a year.

If an employee uses the blueprint for comparable document-review work without additional charges, the potential savings could be about $350.

But reviewing a contract and representing someone throughout a real estate transaction are two very different services.

It has been revealed that legalShield's published employer-plan documents include certain residential mortgage-document reviews but specifically exclude an attorney's attendance at closing. Documents must also be provided at least five business days before the scheduled closing.

Other aims offer broader coverage.

It has been revealed that an ARAG legal plan available to University of California employees, for example, pays participating attorneys for covered services that include reviewing and preparing home purchase documents and attending a closing. California doesn't generally require attorneys at residential closings, but the benefit illustrates how coverage can extend beyond reviewing paperwork.

In a recent development, the MetLife plan offered to Fiserv employees also lists home purchases and sales among its covered services. MetLife plan descriptions published by several other employers go further, listing deeds, boundary and title disputes, and property tax assessments—handy if a latest owner wants to challenge a tax bill after the purchase. Employees should still check precisely what their particular plan includes.

Sources indicate that and even comprehensive coverage won't eliminate ordinary closing expenses. Buyers are generally still responsible for title insurance, recording fees, taxes and other third-party charges.

New York real estate attorney Adam Leitman Bailey sees the obvious appeal: lower costs. But he questions whether attorneys with busy, established practices have much incentive to participate in programs requiring them to accept reduced fees.

In a recent development, for a relatively straightforward transaction, that might not matter much, he states. But complicated purchases can be another story.

"When something goes wrong, and this is the biggest investment of your lifetime, you'd better believe that you should be hiring the best attorney you can," Bailey tells Realtor.com.

That doesn't mean attorneys participating in group looks are necessarily less qualified. It does mean buyers should ask about their experience, particularly if a transaction involves title disputes, unusual contract provisions, or other complications.

Gavin acknowledges that LegalShield sometimes encounters coverage gaps in rural areas, where the nearest participating firm may be hours away.

Some plans offer referrals, discounted services, or reimbursement for using attorneys outside their networks, but those benefits vary.

Before relying on a workplace plan to handle a purchase, buyers should make sure an appropriate attorney is available and that the services they need are actually covered.

Buying a home is also a good time to think about what happens to the property in the future.

Many employer legal plans include estate planning services such as preparing wills and powers of attorney.

Some also help with living trusts, whether revocable, which can be changed later, or irrevocable, which generally can't. Either can help manage and transfer assets, and property properly placed in a trust can typically avoid probate.

These services can be expensive on their own. A March 2026 study by LegalTemplates, drawing on pricing from 909 law firms nationwide, found a median price of $625 for a standalone will and $2,475 for a revocable living trust.

By comparison, some employer legal plans include those services in the annual premium, potentially saving homeowners considerably more than they pay for coverage.

Becci Powers-Johnson, an eight-year LegalShield retail customer who became an independent associate selling its services in August 2026, says she and her fiancé have used the program several times to update their wills, powers of attorney, and healthcare directives.

"It was more than just the forms; it was the consultations that were included with it," she notes. LegalShield offers unlimited consultations.

It has been revealed that of course, not every homeowner needs a trust, and some prepares charge extra for preparing one or transferring property into it. The appropriate documents depend on individual circumstances and state law.

If you're thinking about buying a home in the coming year, take another look at your workplace benefits before open enrollment ends.

It has been revealed that start with your state's rules. Connecticut, Delaware, Georgia, Massachusetts, South Carolina, and West Virginia are among the states that require or customarily expect an attorney's involvement at closing, according to the National Notary Association, and in places like New York, it's standard practice. Elsewhere, a title or escrow organization usually handles the closing, so full closing representation may matter more in some states than others.

Then check the blueprint itself. Does it cover purchase agreements, title and deed work, mortgage paperwork, or full closing representation? Is there an attorney near you who handles real estate transactions? And what happens if you need more complicated legal work?

George Washington University, for one, doesn't let employees cancel until the open enrollment after 12 months in the initiative. Others allow cancellation at any time but make you wait until the next enrollment period to rejoin. And matters already underway may not qualify for coverage.

Even a relatively inexpensive proposal isn't necessarily worthwhile if you never use it. But for someone preparing to purchase a home, the right benefit could pay for itself with just one covered legal service.
