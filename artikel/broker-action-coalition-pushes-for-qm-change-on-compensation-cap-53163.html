📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The Broker Action Coalition (BAC) is proposing a change to federal mortgage rules that would exclude lender-paid mortgage broker compensation from the Qualified Mortgage (QM) points-and-fees calculation, aligning its treatment with compensation paid to retail lenders’ employee loan officers.

In a white paper recently released by BAC, the coalition argues that the current rules create an uneven playing field between independent mortgage brokers and retail lenders, particularly on smaller-balance loans.

Under the Ability-to-Repay/Qualified Mortgage rule, points and fees generally cannot exceed 3% of a loan amount for loans of $137,958 or more in 2026. Smaller loans are subject to graduated percentage and dollar limits.

Right now, compensation paid by a wholesale lender to an independent mortgage broker counts toward that limit, while compensation paid by a retail lender to its own employee loan officer is excluded.

Sources indicate that the coalition argues that both types of compensation are often funded through the interest rate and are indistinguishable from the borrower’s perspective. The difference can leave brokers with less room to cover other fees that count toward the cap, potentially causing otherwise qualifying loans to exceed the limit while losing QM status and its associated liability protections.

The disparity is particularly pronounced on smaller loans, where the dollar limits leave less room for additional charges. The coalition noted small-balance and rural loans are particularly vulnerable because fixed-dollar charges consume a larger share of the available cap.

The white paper also points to a decline in small-dollar mortgage lending. The share of home purchase loans under $70,000 fell from more than 11% in 2011 to less than 4% in 2020, citing research from Pew Charitable Trusts, the Urban Institute and the Department of Housing and Urban Development.

In rural and lower-cost markets, the group argues, the restrictions can make it harder for brokers to compete for loans that are already less financially attractive to originate.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau‘s 2013 rulemaking established the distinction between employee and broker compensation. At the time, the bureau remarked including broker compensation in the calculation could help reduce the risk of mortgage brokers steering borrowers toward more expensive transactions.

The coalition acknowledges that consumer protection concern but argues that subsequent regulations addressing loan originator compensation and steering have made the points-and-fees restriction a less targeted way to address the issue.

Specifically, the group points to existing rules that prohibit loan originator compensation from varying based on a loan’s interest rate or other terms, as well as a separate prohibition on steering borrowers toward transactions that provide greater compensation.

The proposed reform would take two approaches: The primary proposal is a statutory amendment to the Truth in Lending Act that would exclude rate-funded creditor-to-broker compensation from the points-and-fees definition when it is paid under a compliant loan originator compensation agreement.

The parallel regulatory proposal would ask the CFPB to amend Regulation Z to create the same exclusion.

In a recent development, “The proposed reform would remove an inequitable regulatory constraint without changing the competitive forces that influence compensation today,” the paper reads. “While individual brokerages may adjust their compensation arrangements, there is little evidence to suggest that the reform would materially diminish the wholesale channel‘s overall pricing competitiveness.”.

Under the proposed language, the exclusion would apply only when compensation is funded through the interest rate, is not paid directly by the borrower or financed into the loan amount, and is established through a standing agreement between the creditor and broker that does not vary based on individual loan terms.

It has been revealed that the coalition remarked the proposed requirements would prevent brokers from adjusting compensation on individual loans to circumvent the cap. The change would leave the 3% limit and existing compensation and anti-steering rules intact, with borrower-paid compensation and other fees still subject to the cap.

It has been revealed that data from two wholesale lenders representing $13.4 billion in combined 2026 production showed that loans with 2.75% broker compensation accounted for 14.53% of units and 12.41% of volume. BAC said the figures indicate most brokered loans close with lower compensation.

Recent findings suggest that the group also argued that the CFPB has authority under the Truth in Lending Act to make the change to preserve access to affordable mortgage credit. It cited the bureau’s 2019 assessment of the Ability-to-Repay and Qualified Mortgage rule, which documented fee waivers and other challenges affecting smaller brokered loans.
