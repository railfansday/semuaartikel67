Breaking: Scientists found a star inside of another giant star

🌐 Source: Mashable • 📅 19 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 5 min read (906 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Astronomers have detected a star slightly less massive than the sun inside a giant star for the first time.

The two stars, in the nearby Andromeda Galaxy, had circled each other for thousands of years. Then the giant swallowed the smaller star. The trapped star took only about seven years to spiral to the center, where the two merged into a single star. NASA's James Webb Space Telescope supplied the final evidence.

Scientists think the same kind of spiral explains how many star pairs end up circling each other very closely. In this case, the small star didn't survive. But in other pairs, the giant's gas gets blasted away first, leaving both stars in an orbit that takes just hours or days instead of years. Those tight pairs can later become black holes or super-dense dead star cores called neutron stars, or explode as supernovas. Some also send out gravitational waves, which are ripples in space and time.

Lead author Viraj Karambelkar, a NASA Hubble Fellow at Columbia University, and an international team reported the discovery Wednesday in the journal Nature.

The researchers nicknamed the larger star "Significant Bao," and it earned the name. It had about seven times the sun's mass and 350 times its width, shining 18,000 times brighter.

"It was an absolute beast of a star, completely outshining its companion in the data," stated Karel Temmink, a doctoral student at Radboud University in the Netherlands, in a statement.

Its partner, "Little Bao," had about 70 percent of the sun's mass.

Stars like Substantial Bao swell up near the end of their lives. If our sun did the same, it could grow past Earth's orbit. As Big Bao swelled, Little Bao began skimming its outer layer and stripping away gas. Soon the small star was orbiting inside the giant. The gas there was spread so thin that Little Bao stayed intact for years, according to the paper. Still, the gas slowed it down, much like air slows a thrown ball, until it eventually sank toward the giant's core.

No telescope can literally see inside a star. But friction from the small star's plunge churned the giant's gas and carried that heat to the surface. There it highlighted up as seven years of steady brightening. When the team compared the starlight from before and after the eruption — and tested computer models against it — the best explanation was a small star spiraling inside the giant.

It has been revealed that when the smaller one finally reached the giant core, gravity likely ripped it apart. That triggered a blast that hurled about one-tenth of the giant's outer material into space.

Their encounter was "almost like a bomb exploding inside a living star," mentioned Kishalay De, an assistant professor at Columbia.

The explosion itself came on surprisingly fast. The team thought an outburst would take years. Instead, it took just a week from when its brightness started rising to its peak, said Nadia Blagorodnova, an associate professor at the University of Barcelona in Spain.

Scientists expect many star pairs to go through this phase, but catching one in the act has taken incredible luck.

"The timescales involved are short, months to years, and the rates at which they form are low," astrophysicist Jason Nordhaus wrote in a commentary published alongside the study. "Just a few stars exist in this phase at any given time in a galaxy such as the Milky Way.".

Astronomers usually piece together star mergers long after they happen. This time, a fleet of telescopes followed Bao as the events unfolded. The team first noticed the star acting strangely in Feb. 2023, in data from NASA's now-retired NEOWISE mission. In Sep. 2024, another telescope saw it brightening and puffing out dust. In July 2025, NASA's Spherex telescope happened to catch it fading in visible light while it brightened in infrared light. Then, on Oct. 17, 2025, the Zwicky Transient Facility, a sky-scanning telescope in California, spotted the eruption.

Sources indicate that webb took its look on Dec. 24, 2025, just 47 days after the eruption peaked. No telescope had ever followed up on an event like this so quickly, Karambelkar mentioned.

Sources indicate that what the newly single star, now just called "Bao," will become remains an open question. Only future observations will show how it settles down.

According to recent reports, NASA's newly introduced Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope and the Vera Rubin Observatory in Chile will scan huge areas of sky over and over. They should spot events like this in hundreds of nearby galaxies.

"There's going to be a large influx of similar discoveries just ahead," Karambelkar noted.
