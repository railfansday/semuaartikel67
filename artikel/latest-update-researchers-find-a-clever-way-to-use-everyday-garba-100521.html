Latest Update: Researchers find a clever way to use everyday garbage to make greener cement instead of burning fossil fuels

A review of 211 studies finds refuse-derived fuel could replace coal in cement kilns, but fuel standards and rules still don't line up.

Industry observers note that plastic bags, blended fabrics, and treated wood are hard to recycle, and a lot of waste like that goes to landfill. Cement kilns, meanwhile, burn coal for the heat that turns limestone into cement, a product behind an estimated 8 percent of the carbon dioxide people put into the air each year.

Industry observers note that feeding kilns with processed trash instead of coal could ease both problems at once. A review of 35 years of research finds the idea gaining ground while the basics stay unsettled.

Geethmi Thisakya, a Ph.D. candidate in the School of Civil Engineering and Construction Management at Adelaide University, led the review with her supervisors Navodana Rodrigo, Chamitha Wijewickrama, and George Zillante.

Recent findings suggest that her subject is refuse-derived fuel. It’s waste processed into something with more heat per pound, less moisture, and more even particles than raw trash. Burning it alongside coal in a kiln is known as co-processing.

“Previous research has highlighted the potential of refuse-derived fuel to turn suitable waste materials into a useful resource rather than sending them to landfill,” Thisakya mentioned.

Sources indicate that the team searched the Web of Science database for every study on refuse-derived fuel in cement making between 1991 and July 2025, and found 211 in English. Almost all of that interest is recent.

The earliest paper that fit dates from 1992, and output stayed low until a rise after 2013.

According to recent reports, more than 80 percent of the papers were published in the past 11 years, with a peak of 24 in 2023 alone. Forty-eight records, including seven regulatory documents, were read in full.

China produced the most studies, 29 of them, followed by Germany with 21, Poland with 19, and Brazil with 12. Africa and Latin America barely appear, even though the team sees them as places where the fuel could catch on.

The confusion starts with vocabulary. Refuse-derived fuel has a stricter cousin, solid recovered fuel, which has to meet an international standard called ISO 21640.

That standard sorts the fuel into five classes by how much heat it gives off and how much chlorine and mercury it contains.

Refuse-derived fuel typically has no such standard, yet studies and regulations use the two names interchangeably, the review found.

It has been revealed that some papers call the ISO classes “RDF classes” when the standard defines them for solid recovered fuel, and fuel that meets the standard is sometimes labeled refuse-derived fuel anyway. Sometimes that’s deliberate shorthand, since the standardized fuel is a refined subset of the broader one.

For a kiln operator, the muddle isn’t academic. It affects which fuel a plant will accept, what the emissions paperwork says, and what a supply contract means.

“However, our review reveals that realizing this potential requires more than simply replacing coal with waste. We need consistent fuel quality, clear regulations and stronger evidence about the environmental and economic impacts,” Thisakya noted.

The European Union has the most developed rulebook. Its rules set emission limits for kilns that burn waste fuel, assign the codes that let the fuel cross borders, and ban high-energy waste from landfill.

One rule pulls the other way, however. As Europe pushes plastics recycling harder, the mixed plastics that make up much of the fuel will get scarcer.

According to recent reports, elsewhere the picture is patchy. In the United States the fuel falls under the Clean Air Act and the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act, which governs waste handling.

According to recent reports, germany separates the two fuel types in law. Poland still classifies refuse-derived fuel as waste, so moving and storing it needs a waste license. Indonesia pays a feed-in tariff to encourage it.

Peer-reviewed work rarely compares how these regimes affect uptake in cement plants, the review found.

Regulators rank what to do with trash in a hierarchy: prevention first, then reuse, then recycling, then “other recovery,” then disposal.

The authors place refuse-derived fuel in that fourth tier, and in the “recover” slot of the circular-economy list that runs from refuse to recover.

It has been revealed that recycling should still come first, the authors wrote. Refuse-derived fuel is a route for the burnable leftovers that landfills would otherwise take. Other routes to cleaner cement, from different rocks to kilns that capture carbon, fall outside the review.

The search covered one database and one language, so studies indexed elsewhere or written in other languages were missed.

The authors wrote that regional work from non-English-speaking countries is the likeliest gap. They also excluded power plants and district heating, which burn the same fuel, so lessons from those sectors didn’t carry over.

Nor does the review settle how much carbon the switch saves. Early life-cycle studies have weighed the trade-offs of replacing coal, but the authors found none built around the specific conditions of a cement kiln.

That analysis is on their research agenda, along with tests of how the fuel performs in different regions and plants.

What the review asks for next is that number, measured for a cement kiln, before the construction sector counts the switch as a saving.

The full study was published in the journal International Journal of Building Pathology and Adaptation.
