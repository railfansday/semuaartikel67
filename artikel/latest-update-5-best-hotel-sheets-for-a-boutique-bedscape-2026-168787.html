What Type of Sheets Do Five-Star Hotels Use?

It has been revealed that while it varies from property to property, the majority of the hotels on this list use white cotton sheets. Whether percale or sateen, the best hotel sheets all offer a neat, design-agnostic look that suits the center of New York just as well as the rolling hills of Tuscany. Percale will have an added crispness and cooling factor, while sateen will have an added shine and smoothness. Regardless of material finish, look for an all-natural fiber makeup (like 100% cotton) and a versatile white colorway.

Why Do Hotels Like to Use White Bed Sheets?

From an aesthetic perspective, white sheets offer a cloudlike, serene escape for the guest. They also look the cleanest. Stark white sheets cannot hide stains or wear and tear as darker colors can—you can see exactly where each thread has snagged. White bedding is also the most easily sanitized; it even has its own setting on your washing machine. Bleach is necessary to kill germs, especially in a commercial setting, and you don’t want to risk the color fading and yellow stains that can occur on colored sheets.

Boasting a subtle monochromatic stripe, The Ritz-Carlton’s sheets play with texture without departing from the clean lines and opulent energy of their suites' overall design scheme.

What Fabrics Are the Best Sheets Made of?

Long or extra-long-staple cotton sheets are considered the “best,” in our experience. These typically have a smooth finish and hold up well over time. That noted, so much of it comes down to your own personal preferences. “[Deciding] which one is best for a particular person or climate is relative,” Katie Glusica, professor in the fibers department at the Savannah College of Art and Design, previously shared. “A percale in the 200 to 400 thread-count range will be durable and have a cooler sensation and a lighter weight feel, best for people who run a little warm or where the climate is warmer. Sateen sheets in the 300- to 600-thread-count range are best for someone looking for softness over durability, and better for cooler climates where a heavier feeling sheet is more comfortable.” Then, there are the more seasonal fabrics like airy linen for summer or thick flannel for winter. If you’re still undecided, we often find that Goldilocks-level comfort in cotton sheets with thread counts between 200 and 600 threads per inch.

What Are the Best Bed Sheets for Hot Sleepers and Cold Sleepers?

While percale is recommended for hot or warm sleepers, compared to other cotton sheets, shopping writer Julia Harrison argues the best is bamboo or Tencel lyocell. “AD editors and I prefer these natural fibers because they usually feel cool and gentle against skin, but they also have great temperature regulating qualities since the fabric absorbs excess moisture to help you stay refreshed while you sleep,” she says.

And during the winter months, or for those who run cold year-round, there’s nothing more comforting than sinking into a bed dressed in flannel sheets. “These are great for keeping you cozy and toasty because of a brushed (and sometimes double-brushed) cotton material that feels soft to the touch,” says senior shopping editor Nashia Baker. “It depends on the brand, but some make their sheets with 100% long-staple cotton, like supima cotton, that’s known for its durability without wear over time.”.

What Is the Difference Between Percale and Sateen?

Industry observers note that both are made from cotton, but the weave is what differentiates the two. “Percale is a classic one-over, one-under construction that creates a crisp, breathable fabric with a matte finish reminiscent of fine poplin shirting, while sateen’s longer thread float produces a smoother hand and subtle sheen,” Rebecca Weinstein, vice president of design at Highgate, the management organization for the Atlas Hotel in Boston, previously told AD. Hot sleepers will feel most comfortable in cooling sheets like percale, while those who prefer a less structured, smooth feel will likely splurge on sateen. “Sateen is more dense, with more threads packed in, so the fabric is warmer,” shared Preeti Gopinath, associate professor of textiles and founding director of the MFA textiles program at Parsons School of Design in New York. “Percale is cooler, and with a thread count of 150 to 200, there is less fabric per square inch, which allows for better airflow.”.
