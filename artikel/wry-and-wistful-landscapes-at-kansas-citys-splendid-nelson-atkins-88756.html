Wry and Wistful Landscapes at Kansas City’s Splendid Nelson-Atkins Museum

‘E verything’s Up to Date in Kansas City” — that few minutes of pure effervescence in Oklahoma refers to Kansas City circa 1907, when it was in the middle of its version of the City Beautiful movement. Newly introduced boulevards, new, fancy neighborhoods, hundreds of fountains, and much more were in the works. The sparkle of a new art museum was in the civic eye, too. The Nelson-Atkins Museum of Art didn’t open its doors until 1933, but better late than never. It’s an essential visit for art lovers.

Recent findings suggest that I visited last month and saw the very enjoyable exhibition American Prospects and Landscape Photography, 1839 to Today. It presents Joel Sternfeld’s lovely, often sad, wry, late-1970s and early-1980s landscape photographs in the context of the American landscape-photography genre. I love Sternfeld’s work. Born in 1944, he was the cutting-edge photographer who took color landscape photography mainstream, mostly through American Prospects, his portfolio of photographs published in book form in 1987. Color? Cutting-edge in the ’80s? Well, 1987 is about to be 40 years ago, so it qualifies as days of yore, and what has became American Prospects started in the late ’70s, when Sternfeld started an epic road trip to make art about Americans and the land. Landscape photography was, à la Ansel Adams, commonly in black and white, thought to evoke grandeur and mystery. What pushed Sternfeld to color was the advent of Kodak Vericolor III film in the 1970s. It delivered accurate, vibrant color and was good for various lighting and especially skin tones.

Brian T. Allen is National Review’s art critic.
