It only takes a splash to give homemade gravy the rich, savory flavor you're after.

There are few things on the Thanksgiving table that people have stronger opinions about than gravy. Everyone seems to have a preferred method, a secret ingredient or a little trick they’ve been using for years. My mom has always sworn by Kitchen Bouquet, a seasoning sauce that gives gravy a little color and kick of flavor. I’m fairly certain she’s had the same bottle since the Reagan administration, and that it only comes out of the cupboard on Thanksgiving.

Making good turkey gravy isn’t particularly complicated, but getting that deep, rich flavor can be tricky. Even with plenty of turkey drippings and a well-made stock, sometimes it needs a little something extra. Over the years, I’ve experimented with all sorts of ways to give my gravy more flavor, but the one I come back to every time is an ingredient I always have in my pantry.

I’ve been adding a splash of soy sauce to my gravy for years, and it’s one of those little tricks that makes a noticeable difference without anyone being able to put their finger on why.

Soy sauce is rich in umami, the deeply savory flavor that makes foods like mushrooms, Parmesan cheese and the golden crust of a steak so satisfying. Adding a little soy sauce to gravy enhances those same savory flavors, giving it a richness that might otherwise require a longer cooking time. It doesn’t make your gravy taste like soy sauce, provided you use a light hand. It just makes it taste more like really good gravy.

Worcestershire sauce is another popular addition that works for much the same reason, bringing its own combination of savory flavor, acidity and a hint of sweetness. I’ve tried plenty of different additions over the years, and while they all have their merits, soy sauce remains my favorite. I love the depth it brings without competing with the flavors of the turkey and stock.

The trick with soy sauce is knowing when to add it and, perhaps most importantly, when to stop. You’re not trying to introduce a newly introduced flavor to your gravy so much as coax a little more out of what’s already there. If someone can identify soy sauce in the finished product, you’ve probably been a bit too generous.

In a recent development, I always add mine at the end, once the gravy has thickened and I’m happy with the consistency. Start with about a teaspoon for every two cups of gravy, stir it in, and give it a taste. Depending on the stock and drippings you’ve used, that may be all you need. If not, add a little more, tasting as you go.

One thing worth remembering is that soy sauce is salty. Very salty, in fact, so save your final seasoning adjustments until after you’ve added it. This is particularly notable if you’re using store-bought stock, which may already contain plenty of sodium. Reduced-sodium soy sauce is an option, too, but I generally use the regular stuff and adjust accordingly.

Industry observers note that and don’t save this trick for Thanksgiving! It works beautifully in beef and chicken gravy, too, especially when the pan drippings aren’t quite as flavorful as you’d hoped. A splash of soy sauce won’t make up for a badly made gravy, but it can do an impressive amount of work for an otherwise perfectly respectable one.
