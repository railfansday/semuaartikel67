When planning our 2026 Points of View Summit, we wanted to be sure to set time aside to hear directly from our travel specialists—the behind-the-scenes experts who work tirelessly work to curate ambitious and deeply personal itineraries around the world. Spliced between compelling panels on Lowcountry heritage, summer travel trends, and the evolving appeal of all-inclusive stays, the breakouts allowed for extended discussions with Traveler editors on a range of themes. From culinary experiences inspired by Hilton Head Island's Gullah cuisine, to the evolving dynamics of multigenerational travel and a new guard of ultra-luxe globetrotters, we got the inside scoop on where travelers are going and how they're getting there.

Jim Berkeley, founder and CEO of Destinations and Adventures International, speaks on culinary moments.

Inspired by Hilton Head Island's rich tapestry of culinary traditions, senior features editor Megan Spurrell and editorial assistant Skyli Alvarez led a discussion on traveling for taste—from the rising interest in foraging over traditional cooking classes to the draw of big-name chefs contrasted with harder-to-find local gems, and the hotspots that deserve to be on our food maps.

Recent findings suggest that our specialists all agreed that travelers yearn for “authentic” food moments, though defining authenticity—and actually finding it—often becomes tricky. To Omolola Campbell, sixth-gen Gullah descendant and executive producer of Gullah short documentary The Spirit We Move With, it's about who provides the experience. “Taking a cooking class is much different than getting it from a local chef who can tell stories about why a dish means so much to this locality,” she said, explaining how so many of South Carolina's Gullah dishes contain okra because its seeds were sown into the hair of West Africans who were brought to the South for slavery. The plant has come to represent resilience, and it's that integral context, according to her, that gives a meal its soul. “That's how you differentiate the person who really knows why they're cooking what they're cooking,” Campbell concluded.

Dana Kravis, Madison Flager, and our travel specialists explore multigenerational travel trends.

Sources indicate that when it comes to multigenerational trips, the grandchildren are choosing the destinations and the grandparents are paying—it's their way of leaving a legacy amongst those dearest to them. During a discussion led by global executive director of video Dana Kravis and commerce director Madison Flager, our travel specialists observed that, because people today tend to wait longer to have children, grandparents are older than they used to be. Travelers in their 80s and 90s are increasingly joining trips, shifting the multigenerational travel dynamic away from the traditional picture of grandparents in their 70s. To trip designers, this means there must be layers of options catering to different activity and exertion levels.

In a recent development, many in this session agreed the most successful family trips provide opportunities for separating and coming back together. Three itinerary choices in one day are ideal, so younger folks can be out and about, with the whole family reuniting for mealtimes and perhaps an afternoon activity. Boat and yacht experiences were especially recommended as spaces where the family is forced to be together—grandkids cannot simply run off and do their own thing.

What's luxury to one traveler might seem the furthest from it to another; some long for jam-packed, once-in-a-lifetime itineraries, while others want total disconnection and the privilege to do absolutely nothing for a week. Features director Rebecca Misner and commerce writer Jessica Sulima led this breakout, as specialists debated the definition of luxury itself. “Luxury is being able to pass on meaning to kids [and] exposing families to the broader world and its cultures,” Virgi Schiffino Kennedy, managing partner at Lux Voyage, said of its intangible appeal. To Ruchi Harnal, founder of Harnal Travel, true luxury “is not about the resort or the villa but the personalization of the trip.” As for who’s spending money on trips? Thomas Robinson, MR at Dehouche, has observed that Gen Z and Millennials are more inclined to spend but want to know where that money goes. Additionally, the specialists largely agreed they’re seeing a shift away from parents taking their children on trips, and towards 30- and 40-somethings treating their parents to a vacation. In Harnal's experience, milestone birthdays and anniversaries are driving clients to book a trip.

Between rising temperatures and overtourism, our specialists discuss the factors impacting European summer destinations.

Industry observers note that what is the impact of overtourism, price inflation, and rising temperatures on our Euro summers? Director of special projects Lale Arikoglu and commerce editor Meaghan Kenny opened the floor for our specialists. “If you’re thinking of traveling to the Dolomites, consider Switzerland instead. There’s history and beautiful scenery with fewer crowds,” Brian Dore, owner of CIU Travel, noted. For the hiking curious, some added that Switzerland offers plenty of one-day hiking trails or “soft adventure” travel—three-to-four days combined with a lake or city destination. Others agreed that traveling to the Alps offers a significant advantage—higher elevations means cooler temperatures—and that Slovenia is a great alternative to Italy. To dodge the crowds, travelers might also consider Belgium, Denmark, or Norway (where ocean ships and river cruises are opening earlier in March). For families, consider Umbria (hot tip: plenty of festival and craft opportunities for kids), Austria, Ireland, or Croatia.

It has been revealed that for those who want to stick with more tried-and-tested European itineraries next summer, consider going as early as possible, for instance, in May instead of August. When it comes to traveling to France specifically, folks advised flying into Paris or Nice, then venturing out to the countryside or oft-overlooked Atlantic Coast. And our specialists could not stress it enough: Always, always check the temperatures in advance of your trip.

As discussed by Mei Zhang, chairman and founder at WildChina, during the panel on summer travel, the demand for travel to Asia is only increasing. Global features director Erin Florio and editorial assistant Kat Chen continued the conversation with travel specialists on the destinations and experiences driving this growth. For Silk Road Treasure Tours president Zulya Rajabova and her clients, it's the destinations with indispensable craft identities, such as Samarkand in Uzbekistan with its millennia-old design traditions, that make the continent so varied yet distinct. Meanwhile, Marigold Wood, travel designer at Blonde Atlas, explained that she's been recommending routes going through China, such as Yunnan's tea trails, which offer trekking and hiking opportunities. For Ethan Crowley, lead trip designer at Asia Desk Ltd, educating his travelers on navigating countries like Cambodia, beyond their most-visited landmarks, is key.

If 2026 was the year of the fan, 2027 is ushering in even more reasons to travel for entertainment. Associate editor Charlie Hobbs and commerce writer Jessica Chapel helmed this breakout on the sporting events, music festivals, and art fairs that specialists are seeing people travel for (and the subsequent hurdles they must overcome to). From Pink at the Sphere, where access requires a Venetian hotel package to the Ryder Cup at Adare Manor, where full-house and package buyouts, high-demand events are increasingly forcing bundled purchases.

The specialists also highlighted how limited legal protections in the States enables extreme ticket scalping for stadium concerts and makes domestic events increasingly unaffordable. Stephanie Malakie, founder of Empress Travel Club pointed to a related challenge: With so many events and providers, travel advisors find it hard to filter the options and identify the right fit for clients. Rather than just acquiring a ticket, specialists agreed the focus should be on curating the surrounding experience or tapping into evergreen and niche experiential travel, with events like the Jets playing in London or F1 in Shanghai. Cultural milestones like Vatican City trips featuring Papal Blessings or European Christmas markets pair well with entertainment, too. To Zhang, set-jetting to filming locations also offers value, minus the headache of peak-event ticketing.

Hannah Towey co-leads the breakout session on outdoor travel.

Everyone is hiking, or so it seems. Helmed by Jamie Spain, travel bookings commerce editor, and Hannah Towey, news editor, this breakout explored nature as wellness and why it is we're increasingly longing for all things outdoorsy. However, our specialists offered a frank reality check on our dumbphone fantasies. “Guests all want to have access [to Wi-Fi] for emergency reasons at a minimum… I don’t have a single guest who wants to [fully] digital detox," Kate Doty, trip designer at Geographic Expeditions, said. It seems that many travelers want to be disconnected until the time comes for true disconnection, and Lou Lagrange, trip consultant at Luxury Travel Advisor, added, “Even planning for national park trips, [you have to] warn them there won’t be Wi-Fi.” However, the group observed that services such as having photographers on small ships, helps reduce a traveler's dependency on their phones.

There's still a real demand for glamping, as guests want access to nature but with a side of luxury. And when it comes to extreme weather events, instead of having travelers write off entire regions or countries, Doty has figured out collaborative ways to shelve them for potential future trips. In Travel Studio founder Kerri Lisa's case, it's a matter of reframing the perks of a destination and its seasons; the rainy season, for example, might be exceptionally lush and green. There's always something to be learned in welcoming a bit of playful unpredictability into our itineraries.
