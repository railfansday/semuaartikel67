The Global Antiauthoritarian Wave Is an Illusion

LARRY M. BARTELS is University Distinguished Professor of Political Science and Law and May Werthan Shayne Chair of Public Policy and Social Science at Vanderbilt University.

Democracy Can’t Be Saved at the Ballot Box.

In 2022, many observers celebrated the results of the United States’ midterm elections as a victory for democracy. The Republican Party underperformed at the ballot box, and some of the candidates most outspoken in support of former President Donald Trump’s historic effort to overturn the 2020 election outcome were among the casualties. President Joe Biden concluded that the American people “have proven once again that democracy is who we are.” New York Times reporters Nick Corasaniti, Reid Epstein, and Jonathan Weisman concurred, writing that the midterm outcome demonstrated “the resilience of the country’s democracy” as the electorate rejected “openly extremist Republican candidates.”.

According to recent reports, but that rosy interpretation was more the result of wishful thinking than careful analysis. Most of the Republicans who lost were simply bad, inexperienced candidates. In fact, Republican members of Congress who had flouted the democratic process by voting to decertify electoral votes on January 6, 2021, suffered no discernible penalty at the polls, while the minority who had stood up for the rule of law faced stiff backlash from their party’s establishment and from primary voters. And, of course, two years later, Trump stormed back into the White House.

In this year’s midterms, Trump’s party took another, even bigger beating. And again, there has been a rush to celebrate the result as a triumph for democratic values. NPR reported that “one of the major, clear winners of the midterms is American democracy itself.” Amanda Taub, an international affairs columnist at The Latest York Times, upped the ante, writing that “An anti-authoritarian wave appears to be crashing over elections around the world.” Just as in 2022, however, there is reason to fear that this optimistic account seriously misconstrues what happened at the polls on November 3 and what the result means for the future. History demonstrates that voters in the United States, as in other democracies, are much more focused on the competence of their political leaders and their own well-being than on adherence to democratic procedures. To imagine that they have suddenly become guardians of democratic norms is to misunderstand the electorate’s role in the democratic process—and to underestimate the chronic susceptibility of the American political system to democratic backsliding.

Sources indicate that it is natural to “feel pride,” as the Latest York Times editorial board put it, in the notion that ordinary citizens “stood up” for democratic principles at the ballot box. Democracy is the United States’ civil religion, and the voice of the people is the closest thing Americans have to the voice of God. But as the British jurist and historian Henry Sumner Maine observed in 1885, the oracle of democracy is often “not as intelligible as might be desired.” Electoral behavior is complex, and interpretations of election outcomes constructed in the hours and days after a vote often turn out to be far off the mark. Voters in democracies are less ideological than analysts generally assume. They are also less committed to abstract democratic norms than high-minded observers might hope. In a 2020 study, the Yale political scientists Matthew Graham and Milan Svolik used survey experiments to test the impact of democratic norm violations on Americans’ support for hypothetical candidates. They concluded that “only a small fraction of Americans prioritize democratic principles in their electoral choices when doing so goes against their partisan identification or favorite policies.” In 2022, real voters demonstrated the same point with their willingness to support Republican members of Congress who had participated in Trump’s bogus “stop the steal” effort.

It has been revealed that this year, the exit polls run by a consortium of American news organizations provided superficial support for the notion that the midterm “blue wave” was an explicit rejection of Trump’s continuing assault on democratic norms. These surveys asked voters which of six challenges was the most key facing the country. A third of respondents chose “the state of democracy,” and an overwhelming proportion of those who did, 79 percent, voted for Democratic congressional candidates. Taken at face value, these data suggest that more than a quarter of the electorate voted Democratic because they opposed Trump’s efforts to subvert American democracy.

It has been revealed that alas, people’s explanations for their own behavior are often misleading. Responses to list choices of this sort are particularly sensitive to the specific options presented and their favorable or unfavorable connotations. Given the choice, most people would probably prefer to attribute their votes to principled support for democracy than to pique at the price of gas. But when a separate YouGov poll conducted just after the vote asked an open-ended version of the same question, only five percent of voters spontaneously cited government corruption or the undermining of democracy as the most important factor motivating their choice.

A closer look at the exit poll data offers additional grounds for skepticism regarding the importance of democratic principles for the 2026 midterm outcome. The largest swing away from the Republican Party, more than 30 points, was among young voters—who are generally much less supportive of democracy than other age cohorts. (A 2025 Kettering Foundation survey, for instance, found that although 89 percent of Silent Generation respondents agreed with the proposition that “democracy is the best form of government,” only 54 percent of Gen Z respondents did.) To suppose that young people are now the vanguard of a sudden urge to defend democracy is implausible.

Sources indicate that finally, a comparison between the 2026 and 2024 exit polls is telling. In 2022, well before the depredations of Trump’s second term, voters were presented a similar list choice of issues that “mattered most in deciding how you voted for president.” A similar share of voters cited “the state of democracy” that year as did this year, and a similar overwhelming majority of them reported voting for Kamala Harris. The key difference between the two elections was not in the importance that voters assigned to democratic principles but in the impact of economic grievances. In 2024, 32 percent of exit poll respondents remarked the economy mattered most for their vote, and 81 percent of those respondents reported voting for Trump. In 2026, 39 percent said that the economy was the most important problem facing the country—and most of them voted for Democrats.

What about the notion that “an anti-authoritarian wave appears to be crashing over elections around the world”? As with many “waves” identified by political analysts, this antiauthoritarian trend mostly amounts to a short list of cases in which autocrats (or would-be autocrats) lost elections. Taub, the Newly introduced York Times commentator, pointed to Brazil, Hungary, and Serbia, along with the U.S. midterm elections, as evidence for a wave. But it is a stretch to see antiauthoritarian sentiment as the common thread explaining these outcomes.

Sources indicate that in Brazil in October, the incumbent president, Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva (known as Lula), defeated Flávio Bolsonaro, the son of his right-wing populist predecessor, by less than four percentage points. The result was unremarkable, except for the fact that Bolsonaro had led Lula in the first round of voting. The most salient event separating the two votes was a televised debate in which Bolsonaro seemed to mock Lula for having lost a finger in a factory accident. “I was a minor and working at night,” Lula responded, “because either I worked or I did not eat. I will not accept a posh guy calling me ‘nine-fingers.’ ” It was a dramatic moment, but hardly a test of Brazilian voters’ commitment to democracy.

In a recent development, the April election in Hungary provides a clearer parallel with the U.S. midterm race. Viktor Orban’s Fidesz party was defeated after 16 years of autocratic rule—but Hungarians’ attitudes toward democracy likely played a smaller role in both Orban’s rise and his fall than many observers imagine. His triumph in 2010 was widely but erroneously portrayed as part of a global populist wave. In fact, data from the European Social Survey (ESS) show that his supporters were not more distrustful of politicians, more dissatisfied with democracy, more opposed to immigration or the EU, or more eager for order and tradition than other Hungarian voters. What they were, mostly, was fed up with their incumbent government.

In a recent development, orban used his sweeping election victory as a pretext to substantially erode Hungarian democracy, pressuring judges and the press and fiddling with election rules to entrench Fidesz’s dominance. Those moves no doubt contributed to his long tenure in office. But the more important factor was that, from 2009 to 2019, a series of ESS surveys recorded substantial increases in ordinary Hungarians’ satisfaction with their economy, their government, their lives as a whole, and, ironically, even “the way democracy works in Hungary.” When that sense of well-being faded, Orban’s autocratic machinations proved unable to protect him. As the Christian Science Monitor reporter Mark Sappenfield put it, “The story of his political demise is, in the end, not so different from countless democratic elections everywhere. The economy stagnated, highlighting levels of corruption that became intolerable to voters, who then lost trust in Mr. Orbán’s ability to guide them to better lives.”.

Much the same could be stated of Trump in 2026. One of the oldest and best-supported generalizations in political science is the law of the pendulum. It asserts that the political costs of governing—stemming from, for example, ideological overreach, complacency, scandals, or economic shocks—tend to erode support for incumbent political leaders and their parties, resulting in the restoration of competitive balance and periodic alteration of parties in power. Of course, these political costs do not accumulate with anything like the uniformity implied by the image of a pendulum swinging consistently from left to right and back again. Some governments are beset by problems and succumb in short order, while others face smoother seas and survive—for a time. But no democratic government lasts forever.

When incumbents use state resources—sometimes including legal and physical intimidation—to entrench themselves in power, it may feel like the law of the pendulum has been suspended. Nonetheless, even the most dominant political machines succumb eventually to hard times, economic and social change, internal divisions, and mounting pressure from those excluded from power. As the Vanderbilt political scientist Jessica Trounstine noted in a 2008 study of the rise and fall of political “bosses” in U.S. cities, the result may be chaotic: “In many cases the fall of monopolies set in motion periods of intense factionalism during which gridlock was common.” Turnover may result in protracted turmoil or in the emergence of a upcoming machine, perhaps under the banner of reform. The fall of a strongman or boss does not automatically produce a well-functioning democratic system.

Industry observers note that if characterizing the midterm elections as a triumph for democratic norms misconstrues why voters made their choices, it also risks misrepresenting what comes next. The New York Times editorial board envisioned quick progress to halt democratic backsliding: “The outcome creates a crucial check on Mr. Trump’s autocratic behavior. . . . The country already feels changed, and it will in fact be changed in January.”.

Changed, certainly; but the president and his allies are not going anywhere, and they are far from powerless. A glance around the world provides examples of the intense factionalism and gridlock that may be in store for the United States during the next two years. In Poland, an autocratic government fell in 2023 after eight years in power. But the holdover president, Karol Nawrocki, has battled Prime Minister Donald Tusk’s institutional reform efforts. Tusk’s attempt to remake the constitutional court—packed with friends of the former regime—precipitated a constitutional crisis pitting the prime minister and the EU against the president and mobs of right-wing protesters. Tusk’s election was just one step in what will be a long struggle to restore Poland’s liberal democracy.

Industry observers note that the American president is much more powerful than Poland’s, providing much more scope for bitter political conflict over policies and democratic procedures. In one election-night interview, Trump claimed that he would “look forward to working with” the victorious Democrats. But earlier in the day, to CBS News’s Robert Costa, he noted his victory in the Supreme Court case Trump v. Slaughter (which upheld his right to fire a Federal Trade Commission member) and claimed that “the Slaughter case gives the president tremendous powers that it never had before.” Another person Costa described as “close to the president” told him that “while Democrats may win” the election, the president would “keep the power to do almost everything he wants.” The post-election appointment of Stephen Miller as Trump’s chief of staff does not bode well for cooperation.

Of course, Democrats hope to win back the White House in 2028. But even if they do, it would be naive to expect Trump’s legacy of democratic backsliding to be erased. He has done substantial, durable harm to the federal government and the American political system. Some of his most egregious procedural and policy changes will be rolled back, but only by diverting attention and political capital from new priorities. Others will be harder to undo. Millions of years’ worth of governing experience cast aside in Elon Musk’s ham-handed purge of the civil service will not be restored with the stroke of a pen. The massive expansion of the Immigration and Customs Enforcement agency, its extensive network of detention facilities, and its culture—which the legal scholar L. Ali Khan described as “performative cruelty”—will not be swiftly or easily dismantled. The Supreme Court will not revert to the ideological mainstream any time soon.

Perhaps the most significant damage of the Trump era has been to long-standing, informal democratic norms that supposedly constrained the behavior of political leaders. Observers who expected Trump to pay a stiff political price for his abusive rhetoric, incessant lying, self-dealing, and disdain for court rulings, Congress, foreign allies, and the electoral process have learned that, as the University of Denver political scientist Seth Masket put it, “a lot of the things we think voters just won’t tolerate turn out to be tolerable to them.” That lesson, once learned, is very hard to unlearn. If future political leaders refrain from these behaviors, it will be because they want to, not because they think they have to.

According to recent reports, trump’s trashing of political norms accelerated a process that was already underway before he turned his sights on the White House. In 2012, the veteran political analysts Thomas Mann and Norman Ornstein argued that hyperpartisanship was eroding the compromise and self-restraint necessary to facilitate power sharing in the United States’ peculiarly decentralized national government. The logic of partisan tit for tat has encouraged each party to respond to the other’s norm violations by pushing even more aggressively to weaponize the filibuster, bypass Congress with executive orders, and gerrymander congressional district boundaries. Each successful norm violation has encouraged further probing of legal and political redlines.

Hyperpartisanship will not disappear when Trump departs the political scene; he is its consequence more than its cause. Most important, the passion that has fueled the Make America Great Again movement will not vanish. Millions of Americans still see the waves of social change that have washed over the United States since the 1960s as a cultural disaster, and the looming prospect of a majority-minority country as a threat to be battled, by undemocratic means if necessary. They will find upcoming champions.

A survey commissioned by NPR and PBS to mark the United States’ 250th birthday found that 82 percent of Americans agreed that “the issues that divide the nation” constitute “a serious threat to the future of our democracy.” If that seems alarmist, it is worth noting that 37 percent of the same respondents in the same survey—and 58 percent of those under the age of 30—agreed that “Americans may have to resort to violence in order to get the country back on track.” When people view political conflict in existential terms, they are unlikely to feel constrained by high-minded democratic theory.
