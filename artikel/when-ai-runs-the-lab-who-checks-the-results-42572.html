When AI runs the lab, who checks the results?

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A review across 11 fields examines how scientists test AI-generated results as machines take on more of the work of proposing and running experiments.

It’s easy to picture AI in science as a very fast assistant: feed it the data and it finds the pattern. A review by researchers at 94 institutions calls that picture out of date.

Some AI systems now take on several steps in the research process, from reading papers and proposing hypotheses to directing robots in the lab and adjusting experiments based on the results.

Sources indicate that the authors ask how much of what comes out of that loop can be trusted. They rank the field’s claims by how well each has been tested in the real world.

The review is a survey of published work across 11 fields, not a newly introduced experiment.

The lead authors include Yongjun Xu of the Institute of Computing Technology at the Chinese Academy of Sciences and Heyang Li of the University of Chinese Academy of Sciences.

Co-authors come from institutions including the University of Queensland and Berlin’s Hertie School.

Sources indicate that the authors’ own 2021 review, in the same journal, described AI as a powerful tool for analyzing data. AlphaFold, which predicts a protein’s shape from its sequence, was the model case.

That program answered one question that had resisted decades of work and left the rest of the job to people.

Five years on, the review describes systems built from six modules that feed each other.

One reads papers, one analyzes data, one proposes hypotheses, one runs experiments, one writes the report, and one adjusts the whole process based on what failed.

Large language models supply the knowledge and reasoning, software agents sequence the work, and robots supply the hands.

Some groups, the authors wrote, are already testing whether AI can take part in peer review itself.

It has been revealed that the clearest example comes from materials science. An autonomous laboratory called A-Lab was handed 58 target compounds.

Drawing synthesis recipes from the literature and adjusting when a route failed, it made 41 of them in 17 days.

The review treats materials science as the field where the loop is most complete, with generative models proposing fresh crystals and robotic platforms making and testing them.

Other fields are further behind. In medicine, the authors flagged limited prospective validation and the risk of fabricated outputs.

In geoscience, models trained on past conditions struggle when the conditions themselves shift.

Sources indicate that the review’s most practical contribution is a grading scheme. For each field, the authors coded the level of validation behind AI results on a scale from A to D.

An A means a method has been experimentally validated or is already deployed in real workflows. A B means a prototype tested in a controlled, closed-loop setting.

In a recent development, A C means the result rests on benchmarks, simulations, or retrospective tests. A D means the work is still conceptual.

The point, the authors wrote, is that a high score on a benchmark is not the same as a discovery.

Closing what they call the reality gap between benchmark success and real-world use is, in their words, a top priority.

Three constraints recur across every field in the review. Standardized, high-quality data remain scarce.

Deep networks still trade interpretability for accuracy, so a model can predict a molecule’s behavior without explaining it.

And generative models can produce outputs that look plausible while violating physical laws, which the authors tie to the lack of explicit physical constraints in current architectures.

According to recent reports, A fourth limit is human. The review cites earlier warnings that AI’s fluency can create what one group called “illusions of understanding,” with scientists mistaking output for insight and converging on the same few methods.

The authors also put a price on scale. Global data centers used about 415 terawatt-hours of electricity during 2024.

The review projects that figure roughly doubling by 2030, an increase about equal to Japan’s current annual consumption.

For science, the authors wrote, the cost of discovery is becoming tied to electricity prices and hardware supply.

According to recent reports, in the United States, about 56% of data center electricity comes from fossil fuels. The authors put the resulting emissions at more than 105 million tons of carbon dioxide a year.

Training a single large model, they wrote, can cost from thousands to hundreds of millions of dollars, which risks a “computational divide” between well-funded labs and everyone else.

A review maps what has been published, and the authors are candid that maturity is uneven and that the grades are theirs. The open question for any one field is the A-to-D test itself: has anyone checked the machine’s result against the world?

Sources indicate that the authors expect the next decade to be the test. Governments in the United States, Britain, the European Union, and China have all rolled out AI-for-science strategies.

The review’s case is that the advantage will go to whoever best combines data, computing, and people, not to the biggest budget.

Industry observers note that the authors’ closing line is a limit as much as a forecast. AI, they wrote, will serve as a cognitive copilot rather than a replacement for scientists.

The review was published in the journal The Innovation.
