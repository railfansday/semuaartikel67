From a crew of seven to 16 sunrises a day, 20 figures show what it takes to keep the International Space Station running.

Recent findings suggest that the International Space Station is usually pictured as a machine of record-breaking size, but every room, robotic arm and docking port aboard exists so that people can live and work in orbit. Crews moved in long before the builders were done, and construction wasn't completed until 2011, according to NASA. For years, the station was a home and a construction site at the same time, and the people living aboard shared it with the work of building it. It has never been a finished object to admire from a distance. It has been a working outpost that its own residents help assemble and maintain. Once the station is seen as a lived-in workplace, each of its impressive figures raises a question about upkeep and human effort, not just about scale.

In a recent development, A station built while people lived in it grew room by room, and those rooms came from different builders. Its laboratories were built by the United States, Russia, Japan and Europe, according to NASA. No single country owns the rooms where the crew does its work. A home assembled this way cannot sustain itself, and it depends on its partners to keep sending people, parts and supplies. Its headline figures describe the station on the days when everything runs as planned. When crews, supplies or schedules fall short, those figures change, and the strain behind them is as much a part of the station's story as its size.

NASA gathered a collection of notable facts about the International Space Station, ranging from the five-agency partnership that runs it to Peggy Whitson's time-in-space record, the Canadarm2 robotic arm and solar arrays measured against an Airbus A380. Taken together, these facts show what it takes to keep people living off the planet without a break, and how much of that work falls to the crews themselves. They also show where the effort is still strained or incomplete. In several places, the station's routines manage a problem rather than solve it. Each figure ahead reveals something about the people and partners who keep the station working.

It has been revealed that running the International Space Station is a joint job, not one country's operation with guests aboard. NASA, Russia's Roscosmos, the European Space Agency, Japan's JAXA and the Canadian Space Agency operate the station together, according to NASA. Those five agencies speak for a much larger group, with 15 countries standing behind the partnership. A handful of agencies runs the station on behalf of many more nations, and the crews that fly show how that arrangement works in practice.

It has been revealed that the partnership highlights most clearly in who flies together. Crew-12 and Crew-13 each paired two NASA astronauts with one Roscosmos cosmonaut, according to USA Today. The fourth seat went to the European Space Agency on Crew-12 and to the Canadian Space Agency on Crew-13. For the people aboard, the partnership means working beside colleagues from several agencies, not a ceremonial roster.

More nations have sent people to the station than belong to its partnership. Visitors have come from 26 countries, according to USA Today. That count reaches well past the 15 member countries, which means people from nations with no part in running the station have lived aboard. More than 290 people have made the trip, and the station has become a destination for hundreds of travelers, not a private posting for each agency's own astronauts.

Five agencies keep the station running, and much of its reach comes from what that arrangement has opened to others.

Industry observers note that most records stay fixed once someone sets them, but the station's record for human presence has no end date yet. People have lived aboard without a break since November 2000, according to NASA. "25 years ago, Expedition 1 became the first crew to call the International Space Station home, beginning a period of continuous human presence in space that still continues to this day," NASA acting administrator Sean Duffy mentioned. The first crew moved into a home, and nobody has left it empty since. Each day that someone stays aboard adds to the total. A record that is still open can also still end.

No single crew has held this record. Continuous occupation has always meant a long series of different people, never the same crew staying put. Every departure had to be covered by someone already aboard or just arriving, and each of those handovers was a moment when the station could have been left empty. Across a quarter century of crew changes, that moment has never come. The record belongs less to any one astronaut than to the unbroken run of launches, crews and schedules that kept the station staffed through every change.

Each new crew extends a record that no one has yet closed, and keeping it open falls to whoever flies next.

The station's crew size looks like a fixed specification, the same kind of number as its length or its wingspan, but it is a staffing level that people have to keep meeting. NASA lists seven people as the station's normal crew. In early 2026, though, only three remained aboard, one American and two Russians, after a medical evacuation in January, according to PBS NewsHour. The evacuation followed a serious health problem with one astronaut, not a scheduled crew swap. A crew size that a single medical emergency can reduce this sharply depends on the health of the people aboard, and it is a target that has to be met again and again.

According to recent reports, A short crew meant the station did much less of its work, not the same work at a slower pace. While only three people remained, NASA paused spacewalks, PBS NewsHour reports, and it trimmed the research aboard as well. Outside work stopped entirely, and the labs that give the station much of its purpose produced less science. Seven is therefore a measure of what the station can do. With fewer people, the station stayed occupied, but much of its work had to wait.

Industry observers note that the crew had no way to fill the empty seats from inside the station, and only a upcoming launch could close the gap. A replacement crew arrived on Feb. 14, 2026, and brought the station back to its standard seven, according to PBS NewsHour. Refilling the crew took more than one country, because the new arrivals came from the United States, France and Russia.

The crew of seven holds only as long as people keep arriving, and the winter of 2026 revealed how promptly life aboard can fall below the station's standard figures when they don't.

Industry observers note that aboard the International Space Station, sunlight can't tell the crew what time it is. Astronauts see the sun rise and set 16 times in every 24 hours, according to NASA, and a full lap of Earth takes only about 90 minutes. A morning and a night can come and go while an astronaut works through a single task. A crew living with that much light and dark has to keep its day by a schedule instead of by the sun. It is one more part of station life that the people aboard have to manage for themselves.

It has been revealed that not every one of those laps gives people on the ground a view, but the station circles often enough to give them real chances. "But a lot of those times are during the day time, or it passes just on the horizon, but because it's doing it so often there's actually a number of times the space station passes overhead," astrophysicist Brad Tucker told ABC News in 2018. In 2018, how often a watcher could see the station depended on where the watcher lived, ranging from one a month to several a week. Catching a pass from the ground has always depended on place and timing as well as on how often the station goes around.

It has been revealed that the station's orbit is predictable, so a sighting doesn't have to depend on luck. Stargazers could check Spot the Station forecasts for passes up to 10 days ahead, according to a 2018 report by ABC News. That is enough notice to strategy a clear evening around a pass overhead.

The crew can't escape the station's 16 dawns, while people on the ground can pick which passes to watch, with a forecast to guide them.

Industry observers note that no American has spent more time in space than Peggy Whitson, who has logged 665 days off the planet, according to NASA. She holds that record in an astronaut corps that did not open selection to women until 1976, TIME reports, and the American endurance record now belongs to a member of a group that NASA once left out. Her total is more than a count of days. It shows how long one person can keep living and working in orbit, and it shows how recently NASA began choosing women to do that work.

Recent findings suggest that women entered the corps slowly after NASA opened selection to them. The 1978 class of 35 astronauts included only six women, according to TIME, and Sally Ride flew in 1983 as the first American woman to reach space. Women made up a small minority of the people NASA chose at the time, and Ride's flight marked the start of their place in American spaceflight. Women remain a minority of the corps, but a much larger one, with 19 of NASA's 46 astronauts. Once NASA opened the corps, a woman did more than take part in long-duration spaceflight. She set the American standard for it.

The American who has spent the most time in orbit comes from a group that NASA's astronaut corps once excluded entirely.

In a recent development, calling the International Space Station a six-bedroom house suggests a place with room to spread out, where every crew member has a bedroom and a guest could take the spare. The rooms behind that comparison are already claimed. The crew gets six spaces for sleeping, along with two bathrooms, according to NASA. Its residents outnumber those sleeping spaces, and NASA reports that the station supports a crew of seven, plus visitors. Those visitors come aboard a house with no empty bedroom set aside for them, and everyone uses the same two bathrooms. The six-bedroom comparison describes a household that runs close to capacity, not a roomy one. In a home this tightly shared, each room has to stay in working order every day for the crew to rest and keep clean. That makes the house as much a daily routine as a set of measurements.

According to recent reports, among the station's other rooms is a gym. An ordinary six-bedroom house might use its extra space for a den or a guest room. Aboard the station, part of the space a house would leave for comfort goes to exercise and to keeping the crew in shape. That choice matches the rest of life aboard, where the home stays livable only because the people inside it maintain themselves and the station.

The six-bedroom label counts the rooms, but the routine of the seven people who share them is what keeps those rooms livable.

The station's gym is a medical necessity, not a perk, because astronauts who live in orbit lose bone and muscle. NASA lists a gym among the station's facilities. If nothing effective is done to protect them, astronauts' bones thin by 1-2% a month, according to NASA. A loss at that monthly pace becomes serious for anyone who stays aboard for many months. For those crew members, time in the gym is closer to medical treatment than to recreation.

The workouts slow the damage, but they do not stop it. Exercise on the space shuttle and the station has not eliminated bone loss, NASA reports, and it has not eliminated muscle deconditioning either. Astronauts who keep to the routine still lose some bone and some muscle conditioning while they live aboard. Crews use the gym to manage a problem that no one has yet solved, and keeping the human body healthy in orbit remains unfinished work.

The machine at the center of that routine can't leave the station. Astronauts aboard use the Advanced Resistive Exercise Device, known as ARED, but the machine does not fit in exploration spacecraft, according to NASA. Crews bound for destinations beyond the station can't simply pack the equipment that station crews depend on. They will need another way to protect their bones and muscles on trips that keep them farther from Earth.

The station's gym reveals what it takes to keep people healthy in orbit, and how much work remains before crews can leave the station behind.

Recent findings suggest that the people who live on the International Space Station are also its builders, and some of that building can only be done outside the hull. NASA credits both astronauts and cosmonauts with spacewalks for construction and maintenance, so the outside work falls to residents from more than one partner rather than to visiting specialists. One planned walk was assigned to Mike Fincke and Zena Cardman, two U.S. astronauts already serving on the station's crew, according to CNBC. Construction on the station is also current work, with crews installing upcoming IROSA solar arrays. In practice, the crew renovates its own occupied home while wearing pressure suits.

According to recent reports, not all outside work is urgent, and building work can sometimes be set aside. In January 2026, NASA postponed a U.S. spacewalk in which Fincke and Cardman were to spend 6.5 hours outside, according to CNBC. That is most of a working day for two people, and calling it off moved the installation of a latest solar array to a later date. Still, the delay cost the station nothing it needs to keep running. The solar array upgrade is not necessary for station operations, which makes the new array an improvement the station can do without for now. Outside work comes in two kinds: work that keeps the station running and work that expands it. This walk belonged to the second kind, and NASA could put it off without harm to the station's daily operations.

Industry observers note that the building waits on the health of the builders. The walk was set aside when a medical situation led NASA to bring Crew-11 home ahead of schedule, and the crew had been due back around March, according to CNBC.

Crews add to the station only as fast as they are fit to step outside and build it.

The station's solar wings are wider than the largest passenger jet that airports are built to handle. From tip to tip, the arrays span 356 feet (109 meters), well beyond the 262-foot wingspan of an Airbus A380, according to NASA. The A380 makes a demanding yardstick because its designers built it to the largest size that airports allow. Its designers kept its wings within the maximum gate width that airport authorities permit, with a margin of under six inches at each wingtip, according to Smithsonian Air & Space. The station's arrays are far wider than any plane that the gate limit permits. On the ground, a structure that wide could not use a gate even at airports that serve the biggest jets in the world.

It has been revealed that the wingspan has a practical purpose, because the arrays are the surface the station uses to collect sunlight. Crews rely on them for between 75 and 90 kilowatts of electricity, the power that keeps the station running. That supply is still being expanded. Latest roll-out arrays, known as IROSA, are being installed on a station that remains occupied and at work, so the power system keeps growing while people live aboard. The arrays are a record-setting measurement, and they are also one more part of the station that people must keep building to keep it powered.

Even the station's widest feature remains unfinished work for the crews who depend on its electricity.

Industry observers note that the pictures of Earth that people on the ground know best come from the crew members living aboard the station, not from a separate camera program. NASA counts more than 3.5 million images of the planet that crews have shot from the station over the two decades it has been occupied. The photographers include both astronauts and cosmonauts, so the collection is the work of crew members from more than one space program. The size of the archive indicates as much about how long people have lived on the station as it does about photography. Every picture in it required someone at the window with a camera.

The photos often show the human side of Earth and its natural side in the same frame. USA TODAY reports that NASA astronaut Anil Menon took a picture in September of an aurora glowing above the lit-up cities of central Asia, with the station's own solar panels in view. The people behind the camera are working crew members, and they can capture settlements and natural events alike as they pass overhead.

It has been revealed that crews keep adding to the collection with each rotation. Crew-13 lifted off on a SpaceX Falcon 9 and Dragon in early October, according to USA TODAY, and its members joined the people who can photograph the planet from orbit. The total of more than 3.5 million is a minimum, and each arriving crew pushes it higher.

The photo archive is one of the plainest signs that the station is a place where people live, and crews can add to its record of Earth only as long as the partners keep sending them up.

Recent findings suggest that the station reached orbit as a long series of deliveries, each carrying one more piece of a home. Building it took 42 separate assembly flights, according to NASA, and each launch had to succeed before the next module could join the ones already circling Earth. Assembly was a chain of risky trips rather than a single event, and the station's shape depended on every link holding. Most of that chain ran through one country. The U.S. space shuttle flew nearly all of those missions, 37 in total, and Russia's Proton and Soyuz rockets carried the rest. The station has several partners, but getting its pieces to orbit depended heavily on a single American vehicle. A home shared by many agencies came together almost entirely along one narrow route into space.

The station has kept growing since its original assembly flights ended. A module called Prichal joined it on Nov. 26, 2021, long after the shuttle flights that carried most of its pieces. Companies now supply some of the hardware as well, and the station carries the BEAM module and an airlock called Bishop from NanoRacks. Space agencies no longer supply every new room. Some of the building also happens in orbit. The crew is installing new IROSA solar arrays, so the people who live on the station also work as its construction crew, fitting hardware into place years after they first moved in.

The assembly flights put the station in orbit, and the people living aboard have kept building it ever since.

In a recent development, the International Space Station is nearly as long as a football field. Its length from end to end is 356 feet (109 meters), just one yard short of a field, according to NASA. A familiar playing field makes the size easy to picture, and it suggests a long building that a crew could walk through from one end to the other. That picture doesn't match the station's layout. The 356-foot figure covers the station's entire outline, including large parts outside the area where people can breathe and work.

The rooms where the crew lives and works cover far less than a football field. The pressurized modules, which hold the crew's living quarters and labs, are 218 feet (67 meters) long, well short of the station's full length. Much of the remaining length belongs to the truss, an exterior frame that nobody lives in. The truss is 310 feet (94 meters) long, which makes it longer than the entire line of pressurized rooms. Most of the station's reach is therefore open framework on the outside, not enclosed space for people. The football-field comparison mostly describes the structure around the crew rather than the crew's home.

Most of the station's best-known measurement is outside its walls, and the people who keep it running live and work along a shorter, enclosed line inside.

The station's wiring is long because its power supply is spread out. The electrical wiring aboard runs 8 miles, according to NASA, and that length follows from where the electricity starts. The station collected its power with eight separate solar arrays, according to a 2018 report by CNBC. Current from each array had to be routed through the structure to the rooms where the crew works. A station that runs on sunlight cannot keep all of its power sources in one corner. Its wiring has to reach every array on the frame and bring that electricity together.

The station's sunlight is also collected in very small pieces over a large area. Its solar panels cover an acre, and those panels held 262,400 individual solar cells, according to a 2018 report by CNBC. The current from any single cell is small, and it is useful only after it is gathered with the output of thousands of others. The broad panels that make the station look so large are the same reason it needs miles of cable behind them.

Recent findings suggest that most people who picture the station think of its solar arrays, and few give any thought to the cable that carries their output. For the crew, that cable is what turns sunlight into lights, air systems and working laboratories. Behind the station's headline dimensions is a less visible network, and without it the people aboard would have no electricity to live and work by.

The station's crews do their outside work with a limb built to the scale of the station rather than of a person. NASA lists Canadarm2 at 55 feet long, with seven joints. That length covers far more of the exterior than any spacewalker could reach. With that many joints, operators can bend the arm around the station's structure instead of swinging it in a single straight line. Crews use it for jobs that a person in a pressure suit could not manage alone, from moving modules into place to setting up experiments and carrying spacewalkers to their work sites. Its duties cover both building the station and moving the people who work outside it.

The arm's construction work did not end when assembly did, because the station is still being refitted. CBC reports that crews unloaded the newly introduced IROSA solar arrays from their SpaceX Dragon with Canadarm2. On the spacewalk to install the arrays, NASA astronaut Anil Menon is the one who rides on the arm. For that job the arm serves as his transport as well as a crane, and the station's current upgrade depends on it. "There would be no way to accomplish this spacewalk without the help of Canadian robotics," said Mathieu Caron, director of the Canadian Space Agency's astronaut office.

According to recent reports, canada's part in this work does not stop at the hardware. The country runs its own Robotics Mission Control Centre in Longueuil, Que., according to CBC. Caron sees the spacewalk as a sign that the other partners recognize Canada's work in the partnership. Canada is a smaller partner, but this spacewalk could not happen without its arm and the people who operate it, and that work is the basis of Canada's stake in the station.

Sources indicate that crews can keep as many as eight visiting spacecraft docked at the same time, according to NASA. People and cargo can arrive on several ships together. They do not have to come through a single line in which each ship must leave before the next one arrives. With that many docking spots, the crew does not have to send an earlier vehicle away before a new one can attach. Cargo deliveries, crew visits and departures can all overlap. The station is built for steady traffic from the ground, and its crew can initiative around several vehicles at once rather than one at a time.

That overlap matters most when one crew replaces another. The station normally holds seven people, and during a handover it holds more, because the arriving and departing astronauts share it for a while. The extra docking spots make that arrangement possible. The vehicle that carried the new crew up and the one that will carry the old crew home can both stay attached at the same time. The incoming crew can settle in and learn the station's routines while the people who know them best are still aboard to help.

With room for eight ships at once, a latest delivery or a new crew can come aboard while earlier visitors are still attached.

Sources indicate that the International Space Station is in orbit, yet people can reach it within a single workday. Astronauts can travel from the launch pad to the station in as little as four hours, according to NASA. Measured by travel time rather than altitude, the crew lives much closer to the ground than a home in orbit suggests. That is the quickest possible trip, though, and a crew should not expect every flight to be that short.

Planners and crews can match that pace only when conditions allow, and on other days the journey may take longer.

For the people living aboard, the station is only as near as the next spacecraft that can reach them. On the best day, the ground is only a few hours away, but a crew cannot count on every launch day going that well.

The station's crew lives on supplies flown up from Earth, and NASA lists four types of cargo spacecraft that make those deliveries. Two companies and two national programs keep cargo moving to the station. The crew's everyday needs depend on all of them continuing to fly, and no single country guarantees those needs by itself.

Recent findings suggest that equipment aboard has made crews less reliant on those flights for one supply. Food, equipment and every other supply still have to come up on a cargo spacecraft, so the crew still counts on each scheduled flight.

The station has reduced its reliance on the ground without ending it, and its crews manage that dependence one delivery at a time.

According to recent reports, the work done in the station's labs is the clearest result of everything it takes to keep people living aboard. Researchers have used the station for nearly 3,000 separate investigations, according to NASA. That record indicates a working laboratory that researchers have used over and over across its years in orbit, not a showpiece. None of that research could happen without people aboard to tend it and without the power, supplies and equipment that support them. The station's size, its solar arrays, its docking ports and its regular cargo flights all matter for that reason. Together they keep the research possible.
