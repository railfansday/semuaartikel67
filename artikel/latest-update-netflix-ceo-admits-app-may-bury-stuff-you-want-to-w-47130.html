The streaming wars, or the battle to be the top streaming organization, are ongoing, according to Ted Sarandos, a co-CEO of what many would consider today’s frontrunner.

But in an interview with Bloomberg published today, Sarandos noted that people are still spending significant time watching traditional broadcast TV.

“The streaming wars are continuing as we go. Half of television engagement in the United States is still linear, old television,” he stated, without specifying the source of the statistic. According to analysis from Nielsen, streaming represented 49.6 percent of TV viewing in August, with 36.4 percent coming from broadcast and cable. That still suggests a sizable amount of viewing time for streaming providers to target.

As Netflix seeks to strengthen its lead against rivals like YouTube and Skydance, there’s extra pressure for it to deliver hit shows and movies.

I think as Netflix has grown and matured and the business has grown and matured around us, that hit rate really matters. And you’ve got to get ahead of ‘are we on a path to continue [and] maintain that hit rate in the US particularly, but everywhere in the world?’ And I’d say the business is working everywhere in the world, including the US, but we should be doing better in the US than we were. And we’ve got to really focus on our ability to do that.

The CEO remarked that Netflix is focusing on “the ambition of storytelling” rather than “chasing everything that people just happen to spend five seconds on television on” or relying on AI-generated content.

“I think what’s going to happen is that people will desire more ambitious, more accomplished storytelling in a world that is flooded with slop,” Sarandos noted.

According to recent reports, netflix has used generative AI-based workflows for various production tasks, like creating crowds or historical battle scenes, for hundreds of titles. This has helped Netflix save money while it increased the amount it spends on content this year by about 10 percent.

When it comes to streaming people struggling to find something to watch, Sarandos acknowledged that Netflix sometimes “make[s] mistakes about how we present new things” and can “bury things that you might be looking for under new things that you’re not coming for.” He pointed to AI as a tool for content discovery in addition to aiding in the pre-production and post-production of content.

Netflix is also being more open toward theater releases than it has been historically. That change was on the forefront when Netflix was trying to buy Warner Bros. Discovery’s (WBD’s) movie studios business (as well as its streaming business) and convince critics that the acquisition wouldn’t mean fewer theater releases. Although Netflix pulled out of the deal, it’s still eying an increase in theater releases as an opportunity. During the interview, Sarandos noted that the sequel to Netflix’s most popular movie of all time, KPop Demon Hunters (2025), “will be huge in theaters and massive on Netflix.” It has also mentioned that it will put movies in theaters for longer and recently extended the scheduled theater release for Narnia: The Magician’s Nephew from two weeks to seven.
