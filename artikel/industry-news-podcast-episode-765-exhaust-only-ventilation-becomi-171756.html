Good morning Fine Homebuilding Podcasters!

Following up on Episode 757 and your discussion of exhaust-only ventilation, I replaced all of our original 1950s basement hopper windows with premade glass-block units that include tiny 8-in. by 16-in. hopper windows of their own. Leaving one of these partially open on each of a couple of basement walls provides makeup air for our power-vent water heater and the first-floor bath and kitchen exhaust fans. It also augments our not-quite-sufficient active radon mitigation system, offsets the stack effect that otherwise would pull more radon in, reduces basement humidity during our lower Midwest winters, and generally gives us fresher air in the basement.

Industry observers note that the basement is unconditioned and varies between about 55˚F in the winter and about 75˚F in the summer. The uninsulated slab and cement block walls keep a consistent temperature that the outdoor air barely impacts (if at all). And unfiltered air isn’t a problem for us—we throw open our first-floor Marvin Essential casement windows (shoutout to Ian) every chance we get.

This approach wouldn’t work for everyone, but I continue to be surprised at how well it works for us.

Thanks for all the content, Cory.

Sources indicate that I have a question for the group. I’m up in Alberta, Canada, and there’s a potential that I—a carpenter of 20 years, not a teacher—will have the opportunity to teach shop class for a fresh school being built. Because the school will be brand new, the shop will need to be built from scratch for kids aged 13-17. The curriculum will be trades focused, not fine woodworking or crafts.

My question: What tools would you set it up with, and what kinds of projects would you get the kids to build?

I’ve got an 1898 house in the Chicago area, which came with a “finished” basement (i.e., asbestos tiles, fake wood paneling, etc.). I’ve been working on getting it back to its unfinished state and to eventually making it a less creepy place to do laundry and woodworking projects.

In a recent development, I think finishing out vintage basements in the Chicago area is a fool’s errand, generally. That being mentioned, I would love to make the floor less of a grimy, lumpy, cracked mess and put in something like tile (or another surface that can flood and get mopped up infinitely). Right now, it’s a concrete rat slab. The foundation walls it abuts are brick. I can’t find good suggestions on whether tile works in this situation and how to install it (or something similar) on a rat slab. All the information I’ve found assumes you’re starting with a modern slab with a vapor barrier underneath and that you eventually plan to put down something like LVP or carpet after installing a cornucopia of membranes.

Is it feasible to tile over a rat slab? Would you approach flattening the surface with self-leveler, or could you build it up with mortar and aim for flat rather than perfectly level? Or is this the wrong approach entirely?
