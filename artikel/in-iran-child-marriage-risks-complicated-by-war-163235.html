In Iran, child marriage risks complicated by war

For a girl still at school, marriage can mean leaving her education behind, becoming pregnant before adulthood and growing dependent on her family and husband.

In Iran, rights advocates warn that war and mounting financial hardship could expose more girls to those risks.

It has been revealed that the warning comes as the first International Day for the Elimination of Child, Early and Forced Marriage is observed on November 27. The day was established during the UN General Assembly in 2025.

Sources indicate that although there is no evidence linking child marriages to the war, cases illustrate the danger faced by girls married as children, often with limited means of escaping abuse. Gaps in Iran's marriage statistics also make it difficult to measure the impact of the conflict.

In one case cited by the Iranian human rights news agency HRANA, a 14-year-old girl in Kerman province who had recently given birth was reportedly killed by her husband. Skylar Thompson, deputy director of Human Rights Activists in Iran, stated the girl had reportedly been forced to marry at 13.

"While these cases cannot be directly attributed to the war, there are substantial grounds for concern that deteriorating socioeconomic conditions are placing more girls at risk," Thompson told DW.

Iran Open Data's analysis of the Civil Registration Organization's statistical yearbook identified 14,039 registered marriages involving girls under 15 between roughly March 2025 and March 2026. They accounted for just over 3% of all registered marriages.

According to recent reports, the figure excludes girls aged 15 to 17. UNICEF defines child marriage as a formal marriage or informal union involving someone under 18.

Thompson said 31,646 marriages involving girls under 15 were registered in 2020–2021, indicating a longer-term decline in recorded cases.

But the latest figures largely predate the war, which began on February 28, 2026.

"The absence of recent, disaggregated statistics, coupled with the prevalence of unregistered marriages, makes it difficult to determine whether child marriage has increased since the outbreak of the war," she noted.

The conflict has intensified pressures that can contribute to child marriage: lost income, difficulty staying in school and girls' financial dependence on their families.

In its October assessment, the World Bank described damage to infrastructure, disrupted trade and declining purchasing power, forecasting a 7.7% economic contraction in 2026/27.

It has been revealed that thompson said that families facing severe hardship may see marriage as a way to reduce household expenses, transfer financial responsibility or secure their daughters' future. Government-backed marriage loans can also create financial incentives for some disadvantaged families to arrange marriages involving minors, she added.

Hossein Raeesi, a lawyer and adjunct professor in Carleton University's Department of Law and Legal Studies in Ottawa, Canada, described a similar risk.

When families cannot meet a girl's living costs and education or other prospects are unavailable, they may push her toward marriage or force her into it, he told DW, while acknowledging that statistics on changes in numbers of child marriages since the war are still unavailable.

In a recent development, displacement can further weaken children's access to education and protection. On March 12, the UN refugee agency estimated that up to 3.2 million people had been temporarily displaced within Iran, based on preliminary assessments. That was an early-war estimate, rather than a current displacement figure.

Thompson said displacement and disrupted schooling could isolate girls from teachers, social workers and others who might recognize pressure to marry and intervene on their behalf.

Financial dependence, limited independent legal assistance and insufficient shelters can leave girls with few alternatives, particularly when those arranging a marriage are also their legal guardians.

Article 1041 of Iran's Civil Code allows girls under 13 and boys under 15 to marry with guardian consent and court approval. Iranian law therefore provides no blanket prohibition on marriage below the age of 18.

"Although Iranian law formally recognizes consent as a condition of marriage, it provides inadequate practical safeguards against familial coercion," Thompson told DW.

Raeesi mentioned preventing a marriage was insufficient if a girl remained under pressure at home. Leaving home could also expose her to further danger without safe accommodation and practical support.

Recent findings suggest that the concerns also extend to the online space. An investigation published by Human Rights Activists in Iran in October reported that the government-licensed matchmaking platform Adam and Eve displayed girls aged 13 to 17 as prospective spouses. Those findings do not establish how many marriages resulted, or whether the war increased them.

Raeesi called for a minimum marriage age of 18 for both sexes, alongside preventive intervention, financial assistance, education and safe accommodation.

In a recent development, "Education alone is not enough," he stated. Families also need the means to support their children.

Recent findings suggest that thompson urged targeted financial assistance, measures to keep girls in school, confidential reporting channels and independent legal support. Safe accommodation must be accessible without permission from guardians who may themselves be responsible for coercion, she said.

According to rights advocates, effective protection means that a girl under pressure to marry can say no without losing her home, her safety or her chance to continue school.
