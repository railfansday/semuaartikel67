📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Mona Chalabi is a Pulitzer Prize-winning journalist, and as much as it demonstrates across her incredible body of visual data reporting, she has always kept her eye on another prize entirely. I sat down with her earlier this fall at Christopher Park across from the Stonewall Inn, a site of protest and resistance, to talk about her fresh book and her career as a whole.

Sources indicate that the culmination of six years of research, interviews, and illustration, Ten Lives breaks the silence around class in her characteristic visual style. To me, it’s a reflection of her principled approach to journalism and storytelling that feel utterly lost in a world where self-promotion, access, and branding can matter more than the work itself. Chalabi brings a refreshing integrity and playful approach to reporting unflinchingly on the genocide in Palestine, the horrors of the healthcare system, and massive wealth inequality. While she insists her work isn’t “fine art,” it’s perhaps the purest example of what art should do: challenge our ways of seeing. She’s also uncommonly funny, humble, and perceptive. (Can you tell I’m a fan?).

Industry observers note that class is also at the center of photographer Mark Alice Durant’s memoiristic book, which critic Melissa Holbrook Pierson likens to Rainer Maria Rilke’s Letters to a Young Poet for our dystopian world of 2026. Other books in this edition similarly respond to the Orwellian state of art and power. Nitasha Dhillon and Amin Husain, both scholars and activists, have organized protests against the Museum of Modern Art in 2021, the Whitney Biennial in 2019, and the Guggenheim Abu Dhabi in 2015, among other instances of artwashing. They penned a joint review of Italian curator and organizer Marco Baravalle’s book The Battleground of Art: Radical Curating in the Crisis of Neoliberalism, a toolkit for seeing through the mainstream art world’s undermining of political movements and building sustainable alternatives.

Critic Carolina A. Miranda, meanwhile, takes up two newly introduced books by East Los Angeles artist Guadalupe Rosales, who came up in a community of Chicano kids raised in the 1990s amid uprisings and political action. Miranda writes that the anthology Rosales edited, as well as her blended memoir and photo book, perfectly encapsulate “the ways she engages the stories of others even as she tells her own.” A wise observation to carry with us as we face the week ahead; more of the latest in art book reviews and news below.

—Lakshmi Rivera Amin, associate editor, books.
