Why Airliners Cruise Around 35,000 Feet Instead Of Flying Lower Or Higher

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, if you glance at the flight map on the seatback screen, the number indicating the aircraft altitude is almost always the same: 35,000 feet (10,668 meters). No law demands it, and no airline manual treats it as sacred. But jet airliners flying from Seattle to Singapore keep settling into the same narrowband of sky, as if the atmosphere came with a built-in parking spot.

The thin air that makes high-altitude flying cheaper also takes lift away from the wings and thrust away from the engines, while the safety margin narrows with every thousand feet. The result is a constant compromise between aerodynamics, engine performance, aircraft weight, winds, and air traffic control. And the altitude that makes the most sense at the beginning of a long flight may be completely different several hours later.

Recent findings suggest that that compromise starts with something passengers rarely notice from the cabin: there is dramatically less atmosphere at 35,000 feet than there is at sea level. According to AirportFYI, the International Standard Atmosphere puts air at about 31% of its sea-level density at 35,000 feet, or roughly 0.024 pounds per cubic foot (0.38 kg per cubic meter). Just imagine wading through a swimming pool versus walking through an ankle-deep puddle. Less air means less drag, and drag is one of the biggest fuel penalties an aircraft has to overcome. If you prefer to see the idea rather than read it, this YouTube explainer tackles the same question visually.

Industry observers note that that reduction in density is one reason modern airliners spend so much of their cruise above most weather systems. The cabin, of course, cannot simply follow the outside atmosphere. At 35,000 feet, the air outside is far too thin to breathe normally, which is why pressurized cabins are maintained at an equivalent altitude of roughly 6,000 to 8,000 feet (1,800 to 2,400 meters). Temperature helps the equation as well. The standard atmosphere at 35,000 feet is around minus 66°F (minus 54°C), and the speed of sound falls to about 660 mph (1,062 km/h), compared with 761 mph (1,225 km/h) at sea level. AirportFYI notes that modern swept-wing jets typically cruise between Mach 0.78 and 0.85 there, roughly 515 to 560 mph (830 to 900 km/h). As reported by Military & Aerospace Electronics, thinner air reduces drag and lets an aircraft cover more distance for the fuel it burns.

Industry observers note that that is the attraction of altitude. But reducing the air around an aircraft also reduces the air available to its wings and engines. Eventually, climbing higher stops being an efficiency trick and becomes a performance problem.

It has been revealed that the same thin air that makes cruise economical creates the next limitation. A wing still has to produce enough lift to support the aircraft, and lift depends partly on air density and the square of airspeed. At roughly 31% of sea-level density, an airliner has to fly substantially faster through the air, or operate at a higher angle of attack, to generate the lift required to carry the same weight. Military & Aerospace Electronics explains that an aircraft can compensate through speed and angle of attack, but it cannot keep climbing indefinitely and expect performance to remain unchanged.

Engines face their own problem. As Your Mileage May Vary points out, there are fewer oxygen molecules available for combustion at high altitude, reducing engine thrust. The climb itself also costs fuel, so gaining altitude is only worthwhile when the efficiency gained during cruise outweighs the energy spent getting there. NASA describes cruise efficiency as a function of the aircraft, its engines, and its operating conditions rather than a single ideal altitude.

The table also explains why flying lower is not the answer. At 10,000 feet (3,048 meters), a jet encounters more than twice the air density it does at 35,000 feet, meaning considerably more aerodynamic resistance. It is also closer to weather and other traffic. Yet the best altitude is not fixed, because the aircraft itself changes throughout the flight.

In a recent development, A long-haul aircraft leaves the gate carrying tons of fuel and gradually gets lighter as that fuel is burned. That changes the altitude at which it can operate most efficiently. Military & Aerospace Electronics explains that an aircraft's weight can make a higher altitude less efficient early in a flight or leave too little performance margin to climb there. NASA likewise describes optimum cruise altitude as closely tied to aircraft weight.

AirportFYI offers an illustrative example: a heavily loaded Boeing 777-300ER flying from Dubai to London might begin with an optimum altitude around FL310, or 31,000 feet (9,449 meters), before moving toward FL390, or 39,000 feet (11,887 meters), several hours later. The aircraft does not normally climb continuously. Instead, crews can make staged increases known as step climbs. AirportFYI states these steps are usually 2,000 or 4,000 feet (610 or 1,219 meters), and cites industry studies estimating that optimized step climbs on 12- to 16-hour flights can save 1% to 3% of total fuel compared with remaining at one fixed altitude.

The gradual climb reflects the aircraft's changing weight: as fuel is burned, the jet becomes lighter and can climb more efficiently. But even a progressively lighter jet eventually reaches a point where going higher brings a new problem: the two boundaries of safe high-altitude flight begin moving toward one another.

The higher an aircraft climbs, the less room it has between two very different aerodynamic problems. The first is flying too slowly and approaching a stall. The second is flying too quickly and encountering compressibility effects as the airflow over the wing approaches critical Mach. Because the speed of sound falls as temperature drops while the true airspeed required to generate lift rises in thinner air, the two boundaries eventually begin to converge.

SKYbrary defines the coffin corner as the region where the stall boundary meets the critical Mach boundary, and notes that commercial aircraft generally avoid operating close to it. The Lockheed U-2 provides a much more dramatic illustration. As reported by Migflug, at 70,000 feet (21,336 meters), the spy plane can have as little as five to six knots of margin between its stall speed and the high-speed Mach buffet limit. Most commercial jets are deliberately kept well away from such conditions, and Simple Flying has examined what worries pilots when flying above 40,000 feet, where aerodynamic margins, engine performance and operational limitations become even more relevant.

Airliners maintain a significant margin from the edge. Airways Magazine reports that certification standards require a maneuvering cushion of at least 0.3 g at the highest operating altitude. According to AirportFYI, the practical ceiling for efficient sustained cruise on modern widebodies is about 41,000 to 43,000 feet (12,497 to 13,106 meters), with aircraft such as the Airbus A350 and Boeing 787 capable of operating somewhat higher than many older designs.

Recent findings suggest that supersonic aircraft solved the problem differently: Simple Flying has examined how high Concorde flew, with the aircraft routinely operating between roughly 50,000 and 60,000 feet (15,240 to 18,288 meters). A conventional subsonic airliner is designed around a very different compromise. And even after the aircraft has found an altitude that works aerodynamically, the crew does not necessarily get to use it.

According to recent reports, the optimum altitude calculated for an aircraft is only one piece of the flight plan. Dispatchers and flight crews have to account for the aircraft's weight, fuel burn, winds, weather, and the structure of the airspace before the aircraft ever settles into cruise. Per Your Mileage May Vary, pilots work with airline dispatchers to calculate an ideal altitude based on the aircraft's weight and fuel state, then request that level from air traffic control.

Winds can overturn the calculation again. A higher altitude might offer less drag but place the aircraft in a stronger headwind, wiping out some or all of the fuel benefit. NASA research has found that the optimum depends partly on weight and the wind profile. AirportFYI puts jet stream cores at roughly 140 to 290 mph (220 to 460 km/h) and cites United Airlines research indicating that wind-optimized routing saved about 1.5% of fuel, or around 660 pounds (300 kg), per transatlantic 777 flight. Controllers also have to separate aircraft, so a crew may be held at a different level because its preferred altitude is occupied. Simple Flying has also looked at why private jets can routinely fly higher than commercial airliners, highlighting how different aircraft and operating requirements can change the altitude equation.

There is also a deceptively simple reason why an aircraft cannot always choose any altitude it wants: flight levels are organized.

In US airspace, according to the FAA Aeronautical Information Manual, flights on magnetic courses from 000 to 179 degrees use odd flight levels, while those from 180 to 359 degrees use even ones. Reduced Vertical Separation Minimum, or RVSM, keeps properly equipped aircraft 1,000 feet (305 meters) apart between FL290 and FL410. Controllers assign those levels under the FAA air traffic control rules. So FL350 is not a pre-stabilized altitude. Depending on the aircraft's direction and the airspace involved, another aircraft may be assigned FL340 or FL360. Over the North Atlantic, as reported by Simple Flying, aircraft typically operate between 29,000 and 41,000 feet (8,839 and 12,497 meters) within tightly coordinated routes and procedures.

That makes the familiar 35,000-foot number look less mysterious. It sits inside a carefully managed system where aircraft performance, direction, traffic, winds, and fuel economy all meet. But the next generation of aircraft could still change where that compromise sits.

According to recent reports, pushing the cruise ceiling upward is possible, but it requires more than giving an airliner more engine thrust. The wing, engines, structure, pressurization system, and control laws all have to work together in an environment where the aerodynamic margin is already narrowing. Concorde demonstrated that passenger aircraft could routinely operate far above today's conventional cruise levels, but its altitude came with penalties in fuel consumption, noise, complexity and operating cost.

In a recent development, there is also a less glamorous limit to high-altitude efficiency: the aircraft has to be allowed to use the altitude its designers optimized. AirportFYI cites Eurocontrol studies estimating that European flights burn about 5% more fuel than they would with unconstrained optimal altitudes, largely because traffic prevents crews from using their preferred levels. Programs such as Single European Sky ATM Research (SESAR) and the FAA's NextGen are intended to improve aircraft's ability to follow more efficient trajectories. SESAR has evolved from a long-running ATM research program into the EU's principal mechanism for developing and coordinating the digital technologies needed to modernize Europe's fragmented air traffic management system, with its next major deployment phase now being prepared for 2028.

Industry observers note that the future of efficient cruise may require the aviation system to better provide each aircraft with the altitude it already needs. When a long-haul flight climbs from FL340 to FL360 several hours after departure, the aircraft is lighter than it was at takeoff, its optimum has moved, and the crew is taking advantage of the fresh balance between lift, drag, engine performance, and fuel burn.
