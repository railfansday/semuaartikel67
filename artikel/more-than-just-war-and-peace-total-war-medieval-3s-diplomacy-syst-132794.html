📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, creative Assembly's Total War: Medieval 3 is going to do a lot more with diplomacy, the developers promise. Diplomacy! The soul of any Total War game. They're going to build on the example of Total War: Three Kingdoms, which does allow for some gratifying backroom dealing, in fairness. During my first Thringdoms runthrough, I played Cao Cao, a ruler gifted with the ability to hypnotise people into liking him while hating each other. I spent many idle hours instigating proxy wars. Medieval 3 is apparently going to take all that to "new heights". It is going to make Han politicking seem as sophisticated as texting "get your filthy hands off my bae" to everybody you know on Valentine's Day.

Specifically, Creative Assembly want to create a sense of factions with autonomous agendas and evolving intentions, while placing more stress on questions of geography.

In a blog, game director Pawel Wojs hails Three Kingdoms as "a game defined by its dynamic use of diplomacy, where coalitions, alliances and relationships between characters and factions could fundamentally change the course of a campaign." He continues: "We want to build on those foundations, taking what worked well and exploring how we can push it further for the Medieval world.".

According to recent reports, as regards "believable agendas", Creative Assemble want the campaign AI to display both a survival instinct and the ability to "pursue its own interests instead of primarily being an antagonist to the player". These agendas will span the game's Politics, Economy, and Families systems, with dynastic considerations affecting behaviour. They want you to be able to read another ruler's intent by tracking back through their past actions. They want weaker factions to sue for peace or seek sensible alliances, rather than "stubbornly being entrenched in a war it is bound to lose". Aww, but I love it when Scotland does that.

The game will aim for a balance of long-term thinking and opportunism, with AI leaders pouncing on short-term prospects even as they keep the whole in mind. And then there are inconvenient "ripple effects": you might get involved with a neighbouring succession crisis because you don't want to deal with the fallout if, you know, That Guy takes the throne.

Speaking of borders, Creative Assembly also want would-be treaty-makers to think harder about terrain and transportation. "For example: You finally reach a faraway realm with your diplomat, opening diplomatic relations to a realm far beyond your initial sphere of influence," Wojs goes on. "Relations are positive, but meaningless due to the lack of physical proximity. Once that faraway realm has access to a port, a trade route can finally be established. This leads to an exchange in culture and trade, and may finally allow you to recruit foreign units into your service.".

I have to say, I do find a lot of this language either broadly familiar from previous Twars, or too general to reveal much. There is, however, a closing in-game anecdote from principal designer Thomas Schut that digs into the detail. Schut gives the example of you sending a diplomat to negotiate a 10 year non-aggression pact greased by recognition of another ruler's royal title, in return for their vassalage. Sending a diplomat allows you to negotiate on the spot, but if you'd rather not spare a diplomat, you can also dispatch sealed letters at the price of not being able to see and respond immediately to the other faction's reaction.

Rulers can make public claims to cities, calling on other rulers to support them. Simply conquering and annexing them will damage your standing in every court. You can also forge claims to cities, if only as insurance against wanting to own them someday. Get enough rulers to back you, and you'll be able to throw around grander pronouncements about what you're divinely entitled to. You are the Emperor of Everything! Coalitions will apparently form and fragment over years, old friends turning on each other as their dark plots come to light.

The joy of Cao Cao puppetry notwithstanding, I don't play Total War for the chance to thrash out cunning pacts with bots. I am here to put my archers behind my spearfolk - and hope the other side's cavalry don't notice the cutthroats I've stationed in the woods. I'm more interested in what I've read about Medieval 3's regional levies system. Still, it does sound like Creative Assembly are serious about giving you things to do besides putting other wannabe kings to the sword.
