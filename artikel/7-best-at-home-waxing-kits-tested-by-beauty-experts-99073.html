Before waxing, you should know there are two types of waxes: hard and soft.

Industry observers note that if you want to use something stripless, go for hard wax. It adheres to the hair follicle (versus the skin), hardens, and can be removed on its own, eliminating the need for paper strips. For some, it may even feel less painful than soft wax, so it's great for sensitive skin, indicates dermatologist Nazanin Saedi, MD. The only caveat is that you may have to apply it several times in the same area to make sure all the hairs you target are removed.

Soft wax, on the other hand, is soft and applied in a thin layer. A paper strip is then used to adhere and remove the wax. Often, the top layer of skin is also removed, so it can be irritating for people with sensitive skin.

It has been revealed that ideally, beginners should look for kits that contain everything they need to get the job done—wax, strips, and finishing oil, at minimum. Novices may also appreciate waxing kits that come with pre-loaded strips, since they're less messy and require less precision. The tradeoff? You're limited to the size of the included strips, so look for kits with multiple sizes if you're targeting different areas of the body. Before you head off to wax unwanted hair, these are a few other tips you should keep in mind.

Sources indicate that waxing removes hair by lifting it out at the root. With traditional waxing, hot wax is applied to skin and the hair is pulled off in the opposite direction of the way it's growing. The key word here and what can make at-home waxing dangerous is that it's hot.

According to recent reports, “Hot wax can burn the skin—and it is a particularly dangerous type of burn because the hot product adheres to the skin,” notes Dr. Murphy-Rose. Always make sure you're following the heating directions closely and that you hold your hand over the wax after it's been heated to gauge the temperature before applying it to your skin.

Then, first test the wax in an area on your body that isn't too visible. "That way you can check the temperature, but also see how your skin reacts," says Dr. Saedi. Testing a product will also allow you to see if it is gentle enough for your skin. “It's important to use the safest and gentlest wax kits available to avoid irritating, burning, and potentially scarring your skin," says Dr. Murphy-Rose. You should also avoid any ingredients you have a known allergy or sensitivity to. If you experience a bad reaction, you'll know it's time to pivot to a different product.

Recent findings suggest that "Hot wax tends to adhere the strongest, so while it may remove hairs more efficiently, it can also stick to skin more and pull off those top layers of the skin as it's also pulling out your hair,” says Dr. Murphy-Rose.

In a recent development, waxing exfoliates the top layer of skin and is more likely to cause hyperpigmentation a.k.a. dark spots, notes Dr. Okereke. She says to think of the skin after waxing like a fresh, pink wound. "Ultraviolet rays are attracted to wounds, making the freshly waxed skin more susceptible to darkening as melanin (the dark pigment in skin) floods the area to try and protect it." Luckily, there is a simple fix: Wear broad-spectrum sunscreen! Better yet, keep that fresh skin out of the sun completely for at least a few days.

It has been revealed that you'll also want to avoid any extra exfoliation, like retinol, chemical peels, and microdermabrasion, a few days prior and a few days following your at-home wax. A few other good things to know before getting started: Always wax on clean skin to avoid bacterial contamination in pores and make sure your hairs are long enough (at least 1/4 of an inch) to be grabbed by the wax.

In a recent development, the key to preventing ingrown hairs (an all too common downside of waxing for many) is to get the full hair follicle. The best way to do this is by keeping your skin taut and pulling the wax strip in the opposite direction of the way that your hair grows.

It has been revealed that sugaring, aka sugared wax or sugar gel, is generally safer because it’s applied to skin at room temperature (read: no risk of burning skin) and warms up with body heat. “Sugared ‘wax’ is not truly wax,” Dr. Murphy-Rose says. Rather, it’s a combination of sugar, lemon juice, and water. Some brands will infuse glycerin, fragrance, honey, or essential oils. Regardless of which brand you opt for, sugaring is water-soluble and easily rinses off the skin compared to hot wax, she says.

Sugaring and threading remove the hair follicle from the root but with less exfoliating, offering a gentler process, Dr. Okereke says. Though she warns that she's also had patients with coily-textured hair that still develop ingrown hairs and scarring from sugaring as well. The bottom line: "If you know you're prone to hyperpigmentation, ingrown hairs, or scarring, see a dermatologist to discuss the best hair removal practices for you," says Dr. Okereke.
