Breaking: Anthropic IPO, AI wealth spawn a upcoming, leaner kind of family office

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The Anthropic IPO is expected to mint hundreds of centimillionaires and spur the creation of newly introduced investment vehicles to manage these windfalls.

In a recent development, anthropic-related wealth could lead to 15 to 20 upcoming single-family offices over the next three years, according to exclusive data provided to CNBC by Altss, a global private markets intelligence platform. Altss also estimates about 70 households related to the Anthropic wealth boom will start working with multifamily offices or outsourced family offices. Altss' CEO Dawid Siekiera added that the firm expects the launch of a flurry of other personal investment companies, venture funds and investor partnerships.

Lawyers and family-office consultants told CNBC that they have received inquiries from current and former Anthropic employees and early investors about potentially setting up their own firms. Many of these newly wealthy tech professionals are interested in leaner operations with few in-house staff and leveraging technology and outside consultants to manage their fortunes, they noted.

It has been revealed that "Why would they be building a traditional office when they can probably see inherently by where they built their wealth how to make it more efficient?" consultant Kirby Rosplock told CNBC. "I think there's a sense that they can do it smarter, and that may not mean having more headcount, but more horsepower.".

Anthropic beneficiaries, like their peers in tech, tend to skew younger, with some even in their late 20s, experts told CNBC. They are typically more focused on hands-on investing than preserving generational wealth compared to traditional family office principals, according to Rosplock. (A Deloitte survey estimated the average age of family office principals at 68.).

"This is very different than having a multi-generational family business that is acquired by some other larger firm," she stated. "These people are likely staying engaged in the space, wanting to take the next bite at the apple and continue to build wealth.".

While these Silicon Valley clients often have better access to hot startups than traditional investment advisors, they typically aren't familiar with a broad range of asset classes, according to Chris Mays of Armanino, an accounting and consulting firm.

In a recent development, "When you look at the financial services folks, a lot of times they would build the investment function inside of their family offices. I don't see that as a likely outcome inside of these [tech] family offices," he noted. "They're going to rely on third-party investment advisors or multi-family offices. They're not going to go and hire a buy side guy out of x, y, z hedge fund or private equity fund to run their money.".

Jennifer Katrulya, CPA and partner at PKF O'Connor Davies, mentioned she recommends relying on outside advisors rather than building out an in-house investment team.

"You're going to get the deeper expertise from the wider bench. The minute you try to hire someone for that expertise, unless you're investing in their growth, they're becoming stale every day after you hire them," said Katrulya, co-leader of the accounting and advisory firm's family office group.

Recent findings suggest that for young, first-time wealth creators, a founder's office may be more appropriate than a traditional family office, according to partner William Kambas of the law firm Withers. Founder's offices often have similar legal infrastructure as family offices but are typically smaller, with fewer levels of management and a focus on assisting the principal with personal needs and investments.

Recent findings suggest that "A lot of young entrepreneurs have ideas and they want the flexibility to execute on their vision," Kambas mentioned. "A family office or a management office simply gets in the way because they're going to do it all themselves.".

Michael Kosnitzky, a partner at Pillsbury Winthrop Shaw Pittman, said that the costs of a traditional family office aren't necessarily worth it to most Anthropic beneficiaries. He considers $200 million in investable assets to be the bare minimum for starting a family office and that it is still hard to justify with fewer than $500 million in assets.

Industry observers note that instead, he recommends a "starter family office," with a legal vehicle called a family limited partnership that holds investments. The family members hold limited-partner interests while a separate company, typically structured as a C-corporation, provides investment services, hires staff and coordinates advisors to the partnership. The setup confers many of the same tax benefits as a full-blown family office without the cost and complexity, he mentioned.

Rosplock also encourages starting small, perhaps by hiring a chief of staff or executive assistant to coordinate outsourced services. She also cautioned that IPOs typically come with lockups and that high-flying tech stocks can stumble.

"Don't build the Taj Mahal of family office structures when you may have years potentially to realize the position," she mentioned. "What we have to be very mindful of is that new wealth can be a flash in the pan if external things happen that we don't control. We see it all the time with IPOs.".
