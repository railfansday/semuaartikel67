Tech Report: Correcting the TV ad market’s imperfect shift to streaming

Recent findings suggest that the TV ad industry’s shift from linear to streaming is real, but the financial truth underlying that shift may not align with many people’s perception of the transition. That’s because the movement of ad budgets from linear to streaming has hardly been evenly paced.

It has been revealed that streaming’s share of ad revenue is indeed growing at major media companies, including The Walt Disney Organization, Paramount and Warner Bros. Discovery. But their earnings tell a different story: Linear revenue is falling faster than streaming revenue is rising. The shift is happening, but it’s not benefiting content owners as expected.

In a recent development, the core problem is a supply-demand imbalance. Streaming ad revenue is growing through increased volume, not higher prices. Fresh inventory floods in, making cheap inventory abundant. As major subscription services continue to promote free ad-supported streaming tiers, the buyers’ market will only continue.

The solution is to grow demand, not increase prices. Fortunately, the ad dollars needed to support the streaming market already exist, sitting in local and midmarket budgets that haven’t been able to buy national CTV efficiently. The opportunity is to bring these advertisers into the broader video and streaming market, then develop ways to keep them investing.

Correcting the imbalance requires creating new demand, then ensuring that content quality and product performance are strong enough to eventually shift pricing dynamics.

It has been revealed that when it comes to growth, the glut of cheap national SVOD supply may be a problem for programmers, but it’s an opportunity for local and midmarket advertisers. If the current streaming market is largely aimed at Fortune 500 companies, the industry has to consider the thousands of smaller advertisers that make steady annual investments in local linear buys. Taken collectively, these advertisers represent billions of dollars in ad budgets.

Because these advertisers have smaller budgets, many haven’t tried streaming. It is more cost-efficient for them to execute local and spot buys on linear than to invest in targeted or national streaming campaigns. These advertisers are still seeing real success reaching millions of linear viewers, so they understand the power of TV. They just need an entry point for CTV. Solving this matter would allow demand to flow steadily into the market.

In a recent development, once that demand enters the market, additional steps must happen. CPMs fall when inventory is sold as undifferentiated tonnage. With platforms like Disney+ and Netflix exploring free ad-supported tiers, even more low-cost supply will enter the market. But cheap supply that doesn’t perform isn’t worth an advertiser’s investment. If that inventory doesn’t deliver results for advertisers outside the Fortune 500, those advertisers will invest their budgets elsewhere, creating another demand imbalance.

In a recent development, the answer lies in household addressability. While this is available on some streaming platforms, it’s still largely absent from the overall TV market, including linear. Household-level addressability turns cheap impressions into targeted impressions worth buying, even within highly premium inventory. The more information brands have about who will see their ads and whether those ads are likely to resonate with the viewer, the more they are likely to pay.

Sources indicate that it is no secret that streaming is the future of TV, and shifting revenue numbers reflect that. To build a sustainable future for media companies and advertisers, the entire ecosystem must first provide ease of access and then addressability.

Making streaming easier to buy at scale would help solve the demand problem. Once more advertisers are in the market, addressability creates greater competition for impressions, solving the pricing imbalance.

This future is out there, provided the industry takes a few steps in the right direction.
