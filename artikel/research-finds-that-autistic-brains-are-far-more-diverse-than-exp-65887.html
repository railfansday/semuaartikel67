Research Finds That Autistic Brains Are Far More Diverse Than Expected

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

On average, autistic viewers’ brain responses more closely resembled those of non-autistic viewers than those of other autistic viewers while viewing movie scenes.

Industry observers note that what happens in the brain when we watch a movie? As characters interact, emotions shift, and storylines unfold, our brains continuously interpret a complex stream of sights, sounds, and social cues. Scientists have long sought to understand how this process differs in autistic individuals, but newly introduced research suggests the answer may be far more complicated than previously thought.

Industry observers note that in a study led by University of Iowa researchers Dorit Kliemann and James Traer, scientists examined functional MRI scans from 48 autistic adults and 90 non-autistic adults, collected at Indiana University and Caltech. Their findings, published in The Journal of Neuroscience, reveal surprising similarities between the two groups while highlighting crucial differences that emerge when individual brain responses are examined.

Industry observers note that rather than relying on still images or simplified laboratory tasks, as many earlier studies have done, the researchers used six movie clips lasting five to 20 minutes each. The selections spanned animation, romantic comedy, action, and Hitchcockian thrillers, allowing the team to investigate how the brain responds as visual information, sounds, emotions, and social interactions unfold together in realistic, rapidly changing situations.

According to recent reports, the researchers trained a computer model to associate features of each clip with activity in different brain networks. Those features included what viewers saw and heard as well as subtler details, such as whether a scene had a positive or negative emotional tone.

“The computational model allowed us to look for robust brain responses to many different aspects of the complex, dynamic contexts in the movies,” Kliemann remarked.

Sources indicate that the model predicted brain activity better than chance across the clips and networks tested. It was most accurate in networks involved in hearing and language, and its average accuracy was similar in both groups.

The model’s predictions varied by about the same amount within a given person in either group. The difference emerged when the team compared one viewer with another. The patterns linking scenes to brain activity were more varied among autistic adults, with the biggest gap between groups appearing when the researchers analyzed social cues alone.

According to recent reports, “We were surprised by the similarity of group averages. The results suggest that we need to focus on understanding the details of individual brain responses. How and why do they differ?” Traer noted.

The results point to substantial differences in how individual autistic adults’ brains respond to movies, particularly when processing social information. These differences were greater than those observed among non-autistic participants, even though the two groups showed similar average brain responses. The findings suggest that understanding autism may require closer attention to individual patterns of brain activity rather than relying primarily on group averages.

The team now wants to find out when and where those differences appear in the brain and how they relate to differences in behavior. “Identifying ASD subgroups may help us build a more precise understanding of the condition and help develop ways to provide individually tailored support,” Kliemann noted.

Reference: “Heterogeneous Encoding Patterns of Dynamic Audiovisual Social Stimuli in Autism Spectrum Disorder (Heterogeneous Encoding Patterns in Autism)” by Avery L. Van De Water, Lisa Byrge, Daniel P. Kennedy, Dorit Kliemann and James A. Traer, 14 November 2026, Journal of Neuroscience. DOI: 10.1523/JNEUROSCI.0055-26.2026.
