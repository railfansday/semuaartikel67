Can smart cities attract the talent they need to thrive?

More than half of the world’s population now lives in cities, and that figure is projected to increase to nearly 70% by 2050. With the numbers of urban dwellers set to expand rapidly in some regions, a latest generation of towns and cities is on the rise: communities that are designed to be cleaner and more efficient, driven by a host of technologies such as artificial intelligence and the Internet of Things — a network of sensors and devices that collect and transmit data about their surroundings.

According to recent reports, as the global competition for talent and investment intensifies, many governments see these latest cities and urban areas as strategic resources for boosting their innovative and economic competitiveness. Research facilities, both public and private, are central to this strategy.

Recent findings suggest that in Songdo, South Korea — an early experiment in building a smart city from the ground up — biopharmaceutical giants such as Samsung Biologics have built major manufacturing facilities, and academic institutions such as Yonsei University have set up training and research centres to nurture scientists. In Hengqin, a rapidly developing district in the Pearl River Delta of southern China, research institutions also play a key role in a government strategy aimed at attracting talent, diversifying the economy and building a reputation for high-tech innovation.

For some researchers, purpose-built tech-centric cities provide them with the ideal environment to conduct experiments. Air-quality and traffic-flow research benefits greatly from consistent, real-time data gathered from many different locations, for instance. “Smart cities can offer something very valuable: a real-world laboratory,” says Dakota McCarty, a researcher of urban planning at George Mason University’s Songdo campus.

According to recent reports, but building a liveable city from scratch takes more than implementing advanced technologies and creating favourable conditions for institutions. Many large-scale urban projects across the world are facing a collective challenge: how to convince talented people to live there?

The term ‘smart cities’ generally refers to places that are made to run more efficiently and sustainably through the extensive use of digital technologies. Songdo, located about 40 kilometres southwest of South Korea’s capital, Seoul, is often described as “the world’s first smart city”1. The idea for Songdo dates back to the 1980s, but construction did not begin until several years after the 1998 Asian financial crisis, when the Incheon metropolitan government proposed developing Songdo as part of a free economic zone aimed at attracting foreign investment2. Today, Songdo spans about 5,260 hectares of coastal land, roughly the size of Manhattan.

In 2011, a series of futuristic technologies was built into Songdo’s infrastructure as part of South Korea’s campaign to establish ‘ubiquitous cities’, its name for smart cities at the time. One example is its underground waste-collection system — a network of subterranean pipes that suck rubbish directly from individual households, rather than transporting it by road. The system uses bins fitted with sensors and built into the stairwells of residential buildings. Once the sensors detect that the bins are full, the system will automatically empty them and send the rubbish through the pipes to a waste centre. Recyclable rubbish is cleaned and reused, and food waste is processed into compost.

Industry observers note that in addition to the use of sensors in waste disposal, Songdo has deployed thousands of sensors to help manage other city functions such as traffic and security. These sensor networks link to a control centre run by the Incheon Free Economic Zone Authority (IFEZA), which manages Songdo and two other free economic zones in Incheon.

Extensive sensor networks and integrated data systems are a major asset for Songdo’s researchers and students, says McCarty, who moved from the United States to South Korea nearly a decade ago and has spent the past three years in Songdo. “In a place like Songdo, which was planned from the start with smart infrastructure, and has universities, industry and public agencies relatively close together, there is much more potential to test ideas in real urban conditions,” he says.

Data can be accessed through a website run by IFEZA, with additional data sets available on request. McCarty and his students are combining traffic data obtained from IFEZA with observations collected on the ground to build a model of Songdo’s walkability. The team hopes to show that adjusting traffic-light timings could make the city more accommodating for pedestrians without increasing congestion3.

Sources indicate that there is a downside to building a city’s renown around cutting-edge technology, however: other cities can catch up. As the novelty of Songdo’s original smart-city infrastructure started to fade, it increasingly sought to build its identity around industry clusters that are harder for competitors to replicate.

Biopharmaceuticals, for example, is a strategic industry for South Korea, with the government committing US$1.5 billion to support the sector over this decade. Celltrion, one of South Korea’s largest biotechnology companies, was among the first firms of its kind to commit to Songdo in 2003. Samsung Biologics, a major biopharmaceutical manufacturer, established its headquarters there in 2011.

Moves like this helped to create a upcoming development path for the city, states Sohyun Park, a researcher of economic geography at George Mason University in Songdo. “As these firms expanded, local and national governments increasingly concentrated resources on developing Songdo as a biotechnology cluster,” says Park. As of 2021, Songdo was home to around 60 biotechnology companies, and the government’s plan is to expand that number to 700 by 2030.

Dakota McCarty and his team at George Mason University’s Songdo campus are using traffic data to come up with ways to make the city more pedestrian-friendly.Credit: Dakota McCarty / George Mason University Korea.

Industry observers note that such expansion depends on the support of Songdo’s university sector to train a fresh generation of biomedical talent. Yonsei University, which has its main campus in Seoul and an international campus in Songdo, opened the Korean National Institute for Bioprocessing Research and Training (K-NIBRT) in Songdo last year. The project has received around 60.9 billion South Korean won (US$41 million) from national and regional authorities and aims to train 2,000 biopharmaceutical specialists each year to address labour shortages locally and across the country.

K-NIBRT benefits from Songdo’s complete biopharmaceuticals value chain, notes Gyoonhee Han, the institution’s director-general, because its research institutes, drug manufacturers and raw material and equipment suppliers are all conveniently packed into a radius of a 10-minute drive. This tight-knit ecosystem means that companies, researchers and academics can collaborate efficiently where industry is located, and trainees are able to enter the workforce as soon as they graduate, says Han.

According to recent reports, beyond biopharmaceuticals, Songdo looks on expanding its university base more broadly, particularly within its Incheon Global Campus (IGC), a higher-education zone established in the early 2010s and backed by US$1 billion in investment from the Korean and Incheon Metropolitan City governments. Campuses of three US universities — George Mason University, the University of Utah and the State University of Latest York (which has two Songdo campuses) — plus Belgium’s Ghent University have opened in the IGC, and six more universities are expected to join in the coming years.

Songdo’s evolution reflects a broader challenge for smart-city projects: advanced technologies and digital infrastructure can attract investment and attention, but long-term R&D activity depends on assets that are harder to build: universities, skilled workforces and specialized industry clusters.

In Hengqin, an island in southern China’s Guangdong–Hong Kong–Macao Greater Bay Area (GBA), regional and central governments are trying to combine both approaches. In addition to the autonomous-vehicle technologies that Hengqin and the wider GBA area are known for — including driverless buses, taxis and delivery services — universities and research institutes are being positioned at the centre of Hengqin’s development strategy.

Industry observers note that hengqin entered a latest phase of cross-border cooperation in 2009, when China’s central government passed legislation enabling Macau, a special administrative region with its own governance system, to exercise jurisdiction over part of the neighbouring island. The goal was to allow the University of Macau to move its campus away from a densely populated casino hub, and more broadly to have Hengqin serve as a bridge between Macau and the neighbouring Guangdong province.

These efforts were accelerated in 2021, when China established the Guangdong–Macao In-Depth Cooperation Zone — an economic and administrative area covering more than 100 km2 of Hengqin, run jointly by the Macau and Guangdong governments. One of the biggest projects within this new zone is an international university town, expected to be completed by 2030 at a cost of nearly 20 billion Macau pataca (US$2.47 billion). At least three universities plan to open campuses there — the University of Macau (which will build an additional campus alongside an existing one), Macao Polytech University and Macao University of Tourism — to accommodate roughly 20,000 students.

Students develop flexible electronics in the Soft Sensors-Actuators-Robots Laboratory at the University of Macau’s main campus in Hengqin.Credit: The University of Macau.

“Academic and research capabilities are essential, because they provide the talent, knowledge and innovation infrastructure that Hengqin needs to become a science and technology hub,” says Yonghua Song, president of the Macao Association for Higher Education Development and rector of the University of Macau. Song adds that new facilities such as the University of Macau’s Faculty of Information Science and Computing, opened in August, are expected to train talent in highly competitive areas such as artificial intelligence, microelectronics and financial technology.

In a recent development, like Songdo, researchers in Hengqin benefit from its extensive smart-city infrastructure, including the large networks of sensors in operation across the island. According to Ma Shaodan, a wireless-communications researcher at the University of Macau’s State Key Laboratory of Internet of Things for Smart City in Hengqin, having smart-city infrastructure right outside the laboratory removes many of the constraints of conventional research environments. “It provides an invaluable physical context that accelerates our research, strengthens our regional impact, and serves as a tangible blueprint to inspire the next generation of engineers,” she states.

Early signs suggest that Hengqin’s strategy to become a place of high-tech innovation is gaining momentum: the University of Macau has expanded its presence, research infrastructure has proliferated and authorities continue to invest heavily in the cooperation zone.
