Breaking: Workers pay all of the $28,303 family health blueprint. Their benefits consultants earn more when it rises

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that the average employer-sponsored family health plan now costs $28,303. Workers see about a quarter of this cost in their paycheck and assume that their employer pays the rest. The rude shock is that workers pay all of it.

When an employer thinks about hiring someone, they think in terms of total compensation, not the individual buckets such as cash wages, paid leave, retirement benefits, workers’ compensation premiums, and health insurance. When the cost of one of these items balloons, the others grow less. Economists have documented the trade-off between wages and fringe benefits for decades. Thus, inevitably, rising health insurance premiums crowd out cash wage growth.

Industry observers note that nowadays, the cost of an average family health strategy is 44 percent of the typical full-time worker’s (pre-tax) cash wages. You would think somebody would push back on these costs on behalf of workers. Sadly, almost no one is. The real question is why, and what can be done about it.

It’s vital to clarify that roughly 100 million people get their health benefits from large companies that self-insure their health plan. That is, the employer doesn’t buy health insurance; rather, it pays the health care bills for its workers and family members. So this is not a story about insurers gouging employers on premiums. Insurers are just hired administrators.

Instead, let’s start with the people large employers are paying to design their benefits. Most large self-insured companies use a handful of big benefits consultants to coordinate and manage health benefit design and contracting. There’s a very good chance these consultants look out for their own interests first and foremost, rather than the companies’, let alone the workers’ interests.

Industry observers note that begin with how “value” is measured. For hospitals, the standard approach is to examine the discounts hospitals provide off their billed charges. This almost makes sense, but hospital charges are widely known to be nonsense values set by the hospitals themselves and charged to virtually no one. Highmark, a Blue Cross blueprint, makes this exact point in its own employer materials: a facility that charges $100 and gives a 60% discount is paid $40, while one that charges $70 and gives a 50% discount is paid $35. The bigger discount costs more. Milliman, an actuarial firm, notes that charges are far less constrained than negotiated prices and thus rise faster, so discounts appear to grow larger at the same time prices rise.

The same pattern is evident throughout the health initiative. Out-of-network claims are often repriced by vendors paid a percentage of the “savings” off billed charges, as a 2024 investigation of MultiPlan documented. The higher the bill, the bigger the fee. Drug benefits have long been judged by rebates off list prices set by manufacturers.

In every case, the rewards go to whoever sets the highest list price. The price that matters is the price actually paid for health care, net of any discounts. This is the equivalent of withholding more in taxes throughout the year only to be delighted with your large tax refund next year. Employers need to judge prices against a benchmark the provider doesn’t control, such as Medicare prices.

Additionally, large companies need to interrogate their benefits consultants on how exactly they are being paid. Federal law requires that brokers and consultants disclose their direct and indirect compensation, but not necessarily how the incentives are structured. If benefits consultant revenue rises with employer spending, or is higher for the vendors it recommends, employers have their answer.

Recent findings suggest that what about the corporate benefits department? Isn’t it their job to directly interface with the consultants and monitor this? They need shaking up, because their incentives are all wrong. Corporate benefits staff are in a difficult position. If health strategy changes go wrong, they get all the complaints, often with the CEO copied. If their decisions save the company (and its workers) millions of dollars, they get none of that. From their perspective, there is only downside risk. So they avoid any change that might generate complaints. Nobody gets fired for sticking with the status quo.

In a recent development, meanwhile, workers are paying the bill. But their costs are diffuse and nearly invisible. Their share of the health strategy cost is commonly spread across 26 paychecks. That’s a few hundred dollars per biweekly pay period. This is minimally salient to them. This is the classic case of concentrated blame and diffuse, hidden costs. Absent some outside pressure to change, the benefits office is doing exactly what its incentives reward.

Sources indicate that everyone in the employer health care supply chain does better when spending rises with minimal pushback. Hospitals, physicians and pharma all gain from higher prices. Insurers have little reason to push for lower net prices if payers focus on discounts from make-believe list prices. Benefits consultants paid a percentage of premiums or bonuses for steering clients to the same insurer earn more as costs climb. The only people who gain nothing are the ones paying the bill: workers.

Employers are not only stewards of their workers’ health care dollars, but they also have a fiduciary responsibility to run health plans in the best interests of their employees. It is time they took that role seriously.
