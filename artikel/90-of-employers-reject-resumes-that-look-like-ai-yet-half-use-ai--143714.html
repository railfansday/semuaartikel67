90% of employers reject resumes that look like AI – yet half use AI to interview

Sources indicate that hiring managers are more suspicious than ever of AI use in applications, and it’s impacting who they consider. At the same time, they’re turning to AI to help screen more candidates — meaning those videos you’ve seen on the internet of people pranking AI interviewers aren’t going away anytime soon.

Latest data from hiring systems firm Greenhouse found that “nine in ten US employers (90%) believe they’ve turned away real candidates because those applications seemed fake or AI-generated.” Greenhouse clarified that it doesn’t know whether employers are putting applications through AI detection tools or not.

“The survey data is self-reported, so it reflects what employers believe has happened, not a measured outcome,” Greenhouse’s Head of Voice AI Ophir Samson told ZDNET via email.

In a recent development, employers are also reacting to AI by adjusting their hiring processes: 86% of those surveyed reported changes to account for AI’s impact. 57% of them did so by adding another human interview.

It has been revealed that “As jobseekers rely more on AI to polish and send out their job applications, employers are finding it harder to tell who’s actually right for the role,” the report stated, echoing an ongoing push-and-pull between applicants and hiring teams. AI resume screeners automatically toss a majority of applications they deem unfit, so applicants try not to get tossed by formatting their material for the screener, which then backfires anyway.

The report indicates how a tough economy and an influx of AI hiring tools are colliding, arguably to benefit employers over applicants. Greenhouse noted that, ironically, AI tools are helping hiring managers while also making resumes untrustworthy, and applicants would rather be interviewed by AI than not at all.

“A growing number of employers are using AI not just to filter candidates out, but to have conversations with more candidates, earlier, through AI-powered voice technology,” Greenhouse said, adding that “half now use AI interviews to hear from more candidates, and 40% of those have found strong candidates they would have missed.”.

Recent findings suggest that greenhouse itself offers a voice AI screening interview tool. Samson told ZDNET that an HR professional ran 200 candidates she’d rejected through Greenhouse’s tool, and it suggested 19.

“All five of the finalists came from that group, and she ultimately hired one from the group,” Samson said.

Sources indicate that what we don’t, and maybe can’t, know is the inverse: whether AI screeners influence hiring managers to turn away candidates they actually should have considered. At least in Greenhouse’s case, Samson remarked Voice AI doesn’t make hiring decisions or “automatically advance, reject, or rank candidates.”.

“Recruiters review every output, and one recruiter owns the outcome and can explain how they reached it,” he said.

Greenhouse’s report surveyed 1,347 hiring managers and recruiters across the US, UK, Germany, Australia, and Ireland from late September to mid-October, and found that half of employers using voice AI “say the early-stage candidate experience has improved.”.

It’s unclear what “improved” means there, though, and whether applicants themselves would agree with that. Still, when given the choice, only 7% of applicants in the US would turn down an AI interview, per an earlier Greenhouse report. Additionally, “70% of those who’ve had a live AI interview say it helped them show their skills,” that report mentioned.

In a recent development, but how swayed is that by the state of the US job market? October’s job report was better than expected, but it’s still been a tough year overall for job-seekers. Greenhouse’s findings could be as much a reflection of how desperate applicants are as an endorsement of or comfort with AI screenings.

Sources indicate that “The job market could be a factor. Our Future of Hiring Candidate Report indicates 81% of job seekers were rejected from a job in the past year without speaking to anyone at the tech giant, human or AI,” Samson said. “But I’d be careful about reading the data as desperation. Candidates are setting clear conditions: nearly half (49%) want to know if a human will review their responses and 40% want to know how they’ll be evaluated.”.

Samson said that a software engineer named Ryan Quinlan, who was hired at Blissway, initially thought “talking to an AI” sounded stranger, but that it ultimately “felt like a conversation” that let him mention skills he’d forgotten to include on his resume. Samson said that Ryan has been “a ‘maybe’ on paper,” but that the AI interview helped him get over the line to be considered.

In a recent development, “For most applicants, a human conversation was never on the table,” Samson said. “The real choice is a resume lost in a pile, or 15 minutes to tell their story.”.

Greenhouse found that while employers are using AI to screen more candidates, most are maintaining human oversight over the most key decisions in the hiring process.

“Of all the tasks employers would hand to an AI agent, deciding which candidates to advance came last, at just 7%,” Greenhouse stated.

It has been revealed that A recent California law aims to protect employees from being fired by AI systems that make determinations about their performance without a human co-signing the assessment. As with many AI productivity tools in the workplace, proper adoption ideally equates to more time for human workers to handle more vital tasks.

“Of employers who’ve saved time with AI, 85% put it back into candidates, most often by spending more time with shortlisted candidates (45%) and interviewing more candidates earlier (40%),” Greenhouse mentioned.

The main question around AI interviewers remains whether they’re correctly capturing your interview the way you’d hope a human interviewer would (though that’s still deeply subjective and out of applicants’ control anyway).

Recent findings suggest that “When AI interviews are implemented thoughtfully and transparently, they pay off for both candidates and recruiters,” Samson noted. “Every candidate who uses our Voice AI can opt out, and about 90% rate the experience as excellent.”.
