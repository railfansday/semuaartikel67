According to recent reports, life presents many reasons to worry and fret, but there is one that universally affects everyone. An inevitable aspect of life is that it will end, a reality that you face from the moment you’re old enough to realize death’s universality. The question is how much fear over this inevitability will interfere with your ability to appreciate what your life now has to offer.

An area of research in psychology that goes back several decades tackles this fear head-on. Death anxiety, as it is called, affects everyone, but to varying degrees. Why some people become obsessed with their mortality and others remain more placid over the whole matter is an concern at the heart of this field. Research and theories devoted to the understanding of death anxiety can provide insight into how to transform those who are obsessed with this fear into those who can approach their mortality with greater acceptance. A new paper by Holy Spirit of Kaslik (USEK)’s Ruth Younes and colleagues (2026) lays out the prominent theories of death anxiety, theories that can provide a guide to intervention.

Industry observers note that one theory receiving the greatest empirical support is terror management theory (TMT), whose name says it all. We fear death, the theory proposes, because we live in “terror” of our mortality. However, we can’t live our lives continually in terror, so instead we “manage” the fear by suppressing it. Once in a while, though, that suppression stops working, and we face mortality head-on.

It has been revealed that A second theory has its origins in Erikson’s psychosocial approach to lifespan development. Erikson proposed that at any time in life, but especially in later adulthood, we take what Younes et al. call a “retrospective look-back on life.” If people are able to look back not in anger, but with satisfaction, they can cope more successfully with mortality than if they believe they failed. “Ego integrity,” the positive resolution of this problem, means that you view life, and the inevitability of death, with acceptance. “Despair,” the opposite, means that you see yourself as a failure and wish you could have a do-over to fix the mistakes you made when you were younger.

Sources indicate that the third theory relevant to death anxiety is called gerotranscendence, and proposes that aging allows people to experience “transformative” shifts in their outlook on life. This occurs through the Cosmic dimension (deepening connection with the universe), the Self (unity and cohesion within the self), and Social/Personal relationship dimension (need for more selectiveness in social interactions). By developing these three qualities, individuals should experience less fear of death.

According to recent reports, developed as an approach based on Erikson’s theory, reminiscence therapy uses the life review as its main tool. In life review, you return in time to the main events of your life and rework them from your present vantage point. Perhaps you jilted a lover who could have become your life partner by carelessly breaking things off over a silly argument. In retrospect, you ponder why you hurt this person so badly through this insensitivity. You can’t repair the damage, but you can gain perspective from your life as it unfolded since then. This breakup helped you learn to be kinder and to appreciate the importance of healing within close relationships.

According to recent reports, in this approach to therapy, people also relive their past, but they do so in an attempt to form an integrated “narrative” or story of their lives. Instead of reworking the past as in reminiscence therapy, you attempt to come back into awareness of it. As you do so, your life takes on shape and meaning. For example, you might try to prompt your recall of such events as the birth of your children and their early development up to the present. This allows you to piece back together events that you may have forgotten simply because your life was so busy at the time, balancing work and family demands.

Sources indicate that instead of rehashing the lows and highs of past events in your life, you might try instead to focus on the highs. This could be useful if reminiscence proves too painful. By starting with the positive, you can eventually confront the negative. Regarding yourself as someone who made good decisions in life, you may then approach the times you didn’t with greater self-compassion.

According to recent reports, this might not be something you should “try at home,” but insights from the way that psychodynamic theory works could be useful. Younes et al. mention, for example, the “grieving” process that people experience as they think about the end of their lives. Research on end-of-life complications suggests that people engage in anticipatory grief about their own lives as a way to come to greater acceptance. If you run from this form of grief, as TMT might suggest, it could come back to haunt you when you come much closer to your life’s end.

5. Acceptance and Commitment Therapy.

This is a form of therapy used for a number of psychological disorders in which an individual learns to accept distressing events and pain without avoidance. The “commitment” piece of this involves setting and acting toward your goals. Rather than run away from thoughts of death, you would therefore confront them and reflect on how you can live your life according to your values.

6. Dialectical Behavior Therapy.

An approach used in the treatment of people with borderline personality disorder (invented by the late Marsha Linehan), this therapy involves accepting your feelings and behaviors while also trying to change your behaviors. Again, you may not be able to do this on your own as a formal therapy, but the idea of rethinking your feelings and behaviors is very doable.

Recent findings suggest that each of the six ways of addressing death anxiety takes a slightly different slant on the processes of reflecting back on your life, reworking some old issues, and tackling fears of death through acceptance. The authors suggest that people vary in which approach works best for them.

Sources indicate that additional factors from the study that might influence the way you go about trying these yourself or with someone close to you include your age and health. A health scare, such as an acute cardiac event or bad news from a screening test, can set anyone off, regardless of age. On the other hand, social support can help you get through these and other tough times as you confront your feelings about mortality.

To sum up, death anxiety stems from the inevitability of our own mortality, but it need not be an inevitable feature of your mental health. Being able to recognize and work through these feelings can prepare you for whatever lies ahead.
