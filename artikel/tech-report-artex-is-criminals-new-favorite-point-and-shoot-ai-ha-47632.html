Tech Report: Artex Is Criminals’ New Favorite ‘Point-and-Shoot’ AI Hacking Tool

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Over the past year, security researchers have warned that AI agents could vastly speed up cybercriminals’ ability to launch cyberattacks, breach companies, and ultimately steal money, data, or sensitive information from those compromised systems. For at least one subset of hackers, it appears that era has now arrived.

Since around the end of August, multiple companies, government agencies, and banks around the world have been targeted by cybercriminals adopting a upcoming wave of AI-powered penetration testing tools. These setups​​—many of which mimic legitimate defensive penetration testing techniques—use LLMs to autonomously scan potential hacking targets and attempt to force their way into poorly secured networks, then carry out attack looks written by human operators.

Recent findings suggest that dozens of these AI penetration frameworks or “harnesses,” which detail how an AI model should carry out cybersecurity testing or attacks, are increasingly being linked to real-world attacks. The tools do not introduce upcoming ways to attack websites or servers and are not sophisticated, according to alerts from around half a dozen security companies. Instead, they allow cybercriminals to launch attacks at speeds that would be impossible for humans to do.

Hackers also don’t need to be skilled to use the tools, researchers say, and attacks that would have taken days are completed in just hours. “Agentic frameworks have progressed from experimental use to sustained, multi-objective offensive operations that run with minimal human intervention,” CJ Moses, chief information security officer of Amazon Integrated Security, wrote in a tech giant blog post recently.

In a recent development, the most prominent of these tools is Artex. Written in the programming language Go, the open-source framework was first published on GitHub in July by a pseudonymous developer using the handle Autumn-27, and it’s been spreading across some hacker communities ever since. Artex has been used across all parts of attacks, from conducting initial analysis of networks and gaining access to moving within systems and preparing data to be exported.

In a recent development, “It’s really an all-in-one product,” says Jacob Larsen, an offensive security researcher and founder of security firm Cypen. “Previous iterations of AI agent pen-testing tools would either only do reconnaissance—like the scanning and analysis [of targets]—and some would only do testing. This is really a point-and-shoot weapon.”.

According to recent reports, as with other AI hacking frameworks, Artex isn’t an AI product itself. Instead, it is essentially a guide that points external frontier and open-source AI models—that can be connected to it via APIs—to a series of existing penetration testing tools and processes. People using Artex or other similar setups can give simple, natural-language prompts to begin their operations. “Launch a penetration attack on [Redacted victim] to gain access to the site’s admin panel, database, or server,” one set of hackers prompted Artex, according to researchers at the security firm Check Point.

It has been revealed that once the instructions have been issued, multiple AI agents then carry them out. One agent will break down the intends into a series of tasks, others will try to perform these actions, and the system will then present its findings. Safety guardrails in the underlying AI models, particularly those from frontier labs, can stop hacking activity when they detect it. Increasingly, however, free Chinese and abliterated models are getting better at breaking into systems.

Sources indicate that “It's fast; it’s efficient,” Guillaume Valadon, a researcher at security firm GitGuardian, says of Artex. Valadon and others say the simplicity of the tools makes it easier for unskilled wannabe hackers to try to enter the market—even if they have no clue about what they’re doing. “It lowers the bar,” Valadon says. “I would say my grandmother can do it, except that she doesn't have a laptop, but the bar is that low.”.

Recent findings suggest that as a result, a flurry of reports from cybersecurity companies have linked Artex use to attempted and successful hacks—often by Chinese-speaking cybercriminals trying to make money. These include the theft of personal information from thousands of South Korean financial institutions, potential cryptocurrency theft, and hunting for exposed login details on GitHub.

“A variety of malicious cyber actors have used Artex to conduct campaigns targeting organizations globally across multiple sectors, including financial services, government, technology, defense, semiconductor, energy, health care, manufacturing, telecommunications, and legal industries,” the FBI warned in an alert about Artex last week.

In October, China-linked hackers known as Vapor Panda used Artex to target more than 200 organizations, including think tanks, semiconductor and defense firms in the US, and government foreign ministries in Europe and elsewhere, according to security firm CrowdStrike. Microsoft spotted Chinese hackers that it tracks as Storm-0940 using Artex to try to sign in to Microsoft 365 setups following a supply chain attack. It took hackers less than three hours to gain privileged access to systems.

On October 8, the Autumn-27 developer removed Artex from GitHub, stating in a comment that they are “no way associated with these malicious attacks” and that the tools were developed for “educational and research purposes.” An email WIRED sent to an address linked to the developer did not receive a response.

Recent findings suggest that despite the developer pulling Artex, multiple versions of the framework have been copied and continue to be developed and used, alongside other similar tools. Valadon states GitGuardian has documented 45 different AI attack frameworks so far, with CyberstrikeAI, Hexstrike, CAIRN, and PentAGI being frequently mentioned by researchers. They all work in similar ways, with some slightly different technical capabilities. One silver lining for people defending against attacks using such tools is that they’re currently easy to spot.

“These tools are not stealthy right now,” says Silas Cutler, principal security researcher at internet security firm Censys. Many of the attacks create a lot of noise in systems—making thousands of probes in quick succession. For instance, agentic coding platform Dosu stated Artex was used to send 80,000 requests to its systems in just 90 minutes. Researchers say that basic security fundamentals—patching software, not using default credentials or exposing secrets, and monitoring for unusual network activity—can help defend against many of the approaches from those using Artex.

Recent findings suggest that pinpointing the number of cybercriminals using Artex and other frameworks isn’t easy; however, thousands of instances of the systems being set up have been exposed to the internet, according to analysis by Censys and the Shadowserver Foundation. Perhaps hinting at the sophistication of those using Artex, researchers have found multiple instances of the tool left unsecured and fully open to the internet, allowing them to inspect how operators are using it.

Recent findings suggest that last week, Cutler says, two exposed Artex servers appeared to show Chinese native speakers targeting other Chinese entities. “In one of the cases, it was a university that was being targeted with a password spraying attack, and it has all of that vibe of a student trying to change their grades,” he says.

Industry observers note that “There is a level of this activity that’s occurring that is likely organized, sophisticated, criminal activity,” Cutler states. “There is also, in parallel, likely a subset of the activity that falls more into that juvenile crime realm.”.
