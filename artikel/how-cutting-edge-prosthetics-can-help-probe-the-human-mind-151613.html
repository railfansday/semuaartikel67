How cutting-edge prosthetics can help probe the human mind

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Over the last few weeks, I’ve been talking to Alexander (Avi) Davidson. There are a lot of remarkable things about Avi. He sustained a horrific injury as a teenager, which left him paralyzed and without a left hand. Today, he uses his own experience to guide his practice as a trauma therapist.

He’s also taking part in a clinical trial of some upcoming tech that allows him to move—and feel with—a prosthetic hand. Unlike standard prosthetics, this one connects directly to nerves in Avi's arm. He literally has wires coming out of his arm to plug it in with.

Avi’s the ninth person to trial this tech. But he’s the first to take it home and use it independently, for things like drinking from a cup (which is not easy) and writing (also not easy).

What struck me the most over our conversations was Avi’s outlook and approach to not just his injuries and learning to use this cutting-edge technology, but to life itself.

Sources indicate that he has learned how to use something like seven different prosthetics in the 17 years since his accident. By taking part in this trial, he’s had to fit hundreds of hours of lab-based training and physical therapy around his demanding job. He had surgery to implant electrodes in his arm and will eventually need another to remove them. And he won’t even get to keep the latest prosthetic long term.

“While it’s unfortunate at times, and irritating at times, I do not regret what has come from [the accident], or the fact that it happened,” he told me. “I am not going to benefit from this … I wanted to be a part of this in the context of being able to help push the technology forward.”.

The research team at the University of Utah that is running the trial hopes it will answer longstanding questions about the technology. How does it fare in a real-world setting, outside of the lab? What do people end up using it for? Where can improvements be made?

Sources indicate that there may be some surprises along the way. Avi has found that his prosthetic wrist can feel sore and stiff when it’s been overused, just like his flesh-and-blood wrist. Weirdly, that feeling can be relieved by stretching it out. What’s going on here? No one really knows.

Scientists don’t know whether Avi’s brain will change as a result of using the bionic hand, either. Before the trial started, they performed brain scans to see how his missing left hand was represented in his brain. Will that change? And what might that mean for people who are born without limbs—how might a bionic limb affect their brains?

Avi will have the device until late January 2027 at the least, and potentially for another year. After that? Again, no one knows. It will depend on how the research is progressing, how Avi feels, and how much funding the team can secure. As a result, it’s not clear how long the device might even be expected to last in a person’s body.

I’ll be following this study, though, and will keep you posted.
