Don Was Pays Tribute to Bobby Weir by Reimagining a Classic Dead Album

When Blues for Allah was recorded by the Grateful Dead at Bob Weir’s home studio in 1975, the band had been looking far to the East, incorporating North African melodies and instrumentation to accompany lyricist Robert Hunter’s conceptual imagery. Some 50 years later, noted producer and Blue Note Records President Don Was only had to travel to the Midwest to interpret the songs of the seminal album (fan favorites “The Music Never Stopped,” “Help on the Way/Slipknot,” and “Franklin’s Tower” among them), linking with jazz trumpeter and composer Terence Blanchard for a one-off show for his curated Paradise Jazz Series at the Detroit Symphony Orchestra.

Industry observers note that the idea of revisiting Blues for Allah was prompted by Laina Cohen, the Golden Road Festival founder who suggested Was and his band perform the album at the 2025 gathering in Buford, Georgia, to celebrate the LP’s golden anniversary. After years of playing with Bobby Weir and the Wolf Bros, Was was the ideal candidate to reinterpret the album with elements of blues and jazz and a Detroit swing. The only problem was he didn’t have his own band. So, Was set forth to form one.

“I called people who I knew were awesome musicians, but I also knew we all grew up listening to the same radio stations and playing in the same bars and playing for the same tough audiences,” he says. “They’re open and receptive and as enthusiastic as you can be.” Dubbed “the Pan Detroit-Ensemble,”  the group, featuring saxophonist and Blue Note Records artist Dave McMurray, keyboardist Luis Resto, trombonist Vincent Chandler, and vocalist Steffanie Christi’an, proved to be the perfect vehicle.

According to recent reports, still, confronting the odd time signatures and elaborate instrumentation of the album left Was stumped. “‘King Solomon’s Marbles’ was one that really required some work,” he notes. “I always thought it was just a jam, and I thought the reason it didn’t get played was because it was just something that happened once. According to recent reports, was worried the fanbase wouldn’t respond positively. “I was [concerned] that we’d gone too far for some fans, that it was sacred ground, but it wasn’t the case at all,” he states. “No one ever had a problem with adapting it because they could tell it was done in service to the song.”.

“Crazy Fingers” was another tough one for Was and his band. “We used to play that in Wolf Bros, and it’s sort of reggae, sort of Irving Berlin. It’s a very weird feeling, and if you don’t get it exactly right, it falls flat,” he notes. “It didn’t come naturally to nine people from Detroit. So what did come naturally was to turn it into a six-eight thing, like Otis Redding’s ‘Dreams to Remember.’”.

Sources indicate that was started working on this project before Weir’s death last January, and played some of the music for his longtime friend and bandmate. “He dug it. And my guess is that had he survived a year, he’d have come out and played some,” Was indicates. “He heard a little rehearsal thing, and he’d seen YouTube stuff of  ‘Shakedown Street’ and ‘Loser’ in our very first reveals, and he loved it. He was very proactive about making sure that the music would outlive all of us, and he really welcomed that.”.

In a recent development, as fate would have it, the first date of Was’ Blues for Allah tour fell on the day Weir died. “We were in Ann Arbor, Michigan, and I had to go out and tell them. I didn’t feel like doing it, but I remembered Bobby talking a lot about the night Jerry died, and they were in New Hampshire, and they went out and played. He said, ‘You bring comfort to people by playing good music for them.’ So we played the songs, and then I went out and sat with the audience for a while, and we talked about Bobby, and everyone had stories to tell. I found it incredibly comforting. According to recent reports, bobby’s presence is still felt. As Was shares, “The weird thing is that I don’t feel like he’s gone. He’s ubiquitous, man. You turn on SiriusXM radio anytime and he’s on, and the music’s everywhere, and we’re out playing the music. When he talked about Jerry, he would say that he could feel like Jerry was kind of on his shoulder. I thought he was being hyperbolic, but I liked the image of it. It’s actually how I feel. I kind of got him in there when I’m playing, because I’m used to playing these songs with him, and playing in the Wolf Bros changed the way I played in a big way.”.

And like the Dead, the musical experimentation continues: Was’ next project is the Dead’s prog epic Terrapin Station, coinciding with the album’s 50th anniversary in 2027.
