Bitcoin Miners Go 98 Minutes Without Finding a Single Block

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that bitcoin miners experienced their longest dry spell since July on Nov. 26. According to Galaxy's Alex Thorn, it took Bitcoin miners 98 minutes and 19 seconds to find the next block.

That's the third time in 2026 that miners have gone this long without finding a block. The wait ended at 18:28 UTC on Thursday.

For anyone trying to get Bitcoin transactions confirmed shortly after the previous block, the gap meant waiting more than an hour and a half.

Even if you paid a very high transaction fee, you would still have to wait until a miner found the next block before your transaction could receive its first confirmation.

Sources indicate that thursday's gap was the 69th longest since 2011, according to Galaxy Research. It was just three minutes shy of the threshold for the top 50.

Industry observers note that and this year's other long gap lasted nearly two hours. On July 6, miners took 112 minutes and 26 seconds to find the next block (number 956,953). That was the 19th longest gap on record, according to Galaxy.

Bitcoin automatically adjusts mining difficulty every 2,016 blocks to keep the average block interval at around 10 minutes.

Sources indicate that however,  blocks sometimes are found seconds apart. Other times, it takes well over an hour for the next block to be found.

At any given mining difficulty, the chance that a single attempt to find a block will succeed is incredibly small.

If miners have spent 90 minutes trying to find the next block, the chance of succeeding on the next attempt is the same as it was on the first attempt.

So the expected time until the next block is found is still 10 minutes, even if it's already been 90 minutes since the last block was found. That's assuming the network's total computing power and mining difficulty remain unchanged.

Assuming an average block interval of 10 minutes, Thorn noted you could expect a gap of at least 98 minutes and 19 seconds about once every 18,600 block intervals.

That means such a gap should occur roughly once every four months, based on an average of about 144 blocks per day.

Galaxy examined 877,290 block intervals going back to Jan. 1, 2011.

Researchers identified 1,948 intervals where at least an hour had passed between the timestamps of successive blocks.

However, Galaxy flagged 97 of those intervals because an adjacent block had a timestamp that was off by at least 10 minutes. This raised questions about the actual length of those gaps.

The longest recorded interval is particularly interesting.

Sources indicate that on Nov. 30, 2011, block 155,290 seemingly marked the end of a 144-minute-and-50-second gap.

However, the miner who found the next block had set its timestamp back by 65 minutes.

After excluding apparent timestamp errors, the longest gap was 141 minutes and one second, recorded on Oct. 13, 2011.
