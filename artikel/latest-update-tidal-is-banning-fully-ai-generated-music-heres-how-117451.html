Latest Update: Tidal is banning fully AI-generated music. Heres how other streaming services compare.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Music streaming services add thousands of fresh songs every day, and a growing share of them are made entirely with AI. Some apps have started labeling those tracks or keeping them out of recommendations.

In a recent development, starting Dec. 9, Tidal will begin to remove all fully AI-generated songs from its catalog and block fresh uploads. Robert Andersen, who leads Tidal at parent manufacturer Block, expects about 29 million tracks to come down by the start of 2027.

"We are the first DSP to do so, and we encourage our peers to follow suit. TIDAL will continue to make decisions that support real artists first," Andersen wrote in a post on X. (DSP stands for digital service provider, which is a platform that streams or sells music — like Spotify, Apple Music, or Deezer.).

Industry observers note that tidal started labeling tracks as "wholly" AI-generated and cutting off royalties back in July. It also moved to take down songs linked to fraud, including artist impersonation. But, according to the service, the uploads kept coming. Tidal flagged 43 percent of the tracks it received over the past 90 days as fully AI-generated, and Andersen estimates the service will turn away 80,000 to 85,000 tracks a day once the ban takes effect.

It has been revealed that most of those songs go unheard (thankfully). Tidal has already tagged about 27 million tracks as fully AI-generated, roughly 6.3 percent of its catalog. The tech giant notes 96 percent of those tracks went unplayed over the past three months.

Some listeners treated the ban as a reason to stick with Tidal or switch to it. "I’m genuinely so happy to have switched from Spotify to this," one commenter wrote in the subreddit, r/TIdaL. On X, another tagged Spotify and asked, "yours when?".

Other replies zeroed in on the word "wholly." One user joked that AI artists would start hiring someone "just to clap on beat." Another asked, "so 1 real cough in the intro and it’s allowed?" Behind the jokes was a practical question for Tidal: how much human input makes a recording AI-assisted?

Recent findings suggest that even some people who liked the policy wondered how Tidal would enforce it. "Fair play to Tidal, no idea how they’re going to actually catch it all though," wrote one X user. That's the challenge facing the service once it starts deciding which tracks can stay and which come down.

Apple Music is relying on labels and distributors to disclose how AI was used. Its current delivery specifications include tags for cases where AI generated a significant part of a recording, composition, music video, or artwork. A separate tag covers AI-generated artist personas that could pass for real people. In August, Apple told its music partners that the disclosures would become mandatory and that listeners would see a "Made With AI" label later in the year. (What gets labeled depends on what labels and distributors report when they submit the music.).

It has been revealed that deezer uses its own detection tool to label tracks that are fully AI-generated and keep them out of algorithmic recommendations and editorial playlists. In July, the company also stated it would take down AI tracks used to rack up fraudulent streams or left unplayed for at least six months. Other AI tracks can still turn up in searches, but Deezer notes it aims to give listeners a switch to hide them by the end of the year. The company says it receives nearly 90,000 fully AI-generated tracks a day.

According to recent reports, bandcamp put a broader ban in place in January. Its policy covers music and audio made entirely or in substantial part with generative AI, and it also prohibits the use of AI to impersonate artists or their styles.
