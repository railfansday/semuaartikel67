Can This Supplement Really Fight Inflammation? Here's What Doctors Say.

Sources indicate that IS THERE ANYTHING NAC can’t do? Well, if you listen to the supplement industry, no, not really.

N-Acetylcysteine, or NAC, in its supplement form, has managed to accumulate a large number of alleged benefits in recent years. Depending on who you listen to, NAC supplements can allegedly boost your immune system, detoxify your body, improve cognitive function, increase energy levels, slow aging, increase fertility, reduce inflammation, prevent respiratory infections, and decrease oxidative stress.

That’s a whole lot of responsibility to place on one single supplement.

Sources indicate that add that to the fact that NAC is a proven medical treatment for a couple of specific diagnoses, and you have the perfect setup. Supplement companies can extrapolate evidence into claims that go way beyond current scientific evidence. That’s been a blessing and a curse for NAC manufacturers—and a legal headache that the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) just made a major move to solve with the proposed creation of a latest regulatory category just for NAC.

It has been revealed that the legal maneuvering is complicated and, if finalized, would allow NAC to be considered a supplement but still a medical treatment which technically isn’t allowed—yet. Basically, there’s a lot to unpack here.

In a recent development, so what can NAC supplement do? And what even is NAC? Is it safe? Let’s break it all down.

Industry observers note that NAC IS A sulfur-containing compound, a precursor of the amino acid L-cysteine. In medicinal or supplement form, NAC is converted directly into cysteine, which is then used to make and deliver glutathione, a powerful antioxidant that the body uses to scavenge free radicals and manage oxidative stress. (Yes, the body is a complex system.).

While NAC isn’t found in food, cysteine is readily available in animal proteins, legumes, dairy, nuts and seeds, and some vegetables. Compared to dietary cysteine, NAC supplements are a faster, more stable way of increasing glutathione levels.

Recent findings suggest that NAC supplements are available in powders, capsules, liquids, and tablets. And while NAC has legitimate medical uses, it’s not officially FDA-approved as a supplement as of this second. Of course, this hasn’t stopped the supplement industry from selling it—or influencers from promoting it.

NAC has been in a weird legal gray area for years. Because it’s an actual medical treatment, it technically can’t be classified as a supplement, even though it’s often presented that way.

The FDA’s proposed rule would classify NAC as the first-ever “Dietary Ingredient Not Excluded from the Dietary Supplement Definition.” Basically, it will be allowed to span the categories of supplement and medication, which has never been done before. If finalized, this ruling would “provide for the lawful use of certain ingredients in dietary supplements that would otherwise be excluded from the dietary supplement definition,” per an FDA constituent update.

The proposed rule would allow products that contain NAC to be lawfully marketed as dietary supplements, as long as they’re not otherwise in violation of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act). In theory, this may eventually apply to other supplement-ish ingredients; Right now, this is centered around NAC.

The proposed rule is open for public comment for 60 days. It’s not clear when the FDA when issue a final rule.

Sources indicate that this won’t really impact the general public if it’s finalized. Instead, it clears up potential legal complications surrounding NAC. “This will more formally allow sales of NAC over the counter, without a prescription,” says Jamie Alan, PharmD, PhD, a professor of pharmacology and toxicology at Michigan State University in East Lansing.

It has been revealed that while some members of the MAHA movement have pushed for this, Alan states it’s also a case of “well, why not.” She explains, “It is generally safe, and I agree with that assessment. It is also being sold as a supplement already so, why not?”.

Alan stresses that this move doesn’t suddenly legitimize all NAC supplements or claims surrounding them. “If you are buying a supplement, you aren’t necessarily guaranteed that the bottle contains what it says it does,” she says. “There really isn’t evidence yet in my opinion that supplementing with NAC as a dietary supplement will be life-changing for the vast majority of people.”.

Industry observers note that NAC is the standard treatment for acetaminophen (Tylenol) overdose. High levels of acetaminophen deplete the liver of glutathione, which is required for the body’s detox pathways. If you’re curious about the geeky scientific mechanism: Quickly repleting glutathione by way of an intravenous NAC infusion helps clear the excess metabolites from the overdose and prevent liver damage.

According to recent reports, NAC is also a mucolytic, meaning it can break down thick mucus in the respiratory system. (Yum.) While recent research found that NAC may help with with COPD and chronic bronchitis feel less worse, more investigation is needed to clearly establish effects and benefits.

It has been revealed that WELL, NOT ALL that much. Other scientific trials have looked at NAC’s effects on conditions including mood and compulsive disorders, Alzheimer’s, Schizophrenia, and chronic pain. Overall, results have been mixed and largely unremarkable. For most of these conditions and disorders, larger-scale clinical trials are still needed.

So while it’s true that research does exist on NAC and wider health benefits, most of the fresh research trials examining NAC’s effects use doses that are far higher than what the average person would take, and in forms that are not available to the public.

This hasn’t stopped wellness influencers from broadcasting that NAC supplements can help heal whatever ails you.

Sources indicate that many of these influencers argue that because glutathione may reduce oxidative stress, NAC is amazing for longevity and anti-aging. It’s simple, they argue: Because oxidative stress is associated with aging, increasing glutathione with NAC supplements slows biological aging.

Except raising levels of glutathione in the body does not necessarily translate into a desired outcome. Science agrees: There is no long-term human evidence that showing that this suppresses oxidative stress to the level of having a clinically meaningful outcome. Taking NAC for “healthy aging” is more marketing than anything else at this point.

According to recent reports, FOR MOST HEALTHY people, NAC supplements appear to be safe in doses between 600 milligrams (mg) to 1800mg per day. However, doses on the higher end of that range may have a higher risk of side effects such as gastrointestinal upset.

NAC is not recommended for people with clotting disorders. There are also some potential drug interactions between NAC and immunosuppressants, so it’s always a good idea to get a doctor or pharmacist to cross-reference any supplements with the medications you’re taking.

In a recent development, again, remember that the supplement industry is largely unregulated. Always buy from reputable companies, and look for products that are third-party tested to ensure that what’s on the label is actually what’s in the bottle.

NAC has some legitimate—and even life-saving—medical uses, and research is constantly evolving. But what the supplements don’t have at this point, is compelling human evidence showing that NAC has any clinically meaningful effects in healthy people.
