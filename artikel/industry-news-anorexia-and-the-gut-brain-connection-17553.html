Sources indicate that for generations, scientists and doctors believed the brain was the unquestioned command center of the body. It sent instructions and the rest of the body obeyed. Today, we know the relationship is far more complicated.

Recent findings suggest that your digestive tract is home to an intricate network of more than 100 million nerve cells—so many, in fact, that scientists often refer to it as the body's second brain. Known as the enteric nervous system, this remarkable network coordinates digestion, communicates with the immune system, influences hormone production, and remains in constant contact with the brain inside your skull.

These two "brains" are linked by an elaborate communication system known as the gut-brain axis. Messages travel in both directions, carried by nerves, hormones, immune cells, and chemical messengers produced by the trillions of bacteria that live within the intestines. It's a constant conversation—one that helps regulate not only digestion, but also mood, stress, appetite, memory, and behavior.

This discovery has transformed the way we think about both physical and mental health.

For decades, psychiatrists understandably focused almost exclusively on the brain. Depression, anxiety, obsessive-compulsive disorder, ADHD, and anorexia nervosa were viewed as disorders of brain chemistry. But over the past 20 years, an explosion of research has revealed that the gut is an active participant in all of these conditions. Rather than functioning as separate organs, the gut and the brain operate as partners. When one suffers, the other often does, too.

That insight is especially important in anorexia nervosa.

For people with anorexia nervosa, this two-way conversation has profound implications. Recovery isn’t only about restoring body weight or correcting abnormalities in the brain. It’s also about rebuilding a healthy digestive system capable of supporting the brain’s healing processes.

One of the cruelest aspects of anorexia nervosa is that the illness often convinces people that food and eating are problems.

According to recent reports, as recovery begins and eating increases, many patients experience bloating, constipation, abdominal pain, nausea, and a feeling of fullness after only a few bites. These symptoms can be frightening. They seem to confirm the eating disorder's central lie: See? Eating makes you feel worse.

Sources indicate that but that's not what's happening. In most cases, these digestive symptoms are not caused by eating. They are caused by starvation.

It has been revealed that when the body is deprived of adequate nourishment for weeks, months, or years, it begins conserving energy wherever it can. Digestion slows dramatically. The muscles that normally move food through the stomach and intestines become less active. The stomach empties more slowly, intestinal motility decreases, and constipation becomes common. Food remains in the digestive tract longer than it should, producing bloating, abdominal discomfort, and an uncomfortable sense of fullness after meals.

Ironically, the very symptoms that make eating difficult are often the result of not eating enough in the first place.

According to recent reports, understanding this dynamic can be enormously reassuring as it highlights that the digestive system has not been permanently damaged. The symptoms are signs of a body adapting to prolonged starvation and so, as nutritional rehabilitation progresses and the gut begins receiving the nourishment it needs, digestive function usually improves—although it often takes weeks or months for the gastrointestinal tract to recover fully.

In anorexia, another notable change occurs deep within the intestines, where trillions of bacteria collectively known as the gut microbiome reside. These microorganisms help digest food, produce vitamins, regulate immune function, maintain the integrity of the intestinal lining, and manufacture compounds that influence the brain. Like every other living organism in the body, they depend on a steady supply of nutrients to thrive.

When food intake becomes severely restricted, then, the microbiome changes as well.

Over the past decade, researchers have consistently found that people with anorexia nervosa have a gut microbiome that differs significantly from those of healthy individuals. Some beneficial bacterial species decline, while others become more abundant. Overall microbial diversity—the hallmark of a healthy ecosystem—often decreases. These changes are now believed to contribute not only to digestive symptoms, but also to inflammation, anxiety, and difficulties with weight restoration.

The good news is that the gut is remarkably resilient. Unlike many organs, the digestive tract is constantly renewing itself. Cells lining the intestines are replaced every few days. The microbiome is continually adapting to changes in diet, medications, stress, and environment. Even after prolonged starvation, the digestive system has an extraordinary capacity for recovery—provided it receives the nourishment and support it needs.

Sources indicate that eating enough food. No supplement can substitute for dietary rehabilitation. Restoring adequate calories, protein, healthy fats, and food-delivered vitamins and minerals remains the cornerstone of recovery from anorexia nervosa.

Industry observers note that digestive enzymes. Digestive discomfort can make every meal feel like a battle. Bloating, cramping, constipation, and abdominal pain often reinforce the fear that eating is making things worse. Breaking that cycle sometimes requires giving the digestive system additional support while it heals. One of the simplest ways to do that is through digestive enzyme supplementation.

It has been revealed that digestive enzymes help break food into smaller molecules that can be more easily absorbed by the body. Although digestive enzyme supplements have not yet been studied in anorexia, decades of clinical experience suggest they can can substantially reduce gastrointestinal discomfort during nutritional rehabilitation.

Industry observers note that probiotics. Another important tool is the use of probiotics—live, beneficial microorganisms that help restore a healthier balance within the gut microbiome. For centuries, traditional cultures have consumed probiotic-rich foods like yogurt, kefir, kimchi, miso, and sauerkraut. Today, probiotic supplements provide carefully selected bacteria strains that can help replenish beneficial microbes disrupted by illness, antibiotics, stress, and prolonged food restrictions. Their companion nutrients, known as prebiotics, serve as food for these beneficial bacteria, helping them grow and flourish.
