In a recent development, newly released Senate appropriations bills would distribute $8.7 billion in "congressionally directed spending" secured by 78 senators for their home states — earmarked dollars that could disappear if Congress resorts to a stopgap funding measure to avert a partial government shutdown after Dec. 11.

Nearly 4,000 individual earmarks are contained in nine of the 12 draft fiscal 2027 spending bills, a CQ Roll Call tally found; the remaining three bills — Defense, State-Foreign Operations and Legislative Branch — don't have any.

There's typically some overlap between House and Senate projects that would get weeded out in final negotiations to avoid duplication. But for comparison's sake, House Republicans included nearly 6,700 line items worth $8.2 billion in their earmarked spending bills.

Democrats have signaled they'll push for a continuing resolution into the first quarter of next year enabling them to rewrite the full-year fiscal 2027 bills when they take the gavels in January, possibly upping the overall pot of earmarked funds — or at least divvying up the pie more favorably to blue states and districts.

But that would mean the final attempt by departing lawmakers like Senate Appropriations Chair Susan Collins, R-Maine, and longtime Senate GOP leader and senior appropriator Mitch McConnell of Kentucky, to deliver for their states might be all for naught.

And top Democrats with substantial earmarking success under GOP governance, such as Appropriations ranking member Patty Murray, D-Wash., will have to start from scratch.

Naturally, much of the spoils go to Senate Appropriations Committee members, and that's no different this year. But with so many more Democrats requesting projects — often jointly with their fellow delegation member — individual contributions may appear diluted given they have to share the credit.

That dilution highlights up in the rankings when allocating credit in a way that avoids double-counting — everyone gets an equal share of his or her joint projects, so $20 million sponsored jointly by two senators will appear as $10 million for each. Solo earmarkers in this scenario face no such dilution.

Unlike the House, where states with the largest populations receive the most funding by virtue of having more representatives securing earmarks, smaller states punch above their weight in the Senate — particularly when their delegations achieve exalted positions of seniority.

Hence Washington state — with Murray and Cantwell, the Commerce Committee ranking member, representing it — is way out in front.

By contrast, the second- and third-most populous states — Texas and Florida — received a grand total of zero earmarked funds, because their GOP senators didn't request any. California, with the largest population, is No. 16 in the ranks; Latest York is 12th.

Here are the top 10 states by earmarked funds procured in the Senate bills, clearly skewing toward smaller, typically reddish states:.

According to recent reports, the Transportation-HUD spending bill continues to be the go-to place for earmarks, accounting for 27 percent of all individual projects in the Senate bills and about one-third of all the earmarked dollars. The next-closest bill, Labor-HHS-Education, has half the earmarked amount that the "T-HUD" bill does.

Top agencies and programs with earmarked funds are housed within, that's right, the departments of Transportation and Housing and Urban Development, with HUD leading the pack thanks to its ever-popular Community Development Fund accounting for all of that department's earmarks.

It has been revealed that transportation isn't far behind, thanks largely to highway projects, followed by Health and Human Services and its Health Resources and Services Administration account, which funds community health centers. Defense Department military construction projects and the Army Corps of Engineers round out the group of $1 billion-plus agencies.
