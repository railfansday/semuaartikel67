A Study In Red, White and Blue: Sir Arthur Conan Doyle in the USA

When I was choosing the setting for my fresh book, The Mark of Moriarty, which re-imagines the titular Professor survived his last battle with Sherlock Holmes, I found myself genuinely spoiled for choice.

Recent findings suggest that holmes, for all his fantastical adventures, rarely travelled beyond London, glimpsing the wider world only when it came to his doorstep. Professor Moriarty, presumed dead post-Reichenbach and presumably still notorious in the UK, is in the exact opposite position.

He can go anywhere in the world except his homeland.

In a recent development, while I was scouring the globe for an interesting place to send the master criminal, I kept coming back to a recurring theme in Sir Arthur Conan Doyle’s books. Indeed, even as a child, reading the classic Holmes adventures, one thought kept jumping out at me….

“Wow, this guy really likes the USA.”.

As I grew up, I discovered just how true this is. Sir Arthur Conan Doyle’s enthusiasm with the United States was a matter of public record. He took great inspiration from this newfound nation, recovering from a civil war and forging its future before his eyes.

In the process of looking into this transatlantic fascination, I thought I’d share some weird and wonderful findings about Doyle’s brushes with the states, his encounters with citizens of that nation…  and one confrontation with a visitor from Atlantis.

It has been revealed that even before he set foot in the States, Doyle devoured news from America. In fact, factions and societies from the USA featured prominently throughout his work and across the Sherlock Holmes canon.

Sources indicate that when he was just twenty-one years old, Doyle anonymously published “An American’s Tale”, a short story in which an Englishman is accused of murdering a rival in Arizona. Taking the Englishman to “Flytrap Gulch” to hang him for his crime, the supposed victim is unexpectedly discovered inside… a giant man-eating plant.

One of Doyle’s more fantastical tales, it certainly paints the USA as a strange and undiscovered country.

As he embarked on the Holmes canon, Doyle still injected America into his stories, with a particular interest in the factions and societies across the pond.

His story, “The Five Orange Pips” features allusions to the infamous KKK. His final of the Sherlock Holmes novels, The Valley of Fear, was born out of his fascination with the Molly Maguires, a secret society that engaged in radical industrial action against Pennsylvanian mining companies, and the Pinkertons, the brutal detective agency who were hired to stop them.

Doyle’s enthusiasm for American goings-on did sometimes outpace his knowledge, however, particularly due to his reliance on secondary sources which filtered to the UK.

Holmes’ very first appearance, A Study In Scarlet, featured an organisation called the Danites, an avenging division of the Mormon Church’s mysterious “Council of Four”.

While the Danites certainly existed, historians consider them a short-lived faction based in mid-century Missouri, and far from the organised assassin squad from the book, with Doyle’s own daughter explaining that her father “would be the first to admit that his first Sherlock Holmes novel was full of errors about the Mormons”.

For the record, there’s no evidence that he believed in man-eating plants.

Eventually, shortly after Sherlock met his end at the Reichenbach Falls, Sir Arthur Conan Doyle finally visited America for the first time.

The Sherlock Holmes stories had proceeded him. He was met with an incredible reception from his already prominent U.S. fanbase, with the Boston Globe reporting him as; well proportioned, attractive and with a handsome face.

In his journeys, Arthur Conan Doyle only grew fonder of America; reporting an incredible time spent on an elevator in Manhattan’s Woolworth Building, whose speed he compared to an artillery weapon, and defending the USA against nitpicking critics by saying;.

“Every globe-trotter has paragraphs about the number of spittoons in a hotel bar (as if it matters!)”.

Doyle also fell in love with the sport of baseball, to the point of committing a cardinal sin for any Brit; suggesting British football teams should adopt the sport.

“I will venture to say, that if this was done, we should in a few years have as many to see a baseball final between Tottenham Hotspur and Preston North End as come now to the football.

As to the furore which a decent British team, could we evolve one, would create in America, it is impossible to exaggerate it.”.

Sadly, the United Kingdom has failed to assemble Doyle’s dream team.

Despite the international success of his books, it was his forays into Spiritualism which brought Doyle back to the United States in the 1920’s, and which brought him in contact with his most prominent trans-atlantic friendship.

One of the dramas that marked Doyle’s later American travels was his fractious friendship with magician Harry Houdini.

It has been revealed that admirers of each other’s work, Doyle and Houdini held the opposite opinions on the supernatural that one might expect. Doyle, the creator of super-logician Sherlock Holmes, had become a devout spiritualist, while the magician Houdini was an ardent sceptic.

Strangely enough, so strong was Doyle’s belief, that he insisted Houdini’s escapist tricks involved some form of magic, refusing to budge when Houdini himself told him otherwise.

“My dear chap.” Doyle wrote in a letter to his friend. “Why do you go around the world seeking a demonstration of the occult when you are giving one all the time?”.

Houdini even invited Doyle to the annual dinner at the Society of American Magicians. As a prank, and to prove Magicians could be fooled as much as they claimed Spiritualists were, Doyle screened footage of “live dinosaurs” to an astounded audience.

These live dinosaurs would prove to be stop-motion animations, taken from the upcoming film adaptation of Doyle’s novel; The Lost World.

Despite their spirited back and forth, tensions between the pair came to a head during a 1922 visit to Atlantic City, where Doyle’s wife Jean, channelled Houdini’s mother at a séance. The incident forced Houdini to publicly denounce the events in question, placing Doyle & Houdini publicly at odds.

It was on his Spiritualism tour that Doyle had the strangest encounter Doyle he would perhaps have in America, when Doyle met one of his fans, an old Colonel from the South.

While in Cleveland, described unflatteringly by Doyle as “a grim city of iron and shipping”, Doyle was advancing his lectures on Spiritualism, when he was confronted by an old Southern Colonel with an incredible story to tell.

“The Colonel thought he was an Atlantean by origin,” Doyle reported, with the Colonel continuing to explain that his mother was the former Atlantean Queen.

Though the author tolerated this eccentric visitor in the daytime, when the Colonel revealed up at his hotel room at night, with a message from the former Queen, Doyle was forced to confront the more troubling side of fandom.

In the end, long after Doyle’s love-affair with the USA began, Sherlock Holmes himself finally set foot in the States.

It was in 1917, in “The Final Bow”, the last story in the Sherlock Holmes canon, that the detective confirmed he had crossed the Atlantic, travelling to Chicago and beyond.

It’s a fitting end to a canon which, despite taking place primarily in London, is so deeply suffused with Doyle’s love for the States.

The more I’ve learned about it, the more of a privilege it has seemed, to get the chance to send the iconic Professor to 1890’s Upcoming York.

A place full of the societies, mysticism and people his creator adored so much, where he’ll pull in to the same dock that Sir Arthur Conan Doyle did when he first set foot in America.
