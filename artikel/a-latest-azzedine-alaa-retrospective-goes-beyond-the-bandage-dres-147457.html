A Latest Azzedine Alaïa Retrospective Goes Beyond the Bandage Dress

The bandage dress. The zipper dress. The perforated leather. The jersey hoods. The peplummed chemises. Azzedine Alaïa’s designs may not look so radical now, but almost 50 years ago, he changed the collective tide of fashion. The bandage dress is perhaps the most pervasive bit of his legacy, and it is emblematic of how the late French-Tunisian designer ushered in a fresh body-conscious silhouette in the ’80s, one made possible by technological innovations in materials that sculpted without any rigid, waist-cinching structure.

Alaïa’s designs “were completely shocking” to the public at the time, recounts Laura Camerlengo, curator in charge of costume and textile arts at the Fine Arts Museums of San Francisco. She has spent the past four years developing a new exhibition titled “Azzedine Alaïa: Sculpting Fashion,” the first major U.S. retrospective dedicated to the work of the famed couturier. It opened November 21.

In a recent development, born in 1935 in Tunis, Alaïa first trained as a sculptor, a background evident in his body of work. In 1956, he decamped to Paris to pursue fashion full-time. After a brief internship at the house of Christian Dior, he worked under Guy Laroche for less than two years before taking on his own private clients, then launching his own atelier in the late ’70s. From there, he built the house we know today. His obsession with construction and material innovation allowed him to both celebrate and liberate the female form. He passed away in late 2017 at the age of 82.

In a recent development, camerlengo first encountered Alaïa’s work as an assistant on the “Patrick Kelly Runway of Love” exhibition that the Philadelphia Museum of Art organized in 2014. “I was intrigued by how many of the upcoming designers in Paris at this time were being supported by US consumers and retailers,” she indicates. After ascending to her new position in San Francisco years later, she cold-emailed Olivier Saillard, director of the Azzedine Alaïa Foundation in Paris. He was intrigued, and the two began working together on the exhibition almost immediately.

It has been revealed that the exhibition focuses on 80 garments from across Alaïa’s celebrated career, divided over a series of archetypes—the bandage dresses, sculptures and shapes, black and white, silhouettes and suiting, and construction and craftsmanship—to explore how his design aesthetic evolved. Interspersed are designs worn by devotees like Tina Turner, Grace Jones, and Rihanna. One final gallery highlights his personal collections of the work of designers he most admired, like Christian Dior and Charles James.

Ahead, Camerlengo speaks to Bazaar about curating the new exhibition, contextualizing Alaïa’s work in today’s fashion landscape, and his enduring legacy.

The exhibition is not chronological. We look at a series of Alaïa’s archetypes like the bandage dress to explore how his design aesthetic evolved over the course of his career. He often himself said that he didn’t want to conform to seasonal trends; he was more interested in the evolution of his designs. And so the gallery reveals a mix of objects dating from the 1980s to his final collection in 2017, and they’re largely grouped by type.

When you come in, you are greeted by Grace Jones, as we say, and the seminal purple hooded bandage dress from the fashion Oscars [a French event held in 1985]. As you move through, you’ll see a selection of the zipper dresses, the chiffon gowns, and other works that tie into some of the other themes. In each space, we mix the time periods. And that was something Alaïa himself often stated. [He had a running joke that he didn’t know his own age.] He often said he was “as old as the pharaohs.” Basically, time was a construct for him.

Sources indicate that that’s a great word for it. We could think of the popularity of the Japanese fashion designers who obscured women’s bodies with their voluminous styles. We could think of the suiting of the period. The 1970s is this period of workplace equality. Women are wearing suits in the style of masculine dress, but that also means they're obscuring their bodies. And then we can think of pop culture. I always think of Diane Keaton’s Annie Hall in 1977.

Industry observers note that some of the individuals I interviewed for my catalog text who were retailers in San Francisco at the time described Alaïa's designs as being like a whiplash. It was a complete 180 from the styles that they had gotten used to over the past decade, which also includes the rise of T-shirts and jeans for women as a mode of dress.

It has been revealed that so here you have these very sexy, very form-fitting clothes. And part of the Alaïa aesthetic, as I see it, is the reclamation of the gaze. Rather than women being passively looked at—usually by men—they are instead commanding their own agency. As you said, it was completely earth-shattering and very shocking at the time.

In a recent development, azzedine Alaïa was one of the few couturiers who could work completely by the methods of tailoring and by draping, start to finish, from the initial concept to the final product. I did not fully appreciate his tailoring and the suits he constructed beforehand. We have a selection of them in a gallery of the exhibition, and their construction is absolutely incredible. The way they fit the body, the way they uplift and empower women through cloth—again, very antithetical to what we saw in the decades prior in the 1970s. These are very form-fitting, tailored at the waist, but they have soft feminine touches as well that give you this interesting mix of femininity and strength at the same time.

In a recent development, very key. We talk in the first gallery about how women, speaking generally, were the architects of his life. He learned to sew from his sister, who was working for a seamstress. He was encouraged by a local midwife in Tunisia to go to school at the Institute of Fine Arts. She helped him forge his papers to read 16—he was only 15 at the time. He learned his skills, not in formal training, but from women in Tunisia.

In a recent development, and then his early work was supported by a lot of leading figures of international society, including various Rothschilds, the French performer Arletty, the Swedish American actress Greta Garbo, and that's all before he even started to work formally under his own label in the 1980s. Of course, in the 1980s, he adopts Naomi Campbell, who becomes his protégé. He has close relationships with Grace Jones and Tina Turner. They were hugely important to him. In the exhibition, the clear invisible mounts that we use are actually based on the body of Naomi Campbell.

It has been revealed that probably the Grace Jones bandage dress from the fashion Oscars in 1985. Not only is it the progression of the bandage dress style that he experiments with throughout the course of the 1980s, but it represents a key moment in his career where he receives two very high accolades as part of the fashion Oscars and he’s recognized for his genius.

In a recent development, as far as the designs themselves, I think we would consider the bandage dress, for example, an iconic symbol of late 20th- and 21st-century fashion developed by Azzedine Alaïa, but of course iterated upon extensively. We've seen surges and resurgences in its popularity ever since.

According to recent reports, and then as far as the landscape of the broader fashion system, Alaïa was someone who really eschewed seasonal calendars and trends. He was unconcerned with making things for the market. He often wanted to just produce clothes and share them when he felt they were ready. So I think his method of working foreshadows some of the conversations we’re having now about the overproduction of fashion and the overconsumption of fashion, even the detrimental environmental impacts that we see connected to it. He was really a forerunner, in a sense, to question the fashion system.

It has been revealed that you can see how he really studied the construction of other garments and then adapted it in his own way in his designs. For example, he admired the work of Charles James, the great American fashion designer who was able to manipulate fabric to achieve weightless volume. And you see in Alaïa's own collections as he continues in his career, he does radial tucks and pleating to also get a certain amount of weightless volume in some of his gowns, very much in the spirit of James.

I hope our visitors appreciate the artistry of his work, first and foremost, and also the importance of handcraftsmanship. We are increasingly in an age of technology, especially with the rise of AI. What he created by his own hand is something that we can really marvel at.
