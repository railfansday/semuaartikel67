3 ways to check your car for an open recall and keep the answer current

According to recent reports, NHTSA's recall lookup answers for one car by VIN or plate, leaves some recalls out, and needs a recheck twice a year. Here is how to use it.

It has been revealed that most drivers assume that if their car had a dangerous defect, someone would tell them about it and a mechanic would fix it. Safety recalls are a routine part of owning a car, and they reach a large share of the vehicles on American roads every year. Manufacturers issued recalls covering more than 29.2 million vehicles in 2025, according to NHTSA. Many recalled cars have never gotten the repair. NHTSA reports that, on a weighted average, only 65.8 percent of the cars in recalls covering vehicles alone between 2012 and 2022 were fixed. That left a large share of recalled cars on the road with the defect still in place. A car in your own driveway may have a known safety problem that nobody has fixed.

Industry observers note that an open recall is a repair the manufacturer still owes the car, no matter how long ago the recall was confirmed. A recall never expires once a vehicle is recalled, according to NHTSA. The repair is also free to the owner. A driver who buys a used car years after a recall was issued is still entitled to the fix at no cost. That right is useful only to an owner who knows about the recall, because nobody can ask a dealer for a repair they have never heard of. The owner is the one person who can make sure the car gets fixed.

The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration set out to show owners how to find out whether their vehicle has an unfixed safety recall. The guidance comes from the federal agency that runs the recall system, and it covers what a clean result fails to show, a free app that checks for you, and the 17-character number in the corner of the windshield. An owner can type that number into a search box and get an answer in seconds. Staying protected depends on reading the answer correctly and checking again later.

Sources indicate that A recall notice names a type of car, and an owner reading it still has no way to know whether the one in the driveway has had its repair. An owner who searches by year, make and model gets the general recalls, investigations and complaints for every car of that type. Those results can't show whether that owner's car is one of the cars still waiting for the fix. The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration claims that a search by plate number or by the vehicle identification number, or VIN, shows whether one particular car has a recall repair outstanding. That is the difference between knowing a defect exists and knowing your own car has it.

The VIN is the backup for the times a plate search goes wrong. The license plate is quicker to type, but a plate search is only as accurate as the registration records behind it. An owner who searches by plate may see a vehicle they previously owned until the DMV updates its records, and a search by VIN gets around that problem in the meantime. Owners don't have to dig through paperwork to find the VIN, because it appears in the lower left corner of the windshield. An owner who is away from the car can find the same number on the registration card and possibly on the insurance card. A complete VIN runs 17 characters, and an owner can count them before searching to make sure none were dropped.

A match on your car is a reason to book the repair, not a fact to file away. The repair is done at the manufacturer's dealership, according to NHTSA. An open recall means calling that dealer to schedule the work. An owner who finds a recall but never makes the appointment is driving a car exactly as dangerous as it was before the search.

A clean recall result covers only the recalls the lookup holds, and for long-standing recalls and less common makes that is a narrow set. The search generally leaves out safety recalls issued more than 15 years ago unless a manufacturer chooses to cover them, according to the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration. An owner whose car was recalled that long ago and never repaired may still get a clean result. Open recalls appear only for certain manufacturers. For owners of cars from other makers, a blank result marks a gap in coverage, not a sound car. The older the recall or the less common the maker, the less weight an owner can put on a clean answer.

Industry observers note that even for a car the lookup covers, an owner can't tell from a blank result why it's blank. Safety recalls that have already been repaired don't appear in the results, and a VIN search, NHTSA reports, shows only recalls still awaiting a fix. On screen, a car whose recall was fixed last year looks the same as a car that was never recalled. An owner can learn whether any repair is still owed, but not whether past work was done, which is the question that matters when a car changes hands. For questions about repair history, the agency's advice is to contact the manufacturer.

According to recent reports, the recall lookup gives owners answers about recalls and nothing else. International campaigns are missing from it, and so are non-safety campaigns. NHTSA reports that notices and bulletins submitted by manufacturers are kept in a separate search from recalls. A fault that a maker has already addressed in a bulletin can stay hidden from an owner who runs only a recall check. Owners trying to track down a recurring problem should run that other search as well.

The plate search is convenient, but its data comes from state departments of motor vehicles rather than from the carmaker. If those state records contain a clerical error, the owner may see the wrong vehicle. In that case, the owner should contact the state DMV and search by VIN until the record is corrected.

The plate is the quicker way to an answer, but the VIN is the one to trust.

A clean recall result describes your car only on the day you checked it, and a upcoming recall can be added to that same car later. "VINs are added continuously so please check regularly," the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration stated. The agency recommends checking again twice a year, which gives every owner a simple schedule for the calendar. That habit matters because a notice mailed to warn owners may never reach the person now driving the car.

Industry observers note that recall repairs get done most often while a car is young. NHTSA reports that cars up to three years old have historically had recalls completed around 87 percent of the time, and that in older campaigns, up to 30 percent of the cars still awaiting the fix get a latest owner or a new address every year. The longer a car stays on the road, the less likely it is that its current driver is the person whose name and address a mailed notice goes to. People who bought a car used, or who have moved since the last notice, are the owners most likely to miss one. For them, their own check is the warning.

Recent findings suggest that the surest way to keep up is to have recall news sent to you instead of relying on memory. The agency offers a free app, NHTSA SaferCar, that alerts owners to recalls. Few people look on their own: in a 2019 survey, only 18 percent of people had used the agency's website to check for recalls, according to NHTSA. Any owner who gets a notice or an alert should heed the agency's temporary safety instructions and call a nearby dealer to schedule the fix.

Turn on the app's alerts, put the twice-yearly check on the calendar, and answer any notice with a call to the dealer.
