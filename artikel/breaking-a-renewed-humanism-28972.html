Pope Leo’s Magnifica Humanitas (2026), subtitled “On Safeguarding the Human Person in the Time of Artificial Intelligence,” has been heralded as one of the most significant and morally serious visions for how humanity ought to think about and navigate the advent of the AI era. Acknowledging “the ambiguity of tools,” which are neither morally good nor bad in and of themselves but “can cause harm when not oriented toward the good,” the Pope insists that the end toward which our aspirations aim is as vital as the aspirations themselves.

For Leo, protecting and fostering the dignity of the human person must be the goal of everything we do. Contrasting two images taken from Scripture, the Pope calls us to consider how the desire that orients our ambitions changes the things we create.

In Leo’s first example, the construction of the Tower of Babel (Genesis 11:1–9) is motivated by fear and vainglory:.

Fearing being scattered across the earth, they [the builders] sought to guarantee stability and power for themselves, and above all to ‘make a name’ for themselves.” Such motives cannot but end in ruin. Leo continues, “When a city is built on pride and the claim to self-sufficiency, communication breaks down, languages are confused and people no longer understand each other. The result is not unity, but dispersion. Babel thus reveals the limits of any effort that, however grandiose, arises from self-affirmation, sacrifices human dignity for efficiency and aspires to reach heaven without God’s blessing.

In a recent development, in his second, which comes from the Book of Nehemiah, Nehemiah returns to the city of Jerusalem after the Babylonian exile only to find it in ruins. Heartbroken, he takes in the destruction in silence. Then, before setting to work, he “convened the families, assigned each of them a section of the wall to rebuild, listened to their concerns, coordinated their efforts and addressed any opposition.”.

Industry observers note that nehemiah, that is, kept the dignity of those around him at the center of his project rather than running headlong toward apparent solutions. In so doing, he was able to resurrect what had been lost while simultaneously creating something upcoming. The Pope writes:.

The narrative reveals how the city is reborn, not through the initiative of one man, but through the shared responsibility of all: men, women, priests, artisans, heads of households and young people all play a part. It is an undertaking with God at the center, which rebuilds relationships before rebuilding with stones. Thus, ancient Jerusalem rediscovers a common language—not one of uniformity, but one of communion, namely the harmony that arises when all persons assume their own role and recognize that their strength comes from the Lord.

It has been revealed that what makes these images apt for our current moment—and what many commentators have overlooked—is the role language plays in each. For, if the desire of those constructing the Tower of Babel was to create a single, unified language with which all could understand and be understood, so too is the aim of Artificial Intelligence to provide a kind of totalizing language capable of flattening ambiguity and rendering difference obsolete. And if Nehemiah’s success was rooted in the fact that he sought out the input of those he hoped to serve, and even (perhaps especially) those with whom he disagreed, so too must dialogue be at the foundation of technological advance.

Consider another example, this one taken from contemporary fiction, that draws out this tension inherent in language—the tension between the desire to communicate directly, if only superficially, and the desire to express how fraught and complex human experience actually is, even if that expression can only be accomplished indirectly and ambiguously.

Sources indicate that it comes from Paul Auster’s great work of metareal fiction City of Glass (1987), in which the story’s chief antagonist Peter Stillman—is he still a man, still human, or has he been deformed by his mad pursuit of “perfectibility”?—obsesses over the duplicity of language, the way one word can mean two equal and opposite things. For instance, the word cleave “means both ‘to join together’ and ‘to break apart.’”.

For Stillman, the Genesis narrative to which the Pope alluded “records not only the fall of man, but the fall of language” from clarity to opacity. Once, he conjectures, language was simple, direct, and stated exactly what it meant to say without confusion or ambiguity. There was a time when “words had not been merely appended to things . . . they revealed their essences . . . A thing and its name were interchangeable.” But after the destruction of the Tower of Babel, this was no longer true. Now words conceal as much as they reveal, and deception is built into the very nature of how we communicate.

Driven mad by the desire to use language to reunify what appears to him to be a fractured and fragmentary existence, Stillman sets out to realize a utopian future in which his model of a single, unified language makes absolute knowledge and mastery possible. (Sound familiar?).

If the fall of man also entailed a fall of language, was it not logical to assume that it would be possible to undo the fall, to reverse its effects by undoing the fall of language? . . . If man could learn to speak this original language of innocence, did it not follow that he would thereby recover a state of innocence within himself?

The trouble is that Stillman is not able to conjure up this mythical language model, and his attempts to do so inflict immense suffering on those around him.

In an ironic twist, Stillman ultimately resorts to deception himself in a vain attempt to accomplish his goal. Creating a character named Henry Dark, a fictional disciple of John Milton, he attributes his most controversial ideas to Dark and even falsifies the historical record to make it appear as though Dark had heralded his, Stillman’s, project over 300 years in advance.

In a recent development, what could offer a better image of the LLMs that seem so powerful and omniscient today—with their false citations and hallucinated facts—than a fictional character invented by a mad genius to dupe others into believing that his language model could solve the mysteries and riddles of the human condition? And yet, what could offer a better reason to hope? For, although Dark was invented by Stillman to serve darker ends, still his creation was—as Pope Leo recently called the craft of writing fiction—a profound “act of humanity,” one which points to the supreme dignity of its author even if he, like many of us today, is too consumed with his utopian ideas to appreciate it.
