Batima Zaurbekova’s Textiles Meld Traditional Kazakh Techniques with Modernist Influences, Defying Easy Categorization

Sources indicate that how does an artist sustain five decades of artistic production while remaining faithful to her cultural heritage? For Batima Zaurbekova, one of Kazakhstan’s most respected artists, the answer seems to be an unrelenting commitment to her craft. “You can’t be lazy,” the 80-year-old artist told me during a recent conversation.

That maxim is borne out in “Threads of Heritage,” a retrospective of more than 80 works at the Abylkhan Kasteyev National Museum of Arts in Almaty, Kazakhstan. Her largest exhibition to date, it features the textiles she is known for as well as drawings and paintings. Curated by Alina Fedorovich, a curator at the Museum of Moscow, the exhibition traces a career in which tapestry becomes both a repository of cultural memory and a vehicle for artistic reinvention. In a recent development, rooted in traditional Kazakh textiles, her practice is also shaped by the influences of modernist painting and music, moving fluidly between abstraction and figuration and in turn, defying easy categorization. Her tapestries hold seemingly disparate influences, transforming traditional motifs into a distinct visual language. Ornamental Rug (1969), for example, revives motifs associated with horses, spiritual symbolism, and petroglyphs depicting mountain goats. Completed just after her graduation and the earliest work in the exhibition, Ornamental Rug provides a recognizable reference to the country’s landscape via the distinctive horns of the wild argali, a local mountain goat, while distilling these forms into rhythmic, stylized arrangements. The work’s title foregrounds the relationship between ornament and abstraction, suggesting how decorative forms can become a vehicle for modernist experimentation.

Industry observers note that zaurbekova grew up in the mountainous Zhambyl region of Kazakhstan, where textile-making was part of everyday life. She remembers watching older women create syrmak, traditional felt rugs, and kurak-korpe, patchwork quilts meaning “stitched from scraps.” Neighbors gathered to work, sing, cook, and share food, embodying the Kazakh tradition of asar, in which a community joins forces to help a household undertake a task, be it making a carpet or building a home.

Textile-making was as much a social practice as a technical one, transmitting skills and cultural knowledge across generations. This collective spirit is evoked in In the Yurt (1985), a weaving in which a maternal figure presides over the dastarkhan, the table prepared for guests. The yurt, a traditional felt dwelling associated with Kazakh nomadic life, becomes a symbol of hospitality, family, and belonging. Its ornaments merge with the surrounding figures, establishing a visual language equally rooted in the formal and the domestic.

In her youth, Zaurbekova had alternative ambitions for herself. “I really loved music, so I wanted to become a musician,” she recalled. However, her seamstress mother encouraged her to pursue painting instead. She is equally accomplished in oil painting and tapestry, and occasionally the same image is translated in both mediums.

According to recent reports, nevertheless, music has remained central to Zaurbekova’s life and practice, in which her visual compositions are animated by repetition, variation, and rhythm—the basic structure of music. This way of working is best observed in one gallery showing three works, each titled Composition and made in 1980, 2019, and 2024. Their stained-glass aesthetic, contrasting color planes, and rhythmic linear forms evoke Henri Matisse’s celebrated “Jazz” series. Undulating figures and vegetal motifs merge within an ornamental field, dissolving the distinction between figure and ground.

“Threads of Heritage” is being exhibited in dialogue with the museum’s collection of Kazakh decorative and applied art. Traditional felt objects, carpets, and ornaments establish the historical context out which Zaurbekova’s work emerges, while the installation reinforces the relationship between inherited forms and contemporary expression. Here, custom-built walls reference the architecture of the Khoja Ahmed Yasawi Mausoleum, an notable historical monument whose forms recur in the artist’s imagery, like the monumental Antiquity (1993). The display also evokes the balbal, ancient stone monuments that connect contemporary life with earlier cultures.

The exhibition’s largest gallery showcases Zaurbekova’s technical mastery and evolving visual language. Each tapestry offers a dazzling array of color that boggles the mind. How, exactly, does she coax such subtle gradations from a seemingly limited collection of threads? “Selecting the right colors is a big part of the work—and it takes me a long time,” Zaurbekova explained. During the Soviet period, artists often dyed threads by hand, painstakingly creating transitions from darker to lighter shades. Although commercially produced threads from Belarus expanded her palette in the 1990s, Zaurbekova has continued to experiment, combining those threads and ones she has dyed to create colors all her own. “Kazakhstan, our land, is very rich, and this is represented in the rich colors of my work,” she told me.

Zaurbekova’s textiles are woven over several weeks to multiple months, and the artist still works for up to 18 hours a day. The vertical threads form the foundation of a given work, she explained, while horizontal threads of different colors construct the image. Through subtle variations in their combinations, Zaurbekova achieves tonal effects that can make woven surfaces appear almost painted. She is also deft at making alasha, a traditional yet simple Kazakh carpet.

Industry observers note that rather than presenting national identity as a fixed collection of symbols, her compositions transform familiar motifs through color and pattern. In keeping these traditions alive, Zaurbekova remarked she thinks “all of my work reflects our nationality and national pride.” Kazakhstan (1977) depicts themes of agriculture and collective labor. To make it, Zaurbekova used threads sourced from a Kazakh carpet manufacturer, working within the limitations of an existing palette to achieve subtle distinctions of tone. Her exacting yet delicate manipulation of the threads’ colors gives the woven surface a gentle vibration and shimmering quality. A work like this makes visible the precision and patience necessitated by such a craft.

In a recent development, A centerpiece of the exhibition is Vastness (1975), a large tapestry from the museum’s collection showing a bride associated with spring and Nauryz, the celebration of the spring equinox and new year. Around her, women gather at tables and a young man on horseback appears among the birds. Elsewhere, figures emerge from the surrounding forest, creating a dense tableau that feels as abstract as it is narrative. The exhibition’s final gallery focuses on the shanyrak, the circular wooden crown at the top of a traditional Kazakh yurt. A symbol of home and family, it serves an architectural motif representing womanhood. In Mother Earth (1984), connections between generations of women evoke the continuity of life and inherited knowledge. Zauberkova’s achievement lies not only in her sheer technical mastery, but in her ability to reinterpret Kazakh textile traditions through a vision that is unmistakably her own, creating work that is deeply rooted in its cultural origins while remaining formally inventive and alive.
