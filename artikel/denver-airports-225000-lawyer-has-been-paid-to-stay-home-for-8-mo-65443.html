📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Denver airport’s top lawyer has spent eight months on paid leave, collecting a salary of roughly $225,000 a year. Now Denver wants a federal judge to let it fire him. But the airport’s general counsel Everett Martinez claims city officials retaliated against him for raising legal objections to their improper dealings and for refusing to help push out airport CEO Phil Washington. The city says he violated his professional obligations and wants to stop paying someone who can’t perform the job. Meanwhile, Denver has authorized up to $970,000 for outside lawyers handling his case and a separate employment lawsuit. The $470,000 budget for the first phase of his case is quite an expense for a fight between the city and its own airport lawyer. I’ve called Denver the worst airport in the country. It can work reasonably well as a connecting hub. Actually getting to and from Denver, through the terminal and out to a gate is another matter. This latest fight brings together several of the reasons the place inspires so little confidence: political interference, weak financial oversight, and executives spending their time fighting each other. The city’s November 10 filing argues keeping Martinez on leave is harming Denver during a budget crisis. Martinez wants paid leave to continue while he litigates, potentially for years. At approximately $225,000 annually, eight months works out to about $150,000 in salary, before benefits. That’s arithmetic, rather than a payroll accounting. The distinction between airport money and city money matters here. His salary is airport money. Martinez’s complaint says it comes entirely from the airport’s enterprise fund. That’s separate from Denver’s general fund. Airport revenue consists of things like airline rents and landing fees, parking and concession. That’s not money that can get moved to the city’s programs anyway. The outside defense contract is budgeted to city money. The council’s contract worksheet identifies the general fund as its funding source. So it’s actually the city that’s draining funds amidst a budget crisis, although it’s possible they try to bill it back to the airport. The $970,000 covers two cases. The city’s budget presentation allocates $470,000 to the first phase of Martinez’s case and $500,000 to a separate case. They’re authorizing spending ‘up to’ this amount at this stage. The amended agreement sets rates of $800 an hour for attorneys and $375 for paralegals. The firm is Wheeler Trigg O’Donnell, where city attorney Miko Brown previously worked. Funny that. Brown says a five-person panel considered four firms and unanimously selected this one, which Denver had used since 2011. She says she cast her vote for her former firm last. Martinez alleges he was punished after objecting to the Park Hill Golf Course land swap, warning against withholding information from federal aviation officials, and resisting pressure to help remove Washington. He also alleges discriminatory treatment and an ugly remark from Brown suggesting he get a noose. The city denies his allegations. The city didn’t want to lease airport space to a carrier involved in ICE deportation flights and Martinez wasn’t helpful in covering this up (it’s against the law). There are federal restrictions involved in transferrin ghte land. Martinez appears to have stood in the way (likely as he was obligated to). But the city has counterclaimed for breach of fiduciary duty, conversion and civil theft. Martinez may have a legitimate employment claim but that doesn’t give him the right to remain on the payroll throughout the litigation. He says his first amendment rights are being violated, but the Supreme Court generally doesn’t treat speech made pursuant to an employee’s official duties as protected citizen speech and giving the city legal advice was his job. And while he ostensibly also helped protect then-airport CEO Phil Washington, it’s not at all clear that Washington was doing a good job. (He retired August 1.) I covered Washington’s nomination to lead the Federal Aviation Administration and its collapse. He’d spent less than two years running an airport after a career in the military and public transit.
