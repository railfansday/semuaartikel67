📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

He-Man liked to exclaim: “I have the power!” AI should be so lucky.

When it comes to the wielders of artificial intelligence, and the data centers needed to fuel it, language and numbers become inescapably extreme: trillions in investment, gigawatts of electricity, the extinction of the human race. It’s almost cartoonish, until you get down to some brass tacks: The power is not there to fuel these dreams of the would-be Masters of the Universe.

It has been revealed that “The U.S. grid is not designed for large data-center loads,” says Anubhav Arora, senior director of global infrastructure at Fitch and a coauthor of a recent paper the ratings agency published on data centers. “Securing reliable, sufficient power is the single greatest bottleneck in data-center development,” the paper’s authors write. (Esquire and Fitch share the same parent company, Hearst.).

In the U.S.—which is by far the world leader in data centers—electricity demand from the facilities could reach up to 649 terawatt-hours (TWh) a year by 2030, according to a 2026 Berkeley Lab analysis, or roughly 12 percent of all U.S. electricity consumption. Powering New York City for an entire year requires one tenth that energy.

Recent findings suggest that meeting global demand for compute could require nearly $7 trillion in capital investment by 2030, including $5.2 trillion for AI-related data centers, according to McKinsey estimates. Estimates from Goldman Sachs Research say that Europe will need to invest more than $3.5 trillion on its grid in the next ten years to cope with increased demand. (China looks to be fine, but it is slow to the data-center boom.).

According to recent reports, and the problem is not just a lack of juice. “The actual grid infrastructure . . . is also basically limited in terms of transmission capacity, and the interconnection process is a little bit outdated,” notes Arora. That grid—the network of transmission lines, transformers, and power plants that create and distribute electricity to hundreds of millions of users—is constrained, and the interconnection backlog is massive. Put another way: Not only is the shop running low on milk, but the line to get inside is stretching around the block. “You could build a data center in one and a half to two years, but to get interconnection, it takes five to ten years,” Arora says.

According to Cleanview, a manufacturer that tracks U.S. power infrastructure, there are currently 1,454 operating data centers, as well as up to 578 under construction and 2,663 proposed. Companies like Alphabet, Meta, and Anthropic are scrambling. With that scramble, there is money to be made. PJM, the largest power-grid operator in the United States, has been trying to devise a newly introduced way to accommodate the data-center boom. Its proposed “Bring Your Own New Capacity” (or BYONC) framework would have allowed new data-center projects to buy energy in the form of a battery and add it to the grid. But in October, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission rejected that portion of PJM’s proposal, sending the grid operator back to the drawing board.

Important here is what kind of data centers we’re talking about. Of the more than 3,200 data centers proposed globally, 800 of them are hyperscalers. These are the biggies. The campuses. Sprawling centers with square footage in the millions and power demands upwards of 100 MW (which could power 100,000 homes). Collectively, some estimates put the global power usage at 200 TWh annually. (That’s juice for 11 million homes.).

How can companies even think about the grid beginning to handle that demand? Increasingly, they aren’t. Many hyperscalers are looking to “bring your own power [BYOP],” notes Jonathon Smith, associate director of Sustainable Finance Research at Fitch. In other words, build their own power plants. The problem here is twofold: First, very few people on the planet know how to build something like this. You could buy the biggest army the world has ever seen; try moving it without engineers. Second, it poses very real and very dangerous environmental threats.

Recently, the journal Nature Sustainability published a study revealing that the annual carbon dioxide emissions of the largest data centers in the U.S. are equal to that of a small country, like Paraguay or Denmark. And that study looked at data centers from 2024 and 2025. Data centers have exploded since then, with as much as a 190 percent increase from 2025 to 2026.

Sources indicate that when companies like Anthropic say, “Don’t worry, we’re not even touching the grid,” it brings up a cartoon situation more akin to The Simpsons than a Saturday-morning serial. Picture it: a giant data center, clean, cool, not an emission in sight, just humming machines and maybe a security guard. But there’s a pipe going out the back, and if you follow it as it snakes around the corner, it connects to a behemoth power plant, burning enough natural gas to power a city, with plumes of smoke assaulting the sky above. Animated, but closer to the reality of BYOP.

“From an environmental-impact perspective, most of these developers are deploying natural-gas-based assets for power, which release GHG [greenhouse gas] emissions and air pollutants,” states Smith. The cleaner alternatives are renewables (unlikely), nuclear (terrifying), and batteries (no environmental picnic, either).

Industry observers note that among the environmental concerns—which inevitably bleed into the social and economic ones—are these centers’ use of water, which they need in great volume to keep those humming stacks of servers cool. While many experts point to these issues as overblown, especially as data centers look to closed-loop systems (there is an initial dump of water that gets cooled and reused), the reason you won’t stop hearing about water use is because 1) it’s concerning, and 2) water is inherently political and always has been. (Traditionally, we’ve needed the stuff to feed our animals, grow our crops, and, you know, live.) Go back to the early chapters of the Bible and what’s causing strife? Water. Go back even further and some of the first wars ever recorded—4,500 years ago in ancient Sumeria—are over the stuff. And data centers have become a fast-moving political football.

Industry observers note that A recent Gallup poll indicates that 71 percent of Americans do not want an AI data center near where they live. Those are some very big numbers. It’s crucial to clarify that the data centers big tech wants to build near dense populations are for cloud computing, and they make up roughly 70 percent of all data centers. While centers for AI computing and AI training are catching up, these can be built farther away, for the simple reason that we’re happy to wait a few minutes for AI to do our taxes but not for the cloud to recombobulate an email or Instagram post.

Sources indicate that that’s still a huge number of data centers that need to be near you and me, so what will we do about it? The politicians are listening. Upcoming York State has issued a moratorium. There’s legislation limiting the centers on the table in nine other states. Gina Hinojosa, who just pummeled Greg Abbott in the Governor's race in Texas, campaigned on promises of a moratorium for permitting and construction on new data centers. Norway, the Netherlands, and Denmark are leading the charge on moratoriums and other restrictions in Europe as governments catch up with popular opinion.

In a recent development, moreover, back in the U.S.A., Donald Trump has touted a “deal” with substantial tech on data centers, the Ratepayer Protection Pledge, supposedly protecting grids from the biggies. But never has Tip O’Neill’s maxim that “All politics is local” been more salient. More than 500 localities are forming their own moratoriums, zoning freezes, and permit pauses. Even after the midterms, how and if to move forward with data centers is a debate for states, counties, cities, townships, and any other geographic entity that makes laws, collects taxes, and breathes collective air.

As the Democrats take over Congress and data centers become every pol’s favorite upcoming cudgel, as the European Union and member states enact regulatory frameworks (often tied to renewable energy and climate), as elected officials turn away from AI’s once sunnily vague promises, something is becoming clear: It’s humans who have the power.
