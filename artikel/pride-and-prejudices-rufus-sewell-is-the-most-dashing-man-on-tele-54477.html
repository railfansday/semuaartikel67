In a recent development, it’s possible that Rufus Sewell is having too much fun right now. Between playing morally ambiguous Vice President (and husband to the U.S. Ambassador to the Court of St. James’s) Hal Wyler in Netflix’s frothy, prestige-y series The Diplomat and the beloved but deeply flawed Mr. Bennet in the streamer’s upcoming adaptation of Pride and Prejudice, he’s cornered the market on lovable rascals. He thrives on “exercises in gray” and relishes “when it goes wrong” for a character. “It’s the opposite of what you want in life,” he tells me. “You want drama, you want mistakes.” These roles offer plenty of both.

Wyler, of The Diplomat, is navigating how to work with the increasingly autocratic and bloody-minded President Grace Penn (Allison Janney) thousands of miles away from his wife Kate (Keri Russell), who is starting to exhibit some of his own manipulative behaviors she claims to abhor. Two centuries prior, Mr. Bennet is reckoning not with geopolitical crises and egomaniacal heads of state, but with the challenges his wife faces in marrying off five eligible, if dowry-poor, daughters.

Sewell sat down for a long chat with GQ about the two series, the value of blending high silliness with high stakes in the same scene, and how bodily functions are an under-appreciated key to unlocking a character’s specificity.

Industry observers note that rufus Sewell: The interesting question for me, for the second half of the season, was how much is Hal truly aware of everything that Kate and everyone else are noticing and concerned about with Grace's behavior. How much does he see? How much is he choosing to pretend not to see in order to stay in the room? He's always believed that access is all, but how much do you compromise in order to stay in the room? Where’s the point when you realize you stayed in the room too long without saying anything, and the moment’s passed, and you are just an accomplice? I found it very difficult to play his seeming unctuousness and obsequiousness with Grace this season, and to not be trying to protect my character in the wrong way, because I felt he was failing.

According to recent reports, he’s overestimated his ability to effect change with Grace. Hal was prepared to appear to be supporting her, when he believed in his ability to steer her in a better direction, and I think he goes past that point. When I read the final episode script and saw where he ends up walking out, it gave me the courage to really lean into how much he goes wrong, by his own standards earlier in the season. He begins to lose his way in a way that he never has done before. But I embraced it, knowing that the real gift of the part was getting to play him going wrong and then seemingly correcting himself. We actually shot a speech that he made, just to give us a sense of where the scene was headed and for me to know how to play it. They might end up using some of it in the next season, or they could end up completely changing what was written. Hal coming out of the summit is a momentous decision in terms of what that does for the future and I've got no idea what’s next—I can't wait to start filming season five to find out.

In a recent development, I was always shocked when this show was described as a thriller because I always thought it was a kind of romantic sex comedy with extremely high stakes, because what it reminded me of firstly was some of the comedy of divorce screwball films of the 1940s. The characters may be divorced after a very tempestuous relationship, but they’re interlinked in a way that can never be undone. There's a romance to all of that history.

According to recent reports, the other thing that it reminded me of was the kind of films that were made in the '70s—French films where halfway through an argument one of the characters would sit down on the toilet, you know what I mean? The people inside these films remind you of your own relationships. Your long-term relationship might not go to the same extremes, it might not be in the same setting, but I think people are people. What I love about their relationship is their shared wonkiness, and the getting into the nitty-gritty of the politics and taking someone down and hatching a plot whilst fighting over pastries and getting crumbs on the bed—all of that is part of their foreplay.

Industry observers note that that is what the show is about, as far as I'm concerned. These are people who wear suits that might stand at a lectern and be on TV, but the fact is, how do you scratch an itch? Do you smell? Do you need to go to the toilet? These are the things that the script leans into a lot, and it might put some people off, but I don't care about that. That's the thing that makes it feel real, and it's so refreshing to play.

It has been revealed that he'll flirt with anyone. We all know people like this. It's not even necessarily sexual, he just engages with people on that level and it's a natural thing for him.

I would say Hal’s flirtatiousness being a problem is open for debate, because a lot of what we understand of him is via Kate’s descriptions of him and his behavior. I can and often do see it in a different way. Part of the story of seasons three and four is Kate’s realization that a lot of stuff she thought of as his personality was in fact the mantle of the job that he'd taken on, because we see her doing the same kinds of things now that she is an ambassador. They both have a lot of the same qualities. They're both killers in a sense, and it's been very convenient for her and great for the show for her to introduce the public to Hal through her descriptions. And yet, every time it seems like he's changed character, in fact, it is revealing something from a different perspective.

Recent findings suggest that we learned pretty early on that we were going to be doing some very, very long scenes, and we were given the advantage of seeing the scenes very early so we could slowly gestate the performances. We needed that because Windsor Castle in the show was comprised of several different buildings and locations, some of them with oceans between them, so we’d be shooting some parts the next day, and other bits in four months’ time.

Recent findings suggest that especially with a two-hander, with such long takes, you can go in and out of being relaxed, unrelaxed, free, slightly bound, and actually you get to the point where you stop worrying about it. It's like being on stage, because you end up too tired to be bad all the time and end up inhabiting the moment and things just happen. I love that all of it was a surprise to us.

I think what is most vital in this particular iteration of the relationship between Mr. and Mrs. Bennet is that he comes to realize which ways he's failed his daughters. When Mrs. Bennet states to him in this version, it's easy for you to say that I’m silly or frivolous, but you try being a woman at this age with no means—this is the fight of their lives that he is making these jokes about. And yes, of course he's funny and right in certain ways, but he's also guilty of negligence of his family for the sake of an easy life for himself. The reason they have no money is that so much money has been spent on bonnets. Money could have been saved, but in order to have a quiet life, he's ducked out of fights in order to avoid responsibility.

Exactly. It was a gift in this role to have that moment when he feels that, admits that, and gets to be slightly humbled in this idea of himself that he's been cultivating. He didn’t actively or consciously avoid having this revelation, but that’s the effect of staying in his study, and now he’s facing the consequences. To use very modern terms, he has a revelation of what his pattern has been. It made sense!

Industry observers note that one could be forgiven for thinking that Mr. and Mrs. Bennet are two people that used to be attracted to each other and now have nothing in common and don't like each other. I think it's born out in the book, though, the idea that there is still a love there, and especially a shared humor between them, and a bit of a spark remains.

I love the fact that we get to get drunk together and stuff like that. Olivia Colman and I worked on The Father together a few years ago with Anthony Hopkins, which was a very different kind of gig, but as is often the case with things that are very heavy and intense, we had a hoot doing it. I loved working with her, and was very excited about the chance to work with her again. I love the scenes between Mr. Bennet and Mrs. Bennet. They were such a treat for us.
