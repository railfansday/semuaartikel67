China ‘full stop, is ahead of us’: SpaceX alum building a $13 billion defense startup warns of Beijing’s hypersonic weapons

While Jensen Huang’s resignation has put a renewed spotlight on the U.S.-China race for dominance in chips and artificial intelligence, another technological contest could have even more direct national security implications: hypersonic weapons.

And the U.S. is already trailing behind, according to Andrew Kreitz, cofounder and CFO of Castelion—a $13 billion defense startup focused on developing hypersonic missiles.

“Unfortunately, China, especially, full stop, is ahead of us in hypersonics,” Kreitz told Fortune’s Andrew Nusca at the Fortune 500 Innovation Forum in Detroit, Mich.

The U.S., for its part, has begun fielding its first long-range hypersonic missile system, known as “Dark Eagle.” China, meanwhile, has been steps ahead for years and “has the world’s leading hypersonic missile arsenal,” according to the U.S. Department of Defense’s 2025 report to Congress.

It has been revealed that kreitz pointed to several reasons for the gap, starting with how the U.S. defense industry develops and builds weapons. He noted the Pentagon has often prioritized the most advanced technology without giving enough thought to cost or how easily it can be manufactured. Cost-plus contracts, which reimburse contractors for their expenses, also give companies less incentive to control costs.

The aerospace and defense industry also suffers from failing to test hardware early and often, Kreitz said, arguing that fewer tests mean slower learning and development.

“Unfortunately, our adversaries very much took the opposite approach,” Kreitz said on stage. “The Chinese have tested and iterated very, very fast, and they’ve been relentlessly focused on industrializing and manufacturing these weapons at a very large scale.”.

Kreitz and fellow cofounders Sean Pitt and Bryon Hargis left SpaceX in 2022 to start Castelion, aiming to disrupt the defense industry by building weapons faster, cheaper, and at a much larger scale.

In just four years, Castelion has grown to more than 400 employees, reached a $13 billion valuation, and secured multiple Pentagon contracts. In September, the U.S. Navy awarded the tech giant a contract worth up to $200 million for Blackbeard, its low-cost hypersonic missile.

Much of that success, Kreitz stated, comes from the engineering culture they learned at SpaceX—and can be traced back even further to NASA’s Apollo program.

“It hearkens back to the golden age of American aerospace in the 50s and 60s: get to hardware as promptly as possible, test as early as possible, iterate as fast as possible,” he mentioned.

Castelion is also willing to bring more manufacturing in-house rather than rely on outside suppliers when supply chain complications get in the way.

“Everything has to be backwards planned, from how do we scale production of this weapon—the iteration cycles and the testing, especially though, that just unlocks learning at a really, really rapid pace,” Kreitz added.

Castelion is riding a wave of investment in defense technology. In the third quarter of 2026, defense startups raised a record $2.5 billion—contributing to a total defense tech VC value of $67.4 billion across 726 deals this year, according to PitchBook.

Leading the pack in terms of valuation is Anduril, which raised funding in May at a $61 billion valuation and has since explored a potential deal that would nearly double that figure.

But Kreitz thinks the boom has created a bubble—and not every company will survive the coming shakeout.

“There is froth in the market,” Kreitz remarked. “I think that there will be a shakeout at some point. That’s the truth.”.

Some companies have promising technology and products but lack the ability to bring them to market or manufacture them at scale, Kreitz noted. Others may ultimately be acquired as the industry consolidates. For Castelion, the strategy is to focus more on actually building its business.

“Our approach to weathering this is focus on execution, focus on operations, focus on manufacturing, get ourselves to scale and free cash flow generation as soon as possible to sort of immunize us to any of the noise that might be occurring in the capital markets,” Kreitz said.
