Celebrity chef Dook Chase is intentionally never far from a kitchen, be it inside one of his renowned restaurants or his dream family home in New Orleans.

According to recent reports, chase, 44, is the fourth-generation owner of Dooky Chase’s Restaurant, the legendary culinary destination and cultural institution that’s been operating in the Massive Easy since 1941. The NOLA native also runs his modern Creole eatery, Chapter IV, commuting between both businesses from a Southern-style, two-story residence that he shares with his wife, Gretchen, their daughter, Sidney, and son, Vito.

“It's a seven-minute commute to both restaurants,” Chase tells Realtor.com® in the latest Celebrity Sanctuary. “I can visit each one in its peak time. I can get a text from any server saying, ‘Hey, a table came in that really wanted to see you,’ and I [say], ‘Tell them I'll be there in seven minutes.’ I can hop in the car and get to each.”.

Personally connecting with the local community over a meal is a practice that is baked into Chase’s family backstory and a tradition he proudly upholds today.

“When you think about Dooky Chase Restaurant and my grandmother, chef Leah Chase, in the time that she helmed that kitchen ... the history of our restaurant is really through the Civil Rights Movement, opening doors to everyone in the community: Blacks and whites, together, at a time when laws didn't allow it. But we always allowed it at our restaurant,” the Le Cordon Bleu-educated executive chef explains. “My grandparents, great-grandparents, [and] certainly my parents understood the importance of learning from each and every person ... and we were able to do that through food.

It has been revealed that “One thing that I love about my family's history and what we've been able to do every Thanksgiving is, we open our restaurant on Thanksgiving Day,” he continues. “So we gather with our community first, and then we close, clean up, set the tables all over again, and then [our] families come in. It’s a way to serve others and then enjoy your day right after.”.

According to recent reports, chase’s Thanksgiving menu is typically an “endless” buffet of classic Southern dishes, including his favorite, gumbo, followed by multiple dessert options. However, this year, the “Family Recipe Showdown” star is bringing some fresh inspiration to the table thanks to his time working with fellow chef Christina Tosi and actress Drew Barrymore on the latest “Pillsbury Bake-off Classic” competition show, due to air on Nov. 23 on CBS and Paramount+.

“We have different experiences and different backgrounds, but ... we learn from one another,” Chase claims. “That's what I enjoyed about this bake-off. This really brings people together and unites them [to] think about the memories of their childhoods, their families, their celebrations, and you felt that in that room.”.

In this edition of Celebrity Sanctuary, Chase welcomes a look at his hospitable kitchen where he often hosts gatherings with family and friends on ordinary and special occasions alike. Plus, he explains how the heart of the 1952-built, 4,400-square-foot, five-bedroom, five-bathroom home helps him focus on the moment he’s in as much as the meal he’s making.

Our previous home is a block away from here. I would exercise in the morning, run up and down [this] oak tree–lined street, and I would tell my wife, “If something ever comes up for sale, I'm buying.”.

Industry observers note that as I was running one morning, [the previous owner’s] children were putting up a sign, and I asked, “Can you wait a day? Don't put up the sign. Let's talk. And then if it doesn't work, put up a sign.”.

According to recent reports, they waited that day. It happened to work out. I think why it worked out was because their father understood the family atmosphere of what he wanted that next owner to be. When we shared the story of who I was and all about my family, it was like, “This is what I want to happen in this house.”.

In a recent development, it's a beautiful house. I really enjoy it ... and am excited to raise my family and the next generation of us here.

According to recent reports, the architectural style of this home is Southern Latest Orleans. We have the big oak tree. We have the brick porch. We have the wood doors. We have shutters.

I bought this home in 2009, and we moved in 2010. When we got it, it was completely gutted from the devastation of Hurricane Katrina. We renovated it and brought it to where it is today. We added on to the porch to add a latest front door section where you can be greeted by artwork or by us, and then eventually get to the stairs.

Industry observers note that one of the great things we were able to do in this house is save some of the original hardwood. The rooms upstairs that didn't have hardwood, that had carpet, we brought [the hardwood] to those rooms, refurbished them, and then downstairs, laid all newly introduced hardwood down. Most of the windows we were able to keep.

Downstairs, we wanted to open it up. Back in the day, they had walls where you would walk through a door into the kitchen, so it was closed off to the other rooms. For me as a chef, when I'm cooking, I want to see everybody. It's where people gather, so that kitchen really dictates every movement that's going on downstairs. Whether you're in the living room, the dining room, the den—you can see and smell what's going on in that kitchen.

Sanctuary, for me, it means home. It means peace. It means comfort. It's almost like you're getting a big hug that wraps you and claims, “I got you.”.

My comfort zone is the kitchen. It's where I find peace. That's where I can block out all the noise and really home in on what's happening right now.

The home kitchen allows you to slow down. It's different timing. There's not 200 people in the dining room waiting for their meals; there might be just three hungry people. It allows you to slow down and to really think and to really enjoy the meal that you're cooking or the meal that you're eating.

Recent findings suggest that also, we use my home as a test kitchen. We’ve done many cookbooks. Most of those recipes I've tested in my home versus tested in a restaurant, because it allows you to slow down. It allows you to measure. It allows you to take your time and say, “If I was a home cook reading this cookbook, what do I need to have?” It gives me that hybrid space of really focusing and slowing down and getting it right versus having extra things come through in and out of that kitchen. In your home, you really have that peace to enjoy the process.

The countertops are like a granite marble. I have equipment on top of the countertop: We have a mixer. I love nugget ice, so there's a nugget ice machine.

The cabinets are traditional, white cabinets. They have either copper or brass [hardware]. The wall is painted a gray tone. Going into the den has a navy blue. The colors I like ... enhanced by all of my wife's tastes.

There's a center where I can move a butcher block if we want to roll out any pastry. It's open. It's not an island, but like an island. On the other side, it's a higher top where there’s almost bar seating.

My kitchen overlooks the dining room. There is a little chandelier there. Our table has windows bringing in a natural light that overlooks an oak tree.

To the left of that is a seating area that has beautiful artwork. Art is huge in my family, so we have artwork everywhere. It has four chairs and a sofa, where we have a coffee table, seating, some statues, some other creative art. Then to the right of that, it's the den and another little nook where we keep a bar.

It's the focal point of the downstairs of my house. When you have a party, the kitchen is always going to be the focal point of it. No matter what's going on in any other room, people gather in the kitchen.
