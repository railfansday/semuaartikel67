Training can act as a confidence engine, turning repeated behaviors into habits and culture. In her talk, Engineer Confidence with Reliable Training Programs at GOTO Copenhagen, Jennifer Petoff showed how we can tailor training programs that equip developers and operators with the skills to run their services in line with the organization’s maturity and growth, and apply SRE principles to the training itself.

It has been revealed that software ownership is a human challenge as much as it is a technical one. Most formal education programs are geared towards the first 10% of the software development life cycle, the build phase, Petoff said. Engineers and operators are tasked with maintaining, debugging, securing, and evolving systems they may not even have written:.

Sources indicate that there’s this implicit assumption that coding proficiency translates seamlessly to operational confidence, but it doesn’t. Without systematic training, this may create imposter syndrome, stress, and maybe even attrition in your organization.

Recent findings suggest that AI has magnified the relative cost and complexity of post-build operations. Petoff suggested thinking about how to equip developers and operators with skills to run their services scalably in production.

Culture is not some grand mindset you need to shift. It’s much more tactical. You can change your culture by changing what people do every day, Petoff said. Training people acts as a confidence engine, giving engineers the self-efficacy to adopt fresh operational behaviors. When behaviors are repeated over time and across teams, they become habits, and those habits comprise your organizational culture. Petoff suggests avoiding a sink-or-swim mentality (letting people fend for themselves rather than training them), as it can breed stress or frustration.

Training needs depend on the maturity of your organization in adopting key principles and practices, the familiarity of engineers with the organization and infrastructure, and the experience of individuals. For organizations starting their DevOps or SRE journey, she suggested addressing skill gaps and tailoring the message to who is on your team. Organizations that already have well-developed production best practices should aim at reinforcing their culture and building upon their practices to foster confidence.

Your training program should take into account the size of your organization and how fast it’s growing to get the most from your investment. If your organization is small and growing slowly, focus on one-on-one knowledge transfer through mentoring and shadowing, Petoff suggested. For a large and slowly growing organization, invest in ongoing education. When it’s small but growing rapidly, invest in onboarding. If you’re both large and growing rapidly, invest in a full life cycle training program, onboarding, all the way through ongoing education and beyond, she mentioned.

It has been revealed that to scale production safety training to tens of thousands of developers, they created a self-paced training and piloted it with a 1% cohort, making changes based on audience feedback before scaling it up. Petoff said they automated the distribution, enrollment, and escalation notifications up the management chain.

Industry observers note that A production-ready workforce and organizational culture is non-negotiable in the generative AI era, Petoff stated. Learning drives confidence, confidence drives behavior, and behavior repeated over time drives culture. You need to look at the training costs and benefits to make the right level of investment based on the specific organizational situation. Applying SRE principles to the training program itself led to a more consistent, reliable, and scalable experience, she concluded.

InfoQ interviewed Jennifer Petoff after her talk.

InfoQ: How can we tailor the training message to individual team members?

It has been revealed that jennifer Petoff: In the Generative AI era, we are all moving faster than ever, and it’s easy for training to get sacrificed as something important, but not urgent. Developers and operators may also look upon training with skepticism: Is this training tied to some fleeting corporate hype that will quickly be forgotten?

You need to convince them "what’s in it for me?" (WIIFM). The answer ties directly to what motivates people and varies among team members.

These "WIIFM" elements can be conveyed in written communications, through talking points to managers, or peer-to-peer discussions incorporated into the program.

InfoQ: How did you observe job-related behaviors?

Petoff: We built a hands-on training environment using real production infrastructure, but without real-world consumers and associated stakes. We could "break" our training infrastructure on demand, and students would work in groups of three to troubleshoot realistic production issues.

By structuring the training in this way, instructors could directly observe how participants used system diagrams, diagnosed production issues, and mitigated an outage. We looked at how people engaged with production during the exercises instead of quizzing them or asking for a simple nod of understanding.
