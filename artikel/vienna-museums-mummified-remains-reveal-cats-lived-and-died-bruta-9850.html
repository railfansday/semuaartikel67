Cats in ancient Egypt may have endured nine exceptionally hard lives, according to findings related to feline skulls in the collection of the Natural History Museum in Vienna.

According to recent reports, as reported by Popular Mechanics, research on 131 skulls from a funerary site in Middle Egypt led by zooarchaeologist Wim Van Neer of the Royal Belgian Institute of Natural Sciences revealed “disturbing” evidence that the cats may have been killed for the sake of offerings to the goddess known as Bastet. “Fourteen of the skulls show traumatic injuries, including blunt-force and sharp-force trauma,” the report reads. “Beyond fight wounds, some injuries were inflicted long enough before death to show signs of healing, which suggests that the animals may have endured physical violence from humans while still alive.” Related Articles Monumental Equestrian Statue of Roman Emperor Once Stood in Second Century Scotland Moab Petroglyphs Discovered Vandalized: 'Irreplaceable Cultural Resources'.

Sources indicate that it gets worse: “The cats’ treatment before death also seems to have been deplorable. Although no ancient records describe how the cats were kept, the researchers inferred that they were probably held in crowded enclosures. This could explain the fights, as well as the attacks on domestic cats by jungle cats, which were caught in marshlands or along the banks of the Nile when Egypt was lusher than it is today.”.

The research, published in the Journal of Archaeological Science, also shared grim news regarding the manner in which the killed cats met their ends. “Skull fractures were the most common cause of death, whereas evidence of strangulation occurred less frequently,” the researchers mentioned. “Our observations of traumatic lesions on the skulls provide evidence of fatal injuries, as well as cases of animals that survived violent interactions with humans.”.
