Ukraine accuses Russia of targeting rescue operation after 'double-tap' strike kills 72

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Ukrainian officials have accused Russia of targeting rescue and recovery operations two weeks after a deadly "double-tap" strike at a village close to the front line killed at least 72 people.

Ukraine says Russia carried out two attacks in succession on a humanitarian aid centre in the village of Komyshuvakha on 2 November - including the use of what it noted was a 3,000kg (6,600lb) aerial bomb on a shelter where residents had fled for safety.

Verified images posted in the days after the attack show damage to emergency vehicles and excavation equipment at the site.

The State Emergency Service of Ukraine (DSNS) told BBC Verify there were multiple Russian strikes during the rescue.

The DSNS said three police officers, four workers and two rescue dogs were injured while five vehicles were damaged during the operation, which has now concluded.

With the DSNS saying the death toll had risen to at least 72 people, the attack on Komyshuvakha has become the second deadliest single incident of the Ukraine war.

Emergency service vehicles have also been targeted.

Oleksiy, an intelligence officer investigating the incident for Ukraine's SBU security service, who asked to be identified only by his first name, noted rescue vehicles were "clearly marked" and accused Russia of "deliberately destroying humanitarian equipment".

He added the village's infrastructure and the people still living in Komyshuvakha are also being systematically targeted by drones and bombs. Oleksiy accused Russian forces of "outright terror against civilians, against peaceful residents" to make life on the front line impossible.

Industry observers note that BBC Verify has confirmed videos being shared by Ukrainian police that show rescue workers at the scene shouting "drone, drone" and running for cover on the day after the 2 November attack. A group can also be seen treating a victim.

According to recent reports, oleksiy stated rescue crews operated under a constant threat of attack. "The enemy knows perfectly well that what is being carried out there is essentially a humanitarian operation to search for the dead," he said.

BBC Verify has asked the Russian Ministry of Defence for comment specifically about the Komyshuvakha attacks but has not received a response.

A general statement released on 2 November by the Russian MoD said it had targeted energy and transport infrastructure used by the Ukrainian military across the country.

While there are no military or industrial facilities in Komyshuvakha it is close to a key route used for Ukrainian troop movements, supplies and evacuations. The village was previously reported to have been struck by the Russians on 28 October.

According to recent reports, local people headed to the shelter area on 2 November after being told by a local Telegram group that aid would be distributed between 10:00 and 12:00 local time (08:00-10:00 GMT). Ukrainian authorities noted an initial strike, at 09:45 local time, hit buildings above ground killing one woman.

Video shows moments between deadly Russian attacks on village in Ukraine.

Bodycam footage, released by Ukrainian police in edited segments and analysed by BBC Verify, shows people trying to free a casualty from the rubble before declaring she had died.

Throughout the footage, warnings of an impending second strike can be heard on police radios with increasing urgency until people are instructed to run to the underground shelter.

In a recent development, the same footage, which was taken around an hour after the first attack, goes on to show people in a shelter. As they wait in silence a large explosion can be heard and the video goes dark.

Aftermath video filmed later shows bodies being recovered from a large crater immediately above the shelter. Twisted iron and rubble from the structure below can be seen beneath the surface.

Funerals have been held almost daily for the victims of the attack, including locals working to administer aid distribution, those seeking to collect it and soldiers who officials say arrived to assist with rescue efforts.

Trevor Ball, bomb disposal expert at intelligence firm at Armament Research Services, stated a crater seen in the footage was consistent with the damage and depth left by a FAB-3000, a 3,000kg Russian bomb which can destroy underground structures.

His comments were echoed by Chris Proops, an ex-UK National Crime Agency investigator, who noted the scale of the damage at the scene matched the size of the FAB-3000.

The remnants of the wing from a glide bomb guidance kit.

Ball and Proops both confirmed verified photos of munition fragments obtained by BBC Verify from Ukrainian authorities show part of a guidance kit that is compatible with the FAB-3000, which increases the weapon's accuracy and allows it to be fired from a greater distance from the target.

International law experts told BBC Verify that Russia had committed a war crime if it knowingly attacked rescue workers.

Ben Saul, professor of international law at the University of Sydney, said this "appears to be either a deliberate attack on civilians or civilian objects - a war crime - or an indiscriminate attack - also a war crime".

Prof Samuel Moyn of Yale University mentioned "directly targeting rescuers or their equipment is a gross violation of international humanitarian law". Precautions have to be taken to ensure that strikes target combatants rather than others, he added.
