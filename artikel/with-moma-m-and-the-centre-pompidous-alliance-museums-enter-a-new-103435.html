With MoMA, M+, and the Centre Pompidou’s Alliance, Museums Enter a New Era of Collaboration

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

If you are one of the world’s leading museums of modern and contemporary art, the story you are expected to tell has become global.

In the blink of an eye, it seems, the aperture of attention has snapped open to latest world regions, publics, and technologies. Old narratives are yielding to a liberating, all-embracing polyphony. But this new story demands more, and different, art. Adding to the challenge, art prices are soaring, and so are operating expenses. As the museum pushes into the 21st century, reconfigured as a more open and inclusive enterprise, it must satisfy a host of novel and costly demands. Sources indicate that how do you keep up? You team up. That’s what Latest York’s Museum of Modern Art, Hong Kong’s M+, and Paris’s Centre Pompidou are doing. Announcing their new tricontinental alliance on October 30, they pledged to treat their collections as one. They are not tinkering around the edges. They are going into the heart of what it means to be a museum. Their goal is to kick the old habit of doing things by yourself, for yourself.

According to recent reports, why would institutions consider such a deep strategic alliance, and why now? Maximizing and leveraging the reach of individual collections is only the start. Museums are painfully aware that their current operating model is under strain. Governments, broke and distracted, are dialing back support. Philanthropy, generally still an American exception, has its own limitations. Earned revenues have plateaued. The room to maneuver is narrow. Doing more with less has become a shared predicament and imperative.

That’s the economic argument. But there’s more. Museum-making, like science, has become a truly international endeavor. Now that so many voices are entering the conversation about art history, it’s best to tap different points of view and realms of expertise. Collaboration, in short, is good not just for efficiency, but for better outcomes.

Industry observers note that institutions already share exhibition development costs and revenues, and co-acquisitions are becoming routine. In state-run systems, clusters of museums sometimes share centralized services such as storage, conservation, and marketing. One member of the upcoming consortium, the Centre Pompidou, already has a constellation network stretching from Belgium to China. Other museums are also teaming up. Earlier this month, the Metropolitan Museum of Art and the Shanghai Museum confirmed their commitment to foster “deeper collaboration between the two institutions, including exhibitions, research initiatives, and curatorial exchange.”.

Even so, there is enormous field-wide replication of effort. For the tripartite alliance, research and curatorial collaboration are likely to be just the tip of the iceberg.

Industry observers note that as it happens, I wrote these words from one of the nodes in the partnership, Hong Kong, while participating in the M+ Academy, a upcoming professional development program for museum leaders in the Asia-Pacific region that my firm helped develop. Our meetings were proof positive of the value of closer dialogue and coordination. Each day, we stumbled on new opportunities. Ideas were hatched, arguments sharpened, policies debated. Crucially, in this people-to-people business, we cemented bonds of familiarity and trust. One day, that may translate into loans and projects that would not otherwise have been conceived of, let alone greenlighted.

Some benefits of the alliance are already evident. On a recent busy Sunday, as thousands of visitors crammed into M+ on its fifth birthday, a basement gallery resonated with Janet Cardiff’s acoustic masterpiece, The Forty Part Motet (2001), a long-term loan from MoMA. The Hong Kong curators, meanwhile, are making plans for an M+ capsule gallery to be unveiled inside MoMA next year.

It has been revealed that M+ director Suhanya Raffel sees the roots of the alliance in a decentered art world. “Today, we are all aware how collaboration can add to our cultural authority in various places,” she told me. “This authority is explicit to our audiences, our donors, our corporate partners, the media, our peers, and finally, one hopes, our politicians.” The sustained partnership, if it goes beyond public pronouncements, promises to subtly alter the institutions. Its results will be measured not only in economies of scale, but in fresh undertakings, and perhaps innovative models for museum-making.

Sources indicate that none of which is to downplay the difficulties. The nuts and bolts of managing and building collections are exceedingly complex. So is the diplomacy around museum exchanges, with many spoken and unspoken considerations and crisscrossing commitments in play. Institutional cultures are harder to blend than collections.

It has been revealed that what will success look like? Ultimately, it would mean collections that complement, rather than duplicate, one another. Such integration presumably will mean that one day, a partner institution may pass on an acquisition because another already has rich holdings of the artist in question. But is that realistic? Getting to that place of mutual decision-making will, at best, take time.

For other museums in the US and elsewhere, the announcement sets an example and creates a permission structure to look beyond localized ways of working. In many cities, cultural institutions struggle alone on opposite ends of town, competing for the same pool of visitors, trustees, and money. Their autonomous structure creates few incentives to band together.

Sources indicate that this is one reason why the Toledo Museum of Art has initiated (with my firm’s involvement) a network of museums across the American Midwest to share costs, expertise, and programming, and to explore upcoming ways of working. Bilateral partnerships can pave the way to more ambitious systemwide collaboration. But for now, such strategic operating alliances—let alone full-blown mergers of the sort we are now seeing in higher education— remain vanishingly rare. The integration of New York’s PS1 into MoMA and of the Neue Gallery into the Met are welcome exceptions to the rule.

On the last day of the M+ Academy, our group boarded a bus to Shenzhen, on the Chinese Mainland, where a museum-building boom is in full swing. At the Róng Museum of Art—still a construction site, but already festooned with thousands of digital lanterns on its undulating façades—we met the director, Pi Li, a veteran of M+.

According to recent reports, the conversation promptly turned to how the museum will fill its galleries. The answer was obvious: Institutions across Asia and in the rapidly evolving Global South are hungry to collaborate, chiefly by bringing in exhibitions from more established counterparts. For museums willing to share and open up, a golden age of alliance-building may await.

András Szántó Ph.D., whose most recent book is The Future of the Art World: 38 Dialogues, is the founder of the Fresh York–based cultural strategy firm András Szántó LLC.
