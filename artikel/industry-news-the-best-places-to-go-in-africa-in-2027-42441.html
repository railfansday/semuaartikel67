While editing this year’s list, one thing swiftly became very clear: 2027 is poised to be a banner one for cultural experiences across Africa. While chatting with our writers, I kept finding compelling reasons to go back to my favorite corners—the Kigali Triennale’s return to Rwanda in September, Jazzablanca’s 20th anniversary in Casablanca, and an even more vibrant creative landscape in Tunis than the one I reported on six years ago. But now I’m adding Benin to my list, thanks to four—yes, four—eagerly anticipated latest museums debuting in the year ahead that will showcase everything from modern art to critical artifacts recently repatriated from Europe. Elsewhere on the continent there are fresh ways to experience some of its familiar pleasures, owing to a high-end resort boom on the beaches of Zanzibar, a host of safari lodge openings in Zambia, eight new marine reserves in São Tomé and Principe, and a groundbreaking five-country trans-frontier conservation area. Be sure to make some time for rarer incentives to travel too, like a once-in-a-generation “superbloom” in South Africa and the “eclipse of the century” over Luxor, Egypt (it also helps that there are some swish new places to stay at these places).But one of the most endearing stories of an African destination of the past year may be Cape Verde: Is the “World Cup effect” going to bring a wave of curious travelers to the archipelago? Read on to decide for yourself. —Sarah Khan.

Go for: a museum boom and a vibrant cultural milieu.

Home to ancient Kingdom of Dahomey, Benin has transformed into a destination for heritage tourism.

It has been revealed that benin is a place where history runs deep: It’s home to the ancient Kingdom of Dahomey, the spiritual traditions of Vodun, and Ouidah—one of West Africa’s significant ports in the transatlantic slave trade. Now the country is turning that history into a upcoming era of cultural tourism, while creating ways for travelers, particularly people of African descent, to reconnect with their heritage.

Ouidah, an especially key destination for those tracing their ancestry, is central to that effort. In 2025 the government revealed plans to invest $1.4 billion in tourism and cultural development, including for infrastructure, heritage restitution, and indigenous traditions. In 2027 that transformation is expected to become more visible with a host of fresh museum openings, like the Museum of Modern Art in Cotonou; the International Museum of Memory and Slavery in Ouidah; and the Museum of the Epic of the Amazons and Kings of Dahomey in Abomey. The latter of which is expected to house 26 royal artifacts repatriated by France in 2021.

Vodun Days, an eight-day celebration of Benin’s indigenous spiritual traditions taking place January 2–9, 2027, will showcase ceremonies, music, and dance. Meanwhile, Villa Karo in Grand-Popo—a bridge between artists and researchers from Finland, Sweden, and West Africa—will continue its residency program, which brings artists to its seaside research center to connect with the local community through workshops, concerts, and exhibitions. The famous stilt village of Ganvié, meanwhile, remains open to visitors as its Reinventing the Lakeside City of Ganvié project upgrades tourism infrastructure. Benin is also beginning to make appearances on cruise itineraries. Two latest Swan Hellenic sailings in 2027 sailings feature the country, including an April voyage from Luanda to Accra and an October sailing from Ghana to Angola. —Harriet Akinyi.

Go for: beaches, festivals, and new-found buzz.

Newly introduced Cabo Verde Airlines flight routes launched in May 2026, and coupled with the “World Cup effect,” will make travel to Cape Verde more appealing than ever.

According to recent reports, during FIFA World Cup 2026, one of the most impressive performances came from Cape Verde, an archipelago of 10 islands off Africa’s Atlantic Ocean coast. Now the “World Cup effect” has gotten travelers curious about what the destination has to offer. It helps that getting there is easier than ever, thanks to newly introduced direct flights launched in May 2026 from Rhode Island’s T.F. Green International Airport on Cabo Verde Airlines. On the island of Sal, which boasts white-sand beaches and over 350 days of sunshine a year, adrenaline-driving activities range from swimming with lemon sharks to kite- and windsurfing, while those seeking a more relaxed experience can float in the saline pools of Pedra de Lume. The island’s luxury accommodation options are also getting an upgrade with Hyatt Regency Cape Verde Sal as well as a family-friendly all-inclusive Bahia Principe set to open in 2027. In March the 322-room Robinson Boa Vista will welcome its first guests to the easternmost island of Boa Vista, known as the Sahara in the Atlantic due to its desert-like flat plains. São Vicente and Praia are the islands to hit for local music—the samba-inspired São Vicente Carnival features dance performances, fireworks, and floats, while Praia hosts the annual Kriol Jazz Festival and the Atlantic Music Expo. Africa’s first Four Points by Sheraton resort opened in the port city of São Vicente last March, with 105 rooms and 22 suites overlooking either Laginha Beach, known for its turquoise waters, or the Monte Verde mountains. —Mazuba Kapambwe.

Go for: a vibrant cultural and design scene.

Casablanca's 2027 roster of cultural events include the 20th anniversary of Jazzablanca in July and the newly launched Morocco Design fair.

Casablanca stealthily enters 2027 as Africa’s most creative urban reinvention. It’s an apt year to visit, given the pioneering Royal Air Maroc flights, released in June 2026, linking Casablanca with Los Angeles—the first African connection to America's Pacific Coast. It is a poetic bridge, connecting the dream factory that conjured Casablanca’s mythic character with the modern metropolis redefining itself as a vanguard of contemporary Maghrebi culture. A cornerstone of this cultural renaissance is the 20th anniversary of Jazzablanca in July: Once an intimate gathering, it has evolved into North Africa's best boutique music festival, hosting emerging Afrobeat, jazz, and urban artists beneath the modernist skyscrapers in Anfa Park and on stages in the palm-lined Arab League Park beside the Nevada skate park. Design lovers, meanwhile, can admire the Museum of Photography and Visual Arts, which was inaugurated in June in the medina and is Tadao Ando’s sole architectural project on the continent. The museum and the recently launched annual Morocco Design fair, which spotlights high-end design studios, establish Casablanca as an emerging destination for cutting-edge design, music, and art. The city's heritage sites are getting a revamp too. The iconic 1916 Neo-Moorish Lincoln Hotel by Radisson Collection is being masterfully revived by French Moroccan architects OUALALOU + CHOI, who are pairing its historic façade with an über-minimalist interior. From the hotel’s doorstep, a upcoming tramline whisks you to the Casablanca Finance City (CFC), a glittering new commercial district composed of futuristic, climate-resilient buildings that pay homage to Casablanca’s legacy of concrete experimentation. Nearby, bougie restaurants like Le 7ème Ciel draw crowds and a new Radisson Red will open in 2027 to join an existing Radisson Blu. CFC is built on the old Anfa Airport, from which The Little Prince author and architecture school dropout Antoine de Saint-Exupéry flew a perilous mail route to Dakar in an open-cockpit Bréguet 14 biplane, returned for drinks at Le Petit Poucet, and lived with his fiery Salvadorean muse, Consuelo Suncín. Then, as now, the city was a nexus connecting continents and a frontier to the future. —Paula Hardy.

Go for: a ground-breaking cross-border safari project and birding adventure.

The Kavango-Zambezi Transfrontier Conservation Area, or KAZA TFCA, is the world's largest terrestrial transboundary conservation area.

Zambia, which has forgone visa requirements of 53 countries and has increased international flights, will be the center of attention.

In a world where ecological degradation and global discord are reasons to despair, Kavango-Zambezi Transfrontier Conservation Area, or KAZA TFCA, is more rising sun than ray of hope. Supported by five countries—Angola, Botswana, Namibia, Zambia, and Zimbabwe, the world’s largest terrestrial transboundary conservation area encompasses a region roughly the size of France and is watered by the Kavango, Zambezi, Chobe, Kwando, and Kafue river systems. Officially rolled out in 2012, it languished as a proposal on paper for years, but recent initiatives have seen it shake off bureaucratic paralysis: Green Growth for KAZA, the umbrella initiative, directly links community benefits to conservation. Several depleted parks have been rewilded; Africa’s first cross-border avitourism project, the Great Kavango Zambezi Birding Route, was recently launched; and connectivity improved—Shearzone Safari launched a charter service in Zambia in early 2026. Notably, Angola has entered the guided-safari arena with Luiana Plains Expedition Camp, the first to open in the 10.3-million-acre Luengue-Luiana National Park in July 2026. It will soon be followed by Natural Selection, which is developing a new 500,000-acre game reserve around Angola’s existing Cuatir Reserve. In Namibia’s unsung Zambezi Region, Thitake River Lodge opens on the banks of the Kwando in Bwabwara National Park late in 2026, while Nkasa Linyanti is the fabulous new linchpin in Nkasa Rupara National Park. But the KAZA TFCA region that’s experiencing the biggest proliferation is Zambia, buoyed by the scrapping of visa requirements of 53 countries and increased international flights. Ker & Downey launched its Zambia division with King Lewanika Lodge in remote Liuwa Plains National Park, the first luxury base from which to witness Africa’s second-largest wildebeest migration; its second location, Musesenji River Camp, opens in April 2027. And luring the well-heeled to 5.5-million-acre Kafue National Park are Anantara with Tented Camp Kafue River, and Classic Zambia with new Lwenge River Camp; both opened this summer. —Pippa de Bruyn.

Go for: a wave of gallery and festival openings turning a gorilla trek stopover into an art capital.

Kigali's art scene is ramping up as institutions like Kigali Triennial and Gihanga Institute of Contemporary Art platform Rwandan and East African artists.

Fresh Kigali properties are showcases for art, such as the nine-room Pinnacle hotel and members' club.

For years Kigali has been seen as little more than a pit stop before going gorilla trekking. Now the city is making a compelling case for staying awhile. The city’s vibrant food and fashion have already been putting it on the map, and now the art scene is getting noticed as several institutions build platforms for championing Rwandan and East African artists. September 2027 marks the second edition of the Kigali Triennial, which is primed to rival some of the continent’s more established art festivals, like ART X Lagos and Dak'Art Biennale. The Gihanga Institute of Contemporary Art, which opened at the end of 2025, highlights artists from across Africa and its diasporas, with a focus on East Africa with two exhibitions: “Space Encounters,” exploring human connection and memory, which is currently on through December 2026; and a collection of contemporary work by Sudanese artists opening in January 2027. This follows the debut of the Inyuguti Art Hub this year, which brought together 19 artists from across Africa and the UK, including Rwandan Elisa Niyogisubizo (also the gallery’s founder), Burundi-based Peter Kamale, and Nigerian Pascal Ejiofor. The lines between the art and hotel scenes are beginning to blur too, with a upcoming generation of properties doubling as cultural showcases. Among them is the nine-room Pinnacle hotel and members club that opened in early 2026 as Rwanda’s first women-led ultraluxury boutique property, showcasing the owner’s private art collection. Creative events are rippling beyond the capital, as Rwandan artists Willy Karekezi and Cedric Mizero are each launching new spaces in Musanze, the gateway town for gorilla trekking, in 2027: Karekezi will open a residency alongside his Indiba Arts Space in Kigali, and Mizero will open Umugenzi, an addition to the multisite Ikinyugunyugu art residency. —Vivienne Dovi.

Go for: the eclipse of the century and nostalgia-soaked Nile cruises.

Astrotourism is coming for Luxor and the Nile on August 2, 2027, thanks to the six-minute “eclipse of the century" and a wave of latest boats promising prime viewing spots along the ancient waterway.

According to recent reports, dubbed the eclipse of the century, 2027’s much-anticipated celestial event will reach its zenith in Luxor, Egypt, on August 2, when the moon will block out the sun for a record-breaking 6 minutes and 23 seconds. Fitting, perhaps, for a place where the mystical has always loomed large, the mountains hold secret treasures, the temples and tombs have been perfectly preserved roughly 3,000 years after they were built, and the Nile exudes a life force of its own. Travelers have come to this transcendent stretch of Upper Egypt, nearly 400 miles south of Cairo, for millennia. Luxor itself served as Egypt’s capital from 1570 BCE to 1069 BCE, but today is better known as the home of the Valley of the Kings, and the start or end point of nostalgia-infused Nile cruises to and from Aswan, a leisurely four- or five-day sail away. Both destinations are primed for a momentous 2027: In Luxor the much-loved Winter Palace—archaeologist Howard Carter’s base during his 1922 Tutankhamen tomb discovery—will reopen in July as a Mandarin Oriental after an extensive renovation exactly 120 years after its official inauguration. Aswan’s own grande dame, the Old Cataract, which famously inspired Agatha Christie’s best-selling novel Death on the Nile, has also been taken over by Mandarin Oriental and will reopen its Nile Wing in 2027 after a thorough upgrade. Mandarin Oriental will launch its first Nile cruise that connects the two, joining a flurry of other upcoming boats taking to the ancient waterway. Among them the Oberoi Melouk and the Oberoi Malekat dahabiyas, with only seven cabins apiece, and homegrown operator Kemetale’s second boat, Deshret, with its Nile-view sauna, plunge pool, and spa treatments. —Selina Denman.

Go for: a rare ‘superbloom’ of endemic flora and world-class wines at Africa’s southern tip.

Cape Agulhas, Africa's southernmost tip, offers a new interpretation center focused on the history and geology of Agulhas, as well as a 177-year-old lighthouse.

Chef Michael Broughton, formerly of Terroir in Stellenbosch, now leads the kitchen at historic Fisherman’s Cottage, putting seafood centerstage.

Sources indicate that cape Town. Winelands. Then head off on safari. That’s the typical circuit for travelers jetting into South Africa. But in 2027 look beyond to the Overberg—“over mountain” in Afrikaans—region, where wildfires have triggered a floral extravaganza not seen in decades. Base yourself in Hermanus for memorable whale watching from the seven-and-a-half-mile Cliff Path. The Marine Hotel, a seafront landmark since 1902, has unveiled a fynbos-inspired revamp of its public spaces and seafood-driven Pavilion Restaurant. There’s a newly introduced culinary energy in town too: Chef Michael Broughton, previously at Terroir in Stellenbosch, has turned the historic Fisherman’s Cottage into a hotbed of polished Cape coastal cuisine (don’t miss the spaghetti aglio e olio with local rock lobster). Broughton’s wine list leans into estates from the nearby Hemel-en-Aarde (Heaven and Earth) valley, where cellars are known for exceptional Pinot Noir and Chardonnay. Creation Wines offers a new sculpture-led vineyard walk, and opens a private tasting room in May 2027 aimed at wine lovers looking for a deep dive into the cool-climate terroir. Bouchard Finlayson, one of the oldest estates in the valley, reopens in late 2027 after an extensive renovation, with a new tasting space and art collection.

Industry observers note that set in the heart of the Cape Floristic Region, Grootbos Private Nature Reserve is a biodiversity hot spot. Wildfires that swept the area in 2025 sparked a superbloom of latest growth, including dormant species that haven’t flowered in decades like wild orchids and the fire-dependent Gladiolus overbergensis. Guided botanical walks and drives get you in the thick of the fynbos, but a new equestrian center also makes this a great choice for horse-riding holidays. Long popular with families, Coot Club, on the nearby Klein River Lagoon, expands in 2027 with a pair of new two-bedroom boathouses and brings a new wellness focus with outdoor yoga sessions and in-room wellness treatments. Toast it all with a sundowner in the refreshed Flamingo Bar upstairs in the original homestead. An hour’s drive east, Cape Agulhas, the southernmost tip of Africa, is a fitting finale. A new interpretation center delves into the history and geology of Agulhas, after which you can climb the 177-year-old lighthouse and stand on the rocky shoreline where the Indian and Atlantic Oceans meet. —Richard Holmes.

Go for: new marine reserves and World Heritage Sites.

Often known as the African Galapagos for its high concentration of endemic species, the island nation of São Tomé and Principe is surrounded by clear waters full of manta rays, sharks, and migrating humpback whales.

In a recent development, on the islands of São Tomé and Príncipe, more than 155 miles off the coast of Gabon in the Gulf of Guinea, a thick rainforest punctuated by needle-like rock formations meets shores of white and volcanic black sand. One of the world’s least visited countries, and known as the African Galapagos for its high concentration of endemic species, this island nation is surrounded by clear turquoise waters brimming with manta rays and reef and nurse sharks. Humpback whales migrate through seasonally, and turtles begin to nest on the beaches in November before hatching between February and April. In 2025 the first two of eight newly introduced dedicated marine reserves, Santana and Ilhéu das Rolas, were approved; in 2026, six roças, or former Portuguese plantations, were designated UNESCO World Heritage Sites, recognized for their role in shaping the social, economic, and cultural history of the nation, which was once the world’s preeminent supplier of cacao. Two of these roças are a part of the Príncipe Collection—the hospitality arm of sustainable development group HBD Príncipe—meaning you can stay at these historically significant sites. One of the properties on offer is Hotel Belo Monte, which opened in June and sits high up on a hillside overlooking the rainforest and sea. A stay in this eco-hideaway, chicly styled with splashes of bold color, comes with cultural insight and an immersion into the wild surroundings. Another outpost, Sundy Praia, is set within what was once part of Príncipe’s second-largest cocoa plantation on the remote northwest tip and hosts guests in tented villas backing the golden shores on the remote northwest tip of the island. —Lauren Burris Hill.

Go for: ancient treasures and contemporary culture by the sea.

Villa Kahina, an intimate guesthouse that opened in September in Tunis.

In the blue and white village of Sidi Bou Saïd, the six-room Villa Kahina offers views of the Mediterranean.

Designated 2027’s Arab Capital of Tourism, this languid North African city now pulses with the energy of a new generation of Tunisian creatives and entrepreneurs. Events planned in the historic Medina range from TedX Pasha Street talks and musical retreats to artisanal workshops, many organized by the beloved Dar Ben Gacem boutique hotel and cultural hub, which will also host a rooftop event for August’s solar eclipse. Across the Medina, near the Great Mosque of Zitouna, Dar Ben Turkia's early 2026 opening expands the luxury-hotel options that preserve the area’s history. The elegant eight-suite hideaway has an in-house hammam and spa, so it doubles as a wellness retreat in the heart of the Medina. Beyond the medieval city’s labyrinths, the sun-kissed ruins of ancient Carthage spill toward the coast. Anchoring these archaeological riches is the Carthage National Museum, slated to reopen by year-end after an eight-year overhaul. For more contemporary treasures, head downhill to the city’s leading art gallery, Selma Feriani, which in 2027 will exhibit major Tunisian artists Mohamed Amine Hamouda and Rafik El Kamel.

Nearby, sleek seaside city La Marsa gives way to the historically working-class neighborhood of Bhar Lazreg, where artists and makers have been repurposing spaces. Anchors like the B7L9 Art Centre (built by and for the area’s community), and Phosphor Creative District—showcasing local talent who specialize in everything from fine art to design—are transforming the area into a meeting place for artists, demonstrating why the neighborhood has become known as one of Tunis’s coolest. Meanwhile, fashionable La Marsa remains a firm favorite with the stylish set, and mid-2026 restaurant openings like Oto, a Japanese izakaya, and Sujet, a hybrid café and design store, keep things fresh. When you’re ready for a break, make your way up the coast to Birdie at The Clubhouse in Gammarth, which opened in August 2026, or south to Villa Kahina, an intimate guesthouse that began welcoming guests in September. Perched on the forested edge of the iconic blue and white village of Sidi Bou Saïd, this whitewashed six-room sanctuary with sweeping Mediterranean views feels almost untouched by time. —Lee Middleton.

Go for: a host of fresh hotels from which to experience some of Africa’s best beaches.

Beyond its beaches, Zanzibar is set to welcome two latest luxury properties: Anantara Zanzibar Resort and Residences in 2027, followed by Four Seasons Resort Zanzibar at Pongwe in 2028.

Recent findings suggest that most visitors arrive in Zanzibar with postcard perfection already in mind—turquoise shallows, swaying palms, and dhows tipping into the sunset—but the island’s enduring drawcard is still Stone Town: a UNESCO-listed tangle of coral-rag limestone alleys, carved Omani doors, and clove-scented air, where Africa, Arabia, and India have intersected for centuries. In 2027 Zanzibar gets its most serious branded hotel wave yet with the 181-key Anantara Zanzibar Resort & Residences on the north coast, and in 2028 it will welcome, the 60-villa Four Seasons Resort Zanzibar at Pongwe, widely regarded as the loveliest beach on the east coast. The latter is a short flight from the brand’s Serengeti lodge and has been built with a bush-and-beach pairing in mind. The freshest energy is homegrown, though, and it has moved away from the overdeveloped north. In Jambiani, on the sunrise-facing southeast coast, the seven-villa Ma’a Salama (“go in peace”) opened in mid-2026 as a nonprofit that doubles as a hospitality school, training island youth in exchange for a year’s free education. Ma’a Salama’s spacious villas are named for spices and are cooled by bioclimatic design rather than air-conditioning, and four are crowned with rooftop teahouses, designed in Stone Town’s famous Emerson’s teahouses. On the quiet southwest coast at Kizimkazi, Kizikula is the antithesis of a resort: no sign, no jetty, just a jungly pathway leading to what feels more like a private home or artist’s enclave than a hotel. At low tide, guests access the beach through a secret gap in the coral cliff. Designed by Mumbai’s Case Design, its coral-limestone rooms sit in wild permaculture gardens. In 2026 the resort added 19 rooms, and in December it will open a newly introduced rooftop sunset bar and a three-floor wellness center, where treatments incorporate ingredients like Zanzibari cinnamon, turmeric, and cardamom. As of October 2026 Airlink flies the first-ever nonstop route between Cape Town and Zanzibar, with low-cost FlySafair rumored to be expanding its regular Johannesburg service to include Cape Town too. The UK gets its own first direct link in November 2027, courtesy of TUI Airways’s Gatwick service, along with a direct London flight from Air Tanzania. —Jane Broughton.
