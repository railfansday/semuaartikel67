Breaking: Spain Lets Anti-Money Laundering Unit Freeze Crypto Accounts Without a Judge

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Spain has given its financial intelligence unit the power to freeze crypto accounts on its own authority, without first getting a judge’s approval, when it suspects money laundering or terrorist financing.

The change was published in Spain’s official state gazette on Wednesday as part of Royal Decree-Law 44/2026, a sweeping decree that mostly deals with consumer credit contracts and the sale of bad loan portfolios. Its sixth final provision adds a newly introduced Article 46 bis to the country’s 2010 anti-money laundering law.

The upcoming article lets the Executive Service of the Commission for the Prevention of Money Laundering and Monetary Offenses, known as Sepblac, suspend or refuse to authorize a transaction in progress. It can also suspend the use of a bank, payment or crypto-asset account, or a business relationship, when there are “indications” that it could be tied to money laundering or terrorist financing.

According to the text, the goal is to preserve the funds while Sepblac carries out its analysis and checks whether the suspicion holds up. If it does, the unit passes the information to the competent authorities so they can take further action.

The decree sets two separate time limits. Sepblac can hold a pending transaction for no more than 10 business days, and it can freeze an account or business relationship for no more than five. Either hold must be lifted sooner if it is no longer needed to preserve the funds.

Recent findings suggest that when the suspicion comes from a suspicious activity report filed by a bank or other regulated firm, Sepblac must issue the suspension within three business days of that report. When the case comes from the unit’s own analysis, the decree says it must act “as soon as possible.”.

Once those deadlines pass, the freeze ends unless a court ratifies or extends it at the request of the public prosecutor’s office. Appeals against a court’s decision follow Spain’s criminal procedure law.

The decree also shields Sepblac, its staff and the firms that carry out its orders from liability. Firms that ignore a suspension order commit an offense under the anti-money laundering law. Sepblac can also act at the request of Spanish police and security forces, or of financial intelligence units in other EU states and outside the bloc.

Until now, Sepblac could only use this power when a foreign financial intelligence unit asked it to. The government said in the decree’s preamble that the limit is a deficiency the Financial Action Task Force will assess, and that waiting longer could hurt Spain’s international evaluation.

Recent findings suggest that the government also said the speed of financial transactions, especially online, lets possibly criminal funds move before authorities can finish their analysis. The provision transposes Article 24 of an EU directive adopted in May 2024 on the mechanisms member states must set up to fight money laundering.

Sources indicate that the rule takes effect Jan. 25, 2027, two months after the decree’s publication. As a royal decree-law, it must also be put to a vote in Spain’s Congress of Deputies within 30 days to be validated or repealed.

According to recent reports, the decree does not name individual crypto platforms, and it is not yet clear how exchanges operating in Spain will receive and carry out the orders. Several European governments have been moving the same way. Poland’s parliament voted 240-202 this month to let officials freeze bitcoin and bank funds of people on a national sanctions list before a court reviews the case.

Industry observers note that other moves in Europe have gone the opposite way. France’s Senate rejected amendments that would have forced holders to declare self-custody wallets worth €100,000 or more, while Binance is closing accounts in four EU countries at the end of the year.

Under the decree, crypto-asset accounts fall under the same five-day freeze limit as bank and payment accounts.
