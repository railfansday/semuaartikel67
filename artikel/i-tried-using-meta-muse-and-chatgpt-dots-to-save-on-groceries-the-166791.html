I Tried Using Meta Muse and ChatGPT Dots to Save on Groceries. They’re Not Quite There Yet

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, high grocery prices have been in the news for years now. The cost of eggs, how much you’re paying for a bowl of cereal and whether or not meat is back on the menu are the recurring subjects of nationwide headlines.

So before you drop a fortune on gas and drive out to the grocery store to spend even more on sustenance in your prep for hosting or bringing dishes to your holiday celebrations, you might consider using an AI agent to find you the best deal possible.

Although, in my experience, it’s not as simple as the AI marketing makes it sound. Here’s what happened when I tried to use AI agents to find the lowest possible prices on groceries.

AI agents such as Meta Muse and ChatGPT’s Dots can only crawl so many sites and online fliers for prices. Some stores and e-commerce sites have blocked AI agents from accessing their URLs. The first step in finding cheaper groceries with AI agents is to check which sites and stores they can access for price comparisons.

Where both agents missed a step was in the limitations they put on their searches, at first only looking at the retailers I had shown via my receipts and a few other big-name grocery stores.

I clarified in the chats and in a 20-minute voice conversation with Muse and a 14-minute voice conversation with Dots that they should expand their horizons and look at discount online retailers like Martie.com, and check what they could around advertised pricing and offerings at local farmers’ markets.

Muse stuck to the basics like Ralphs, Target and Walmart, and then expanded to Sprouts, Vons and Martie.com when prompted.

After further nudging, Muse also reviewed user-reported pricing from Costco (I don’t have a membership), gave me vague information about Grocery Outlet, suggested Misfits Market/Imperfect Foods, ruled out Amazon Fresh and gave me some sample pricing from a local butcher for meats.

Muse also checked some local bakery prices after I mentioned I’d like to find alternatives to sweet treats at a more affordable price.

Dots checked all the usual places that Meta Muse checked with a few extras thrown in — Smart & Final for produce, meat and some packaged products, and Super King’s monthly ad.

Recent findings suggest that neither of the tools checked Trader Joe’s. Muse said that the grocery chain doesn’t show prices of its products online, despite me finding that the Trader Joe’s website shows prices of its products online. Both Dots and Muse admitted to only checking the local store’s pages when asked.

You could spend some time making a list from scratch of all the groceries you might need every two weeks and give that list to the AI agent for its search. You could also skip all that and take some pics of your most recent grocery receipts and online payments and have the AI agent build the list itself.

I had some receipts saved, and the AI agents I used for this test were able to put together an accurate grocery list from them. I also provided emails showing receipts from online orders of household items and groceries for the past month to build my orders.

According to recent reports, muse took my receipts and concluded that, surprise, surprise, shopping at Gelson’s was not as cost-effective as shopping at Walmart. It spat out a lot of specific information about comparisons on some of the items in my list, but completely ignored others.

Dots looked over my receipts and recommended I try shopping at Super King for most fresh meat, produce and a few grains, and keep Walmart as my main option for packaged products like pasta sauce.

Both tools tackled this task in a way I was not expecting — as a narrative exploration of my overall grocery spending, with generalized suggestions supported by specific examples of spot pricing.

It was kind of like they’ve been optimized to write essays about grocery shopping as a school assignment, and not to be agentic personal assistants for real-world work.

Maybe it was the way I phrased the task, but I was hoping to get an actual shopping list with price comparisons for each item, or at least equivalent items, and a guide on where and how to procure said items.

Neither AI agent provided this autonomously and had to be directly prompted to provide grocery lists in response to a very specific request.

Sources indicate that AI agents are supposed to be pros at anticipating the needs of their human bosses. I was keen to see how the always-on AI agents would handle managing the recurring needs of a small household and if they might make any autonomous suggestions meant to save me money in ways I hadn’t anticipated.

It has been revealed that at no point did Muse seem to be actually thinking outside the strict parameters of the review of the items on the receipts I sent. This felt more like a conversation about what I’d previously purchased with a side serving of attempts to get me to connect Instacart to the tool.

It was also the most confusing grocery shopping experience I have ever had. That might be on me, but I would assume the agent might ask some questions, like how often I usually shop, what my monthly budget for groceries is, and how many people and animals live in the house. Muse didn’t ask any of those things and had to be prompted consistently.

Muse sent me the final grocery list as a raw Markdown file, which was hilarious. I got a .pdf version after explicitly asking for one.

Sources indicate that my original total with one in-person shop and one online order was about $180. Muse’s total with three in-person shops and one online order came in at around $153.

Dots was slightly better suited to this task after some prodding, and gave me some options for building prep meals with low-effort, high-protein recipes based on how I usually shop.

According to recent reports, dots’ final grocery list included everything I asked for, but still missed some key items, like solid substitutes for pricey prepped foods. Dot’s final total with two in-person shopping trips and a whole lot of missing items came out to around $85.

This test reinforced my findings about how agentic AI tools tackle domestic or traditionally invisible labor tasks. In my limited use cases so far, they are about as useful as a loose dog turd tumbling around in a clothes dryer.

If you’re not looking to book a flight and hotel for a business conference in Philadelphia or put your Sweetgreen order on autopilot, the AI agents exhibit a startling lack of curiosity and autonomous thinking.

There’s still a large gap in common sense on the part of agentic AI for those of us hoping these tools will make our everyday lives outside of a 9-to-5 easier, more affordable or less hectic. I hope that changes as they learn.
