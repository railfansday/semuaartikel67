As FEMA pushes recovery onto states, a Miami startup pitches an AI case manager for disaster survivors

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

When the Palisades fire tore through his Los Angeles neighborhood in January 2025, Jonathon Nostrant’s house was damaged but still standing. Many of his neighbors’ homes were destroyed.

Industry observers note that the fire was only the first ordeal. Next, Nostrant encountered a tangle of federal, state, and local assistance programs, each with its own forms, eligibility rules, and deadlines. Survivors with insurance must also report their payouts to every agency they apply to, because FEMA cannot pay for losses an insurer already covers.

Industry observers note that insurance was already a sore subject in the Palisades. A year before the fire, State Farm dropped roughly 1,600 policies in the neighborhood, Fortune reported as the fire burned.

“Our insurance was fighting with us,” Nostrant noted in an interview, describing his own claim.

That experience led to AidFinder, a Miami-based startup Nostrant cofounded that uses AI to match disaster survivors with programs they may qualify for, explain eligibility rules, and prepare applications. Survivors review everything before submission. The intended customers, though, are states, which would offer the platform to residents under their own banner.

Recent findings suggest that for now, it is a pitch. AidFinder hasn’t signed its first state customer, and survivors have yet to use the platform, Refresh Miami reported on Nov. 17. Nostrant told the outlet the company is in talks with 30 states, including Florida, and that Craig Fugate, who ran FEMA under President Barack Obama, has accepted a seat on its board.

Nostrant has spent much of his career teaching machines to listen. In 2008, as a student at the University of Southern California, he debuted Moshi, a voice-activated alarm clock that responded to spoken commands and, in hindsight, foreshadowed Siri.

Industry observers note that he later founded Ivee, an early AI-powered home assistant that let customers control connected devices by voice. AidFinder applies the same idea to a far messier problem: turning a survivor’s account of what happened into the right applications for the right agencies.

The pitch arrives as Washington pushes more of the recovery burden onto states. FEMA Administrator Cameron Hamilton has told state emergency managers to prepare to handle more of the work themselves, and states have faced unusually long waits for federal disaster declarations.

Recounting Hamilton’s remarks at a National Emergency Management Association gathering, Nostrant described the federal message this way: “We want to empower the states, and we want to help empower the local counties and cities to be equipped, not just to respond to the disaster but help people recover.”.

In a recent development, aidFinder’s answer is a branded front door. “We’re giving the state the ability to launch a landing page that’s powered by AidFinder,” Nostrant mentioned, describing how residents would be matched with recovery programs.

Sources indicate that whatever tools states adopt, recovery paperwork starts with evidence. FEMA advises survivors to photograph or film damage inside and outside their homes before cleanup and to list damaged belongings, records that support both an insurance claim and a federal aid application.

Recent findings suggest that aidFinder is built around the same principle. Its platform lets residents store identification, tax records, and photos of their homes in a secure digital vault before disaster strikes. The question now is whether a state will pay to offer it.
