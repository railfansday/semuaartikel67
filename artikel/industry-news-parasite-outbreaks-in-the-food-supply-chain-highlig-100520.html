Google searches for "cyclospora" predicted where bagged salad sales fell during parasite outbreaks.

A food recall looks like a supply problem: bags pulled from the shelf, a gap in the produce case. During six cyclospora outbreaks between 2018 and 2022, stores revealed no sign of cutting back on bagged salad, and prices barely moved, yet households bought less of it anyway.

Weekly purchases ran 3.89 percent below normal, on average, across those outbreak and recall periods, and the missing sales added up to roughly $374 million.

Where sales fell depended not only on where people got sick but on where people were typing “cyclospora” into Google.

It has been revealed that xuerui Yang ran the analysis as a doctoral student at The Ohio State University. She is now a postdoctoral scholar at the University of Florida’s Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences (UF/IFAS).

The dollar figure is an estimate from household purchase records, and the search counts stand in for a region’s exposure to cyclospora outbreak news, not for what any one shopper read.

It has been revealed that in an interview with Earth.com, Yang put the main lesson in one line. “News travels through media markets, not state lines,” she mentioned.

In a recent development, cyclospora cayetanensis, a single-celled parasite first identified in 1993, can live in soil or water for months. It survives the chlorine washes that processing plants run produce through.

Like other kinds of food poisoning, the infection usually brings watery diarrhea, cramps, bloating and a lost appetite, and most people recover on their own.

People with weakened immune systems can become severely ill or stay sick for a long time.

Industry observers note that large cyclospora outbreaks were rare before 2018. That year, reported outbreaks were tied to 1,165 illnesses nationwide, and the numbers have stayed high since. Packaged salads have been implicated more and more often.

Yang’s team picked six large multistate outbreaks from 2018 to 2022. Four of them traced to bagged salad mix. Together, they accounted for 2,157 illnesses, about two-thirds of every outbreak-linked case reported to the CDC in that span. The rest were smaller events.

Recent findings suggest that recalls followed the 2018, 2019 and 2020 outbreaks. The 2018 and 2020 salad recalls were traced to a single U.S. manufacturer and spread to store brands. The last two outbreaks in the study ended with no recall at all.

Earlier research on food scares measured public attention through news coverage, which is usually counted by state or for the whole country.

Yang’s team used something finer: how often people searched Google for “cyclospora,” week by week, in each of 210 media markets.

Nielsen draws those markets to map which TV and radio stations a household receives, and they follow broadcast signals rather than borders, so one market can straddle two states.

A second index, built from the public Google Trends tool, tracked general searches for salad, lettuce and basil, with outbreak terms filtered out.

The team matched both against weekly purchase records from a NielsenIQ panel of about 46,000 households a year, 12 million household-weeks in all.

The search index behaved the way a news measure should. Spikes in “cyclospora” searches lined up with spikes in news coverage.

During the 2020 cyclospora outbreak, search activity broadly followed where illnesses were being reported, and Illinois and Iowa reported the most.

Searches for “cyclospora” were linked to the steepest declines in purchases, and general searches about salad and leafy greens were linked to much smaller ones.

It has been revealed that the authors read that as information working in two directions. News that names the parasite raises alarm, while ordinary searching about salad tends to settle it.

Recalls mattered on their own. A single week of a cyclospora recall cut purchases in affected states by about 3.2 percent compared with ordinary weeks, and recall periods ran 22 to 31 weeks.

In a recent development, households whose salad purchases included one recalled brand bought no less than usual, but households whose purchases included two or more recalled brands bought noticeably less. One recalled brand, it seems, made little difference; a second one did.

“What people read online shows up in what they put in their carts,” Yang said.

To check that worry rather than empty shelves explained the decline, the team pulled store-level sales records for 2018 and 2020, the two years with salad recalls.

Store by store, across more than 100 million transactions, there was no sign that retailers cut what they stocked, and prices barely moved.

It has been revealed that media markets gave the team a natural test. In the authors’ example, someone in Washington, D.C., watches the same local news as someone in Virginia.

So a Virginia cyclospora outbreak reaches D.C. households even when no contaminated salad was ever sold there.

Industry observers note that in states with no reported illness, households that shared a media market with an affected state bought 3.33 percent less salad per week than in outbreak-free weeks. Those households were a small slice of the data, so the estimate is less precise than the main one.

Yang doesn’t read that as a case for softer warnings. “People in unaffected states still bought less salad when they shared local news with an affected state,” she told Earth.com.

Sources indicate that “And we don’t see this as a reason for quieter warnings. When shoppers stayed away from affected products, they’re also protecting themselves. What matters is clear, timely information.”.

It has been revealed that the 2020 outbreak was the biggest in the study, with 771 illnesses across 14 states. Counting the recall that followed, it reached 33 states and more than 190 million people, and it produced the largest loss, close to $190 million.

The bigger outbreak, though, drew the smaller reaction from shoppers.

Recent findings suggest that purchases fell about 3.1 percent in 2020 against 4.6 percent two years earlier. Furthermore, the 2020 estimate was weak enough that chance can’t be ruled out as the explanation. Searches for “cyclospora” were also lower and more scattered that year.

Asked by Earth.com what surprised her about the smaller reaction, Yang pointed to timing. “Shoppers barely reacted until the recall came,” she remarked.

It has been revealed that “Once specific products were named, purchases dropped. 2020 was also the summer of COVID, and that likely pulled attention away from other health news.”.

The 2018 outbreaks cost about $79 million, roughly 1.6 percent of a bagged salad market worth about $4.91 billion a year by the industry estimate the authors cite.

Recent findings suggest that yang’s earlier work put the retail cost of each of two salmonella outbreaks tied to onions above $1 billion. The authors attribute part of that gap to salmonella being a name that more shoppers recognize.

In a recent development, google searches catch people looking for information online and miss what they hear on TV, on the radio or from a neighbor. Regions that get their outbreak news offline may be underrepresented.

The search data also can’t show what any individual shopper read, so the results describe regions, not people.

The CDC records the date of a first illness, which comes before the public hears anything. So the study measured changes over whole outbreak-and-recall windows rather than the first days of alarm.

This year’s record, roughly 20,000 confirmed cyclospora cases by the CDC’s count, falls outside the study.

Yang said it raises the questions she would like to explore next: how the scale of the outbreak and the shifting information shaped what people bought.

Sources indicate that for a shopper standing in the produce aisle in the middle of one, she had plainer advice. “Follow the official alerts,” she said. “Early on, the source information is often unclear, so if you can’t tell where your salad came from, it’s fine to avoid it for a while.”.

According to recent reports, “Once a product is confirmed, check whether your salad is on the recall list,” Yang mentioned. “Washing won’t effectively remove the parasite.” Cooking, she added, kills it.

The full study was published in the journal Risk Analysis.
