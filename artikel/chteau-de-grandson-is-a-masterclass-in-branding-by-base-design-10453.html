Base Design recasts the thousand-year-old ChÃ¢teau de Grandson as a living identity, heraldry and bold contemporary type built to let people in.

The castle above Lake NeuchÃ¢tel has spent a thousand years keeping people out. It has been a seat of power, a battlefield, a prison, a hospital, a tobacco factory and a museum, and after fifteen years of restoration it reopened in 2026 with a upcoming permanent exhibition and a new identity from Base Design Geneva. The studio calls the idea Castle, Recast, and the work runs on confrontation: medieval stone and carved wood set against loud, flat, contemporary graphics that refuse to behave like heritage branding. A harlequin diamond pattern carries the clash, flipping between blue and purple, pink and navy, orange and burgundy, so the castle reads as a place with a pulse rather than a monument under glass.

The wordmark does the heavy lifting. A condensed grotesque sets CHÃTEAU GRANDSON, SCHLOSS GRANDSON and CASTLE GRANDSON in stacked capitals down the length of three flags, one language per flag, each on its own clashing colour pair. The same voice turns up on the phone screen, where FIND YOUR WAY stacks three words across the harlequin field above a pill-shaped START YOUR JOURNEY button, and on the exhibition guides, where FASHION and WARS run huge over armour and portraits.

It has been revealed that holding the system together is an interlocked C and G monogram, drawn with the weight of a wax seal and used small: stamped on the flags, tucked into the corner of the phone screen, pressed into a silver signet ring. It is the one quiet element in a loud system, and it works because everything else is shouting.

The identity lives on objects, not just on paper. The pattern jumps from the phone screen onto harlequin socks and a skateboard deck lettered SCHLOSS that rolls through a wood-panelled medieval hall, and the monogram turns up on a tote woven with knights on horseback and on the back of a black shirt worn on the castle staircase. Printed guides carry the same discipline: a shelf of exhibition books pairs oversized titles with armour photography and the small monogram at the foot of each cover.

In a recent development, nothing here pretends the castle is a ruin to tiptoe around. The colours are saturated, the type is oversized, and the merch reads like streetwear for a fortress. That is the point Base makes in its own words: built to keep people out, Grandson is now in the business of letting them in.
