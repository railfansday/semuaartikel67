Who was buried in Westminster Abbey's Roman coffin? A 150-year mystery may be solved

A mystery surrounding a skeleton discovered inside an ancient Roman coffin at Westminster Abbey may finally be approaching a solution, more than 150 years after the remains first attracted attention.

The limestone sarcophagus, formally excavated in 1869, carries an inscription naming Valerius Amandinus, a wealthy Briton who lived in London sometime between 300 and 400 AD. However, a large Latin cross carved into its lid dates to approximately the 10th or 11th century, suggesting that the person buried inside lived hundreds of years after the coffin was originally made.

It has been revealed that now, fresh archaeological research points to a surprising explanation. The remains likely belong to a prominent medieval monk, and researchers have narrowed the possible identities to two men who once led Westminster Abbey.

Two Medieval Abbots Emerge as Leading Candidates.

The new findings, published in the peer-reviewed Journal of the British Archaeological Association, come from Professor Warwick Rodwell, Consultant Archaeologist at Westminster Abbey.

Sources indicate that rodwell identifies Aelfwig, an abbot who died around 1020, as the strongest candidate. At the time, Westminster Abbey was a Benedictine monastery, and Aelfwig had served there as a monk before becoming its abbot.

According to recent reports, another possibility is Wulnoth, who succeeded Aelfwig and died in 1049. Both men are believed to have been buried at the Abbey, making either a plausible match for the skeleton.

"We have two viable contenders for the occupant of the sarcophagus," says Professor Rodwell, Consultant Archaeologist at Westminster Abbey.

"We do not know the date of birth of either. But either would fit equally well if we assume the Abbot was in his early twenties when the appointment was made and close to fifty when he died.

"The fact Aelfwig was already a monk of Westminster might favor him.".

According to recent reports, the elaborate burial provides another reason to suspect that the deceased held an notable position. Rodwell suggests that the individual was 'honored' with a 'fine sarcophagus', an unusual distinction that would be consistent with the status of an abbot.

Rather than commissioning an entirely newly introduced stone coffin, those responsible for the burial apparently reused an existing Roman sarcophagus. The man's wooden coffin was placed inside it, and the stone lid was modified with a prominent Latin cross. This may represent an early example of recycling a valuable object for an entirely different purpose.

New Excavations Reveal the Skeleton's Age.

According to recent reports, the Roman sarcophagus and its mysterious occupant have fascinated researchers for generations. Rodwell's latest conclusions draw on several investigations conducted with specialists over the years, including work associated with Channel 4's Time Team.

In 2009, the television program examined the archaeology and architecture of King Henry III's original sacristy, located along the north side of Westminster Abbey.

Recent findings suggest that the same area was the scene of an earlier accidental discovery in 1820, when workers encountered the Roman coffin and broke its lid into six pieces. The damage was repaired around the time the sarcophagus was formally 'discovered' in 1869.

More recently, Pre-Construct Archaeology, one of the UK's largest commercial archaeological and heritage organizations, carried out another excavation of the sacristy. Completed in 2025, the project was undertaken to support construction of the King Charles III Sacristy, a fresh facility at Westminster Abbey scheduled to open the following year.

As part of this investigation, human bone specialist James Young Langthorne of Pre-Construct Archaeology (PCA) conducted a fresh examination of the skeleton.

His analysis indicated that the deceased was a man between 35 and 49 years old who stood approximately 5 feet 9 inches tall. These findings differ considerably from an assessment made in the 1800s, which described the remains as those of a 'robust young man' no older than 30 and approximately 5 feet 7 inches tall.

Carbon Dating Confirms a 1,000-Year-Old Burial.

It has been revealed that one of the most important clues came from scientific dating of the remains. A bone sample was submitted to the Scottish Universities Environmental Research Centre for radiocarbon analysis, which produced a date range of 987 to 1052 AD.

In a recent development, this places the burial firmly within the late Saxon period, centuries after the Roman sarcophagus was originally created. The dates also align with the deaths of both Aelfwig and Wulnoth, strengthening the case that the skeleton belonged to one of these medieval abbots.

"This confirms that the reuse of the sarcophagus, and the associated remodeling of its cover, occurred in the late Saxon period.".

The results provide scientific support for the theory that an ancient Roman burial container was repurposed to honor an key religious figure nearly 1,000 years ago.

A Skeleton Thought Lost Was Hiding in Plain Sight.

In a recent development, the investigation also brought renewed attention to an unexpected discovery made in 2016. For years, researchers had believed that the skeleton was missing, leaving an notable part of the burial's history unresolved.

That assumption proved mistaken when the sarcophagus was transported to a conservation studio. PCA researchers removed its lid and were surprised to find an almost complete human skeleton still inside.

Sources indicate that the examination produced another clue that Rodwell describes as a 'remarkable latest discovery'. Inside the stone coffin, researchers identified the rectangular outline of a structure that had disappeared long ago. Its shape was preserved by a white residue left behind by lime, a material historically used in burials.

Rodwell believes the outline marks the position of the original wooden coffin that held the deceased before it was placed inside the Roman sarcophagus.

"Although no physical traces of that feature remained, it can only have been a timber coffin, which has completely decayed over the course of the seven centuries that the sarcophagus was buried in the sacristy courtyard," writes Professor Rodwell.

Industry observers note that together, the rediscovered skeleton, evidence of a wooden coffin, and new scientific dating have transformed researchers' understanding of this unusual burial. Although the man's identity has not been established beyond doubt, the findings suggest that a medieval abbot may have been laid to rest in a Roman coffin already centuries old.

Visitors can see the sarcophagus today in the Queen's Diamond Jubilee Galleries at Westminster Abbey.
