Posted November 18, 2026 | Reviewed by Gary Drevitch.

According to recent reports, we get a lot of things wrong about one another. We often think we understand what other people think of us, and assume that the handful of actions we observe tells us something meaningful about their personality as a whole. Someone behaves rudely, and we conclude they are an unpleasant person. Someone struggles to find work, and we wonder whether they are trying hard enough. We interpret other people’s behaviour through our own experiences, expectations and beliefs, often with considerable confidence and remarkably little information1.

Sources indicate that psychologists have spent decades documenting the many ways our perceptions of others can be distorted; we are frequently wrong about others and even ourselves. These biases have consequences far beyond our interpersonal relationships. They shape whom we trust, whom we blame, what we believe people deserve and, ultimately, the institutions and policies we create and sustain.

Sources indicate that many of us feel we are living through unprecedented times. Particularly since 2020, rapid technological change, political polarisation and growing uncertainty about the future have made the world feel increasingly difficult to understand. But people have felt similarly at other points in history, and often with good reason.

In nineteenth-century Britain, the Industrial Revolution was transforming almost every aspect of society. Population growth, industrialisation and urbanisation brought extraordinary economic opportunities alongside increasingly visible poverty, overcrowding and disease. Governments were faced with social problems on a scale that existing institutions were poorly equipped to manage.

One response was the Poor Law Amendment Act of 18342.

It has been revealed that for centuries, responsibility for assisting people experiencing poverty had rested largely with local parishes. Someone who became ill, lost their employment or could no longer support their family might receive money, food or other assistance without having to leave their home. The system was inconsistent, often ungenerous and increasingly expensive, but it afforded local authorities some discretion to respond to individual circumstances.

The 1834 reforms sought to make poor relief more efficient, less costly and less attractive to those thought capable of supporting themselves. Administration was reorganised, and assistance was increasingly tied to entering a workhouse. Relief outside the workhouse continued in some circumstances, for the sick and elderly, but even then, many elderly and disabled people still ended up in the workhouse.

Sources indicate that workhouses were deliberately designed to be places people would enter only out of desperation2. We are familiar with these institutions through Charles Dickens, whose Oliver Twist depicts a world in which poverty is often treated as a moral offence. Families were often separated, food really was gruel, and residents were subjected to arduous work and strict discipline. The severity was not an unfortunate consequence of inadequate resources but part of the intended deterrent.

According to recent reports, the reasoning was straightforward enough: If assistance was too easy or readily available, people might become dependent upon it rather than seek employment. In fact, educating children was, in theory, supposed to be part of a workhouse remit, but in practice the standard varied considerably. Make assistance sufficiently unpleasant, and those capable of supporting themselves would presumably find a way to do so.

Sources indicate that of course, this reasoning depended on a particular understanding of why people were poor in the first place. A person unable to find employment, earning wages insufficient to support a family, or living with illness could not necessarily improve their circumstances through the threat of punishment. But policies built around assumptions about individual motivation often failed to account for precisely those conditions.

I don’t think the nineteenth-century reformers were simply cruel or stupid. They were attempting to manage substantial social and economic changes with the knowledge, institutions and political priorities available to them. Some were undoubtedly concerned with improving administration and reducing costs, while others had a more punitive understanding of poverty. The reforms also served the interests of those who wanted to reduce their contributions towards poor relief.

But the consequences illustrate something notable: Our explanations of human behaviour influence the solutions we consider reasonable, even when those explanations are incomplete or mistaken.

Nearly two centuries later, we are living through another period of extraordinary transformation. Technology has changed how we work, communicate, form relationships and participate in society. Our scientific understanding of the world has expanded beyond anything nineteenth-century reformers could have imagined.

And, unlike our predecessors, we now possess the knowledge and technological capabilities to address many problems that once seemed insurmountable.

In a recent development, we have developed effective technologies that can substantially reduce greenhouse gas emissions3. Advances in agriculture have enabled us to produce enough food globally to feed the world’s population, despite its extraordinary growth4. Vaccines, public health measures and medical research have given us tools to prevent and respond to devastating diseases.

But climate change continues, millions experience hunger, and preventable diseases still cause enormous suffering.

It has been revealed that these problems are complex, and scientific and technological limitations remain. But increasingly, the question is not simply whether humanity possesses the knowledge or resources to improve people’s lives. The obstacles are frequently social, cultural, economic and political. They involve how resources are distributed, how institutions operate, whose interests they serve, and whether people can cooperate towards shared goals.

And underlying many of these obstacles are the explanations we give for why people believe and behave as they do.

Recent findings suggest that consider a more recent example of advocacy for punitive measures. Over several decades, psychiatrist James Gilligan studied violence in American prisons and sought to reform the way prisoners were treated5,6. In Massachusetts prisons, where violence, humiliation and abuse were commonplace, he developed reforms that emphasised treating prisoners with respect, providing psychological treatment and creating opportunities for education7.

Gilligan reported significant reductions in violence, and his research into prison education found extraordinarily low rates of reoffending among prisoners who completed college degrees. Even those regarded as among the most violent could, under different conditions, behave quite differently. But when William Weld became governor of Massachusetts in 1991, he opposed providing prisoners with free college education, arguing that people who couldn't afford university might start committing crimes just to get a degree in prison. His administration subsequently withdrew much of the state funding and undermined education delivery, despite reported drops in recidivism7,8. Sound familiar?

Neither understanding nor policy exists independently of the other. Our beliefs influence the systems we support, while those systems shape the circumstances and behaviours we subsequently observe. The resulting outcomes can then appear to confirm the assumptions that helped produce them—making the system self-reinforcing.

Recent findings suggest that institutions are also shaped by competing interests, and some people benefit considerably from arrangements that disadvantage others. Those with influence may understand perfectly well what a particular policy or economic incentive is likely to produce.

But the interests of a relatively small number of powerful actors can become difficult to challenge when much larger populations are persuaded that the existing arrangements serve their interests too, or that the problems those arrangements create are simply the fault of the individuals experiencing them.

This is where our understanding of one another becomes immensely consequential.

The way we explain another person’s circumstances influences how willing we are to help them, what we consider fair, and whether we recognise a problem as something that requires societal intervention. These explanations become part of our cultural understanding of how the world works, and help determine which institutions we defend and which we are prepared to change.

According to recent reports, this blog is about those explanations: How we come to understand other people’s behaviour, why we so often get it wrong, and what the consequences are for the societies we live in. I’ll draw on research across psychology, biology, anthropology, and the social sciences to explore how individual behaviour interacts with the environments in which it develops. Sometimes that will mean examining the assumptions we make about our friends, strangers and, most importantly, ourselves. At other times, it will mean considering how those same assumptions influence our attitudes towards responsibility, inequality, politics and the institutions that govern our lives.

Understanding these processes is only part of the task. If we want different outcomes, we also have to use that understanding to change the conditions that produce them.

Sources indicate that the nineteenth-century workhouse offers one illustration of what can happen when our assumptions about human behaviour inform our attempts to solve social problems. Seeing the same way of thinking manifest centuries later, despite all our accumulated knowledge, tells us something important about the real barriers to solving our problems.

One of the great challenges of our time is learning how to use knowledge to build societies that work better for the people living within them.

Perhaps a useful place to begin is by examining how much we still get wrong about one another and ourselves.
