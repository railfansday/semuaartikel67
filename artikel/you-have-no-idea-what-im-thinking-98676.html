In a recent development, I woke to the sound of my boyfriend getting out of bed, and my heart started racing. This is it, I thought. We’re getting engaged. We were in the Bahamas and had been dating for a few years. He had set an alarm so we could watch the sunrise, which was very out of character. He got up to brush his teeth, which added evidence to my theory.

As he brushed, I tried to make myself look effortlessly beautiful by raking my fingers through my hair. When he came in to “wake me up,” I smiled and followed him doe-eyed to the balcony, where we watched the sunrise. Any minute now, I thought. Any minute. But the minutes ticked by; he seemed relaxed and not at all ready to ask a life-changing question.

Finally, when the sun was up, he confirmed he was going back to bed. I remarked I was going to stay on the balcony a few more minutes so I could collect myself, put my face back together, and not come off as a psycho girlfriend.

According to recent reports, we did eventually get married and now have two wonderful children. Communicating is one of our strong points; however, on that day, I had no idea what was going on in his mind, and he had no idea what was going on in mine.

It has been revealed that theory of mind (ToM) is our ability to recognize that other people have their own thoughts, feelings, beliefs, knowledge, and intentions, and to use that understanding to make sense of their behavior. The name “theory of mind” can sound unapproachable, like an overly academic topic that belongs in scientific journals rather than dinner-table conversation; however, I’d argue it’s a basic foundation of sophisticated human communication, and deserves its time to shine.

You may not have heard the term theory of mind, but you're probably familiar with related ideas: perspective-taking, putting yourself in someone else's shoes, reading the room, empathy, social awareness, emotional intelligence, and people skills. All of these have something in common: ToM. But why is it called a "theory"? Because humans behave like amateur scientists all day, subconsciously creating models of what others are thinking and feeling, collecting data, and adapting their beliefs based on latest evidence. We can’t crack open someone’s skull, so we create a theory of what is in their mind. On the day I didn’t get engaged, my theory of my husband’s mind was that he was planning to propose, and I had collected data to support it. His theory of my mind was that I’d really enjoy the sunrise.

According to recent reports, I was introduced to theory of mind in 2012 while studying speech-language pathology at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. I was focused on children with hearing loss, and I learned how differences in access to everyday language and conversation can shape how kids with hearing loss understand others’ thoughts and beliefs. The research hooked me immediately. I began devouring everything I could find on the subject, eventually designing full-day workshops for educators and clinicians and presenting them across the country and internationally.

According to recent reports, the more time I spent teaching these workshops, the more I started seeing theory of mind everywhere: in misunderstandings with friends, arguments with my husband, and encounters with strangers whose behavior baffled me. My undergraduate degree was in political science, so I also became fascinated by how confidently we assign motives to people on the other side of a political debate. We decide they are uninformed, selfish, or gullible, often without knowing anything about their lives or the rationale for their belief. I began wondering whether recognizing the limits of our own assumptions might make us a little more curious about how someone else arrived at theirs.

Before long, my reading spilled into research on autism, humor, deception, marriage, mental illness, and prejudice. What started as a specialized clinical interest turned into a lens for understanding almost every kind of human interaction. At the same time, I grew increasingly surprised that such a useful concept remained so invisible to the public. I read a lot of popular psychology books. A lot. I cannot tell you how many times I have found myself wanting to scream into the pages, "You're talking about theory of mind! It's all theory of mind! Just call it what it is!" Well, maybe not all of it, but it hides under far more of our daily lives than we realize.

Recent findings suggest that more than anything, I hope to make a case for something we could all use a little more of: intellectual humility. My goal is not to prevent every misunderstanding, or to convince you to agree with everyone you meet. It is simply to help us notice the gap between what we actually know about another person and what we have decided must be true. That awareness can change what we do in a tense moment. Are we working with different information? Do we understand each other perfectly and simply want different things? Have I spent the last 20 minutes planning a wedding without just cause? We could all benefit from a little less certainty and a little more curiosity.
