Why Your Brain Feels Tired When Your Body Isn't

You’re sitting down at your computer to answer one simple email.

Sources indicate that you read the first paragraph—and re-read it to make sure you got everything. But the second paragraph is harder to read because your attention keeps getting pulled away—to other messages, to your calendar for upcoming meetings, to a text message that just came in.

And even though you can go back to reading your work after that, you find that you can’t remember what you were trying to say in the first place.

You are not physically tired. You haven’t done any physical activity, and you haven’t left your desk all day. So, why do you feel like your brain is done for the day?

You might have been concentrating, making decisions, solving problems, or switching between tasks. Sometimes, switching between tasks can be the most tiring.

People who experience mental fatigue report thinking slowly, getting annoyed easily, and struggling to focus on a task in front of them.

This form of fatigue causes people to feel like they are at maximum capacity and don’t want to make any more decisions.

The mental fatigue you feel is not all in your head.

In a recent study, 117 adults participated in a randomized crossover design. After 45 minutes of sustained mental effort, in addition to rating their mental fatigue, participants were given a rigorous set of cognitive tests to complete.

After a 45-minute control task of simple work, the same set of tests and ratings were administered.

Furthermore, in addition to the typical decline in performance associated with mental fatigue, the participants completed a set of physical tests of performance that were rated by the participants themselves for how much effort they required and how much exertion they produced.

Effortful mental activity affects perception of physical effort and performance, as shown by Work Time Varying studies (long periods of work with demanding tasks) of 30 to 90 minutes duration, using either computerized tasks and/or self-report.

Results found that within a work period of extended duration, there was an increase in reported fatigue and a decline in performance on reaction time and accuracy tasks.

Also reported was an increase in decline in attentional control with corresponding changes in EEG measures of attentional control.

So there’s actually something going on when you say that your brain is done.

There is one thing called "tired." That name covers a variety of situations, all of them to be dealt with differently.

Mental tiredness is not the same as feeling sleepy (physical tiredness), and physical tiredness (after, for example, having lifted heavy things or engaging in sport) is not the same as feeling mentally tired after a long and stressful day.

There is a major overlap between these states of fatigue: After a sleepless night, our cognitive functions are affected; and after a long, stressful day, we are tired physically as well as mentally. Yet, they are not the same and must be combated in different ways.

Industry observers note that mental fatigue from task-switching, i.e., alternating focus between different tasks, is a serious distraction from one’s work, and it can feel like a heavy burden. The term "multitasking" is often used to describe such work behavior. However, in reality, the brain isn't doing multiple things at once. Instead, it is constantly shifting attention between different tasks.

These tasks do not have to feel "dreadful" in order to be detrimental to one’s health. Each task-switch seems to be minor in itself, yet in the end, it leads to a lot of starting and stopping throughout the day. Sitting for long periods of time is, of course, bad, but sitting at a desk all day is not necessarily worse than standing for long periods of time and being on the move all day.

While there is no one-size-fits-all solution to fighting fatigue at work, certain things can help.
