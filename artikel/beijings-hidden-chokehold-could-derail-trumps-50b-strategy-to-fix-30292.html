As the Trump administration seeks $50 billion from Congress to replace aging air traffic control equipment after Friday’s nationwide ground stop, China’s control of the rare earth supply chain could complicate the overhaul.

Can the United States modernize its critical infrastructure without relying on China?

Amid the fallout from Friday’s nationwide ground stop that halted air travel and upended plans for millions of passengers, the Trump administration blamed aging air traffic control infrastructure, pointing fingers at past administrations and calling on lawmakers to approve billions in upcoming modernization funding.

"Essentially, what you saw on Friday was very, very old equipment that had a heart attack," President Donald Trump told reporters Monday at the White House before departing for Asia.

Aviation specialists work inside the FAA's SMART Center, where technology combines weather, flight path and air traffic data to help identify potential congestion and improve flight efficiency. (Courtesy Department of Transportation).

The problem, one expert told Fox News Digital, is that China holds a near-monopoly over the global rare earth supply chain. While rare earth elements are relatively abundant in nature, Beijing dominates the processing stages required to turn raw ore into usable industrial components.

"For the FAA, radars, satellite systems, complex electrical systems, precision motors, navigation systems — all of these require rare earths inside them," remarked Anupam Ghildyal, chief growth officer for REalloys, a North American critical minerals and rare earth manufacturer.

AMERICA DEPENDS TOO MUCH ON CHINA FOR ESSENTIAL MINERALS. WE NEED TO DIG OUR FUTURE.

Rare earth magnets are displayed in the showroom at Inner Mongolia Baotou Steel Rare-Earth Hi-Tech Co. in Baotou, Inner Mongolia, China. (Bloomberg via Getty Images).

Industry observers note that "For the FAA to upgrade the infrastructure, you need all of these components, and those components have to be manufactured here because China can limit the supply of these components to the U.S.," he added. "But to manufacture these components in the U.S., we need the basic raw materials to make them from, which is something we do not have.".

Beijing's stranglehold on rare earth minerals could bottleneck U.S. efforts before they get off the ground, Ghildyal warned, noting that China has the ability to slow-walk or block much-needed materials.

"The problem is we are starting from pretty much close to zero," said Ghildyal. "Back in the 1980s and 1990s, we actually processed a lot of rare earths, made a lot of magnets, and then we decided that these processes are too laborious and too toxic, so let's offshore them. So we taught the Chinese how to do it, and over the last 40 years they've been doing it.".

Rebuilding the nation’s airspace hardware relies heavily on specialized magnets and minerals dominated by China, Ghildyal said — an problem looming large ahead of Trump’s Thursday summit with Chinese President Xi Jinping in Shenzhen.

He noted that U.S. demand for neodymium magnets sits around 50,000 tons annually, far outpacing domestic production.
