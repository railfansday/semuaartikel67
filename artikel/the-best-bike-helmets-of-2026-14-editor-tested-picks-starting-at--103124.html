Bicycle helmets have saved my life more times than I can count. I’ve ridden and raced bikes for most of my life, so unfortunately I’ve hit the deck many times on trails and on roads. That’s why I wear a helmet anytime I kit up, clip in, and head out for a ride.

While some lids from decades past were bulky and sometimes looked silly, modern road, mountain, and commuter helmets combine safety, performance, and style. While a helmet’s primary role is to protect your head and brain in a crash, the best bike helmets also look great, and some even improve your speed.

It has been revealed that helmet technology has improved considerably in the last decade. Plus, safety features once limited to high-end helmets have trickled down to affordable models. Rider safety has also improved: independent testing by Virginia Tech has prompted more manufacturers to incorporate anti-rotational features across all helmet styles, categories, and price levels. Additionally, high-tech materials, improved ventilation, better straps, and more aerodynamic shapes make today’s lids lighter, cooler, and faster than those from only a few years ago.

Picking the perfect helmet can be challenging. Brands offer scores of models in every possible riding category. Whether you ride on asphalt, gravel roads, or dirt trails, you can find a helmet that matches your style, fit, and budget needs.

According to recent reports, expert Advice: Buy the helmet that feels the most comfortable on your head. You are more likely to use a helmet that fits and feels great, and that’s the first step in keeping your noggin safe if you crash.

You probably already know where you like to ride. Perhaps you ride on paved roads, or maybe on gravel and dirt. Other cyclists prefer riding mountain bikes in the woods or on the rail trail with the family.

Helmets are generally grouped by cycling discipline—road, gravel, mountain, commuting, or recreational. But don’t feel boxed in by those labels—You can wear a mountain bike lid for gravel rides, commute in a lightweight road helmet, or don a commuter helmet for weekend rides on the bike path. The best helmet is the one that you will wear—something that fits well, feels good, and meets your budget.

However, it’s still helpful to understand what those category designations actually mean in terms of helmet features, ventilation, and protection:.

You can spend as little as $30 for a helmet or more than $400 on a high-end race lid. Generally, pricier helmets are lighter and better ventilated. However, all helmets sold in the U.S. must meet the same CPSC safety standards.

For a deep dive into Virginia Tech’s independent helmet testing program, read Matt Phillip’s excellent story, “Virginia Tech’s Newly introduced Helmet Ratings Raise the Bar. Here’s How the Tougher System Could Affect Your Next Choice.”.

Sources indicate that all helmets sold in the U.S. must meet a Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) standard. However, the standard was adopted in 1998. For example, it does not give specifics for mitigating rotational forces on the brain. It also does not lay down any standards for a full-face helmet’s chin bar.

Voluntary and third-party tests go beyond the CPSC standard. We like Virginia Tech’s STAR tests, which were developed with a more modern understanding of head injuries than the CPSC’s test. VA Tech’s protocols evaluate a helmet’s ability to mitigate linear acceleration and rotational velocity, then assign a ranking of zero to five stars. A helmet receives more stars if Virginia Tech’s testing indicates it does a better job of reducing injury. VA Tech only recommends helmets that receive four or five stars.

According to recent reports, outside of CPSC and VA Tech’s STAR, most reputable helmet makers have their own in-house test protocols. The specifics of those tests and the results are often proprietary and not shared with the public.

Remember, no controlled test can replicate every possible real-world crash, but wearing a helmet is always better than not wearing one. Additionally, manufacturers’ understanding of what makes an effective helmet improves every day, which means helmets are always improving.

Recent findings suggest that sizes vary by brand, and most companies offer fit guides to help you choose the best size for your head. Measure your head’s circumference to determine the best size. If you fall between sizes, choose the most comfortable helmet.

In a recent development, the correct size helmet will sit low and straight on your forehead. The retention system should take up some of the slack so the helmet sits securely on your head—you shouldn’t rely on cinching down the chin strap to hold the helmet in place. The right helmet will fit comfortably, and the retention system will easily snug up the fit.

Consider these other factors when choosing and purchasing a newly introduced helmet.

Typically, road helmets are the lightest and best ventilated helmets. In some cases, the vents even also enhance cooling (over wearing no helmet at all) by directing airflow through carefully designed shapes and vents and over your head. This also helps prevent your sunglasses from fogging. Other helmets with increased coverage, such as full-face and mountain bike helmets, often have fewer vents.

Recent findings suggest that however, don’t get tricked into thinking that “more vents are better.” You can’t judge a helmet’s cooling ability only by its number of vents. The Giro Synthe, for example, has only 19 vents but is widely regarded as well-ventilated, while the Kask Valegro has a whopping 37 vents. The size and design of the vents, plus thoughtful internal channels, are more notable than the quantity.

Recent findings suggest that weight needn’t be your biggest concern because when it comes to a helmet, the one you wear will always win out over the one you don’t. It comes down to comfort—you’re more likely to wear a helmet that won’t get in the way or weigh you down.

Sources indicate that lightweight helmets tend to feel more comfortable, especially on long or challenging rides. Both material choices and design aspects contribute to weight savings. High-tech helmets often save grams by incorporating intricate internal bridges and supports that allow airflow and keep the helmet together in a crash. But be prepared to pay a higher price for these innovations. Opting out of “extras” like visors and mounts will also reduce weight.

While we don’t recommend adorning your helmet with too many accessories, extras such as visors, GoPro mounts, and lights certainly have their place and time.

If you find yourself riding in the dark, light mounts can make your life easier and safer by illuminating the path ahead and providing a rear-facing blinker light to help drivers see you in low-light conditions. Bike commuters especially should consider light mounts to make the morning and evening transitions as smooth as possible. Helmet-mounted bike lights work great in conjunction with handlebar-mounted lights because they move with you when you turn your head.

Sources indicate that for those who ride during daylight, visors provide a built-in sun shield, especially for mountain bikers or rec riders. Road riders may use a cycling cap under the helmet and eschew any helmet-related extras.

Most modern helmets come equipped with safety technology such as Mips, KinetiCore, Koroyd, and WaveCel.

According to recent reports, these technologies are engineered to help reduce the forces transmitted to your brain from impacts and collisions in different ways. However, no system or technology can guarantee a better outcome in every situation.

In a recent development, when researching a helmet’s protection, consider rotational and direct-impact forces. Choose a lid that uses a time-tested safety system. Look for positive reviews from actual owners, not only quotes from brands. Full-spectrum protection might cost more than the most bare-bones helmet, but it’s worth the price for frequent riders and anyone who ventures off the beaten path onto gnarly singletrack or into heavy traffic.

Mips (Multi-Directional Impact Protection System) is the most widely know helmet technology. It comes in several versions and is used by numerous helmet brands.The most common Mips tech uses a liner inside the helmet that allows your head and helmet to move relative to each other in the event of a crash. Upon impact, the helmet can move without pulling on your head, which claims to reduce brain trauma. Mips Spherical found on some Giro models is variant. The MIPS SL pads in the Specialized Prevail III is another example.

KinetiCore uses blocks and channels in the helmet’s EPS foam designed to create crumple zones that absorb energy under impact impact.Lazer claims KinetiCore improves ventilation, reduces weight, and limits plastic use in its helmets.

Sources indicate that koroyd uses miniature tubes thermo-welded to create a honeycomb shape. The material crumples in on itself upon impact. Smith typically applies Koroyd in a zonal manner, built into particular places on the helmet to optimize weight and space savings while protecting the most vulnerable parts of your head. Smith’s latest helmet models employ Koroyd and Mips for a double dose of leading-edge protection.

Recent findings suggest that used in four Trek helmet models, Wavecel is a 15mm-thick grid of layered polyester intended to crumple, flex, and glide on impact. Trek claims this system reduces forces on the rider’s head.

In addition to standard road, mountain, and commuter helmets, some brands offer full-face and e-bike-rated helmets.

According to recent reports, only the Netherlands has an e-bike-specific helmet standard (adopted there in 2017). NTA 8776 is a standard designed to protect against higher-impact speeds and covers a slightly larger part of the head.

Recent findings suggest that in the U.S., there are presently no helmet requirements specific to e-bike use. However, several manufacturers sell commuter helmets built to the NTA 8776 standard. Two of our favorites are the Specialized Mode and the Smith Dispatch MIPS.

The helmets in this story are “half-shells” and cover the top of the head. Their coverage usually ends around the eyebrows and top of the ears, though some—particularly mountain bike helmets—extend to the back of the rider’s head.

Another style of helmet is the full face. As the name suggests, this style covers most of a rider’s head and has a chin bar to protect the rider’s jaw. A full-face helmet covers the ears and the back of the head, with a large opening in front that allows a rider to wear goggles.

Full-face helmets protect more of the rider’s head—particularly the mouth and jaw—but are heavier and less ventilated than traditional cycling helmets. Full face helmets are primarily used by mountain bikers—especially downhill and enduro racers—but all riders can benefit from the extra protection they provide.
