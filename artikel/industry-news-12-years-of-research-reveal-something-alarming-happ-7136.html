📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A 12-year study in Sri Lanka links declining numbers of wild female Asian elephants to abrupt social fractures that may be difficult to repair.

Sources indicate that asian elephants rely on one another to find food, acquire information, and care for young. Keeping that support network intact requires opportunities to build new relationships as old connections are lost.

Recent findings suggest that in Sri Lanka’s Udawalawe National Park, a shrinking population appears to be straining that social system. An analysis of more than 440 wild female Asian elephants over 12 years found that large, densely connected communities had given way to smaller, more isolated groups. The remaining females were changing their relationships with one another as their numbers fell.

Recent findings suggest that “We see individual elephants leaving and possibly dying at accelerating rates, but new individuals are not coming into the population,” said Anastasia Madsen, the postdoctoral researcher who led the study. “With fewer individuals in the population, those who are left will naturally have fewer relationships. However, the remaining elephants are also changing their relationships with each other.”.

Published November 12 in Science, the research comes from the University of California San Diego’s School of Biological Sciences. Madsen works in Associate Professor Shermin de Silva’s laboratory in the Department of Ecology, Behavior and Evolution.

The analysis points to a combination of likely deaths, elephants leaving permanently, and fewer incoming individuals as explanations for the population decline. Poaching and conflicts with humans already cause deaths in many elephant populations. Disrupted connections between habitats and reduced food availability may also contribute as ecological conditions change across the 119-square-mile park and its surroundings, though these remain possible pressures rather than established causes of the social changes observed here.

The researchers interpret the shift in community structure as evidence that the population may have crossed a critical threshold, or social tipping point.

“We found that gradual accumulation of losses from turnover preceded sudden change in the social networks among females in a wild population of Asian elephants,” the researchers explained in the study.

Industry observers note that that sequence raises the possibility that losses can build for years before the organization of an animal society changes abruptly. The resulting fragmentation could be difficult to repair.

It has been revealed that “In general, we need to be more aware of these types of tipping points where we see sudden shifts after changes have been slowly building up,” noted Madsen. “Once we move past the tipping point, it’s much harder to go back to previous states.”.

The findings add to discussions about conserving elephant populations as people expand into areas where the animals historically thrived. For social species, opportunities to share space and develop relationships are part of what sustains a community.

“We can probably all relate to the feeling of social isolation brought about, paradoxically, with the advent of digital communications that seem to remove the physical constraints of needing to be in the same place at the same time,” said de Silva, who also serves as president and founder of the conservation nonprofit Trunks & Leaves Inc.. “But for many social species, which includes ourselves as much as elephants, we have to remember that social connectivity is very much a matter of sharing space and building relationships throughout our lives. Crucially, in order to have resilient communities, we need to be able to replace the relationships that are lost over time.”.

Future studies can use the findings to investigate how animal societies change, how to detect those shifts, and which factors make communities vulnerable.

“We are gaining more evidence that studying social patterns is an important tool for us to see when things are going wrong in natural populations and, hopefully, to be able to do something about it,” said Madsen.
