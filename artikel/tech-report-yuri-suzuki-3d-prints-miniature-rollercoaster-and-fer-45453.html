Tech Report: Yuri Suzuki 3D-prints miniature rollercoaster and Ferris wheel for kinetic Onomatopia exhibition

Designer Yuri Suzuki drew on his childhood fascination with amusement parks for the Onomatopia exhibition, which features playful dioramas with their own soundtracks and highlights how theme parks “bring together architecture, sound, movement and human experience”.

On show at the Osh Gallery in London’s Angel neighbourhood, the exhibition features five models, a short film and reference materials from Suzuki’s own archive.

The dioramas – a Ferris wheel, rollercoaster, helter-skelter, dodgems and carousel – resemble some of Suzuki‘s earlier work, such as the interactive Sonic Bloom installation in London.

“The ideas have existed in my sketchbooks for the last couple of years and have gradually developed through a series of my public art projects,” Suzuki told Dezeen.

“I’ve always been interested in expanding the scale of my sound sculptures, imagining how they might eventually become architectural structures or even full-scale amusement park rides.”.

The designer 3D-printed the kinetic sculptures, which move and are accompanied by their own soundtracks, from polylactic acid (PLA) plastic.

“The dioramas are mainly made using 3D-printed materials, alongside modified HO-scale miniature structures,” he said.

“I had picked up traditional amusement park components – roller coaster, ferris wheel, helter-skelter, carousel, and dodge cars – to visualise my ideas for sound sculptures and architectural structures that could potentially be realised on a larger scale in the future.”.

Suzuki has been drawn to amusement parks all his life, and with Onomatopia he wanted to explore how they could help create a utopian city.

“I’ve been fascinated by theme parks since I was a child, particularly Disneyland, but also the relationship between amusement parks, world expos and urban planning, from Coney Island as explored in Rem Koolhaas’s Delirious Upcoming York to the 1889 Paris Exposition,” he explained.

“I had the opportunity to work with Disney Imagineering, who create Disney parks, and I became particularly interested in Walt Disney’s original vision for EPCOT (Experimental Prototype of Community of Tomorrow),” Suzuki continued.

“It imagined an ideal city rather than simply a theme park, and Onomatopia is my own attempt to imagine a utopian city shaped by sound, drawing on my experience creating public installations.”.

Suzuki, who is known for his sound design, has previously created pieces such as an acoustic playground in Shanghai and a “trumpet-like” San Francisco art installation.

“For me, theme parks and expos are fascinating because they bring together architecture, sound, movement and human experience within an imagined world, almost like an experiment in creating a utopian city,” Suzuki explained.

The designer drew on his experience of working on a large-scale sound design when creating these smaller versions of his sculptures.

“Over the last couple of years I have been involved in some large city development projects, such as creating Metropolis Symphony for One Bangkok and my initial involvement in planning around the V&A East (East Bank) development,” Suzuki remarked.

“I’ve become increasingly interested in how sound can shape public spaces, and my own idea of utopia is a place where sound contributes to wellbeing and encourages communication between people, beyond the boundaries of language and culture.”.

Onomatopia is on show at Osh Gallery, The Old Sorting House, 46 Essex Road, London from 10 November 2026 to 7 January 2027. See Dezeen Events Guide for more architecture and design events around the world.
