Recent findings suggest that I’ve traveled to communities experiencing severe acute malnutrition, and what stays with me is what I didn’t hear. Anyone who has spent time around young children knows the noise—the laughter, the crying, the constant motion. When malnutrition has reached a crisis point, that noise is gone. Children sit still in ways they aren’t supposed to. That stillness is one of the first signals something is wrong. Intervening early is critical. Health workers can use the most basic tools to measure a child’s upper arm to rapidly assess nutritional health. The intervention is surprisingly simple: providing a small packet filled with peanut paste, milk powder, and a precise blend of micronutrients. A six-week course of the packets follows and it can alter the entire trajectory of their life. Thirty years later, it remains one of the world’s most effective tools in global health. WHY PRODUCT INNOVATION ISN’T ENOUGH There’s a persistent bias toward the newly introduced in how innovation gets funded. Moonshots are celebrated and disruption gets rewarded. The assumption is that the most transformative solutions involve the most sophisticated technology. Breakthrough innovation is important, but collective expertise, commitment, and coordination are critical to scaling. Ready-to-use therapeutic food, known as RUTF, is one of these breakthrough innovations. Before it existed, treating severe acute malnutrition meant hospital stays and clinical formulas most families couldn’t access. RUTF changed the equation with a shelf-stable, portable product that didn’t require refrigeration. A caregiver could administer it at home. A health worker could distribute it in a village with no running water. Its success was never tied to a single organization. Over the past three decades, it has been adopted by governments, humanitarian organizations, and health systems around the world. For business leaders, the lesson is familiar. The challenge is rarely inventing the solution. It’s building the conditions that allow it to scale. THE OVERLOOKED NECESSITY We often think of innovation as a product challenge. Build the right solution and impact follows. But scale rarely works that way. What determines success is everything surrounding the innovation: distribution, financing, trust, incentives, and infrastructure. Getting RUTF to a child who needs it is more than a point A to point B challenge. It requires identifying malnutrition before it becomes irreversible, especially in the first 1,000 days of life. It necessitates a health worker who can make that call—and in many communities, that person isn’t a credentialed clinician. It is often a minimally compensated local community member. It requires supply chains that reach remote places where businesses don’t. It depends on coordination between governments, manufacturers, and health systems. If any one of those fails, a child goes untreated. RUTF endures because the ecosystem built around it proved strong enough to sustain it through political shifts, funding disruptions, and supply chain crises. That infrastructure is consistently underestimated and consistently underfunded. THE NEXT INNOVATION The next breakthrough for nutrition is a systems innovation—one that closes the gap between what works and what reaches people at scale. The UNICEF-led Child Nutrition Fund puts that idea into action. By bringing together government leadership, financing, and technical expertise, it helps accelerate the scale-up of proven nutrition interventions while strengthening the systems behind them. In doing so, it helps bridge critical gaps in nutrition services and supports country-led efforts to save lives, build resilience, and improve outcomes for women and children. That’s the innovation this moment requires—a smarter way to build and sustain the systems that deliver it. Don’t just back a product. Back the infrastructure that makes it possible. The right product can change a child’s life. The ecosystem is what gets it there, and scale is what reshapes the future. The leaders who understand this invest in the breakthroughs and the systems that make breakthroughs last. Michele Walsh is executive vice president and chief philanthropy officer of UNICEF USA.
