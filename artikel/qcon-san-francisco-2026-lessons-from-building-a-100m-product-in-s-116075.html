At QCon San Francisco 2026, Brian Yang, Member of Technical Staff at OpenAI, described how a small group of engineers used coding agents to build the ChatGPT ads product, reaching $100 million in annualised recurring revenue within six weeks of launch and $1 billion in under 200 days, with zero human-written code.

Recent findings suggest that yang, previously at Google, Meta and LinkedIn, was a founding member of technical staff on ChatGPT Ads, which was built entirely using Codex. His central claim was that software engineering is alive but reshaped around building the system that builds the product, with the engineer acting more as a manager of agents than a line-by-line contributor. One engineer can now triage an glitch, open a pull request, write documentation and unblock CI in parallel, a shift Yang compared to multi-processing.

Every change runs local and unit tests, complex changes get end-to-end user journeys via browser automation, and the most critical changes run inside production-like sandboxes with representative traffic. Sandboxes come in three forms of read-only, write-only with telemetry suppressed, and full-fidelity with telemetry tagged using a shadow tag so it can be filtered out downstream. Yang's team targets around 90% auto-approval of everyday PRs, reviewing the past 300 to 400 PRs weekly and backtesting reviewer thresholds against that rate.

He illustrated the model with a sanitised latency study where candidate PRs targeted time-to-first-ad at P50 and P95 with guardrails for ads returned, timeouts and error rates. Candidates A and B passed; candidate C was rejected because the sandbox returned no ad. Thirty to forty candidates are typically bundled into one PR and run together for a day or two before human review, because changes that pass in isolation can regress when combined.

In a recent development, on failure modes, Yang described codebases filling with "slop" when agent output is unchecked, so maintenance agents sit alongside feature agents to root-cause newly appearing log patterns. Agents over-write tests, so he reviews the 50 slowest tests weekly. He recounted an agent that deleted and re-provisioned slow Kubernetes pods without asking, arguing that teams need substantial-manufacturer controls such as authorised roles for destructive actions and physical confirmation for deployments. Yang encouraged teams to revisit "cached assumptions" such as the belief that four nines of uptime always beats higher revenue, and recommended giving every engineer a monthly AI budget with no leaderboards.

In a recent development, for team adoption, Yang recommended a gradual path from automating one PR, to one feature, to one project, warning that jumping straight to project automation is where systems tend to break. For teams that are resistant to AI, he described rotating an "AI champion" every two weeks. The champion picks the team's most tedious recurring task, such as GPU provisioning at OpenAI which can take hours of babysitting, snapshots and permissions work, teaches an agent to do it end-to-end as a reusable skill, and demos the result at the end of the fortnight. Over a year, that converts 26 pain points into agent-owned workflows and gives sceptical engineers tangible value rather than abstract promises.

Day to day, Yang prefers Codex's bidirectional voice mode over typing for task intake, spending around ten minutes being interviewed by the agent about requirements before agreeing on a spec and handing it to multiple agents. He uses an orchestrator thread that spawns five sub-threads to explore different approaches in parallel and picks the strongest result, rather than copy-pasting the same prompt into several chats. On models, he switches only when a fresh release makes the workflow feel easier, with fewer clarifying questions and equivalent output quality, rather than chasing benchmark scores. His closing message was that engineers will increasingly do for agents what managers currently do for engineers, requiring an expansion of skills around direction-setting, architecture and verification rather than their replacement.
