After a Decade of Refusing to Use a Treadmill, I Finally Gave In. Here’s How It’s Making Me a Better Runner.

🌐 Source: Runnersworld • 📅 23 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 7 min read (1403 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, when the weather dipped below freezing for the first time last winter and the sun began to set at 4:30 p.m., I knew I’d have to leave my typical after-work miles behind. Cold weather and early darkness have always been an easy one-two punch to my running motivation.

I’d also long held another line in the sand: I didn’t run on treadmills—at least not since high school gym class. I took to calling myself a “purist” because I didn’t think running was worth it if it wasn’t outside in nature. I believed my cause was noble, but really, I was just piling up excuses not to run in the winter.

So I decided to give treadmill running a chance. I set out to complete one to two easy runs per week for a month to see if it could help me stay consistent through the winter. Within a few weeks, I was running more than I ever had in winters past—and reconsidering what held me back all those years.

According to recent reports, my experiment started after work in late fall. I turned to one of the Bowflex treadmills we have at Runner’s World headquarters. Earlier in the day, my editor casually mentioned that my Apple Watch would sync directly to the treadmill for a more accurate reading, a suggestion that made me more excited to get started on my test.

According to recent reports, I walked up to the treadmill and the first thing I noticed was that if I wanted to, I could put my Netflix password into the built-in monitor and fire up an episode of Brooklyn Nine-Nine for the 200th time. While this capability isn’t very advanced as far as treadmill features go nowadays, the shift from the bare-bones experience I remembered from a decade ago in high school gym class amazed me.

I decided to go simple for my first run, tapping in with my Apple Watch and committing to a 30-minute easy effort with just a podcast to entertain me.

Sources indicate that the run itself was good, but left me puzzled. I immediately focused on how each foot strike offered a different sensation than I was used to outside. After a minute of adjusting the belt speed, I settled into a pace that felt similar to my outdoor easy runs. What I found, though, was that the treadmill speed read a completely different pace than my outdoor runs.

Sources indicate that after 30 minutes at zero incline, I completed just 2.56 miles at an 11:51 pace with my heart rate right at my usual 150 beats per minute (bpm). That 11:51 average was nearly two minutes slower than my outdoor 10:00 easy pace, but I felt like I was right at my normal outdoor effort level.

Recent findings suggest that still, I walked away encouraged. I logged a run, avoided the weather, and felt great doing it.

Industry observers note that my experiment continued with more 30-minute easy runs over the next couple of weeks. Each one raised the same question: Why did an effort that felt identical to my normal 10:00 outdoor easy runs translate to a treadmill pace of well over 11:00 per mile? Adding to the confusion, I got a fresh watch (the Coros Pace 4), and it consistently recorded slightly longer distances than the treadmill itself displayed.

After my third run, I brought all my questions to a chat with Birmingham-based RRCA level II- and USATF-certified run coach Alex Morrow, founder of Resolute Running. I described to him how, on the treadmill, I felt I was giving a similar effort compared with my outdoor runs, but was running at a significantly slower pace, and I asked him why he thought this was the case.

Sources indicate that “When you’re outside and you’re running at an easy pace, you’re looking at the trees, you’re waving at other runners, and your mind gets to dissociate from what you’re doing,” Morrow says. “When you’re on a treadmill, there’s very little stimulation,” beyond your body moving.

Recent findings suggest that because I’d been running exclusively outdoors since high school, I was used to looking around at the cars as they passed by, the birds in the air, and any number of distractions along my route. As soon as I hopped on the treadmill, though, none of that was there to capture my attention. Right away, I locked in on the difference in every step, and became consumed by how far off my pace ended up being from what I expected.

Changing a long-held belief is tough, especially when it’s rooted in identity. “It’s like turning a battleship around,” notes Morrow. “It takes time and it’s never easy.” In my case, the belief that “real runners” don’t use treadmills stuck around for years.

Sources indicate that once I started running on one, though, I accepted it as a suitable tool for consistent running almost immediately. The hardest part of flipping my mindset wasn’t logging the miles or dealing with boredom, it was simply stepping onto the belt in the first place.

According to recent reports, if you’re new to treadmill running, or have avoided it for the same reasons I did, my best advice is to try it with an open mind. Don’t worry if your pace looks slower than what you’re used to seeing outside. That mental hurdle of mine ended up not really mattering much. I let effort guide my runs, my podcasts entertain me, and I judge my success based on time on feet rather than actual miles.

My biggest takeaway from the experience was consistency. According to Morrow, that’s where treadmill running becomes one of the most powerful tools a runner can use, especially in the winter.

In a recent development, through my trial efforts, I realized I wasn’t frustrated with the treadmill itself, I was simply focused on learning more about my running. Curiosity helped me keep a steady schedule, and in just a few weeks, I’d already run more in the winter than I had in years.

It has been revealed that “Consistency is so much more crucial than having one killer workout,” Morrow explains. “I would much rather you have four or five average workouts than three killer workouts.” This idea counters how a lot of runners think about training, especially when motivation dips. It’s flashy to chase breakthrough days in perfect conditions, but you build fitness by stacking days together, even when the circumstances aren’t ideal.

Recent findings suggest that for runners who live in areas heavily impacted by harsh winter weather, the treadmill is a saving grace. “Something is always better than nothing,” Morrow notes. “Jogging three miles on a treadmill is better than skipping your workout completely because it’s cold outside.”.

It has been revealed that when weather threatens to derail a week of training, the opportunity that a treadmill provides becomes crucial to your development as a runner. “Consistency creates physiological adaptations in your body that allow you to reach your goals,” Morrow notes. Those adaptations include a boost in aerobic fitness development, running economy, VO2 max, and lactate threshold, he continues. It’s adaptations like these that establish an excellent fitness base for marathon training.

Looking back on my career as a recreational runner, my biggest mistake wasn’t refusing to run on the treadmill; it was letting that ignorance cost me months of consistent running every year. Now, the excuses that held me back are gone. I’ve continued using the treadmill to not only knock out frequent easy runs but conquer the occasional interval workout or speed session.
