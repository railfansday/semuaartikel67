The Trump administration has debuted a new online portal for payers, providers and other stakeholders to manage and monitor disputes under the federal Independent Dispute Resolution process.

Industry observers note that the IDR Gateway went online Nov. 13 and was officially unveiled Nov. 20. It replaces the web forms parties previously used to manage disputes, most of which will no longer be available outside of the centralized platform after Jan. 15, 2027, per a government fact sheet.

Payers will eventually be required to register on IDR Gateway so that it is easier for providers to identify the correct party, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services mentioned earlier this year when finalizing an overhaul of No Surprises Act protocols. That registration feature will be added “in the future on a rolling basis,” CMS said last week, with others like in-portal negotiation similarly rolling out over time “to improve accountability and reduce unnecessary filings,” the agency said last spring.

For now, the IDR Gateway allows parties and their representatives to initiate a dispute, respond to proposed selections of IDR entities and submit a payment offer. Its other tools allow owners to view, search, set up notifications and broadly analyze open disputes for their organization; an IDR Gateway Dashboard, CMS mentioned, offers a color-coded donut chart breaking down an organization’s different types of open disputes by phase, while another tool can generate detailed reports of dispute line items.

According to recent reports, the administration also touted security measures inherent to the new portal. These include a new sign-up process that requires people to verify their identity with an approved credential service provider, and other protocols like a terms and conditions agreement that notes that unauthorized users could be subject to criminal prosecution.

The baseball-style IDR process enacted under the No Surprises Act, intended to handle out-of-network billing disputes away from the patient’s purview, has become a point of contention amid dispute volumes and payouts well beyond legislators’ expectations.

Industry observers note that arbitrators’ decisions have largely favored providers over payers (85% of decisions went toward providers last year), which alongside court decisions that tweaked CMS’ processes further toward provider preferences, has triggered outright condemnation from payers who have turned to lawsuits, restrictive policies and lobbying in a bit to reform the process. Recent analyses have pegged the IDR process’ impact for employer plans at $8.4 billion in 2025 and total costs associated with the process at $22 billion for that same year.

Lawmakers including the No Surprises Act’s chief authors have stated they hope to pass newly introduced legislation to fix or fully replace the IDR process, with December’s must-pass budget bill now looking like a likely vehicle for such a change.
