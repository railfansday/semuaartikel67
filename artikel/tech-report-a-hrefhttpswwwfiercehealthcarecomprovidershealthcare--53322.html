Tech Report: Healthcare purchasers must weigh hospital quality alongside their prices, Leapfrog states

Patient safety watchdog The Leapfrog Group released its latest collection of hospital safety grades hand-in-hand with calls for policymakers and stakeholders to consider, or directly tie patient safety reporting to price transparency.

Sources indicate that the twice-yearly release brought top marks for 900 out of a total 2,366 graded hospitals, and the lowest “F” score for just three. There were 774 hospitals that earned a “B” grade, 655 a “C” grade, and 35 a “D” grade.

In a recent development, no grades were assigned to another 447 hospitals, a decision the group made after a judge’s ruling in a lawsuit involving the scores it would assign to facilities that did not voluntarily submit their quality data to Leapfrog’s survey (Leapfrog appealed that decision in September). Leapfrog’s decision not to score these has generally shifted its letter grade distribution upward, and the organization has said it is reviewing a potential methodology change for upcoming gradings to incorporate non-submitting hospitals.

Still, “a record number of hospitals are choosing to publicly report their safety and quality performance, and we applaud them for holding themselves accountable to their patients,” Leapfrog President and CEO Leah Binder noted in a statement.

At the state level, Connecticut maintained its claim on the largest portion of hospitals earning an “A” grade (64.3% of its total), followed by Utah, Upcoming Jersey and South Carolina. Leapfrog also called out Rhode Island and Maine for jumping up the rankings—from eighteenth to seventh for the former, and from seventeenth to eighth for the latter. No hospitals were given an “A” grade in five states: Iowa, North Dakota, South Dakota, Vermont and Wyoming.

Industry observers note that A hospital’s assigned grade is calculated by reviewing recent data on up to 22 patient safety measures, including a 10-part Medicare composite of reported patient safety and adverse events. Safety scores for specific hospitals can be searched on the group’s website.

Leapfrog and Binder, in a news release outlining its latest scores, made the case that its grades and similar independent measures warrant a seat at the table in a transparency conversation primarily focused on hospital costs.

Industry observers note that “Progress on price transparency is important, but there’s no good price for bad care,” Binder said. “We are calling on the Trump Administration and Congress to ensure price transparency is integrated with patient safety and quality transparency.”.

According to recent reports, the group backed its calls by pointing to a recent analysis (PDF) conducted by the Purchaser Business Group on Health (PBGH) that reviewed employer claims, federal price transparency data, and quality and safety measures including Leapfrog’s. It “found little to no correlation between negotiated prices and standardized quality measures,” as hospitals that received high safety grades often charged cheaper prices than those in the same market with lower grades.

Leapfrog and Elizabeth Mitchell, president and CEO of PBGH, mentioned the analysis—currently being expanded for practical usage by healthcare purchasers—is an example of how a more wholistic view of prices and care delivery should be considered when weighing a hospital.

In a recent development, “Employers have an appropriate duty to pay a fair price for high-quality care, and they can’t meet it by looking at price alone,” Mitchell remarked in the release from Leapfrog. “When we put the data together, some of the most expensive hospitals were not the safest. Our members want to buy high-quality, safe care that is fairly priced. That shouldn’t be too much to ask.”.
