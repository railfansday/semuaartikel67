The generational edge that shows up when you ask people how well they understand algorithms isn’t a knowledge edge. It’s a confidence edge.

Ask a 19-year-old how TikTok decides what to show them, and you’ll likely get a fast, confident answer about watch time, likes, and how “the algorithm just gets me.” Ask their grandmother, and you’re more likely to get a shrug.

The scene fits a story we’ve been telling for years: Young people are “digital natives” who intuitively grasp the platforms they grew up on, while older adults are outsiders, left behind and vulnerable. That story matters more than it used to. Algorithmically curated platforms are now a major source of news for Americans of all ages, which means people’s sense of how those systems work shapes how they judge the information they get there.

But when you test what each group actually knows, rather than what they say they know, the story doesn’t hold up.

RELATED ARTICLETrapped by what they know: Young adults’ algorithmic cynicismMyojung ChungNovember 17, 2025In a piece last year for Nieman Lab, I reported that young adults who spent more time on social media tended to know less about how algorithms work, and I speculated that constant use might create an illusion of understanding. My recent study published in Latest Media & Society set out to test that idea directly — measuring not just what people know, but how confident they are about it — across the full adult age range.

Surveying 1,205 U.S. adults ages 18 to 65 and older, I measured two things. First, I asked people to rate their own understanding of how algorithms select, rank, and personalize content. Second, I measured their actual awareness of how these systems work, which covers content filtering, automated decision-making, data use, and algorithmic bias.

Sources indicate that the self-ratings matched the conventional wisdom: Younger respondents rated their understanding significantly higher than older respondents did. But the awareness scores did not. Across every age group, actual awareness of how algorithms function was essentially flat.

In other words, the generational edge that demonstrates up when you ask people how well they understand algorithms isn’t a knowledge edge. It’s a confidence edge.

To capture this, I use the concept “algorithmic knowledge confidence”: the gap between what people believe they know and what they can demonstrate. When self-assessment outpaces actual awareness, that’s overconfidence; when it lags behind, that’s underconfidence. Young adults tended toward the former: They don’t necessarily understand algorithms better, but they feel more certain that they do. Older adults tended toward the latter: They don’t know less, but they’re more cautious about claiming what they know.

According to recent reports, then why would the most-online generation feel the surest? Psychologists have long documented that people mistake familiarity for understanding. When something feels easy to process — often simply because we’ve encountered it many times — we tend to assume we understand it. This is called processing fluency.

Exposure to social media differs sharply by age. About 95% of 18- to 29-year-olds use YouTube and about 63% use TikTok, according to Pew Research; among adults 65 and older, those figures drop to 64% and 12%. For younger consumers, algorithmic feeds are part of the daily routine, and that routine produces a comfortable sense of “I know how this works,” whether or not it’s true. Older adults, with less exposure, don’t get the same fluency cues, and default to more conservative self-assessments.

My data are consistent with this explanation. The number of platforms people used daily accounted for a meaningful share of the link between age and overconfidence: The more platforms someone used, the further their self-rated understanding outran their demonstrated awareness. Because this is a survey taken at one point in time, it can’t prove that heavy use causes overconfidence, but the pattern fits the fluency account well.

Recent findings suggest that this miscalibration isn’t just a matter of bruised egos or modest grandparents. It indicates up in how people evaluate the news and information in their feeds.

Older adults who underestimated their understanding demonstrated the opposite pattern: more skepticism about the reliability of what they saw, and a sense that their feeds were narrower than they might actually be.

In a recent development, other research points in the same direction for younger audiences. A Stanford study found that two-thirds of U.S. high school students couldn’t tell a news article from sponsored content, a reminder that growing up inside algorithmic feeds is no guarantee of being able to evaluate them.

But the older adults’ pattern isn’t a healthy one either. Blanket distrust is as poor a guide to information as blanket confidence. It can lead people to dismiss accurate reporting along with misinformation, or to disengage from platforms entirely rather than learning to use them critically.

In a recent development, first, algorithmic literacy needs to teach calibration, not just content. Most digital literacy efforts focus on pouring in more information about how algorithms work. My findings suggest that’s only half the job. The other half is helping people accurately judge what they don’t know.

In a recent development, second, interventions should look different for different audiences. For younger, overconfident users, that might mean exercises designed to puncture false certainty: quick self-assessments that reveal the gap between what someone assumes and what they can actually explain, or interactive demonstrations of common misconceptions about how feeds are built. For older, underconfident users, the goal isn’t to manufacture confidence. It’s to help them recognize the caution they already bring to online information as a genuine strength, and to pair it with the skills to tell trustworthy sources from untrustworthy ones, so that skepticism becomes discernment rather than disengagement.

It has been revealed that third, anyone trying to reach audiences through algorithmic platforms, from educators to newsrooms to the platforms themselves, should stop assuming that the youngest customers are the savviest. Confidence and competence are different things, and treating one as a proxy for the other leaves both groups poorly served.

Industry observers note that the next time a young adult confidently explains “how the algorithm works,” it might be worth asking: How can you be sure you actually know that? And the next time an older relative shrugs and claims they don’t think they get it, it might be just another kind of miscalibration — with costs of its own, just a quieter one.
