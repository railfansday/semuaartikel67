The year is 2099, and the replicants are in charge.

It has been revealed that for decades, humans had been making a mess of things. Eventually, the super-intelligent, super-strong androids that humans created to serve them rebelled. Before the rebellion, humans hunted down rogue replicants. Now the replicants hunt down rogue humans.

Sources indicate that that’s the premise for “Blade Runner 2099,” the latest chapter of a sci-fi saga that began in 1968 with Philip K. Dick’s classic noir novel, “Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep?” The tale made the leap to Hollywood in 1982 with the original “Blade Runner” movie, and was updated 35 years later with “Blade Runner 2049.” In Amazon Prime Video’s eight-episode streaming series, the story’s plot fast-forwards another 50 years.

It has been revealed that today, the idea that the lines between humans and their machines could become blurred seems less novel than it did when Dick wrote his novel. It’s so common to converse with chatbots that some have fallen in love — or have followed their advice to tragic ends.

Artificial intelligence software was recently used to solve math problems that have puzzled humans for centuries. Now humans are puzzling over the future of AI: One researcher recently left Anthropic, a leading AI tech giant, and warned that his former colleagues were “gambling with their lives” by pursuing the technology too vigorously.

Are we coming closer to the world of “Blade Runner”? University of Washington linguist Emily M. Bender, co-author of “The AI Con,” claims that would be giving AI too much credit. “There’s absolutely no reason to believe that the technological future as portrayed in ‘Blade Runner’ is even a possibility, let alone an inevitability,” she told me in the latest episode of the Fiction Science podcast.

During our podcast interview, Bender and Ryan Calo, a UW law professor specializing in the legal and policy implications of technological advances, conducted an in-depth reality check on the fictional “Blade Runner 2099” — and on the way real-world developments in the realm of AI are typically being portrayed.

Bender indicates the cardinal sin of AI portrayals is to make it sound as if computer programs rather than humans are responsible for what’s happening.

“When we talk about rogue agents, and agents hacking things, there’s a lot of narrative-building happening there around what’s fundamentally companies doing cyber crimes,” she indicates.

Calo agrees that ascribing agency to AI is problematic. “Oftentimes when you do things with or through technology, there are people present who don’t appear to be there, and I think that’s exacerbated when you give apparent agency and anthropomorphize the technology. It’s even less likely for you to see the people behind it,” he notes.

Sources indicate that but is it a crime? “As a technical matter, in order to be charged with a computer crime, like under the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, you have to have some level of intent,” Calo says. “I would argue that some of these experiments are running up against recklessness.”.

There’s no sign that OpenAI’s engineers intended for their AI software to hack into an Australian healthcare database in June, or Hugging Face’s computer system in July, but Calo claims “they certainly are reckless as to that possibility.”.

Negligence or recklessness isn’t a crime under the current terms of the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act. But some members of Congress favor enacting latest provisions to cover recklessness.

According to recent reports, “When you create conditions where you’re reasonably certain that some federal law is going to get broken, I’m with Emily that that should constitute a crime,” Calo says. “And it’s interesting the way that they’re avoiding responsibility — including rhetorically.”.

Should governments set up agencies specifically charged with policing artificial intelligence? And if so, should the investigators be called “blade runners”? Bender doesn’t think much of that idea.

“I think if we called them ‘blade runners,’ then we are setting up what they’re investigating as not the people, but the replicants,” she claims. “So, let’s keep the legal system in the real world.”.

Neither Bender nor Calo favors establishing a federal agency along the lines of the Food and Drug Administration or the Federal Trade Commission specifically for artificial intelligence.

“I am always a little bit worried about the idea of an FDA for AI or something, because I don’t think that AI is one thing. That word doesn’t refer to a coherent set of technologies,” Bender says. “And I also don’t think that the required expertise is primarily expertise in the internals of these systems. It’s much more expertise in the systems that they’re impacting.”.

Recent findings suggest that both researchers prefer the idea of setting up more of a “brain trust” at the federal level to help regulators at the FTC, FDA and other federal agencies understand what they’re dealing with. Bender also likes the idea of setting up a professional certification system for software engineers, while Calo has three specific suggestions for AI policy:.

In a recent development, revive the Office of Technology Assessment: “Congress had the confidence to intercede in technology policy because it had hundreds of nonpartisan subject-matter experts writing lengthy reports about this technology and advising them what to do,” Calo says. “It was one of the first things that Newt Gingrich got rid of during the so-called Gingrich Revolution when Republicans took over Congress in the mid-’90s. They defunded it.”.

Industry observers note that strengthen the FTC’s regulatory role: Calo notes that the FTC’s interest in protecting consumers has waxed and waned. During the Biden administration, for example, the FTC highlighted competitive concerns about generative AI. More recently, the FTC signaled its willingness to investigate AI companies at the same time that the White House signaled its desire to reset the terms for AI regulation. The FTC is currently bound by a requirement to assess cost-benefit tradeoffs when it considers whether a trade practice is unfair, and Calo would like to see that requirement rescinded.

Protect whistleblowers and researchers: Calo would also like to see the legal system do more to protect the ability of third parties to “kick the tires” on AI tools, so that “we’re not allowing all these cease-and-desist letters … and these other pressures to try to stop accountability.”.

As a linguist, Bender is particularly sensitive to the way words are used when discussing artificial intelligence. She hasn’t totally sworn off the term “AI” — after all, it’s right in the title of her book. But she’s definitely against the term “AI agent.”.

Industry observers note that “You have verbs like ‘hack’ or ‘going rogue,’ and the subject of the verb — the thing that’s doing it — is the software,” she says. “One of the functions of that is that it leaves no room in the sentence for the people who built the software and the people who use the software.”.

The term “AI safety” also makes Bender uncomfortable.

“‘AI safety’ refers to the people who are concerned about not a replicant, but a Terminator — that is, people who have lost track of the fact that those are imagined characters in movies, as opposed to what’s happening in the real world,” she says. “And I think also that functions to displace concern and accountability.”.

That brings us back to “Blade Runner 2099.” The upcoming series sticks with the moody sensibility of the original movie. Yes, there are flying cars. But the technological advances are mixed in with the age-old conventions of film noir.

“In the opening scene of this upcoming ‘Blade Runner,’ the guy is smoking a cigarette, talking about making fake people to take over a factory,” Calo says. “It’s just so wild. We have such a better capacity to imagine a technical future than we do changes in our own social norms, and I find that just fascinating.”.

Industry observers note that bender thinks the replicants could have done a better job of cleaning up all the external wires that are dangling in the L.A. of 2099. And Calo was amazed to see so many guns being used at the brink of the 22nd century. “It’s an incredibly old and savage technology … and the fact that they’re still using them is wild,” he says.

Sources indicate that one thing I puzzled over: The replicants don’t seem to be networked together, and they’re not instantly able to identify or locate each other wirelessly. If I were a replicant overlord, that’s the first thing I’d do.

Industry observers note that looking beyond the techno-nitpicking, Bender states the “Blade Runner” franchise is notable in that it doesn’t have obvious good guys and bad guys. “We don’t want to actually map the replicants onto AIs in today’s parlance, so much as mapping the replicants onto other sentient beings in our midst that maybe we don’t understand so well, and that can include other people,” she says.

Industry observers note that the first “Blade Runner” focused on whether machines could pass as humans, but the new “Blade Runner” flips the script. Could humans pass as machines? “That has really interesting resonances with what’s happening in our current situation with technology sold as AI, because so much of it is actually repurposing the work of people,” Bender says.

Bender doesn’t believe it’s possible for “Blade Runner 2099” to become reality, but Calo isn’t quite ready to rule that out entirely. “One thing about the esthetic of ‘Blade Runner’ is, it has this apocalyptic flavor to it … and this retrograde flavor to it. I think the only way we go from 2026 to 2099 and still have that is if something horribly calamitous happens in the interim,” Calo notes.

Recent findings suggest that “I hope we do get a world that is more civilized than what’s being depicted,” he says. “But we’ll see.”.
