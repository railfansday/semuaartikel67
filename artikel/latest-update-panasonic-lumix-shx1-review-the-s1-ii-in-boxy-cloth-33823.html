Recent findings suggest that when the Panasonic S1 II was announced, one of my first thoughts was, “How are they going to top this with an S1H II?” The answer was, they didn’t. Instead, Panasonic is bringing the internals of the S1 II to a newly introduced form factor, a cinema box camera, and calling it the SHX1 ($4,699).If the box-camera form factor is a benefit for the work you do, the SHX1 is one of the best. However, for content creators, it offers only minor upgrades over the less expensive and still incredibly capable S1 II. If the box-camera form factor is a benefit for the work you do, the SHX1 is one of the best. However, for content creators, it offers only minor upgrades over the less expensive and still incredibly capable S1 II.

Note that throughout this review I will be referring to this camera in comparison to the Panasonic S1 II. If you are unfamiliar with that camera, you can check out our review here. As well, as the SHX1 does not offer any stills capture, this review will be entirely focused on video capture.

Sources indicate that the SHX1 is quite compact for a cinema camera, similar to a RED Komodo and weighing only 948 grams (33.4 ounces). In a departure from Panasonic’s previous BG-Series box cameras, this SHX1 includes a top-mounted LCD monitor, with extensive 4-axis articulation and 1.84M dots of resolution.

According to recent reports, the articulation is extremely useful, as you can shoot above your eye-line, something usually prohibited with top-mounted displays. There are dedicated exposure buttons on the body, as well as Menu, Back and AF-On. These buttons have a pretty unresponsive feel to them, with little haptic feedback to let you know you’ve actually pushed the button. These can be customized, something I recommend to avoid excessive menu diving.

As expected for a cinema camera, the top, right side and bottom of the camera have plentiful 1/4-inch mounting points for rigs and accessories. The camera is powered by Mini V-Mount batteries, most of which are the same height as the camera. There is an active fan system in the body, which allows even 4K/120p footage to be captured indefinitely.

It’s worth pointing out that you can also purchase the SHX1 with an accessory kit ($5,499), which includes a top handle, XLR adapter and a side grip. The grip feels fantastic but is quite large. I consider it almost a necessary accessory, and the extra controls give operators several more options for custom buttons and a joystick for selecting the autofocus point. The top handle is very sturdy, and the removable XLR adapter gives you easily accessed gain controls. Unlike previous S-Series bodies, which relied on an XLR adapter to capture 32-bit float audio, the SHX1 can record 32-bit audio through the 3.5mm jacks and internal mic as well.

Footage can be stored on a UHS-II SD card, CFexpress Type B card, or external SSD. As expected with a cinema camera, there are locking ports for SDI video, timecode, and a full-size HDMI port. For further external control, a Live Production Unit called the AU-LPU1 will be released in early 2027.

It has been revealed that the lack of built-in ND filters is a disappointment, but that was the compromise Panasonic was forced into when keeping the body so small. Screw-in filters or a matte box will be required for controlling light levels.

The sensor is the same full-frame 24.5MP partially stacked unit we saw in the S1 II, which is capable of outstanding results and well-controlled rolling shutter. The only difference is an anti-aliasing filter has been added to minimize incidents of moiré in finely detailed textures.

In my testing, though, I found very similar results to the S1 II, with neither camera giving me moiré even when shooting fabrics at moderate distance.

Like the S1 II, the SHX1 has a massive number of recording resolutions, frame shapes and frame rates. The headline feature is 6K Open Gate recording up to 30P, and 5.1K Open Gate using the full sensor up to 60P. For slow motion, the camera can capture 4K at up to 120P with sound. There is also the option to expand the dynamic range by reducing the readout speed. This does produce appreciably cleaner shadows, but with a corresponding increase in rolling shutter artifacts.

Video can be recorded with MP4, MOV and ProRes codecs for standard 10-bit video. There is also the option to capture 12-bit RAW video in either ProRes RAW or BRAW flavours. Bafflingly, though only ProRes RAW can be recorded to the CFexpress card, BRAW requires an external SSD. This is particularly odd as BRAW offers extremely efficient 12:1 compression rates, which could be easily written to a card. Adding insult to injury, the camera’s startup time is dramatically increased with an SSD attached, taking nearly 10 seconds and often leading to me missing shots.

According to recent reports, the video quality itself from the camera is outstanding, with impressive detail and expansive dynamic range even when Dynamic Range Boost is not active. Log video can be captured in either Panasonic V-Log gamma or ARRI’s LogC3. The Panasonic V-Log gamma remains my favourite log curve to work with, but ARRI’s gamma is very flexible and the industry standard in the filmmaking world.

What sets the video quality from the SHX1 apart from any other box camera is the inclusion of incredibly effective in-body image stabilization, based on the S1 II system. This works great in its default mode, but even better when Panasonic lenses are attached and the “Cropless” stabilization is engaged. The camera’s autofocus abilities are also quite reliable, though perhaps not quite as rock-solid as Sony’s and Canon’s more recent implementations. Humans and animals can be automatically detected, and there is a good degree of “stickiness” when following moving subjects.

If you don’t require the box-camera form factor, then the SHX1 can be a confusing proposition. The S1 II features nearly all of the video capabilities with excellent photography specs and an EVF in a considerably more affordable package.

However, many professional videographers and cinematographers require the box-camera design to make it easy to build rigs or mount the camera in a variety of locations. The forthcoming Live Production Unit will add further versatility for using the SHX1 as a remote camera, something the S1 II cannot offer.

As well, if absolute reliability is important, the more robust cooling system in the SHX1 means overheating will not be a concern even when recording demanding footage in hot areas. The S1 II has very good thermals for a hybrid mirrorless camera, but in professional situations absolute reliability is imperative.

According to recent reports, compact cinema cameras like the RED Komodo-X 6K ($4,995) and Blackmagic Pyxis 6K ($2,249) offer similar form factors and outstanding image quality. Unlike the SHX1, these can record extremely compressed RAW video internally. However, the autofocus performance is vastly behind the SHX1, and neither offers IBIS. RED and Blackmagic may offer more bang for the buck for filmmakers, but the SHX1 is the clear choice for handheld documentary work.

It has been revealed that the Sony FX5 is also a compact full-frame cinema camera with great autofocus and IBIS. The FX5 can accept an optional EVF, has more reliable autofocus and records compressed X-OCN RAW video internally. However, the SHX1 features much more effective stabilization and industry-standard connections. For event work, the Sony makes more sense, whereas the Panasonic will find itself more at home in commercial environments.
