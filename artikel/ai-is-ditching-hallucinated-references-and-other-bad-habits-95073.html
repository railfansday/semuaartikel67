AI is ditching hallucinated references — and other bad habits

Recent findings suggest that hallucinated academic citations have become a hallmark of the artificial-intelligence era. But they might soon be gone for good. Researchers have found that the latest large language models (LLMs) rarely, if ever, attribute information to sources that don’t exist.

Industry observers note that the finding is part of an analysis that reports that many LLM tendencies, such as overthinking simple mathematics problems and complying with illogical requests, fade over time as AI companies release fresh or updated models with improved capabilities. Many of these tendencies disappear about 16 months after the phenomenon is first described in a published paper. The analysis, described in a blogpost, has not been peer reviewed.

“We, as consumers of these systems, need to not have such a fixed view of what AI can do and what it can’t do,” states co-author Owen Queen, an AI researcher at Stanford University in California.

According to recent reports, but just because an LLM behaviour fades as models improve doesn’t mean that the effect immediately disappears, says Maxim Topaz, a health-care AI researcher at Columbia University in Fresh York City. Topaz has found1 that the number of hallucinated citations in academic papers has risen drastically since 2023, probably because more researchers than ever before are using LLMs, and they often aren’t using the latest models.

Queen and his colleague identified 50 widely cited LLM behaviours that were described in publications between late 2022, when some of the first LLMs became widely available, and late 2025. They then gave the same prompts that first elicited the behaviour in the original publications to 20 LLMs that were released from mid-2024 to late 2026 to see if the effect has strengthened or weakened over time. For example, they asked each model to count the number of r’s in ‘strawberry’ — something that LLMs have found difficult but can now do more reliably.

Nearly half of the behaviours they checked have largely disappeared from newer models, leading the researchers to deem those effects ‘expired’. Expired behaviours had an average lifespan of about 16 months after publication, although some were fleeting — models stopped overthinking simple maths problems just a month and a half after this tendency was published. Other traits persisted, such as the stereotyping of Black women by disproportionately describing them as resilient, which lasted for longer than three years.

In a recent development, although most of the retested LLM behaviours have faded over time, three have become more pronounced. Today’s models praise what their people like even more often than they used to, LLMs favour their own writing more strongly than that of other LLMs and people; and LLMs are now even worse than they used to be at generating truly random numbers.
