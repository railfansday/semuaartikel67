Roth 401(k) matches are 'surging,' report finds. What workers should know

🌐 Source: CNBC • 📅 17 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 5 min read (1065 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Many more workers can now or will soon be able to accept a 401(k) match as a Roth contribution — giving them a latest avenue to build a bucket of tax-free savings for retirement.

Historically, workers could only accept 401(k) match money from an employer as pretax, meaning they had to defer income taxes on those funds until they withdrew them in retirement.

Until a recent rule change, workers generally couldn't elect to receive a match as a Roth contribution. With Roth savings, investors don't get an up-front tax break — they pay income tax on the funds now but can take out the contributions and growth tax-free in retirement.

It has been revealed that A law known as Secure 2.0, which former President Joe Biden signed into law in 2022, gave employers the option to add a Roth match as a feature of their 401(k) prepares. If a proposal offers a Roth match, it's not automatic; workers must opt in.

The number of employers that now let workers accept a 401(k) match or other employer contribution as Roth savings has "surged," according to a upcoming report from the Plan Sponsor Council of America, a trade group representing employers.

It has been revealed that about 42% of surveyed businesses that sponsored a 401(k) initiative in 2025 have added or will add an option to accept employer contributions as Roth savings, according to an annual PSCA report published Nov. 10. That's more than double the share — about 20% — that did so in 2024, the report found.

Another 11% are still considering the option, according to the report.

The PSCA report is based on data from 753 employers that sponsor 401(k) intends of various sizes.

It has been revealed that "My guess is we will continue to see [the share] increase," said Hattie Greenan, the group's research director. "Roth is becoming mainstream," she said.

But just because employees have the Roth match option available to them doesn't mean it will be their best option.

There are pros and cons to pretax and Roth savings, and the right choice can vary from person to person, financial advisors mentioned.

"[Roth] is not a bad idea if you're not so worried about your taxes today," said Philip Chao, a certified financial planner and chief investment officer at Experiential Wealth, based in Cabin John, Maryland. "It's personal preference.".

While there are ample benefits to saving at least some money as Roth funds in a 401(k), most investors opt against it.

In fact, while almost all 401(k) prepares already allow workers to save part of their paychecks as a Roth contribution, only a quarter of workers actually do so, according to the PSCA report.

"The list of cons may be short for Roth 401(k)s, but missing tax deferral is a big one," according to a May article written by Fidelity, a retirement proposal administrator. "When faced with a choice of paying more tax now or later, most people choose to pay later, hence the low participation rates for Roth 401(k)s.".

Tax deferral has long been a "key driver" of retirement savings, Fidelity said.

Industry observers note that saving on a pretax basis effectively increases a worker's take-home pay. It also reduces their adjusted gross income today, and may therefore positively impact the calculation of eligibility for child tax credits, financial aid for college or federal aid, according to Fidelity.

A Fidelity example demonstrates the financial benefit of an up-front tax break: Let's say a saver with a $75,000 annual income in the 22% federal income tax bracket makes a $400 monthly 401(k) contribution. Making the full contribution as Roth would reduce their paycheck by $400. Making the contribution into a traditional — i.e., pretax — 401(k) would reduce their paycheck by just $312.

For those who can stomach the up-front tax hit, Roth provides for powerful tax-planning opportunities in future years, according to financial advisors.

Recent findings suggest that "Roth gives you a lot more flexibility at retirement," Chao mentioned. "But you have to be OK with paying years of tax on it first. It's never something for nothing. There's always a trade-off.".

In a recent development, for example, the government doesn't require investors to withdraw a minimum amount from Roth accounts in retirement, as it does with pretax savings. These annual "required minimum distributions," or RMDs, generally start at age 73 for traditional 401(k)s and IRAs, among other accounts.

It has been revealed that additionally, withdrawing money from Roth accounts in retirement can help limit taxes on Social Security benefits and reduce Medicare premiums, which are based on taxable income. Roth withdrawals don't count as taxable income.

Retirees can also make strategic withdrawals from Roth accounts in certain years to avoid jumping into a higher federal or state income tax bracket, advisors said.

"Once you pay the taxes [on a Roth contribution], the government is out of your hair," Chao said. "There's no amount you must draw out. It's your money; it's all tax-free when you take it out.".

According to recent reports, for example, many investors must wait until at least age 59 1/2 to withdraw funds tax-free from a Roth 401(k) account. Additionally, due to a so-called five-year rule, investors must have had Roth 401(k) funds for at least five years to make a tax-free withdrawal.

Because Roth employer matching contributions are included in the saver's income, investors should ensure they have enough tax withheld from their paycheck to cover the income tax, according to Fidelity.

The ultimate goal for savers choosing whether to make Roth or pretax contributions is to pay income taxes when their tax rate is lowest, advisors mentioned.

For example, Roth savings can work well for younger savers over the long term if they anticipate their incomes — and tax rates — will grow over time, said Andrew Bachman, director of tax and retirement income on Fidelity's financial solutions team.

Similar logic applies to people who plan to move from a low-tax to a high-tax state in retirement, such as from Texas to California, he said.

While it's impossible to know future tax rates, investors can hedge by splitting contributions between both types of accounts, advisors said.

Similar to contributions, workers may be able to split a 401(k) match between pretax and Roth savings. However, workers will be constrained by what their employer does and doesn't allow, Chao said.
