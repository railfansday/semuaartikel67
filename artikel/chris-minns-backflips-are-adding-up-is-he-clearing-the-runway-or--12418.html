From train timetables to coffee cups and property underquoting, the NSW premier is making policy reversals well ahead of the March election.

If recent coverage is to be believed, the Newly introduced South Wales premier, Chris Minns, is a talented gymnast. A last-minute intervention by the premier on Monday afternoon, the day before a suite of reforms on underquoting were due to take effect, marks a quadruple or quintuple policy “backflip” by Minns in recent weeks, depending on who you ask.

The laws banning real estate agents from lowering the advertised price of a property beneath a previously rejected written offer have been shelved for up to a year, after the premier flagged on Monday morning he had received concerns from estate agents in his own electorate about their effect amid a housing market downturn.

The NSW opposition leader, Kellie Sloane, remarked the move was “government by 2GB”, referencing the Sydney talkback radio station which had also raised the issue on Monday morning.

“But this isn’t the first time” she told presenter Ben Fordham on Tuesday.

First, the government backed down last month from implementing changes to several local and regional rail line timetables after criticism from commuters.

Next, after a social media post by Sloane, it changed gender-neutral language of “carer” and “lactating parents” in a upcoming parliamentary breastfeeding policy to reflect what Minns said was “commonsense language like ‘mother’ and ‘breastfeeding’”.

After the Daily Telegraph ran a “Nup to the Cup” campaign against plastics legislation, which included requiring cafes to offer dine-in customers mugs and glasses instead of takeaway cups, the government dropped the changes, which would not have been implemented until 2028.

This weekend, it made an election promise of a two-year funding guarantee for community preschools after the opposition had warned the sector was facing a $150m annual cut from January.

But Eamonn Fitzpatrick, a lobbyist and former communications and campaign advisor to federal and state Labor governments, notes the reversals are more about “clear air” than pre-campaign jitters as the government enters the final sitting days before the March election.

Recent findings suggest that “That is the job of this final sitting week, let’s not forget you go into summer. It’s a dead period,” claims Fitzpatrick, who worked alongside Minns in the office of former Labor premier Morris Iemma.

“What you’re seeking to do, actually, in either putting things on hold, changing your minds, revising policies, you’re actually just minimising distraction … clearing the runway.”.

Fitzpatrick says interventions are part of Minns’ brand as a strong leader, but insists his style is no more top-down than previous premiers.

Minns has consistently polled ahead of Sloane as preferred premier but he has made no secret about the threat from One Nation, whom he and his ministers have started to refer to as the state’s unofficial opposition.

Peter Lewis, the non-executive chair of strategic communications company Essential, which undertook qualitative research for Labor in the last federal election, stated recent polling suggested voting intention in November in NSW was at 27% for Labor, 26% for One Nation and 23% for the Coalition.

Lewis says he thinks the threat of One Nation in NSW is being “used to push the political debate towards the right and could be used as an excuse for the government not to embrace more progressive policies”.

“I wonder if there is a bit of a mistake in that. One Nation voters are being driven by dissatisfaction with the status quo and so governments that are seen as actually addressing issues probably have a better case to answer.”.

Defending its underquoting backflip, Labor has noted it is protecting buyers from being shut out of sales at lower prices amid a downturn which has seen home values in Sydney drop by 10.1% from their 2026 peak, according to data from Cotality.

In question time, the fair trading minister, Anoulack Chanthivong, whose department was responsible for the reforms and who has now been overruled for a third time by Minns on policy, said the market had changed and the government had responded.

Both he and Minns pointed out that Sloane and the opposition had supported the underquoting laws at the time, which were conceived for a “rising hot market”.

But, Lewis notes “there is a cost in being seen to change a position” – and points to Peter Dutton’s 2025 federal election campaign as an example.

“Maybe Minns has done this early enough in the cycle for it not to be an glitch, but as you get close to the election it changes, particularly when they’re walking back commitments that have been made to benefit people.
