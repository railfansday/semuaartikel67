Latest Update: Google’s Xreal Aura, Reviewed: The Weird Evolution of VR Into Glasses Is Already Underway

  • It has been revealed that there are VR headsets, and there are smart glasses.
  • Recent findings suggest that the Xreal Aura shoots for the moon, aiming to bridge glasses and VR, and to shrink everything down on the go.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Let me tell you about a strange, fascinating and rough tweener product called Xreal Aura.

It has been revealed that there are VR headsets, and there are smart glasses. And there’s a new class of devices that’s falling right in between. What do we call these things? I have no idea. The category includes the Meta VR Glasses coming next year and now the Aura, a collaboration by Google and Xreal.

Recent findings suggest that the Xreal Aura shoots for the moon, aiming to bridge glasses and VR, and to shrink everything down on the go. It’s often fascinating, and more often frustrating. At times, it almost feels like a VR headset. At other times it feels like a superpowered version of the tethered display glasses that Xreal and several others already make, that act like wearable monitors for your phone or laptop or almost anything else.

In a recent development, I think it’s actually an experimental bridge to a future where phones will play nicer with advanced smart glasses. Those future glasses could create floating displays and workspaces all around you, if you wish. Consider the Aura, in a way, Google’s early attempt to get there.

Emphasis on early. Right now, the Xreal Aura’s features, OS and especially inputs feel rough to use at times and sometimes hard to get into a natural flow with. But it reveals what something of this size could be capable of with more software and input-flow tweaks. And when it does work, it feels wildly like a portable VR/AR headset in a way I’ve never really seen before.

The Aura aspires to be a shrunk-down version of last year’s Samsung Galaxy XR, an expensive mixed-reality headset similar to Apple’s Vision Pro. Like the Galaxy XR, the Aura runs Android XR, a variant of Android that floats apps all around. It even runs VR apps. At times it gets close. Other times, it’s not nearly close enough.

Industry observers note that what’s wild about the Aura is that it folds down into a case that’s truly small enough to slide into my bag. But setting up and using the Aura often involves patience and experimentation. When it works, it’s a heck of a peek at a future that’s likely around the corner. But the hoops you have to jump through to make it happen sometimes make me want to hop out.

In a recent development, android XR is Google’s OS for AR/VR/AI devices and wearables, and aims to connect glasses and headsets of all shapes and sizes to Google and Gemini services and apps. The Samsung Galaxy XR, released last year, was the first. Coming up next, in just a few weeks, are Google and Samsung’s Warby Parker- and Gentle Monster-designed AI smart glasses.

Sources indicate that the Xreal Aura is more Galaxy XR than smart glasses, in concept: It’s an AR/VR headset that you can take on the go. The glasses connect with a USB-C cable to an included processor puck that runs Android XR. It can display Android apps in both 2D and immersive 3D.

In a recent development, the Aura supports hand tracking and can allow full spatial movement to place floating 3D objects or play immersive games. It can float apps all around you, and you can navigate apps at the same time. You can use hand tracking to open or tap those apps, or a trackpad that’s built into the top of the processor puck. You can also pair a Bluetooth keyboard, or game controller, or a mouse, like I’m doing.

The kit tucks into an included carry case that’s smaller than the one I carry my Nintendo Switch 2 in. It’s easy to pack away for travel. I love this part of the Aura so, so much.

In a recent development, it’s $1,279 for the version with 256GB of storage and 12GB RAM or $1,499 for 512GB/16GB, but it’s on sale until the end of the year for $1,099/$1,299. That onboard storage is all you get, though. There’s no expandable microSD card slot. I reviewed the 256GB version.

The display tech in the Aura is an enhanced version of what’s already on Xreal’s other tethered display glasses, and similar to what Viture and TCL RayNeo use. The twin micro OLED displays are impressively bright and crisp, projected via prisms to look like they’re floating in the room with you. The “birdbath” display tech means the lenses are chunky and odd-looking, but they’re also a lot bigger and better-looking than what’s possible on display-enabled wireless glasses like Meta’s Ray-Ban Displays.

They have a 70-degree field of view, something that beats out previous generations of AR glasses like Magic Leap or the upcoming Snap Specs. The fidelity, although capping at 1,920×1,200 pixels per eye, is also really crisp and great for movie watching or games (up to 120Hz refresh). It’s enough to feel like a significant movie screen is projected across the room or to lay out a virtual computer monitor, but there are clear edges where the image is still cut off compared to VR headsets (which tend to have 90 degree or better fields of view).

When running VR apps, which this can shockingly do, the graphics look sharp but narrowly framed. It’s good enough to use VR in a pinch, though: I floated through 3D Immersive Google Maps, dragging and scrolling with my fingers in the air, or played the VR role-playing game Demeo, exploring a tabletop board game dungeon, or played back 360-degree videos on YouTube. But the limits of the viewing field become a lot more stark in these modes, making me wish I had a VR headset instead. Then again, I never travel with VR headsets, and I’d travel with this.

The display’s visuals seemed to tear a few times while playing YouTube videos. Tracking, using the built-in cameras, also sometimes drifted. Mostly, the displays stayed where I put them, but sometimes they’d shift slightly out of alignment.

The processor puck means the Xreal Aura can open up most Android and Chrome-based apps I can think of, but not all of them. The Qualcommm Snapdragon Reality Elite chip on the puck seems more than zippy enough to handle whatever multiple games and apps (even VR ones) I throw at it, but not all Android apps are available: Developers can choose not to opt in, as with Netflix and Disney Plus, but they can be loaded up in-browser.

According to recent reports, other apps aren’t necessarily optimized for the fluid way the Aura can open an app in any size. I watched a Jets game on Paramount Plus on the Aura, and it stayed in a fixed window size. I could move closer or further away, but it wouldn’t enlarge.

The whole Aura interface feels new-ish, even if you’ve used VR. The glasses enable hand tracking that’s surprisingly OK: Tapping and pinching works, and an extended pointer can reach at a distance and click and drag or adjust windows. When my hand passes in front of the Aura’s display, it even gets overlaid with a virtual hand mapped onto mine, like VR.

According to recent reports, there’s no eye tracking, though, something Apple Vision Pro, Samsung Galaxy XR and Meta VR Glasses do. Eye tracking is helpful: It means you can lock into something and click it without lifting your hand. Here, I found I had to lift and aim my hand a lot, like an interactive remote. Hand tracking does work somewhat below the display’s field of view, but you often won’t know if you’re interacting until that hand’s lifted up higher. This is where the Aura’s smaller-than-VR viewing area becomes a problem.

It’s particularly an concern when playing some VR games. I tried Synth Riders, a popular fitness music movement game that’s also on Android XR. I could see what was further off in front of me, but the limited viewing area meant my hands were out of sight when in my peripheral vision, so I couldn’t reach for targets near me or off to the sides.

There’s a second way to control Aura: The processor puck itself has a touchpad across its whole top. Hold it and it’s a touch remote: I can drag my finger and touch-click. On a table next to a paired keyboard, it’s a trackpad.

That’s super clever, but the trackpad’s performance ranged from OK to poor depending on the apps I ran. There are some multitouch elements too, but it still felt less effective as a headset control than I wanted.

Industry observers note that the puck has discrete volume controls and a power button with a fingerprint sensor for secure logins. But the side button wasn’t always in an easy place to press, depending on how I held the puck.

According to recent reports, you could also just use voice to control things: Saying “Hey Google” or launching live Gemini can open apps, handle dictation and feel like a control system of sorts. But Gemini can’t always do things in apps. It’s more of a launcher.

In a recent development, there aren’t a ton of unique immersive Android XR experiences, unfortunately, even a year after the Samsung Galaxy XR debuted. The Aura can technically run almost all of those same apps, including many 2D ones that show up on the Google Play store in-headset. On the plus side, VR apps working at all on glasses this small feels like magic, despite some limits.

It has been revealed that demeo, one of several VR games I found that run on these glasses, looks great and is surprisingly playable. I can cast up a virtual tabletop RPG game in front of me, and my hands bring up cards that I can pick and play on the field. An included Galapagos immersive 3D documentary app looks lovely.

YouTube and Google Maps are once again stars of Android XR, just like they were on Galaxy XR when I reviewed it last year. YouTube has plenty of 3D and 360-degree videos you can view, and Google Maps’ immersive 3D earth modes and, occasionally, 3D scanned interior locations are still great to browse on the Aura’s smaller screens.

Sources indicate that I fell in love with a demo of Sock Puppet Superstar, too. It’s a hand-puppet-singing VR game that works well on the Aura.

Mixed reality VR experiences that try to project a few simple things into a room work best, effectively turning the glasses into augmented reality. Cubism, a hand-tracking enabled puzzle game I’ve played on other VR headsets, works well here too. The accuracy of the hand tracking isn’t always great for precision gripping the corners of the virtual floating blocks, though.

The refresh rate on these VR apps isn’t as ultrasmooth as on Quest 3 or Steam Frame, but they do look sharp. Except, as I stated before, the viewing area ends up clipped more narrowly, so immersion feels blindered.

Industry observers note that some VR apps didn’t run at all, though. A VR air hockey game on Android XR wouldn’t let me use my hands to control the pucks, for some reason. I know this is a prelaunch review of the Aura, but expect some apps to act oddly. You can sideload APKs of other VR apps, which is great. Google’s AI Studio tools also work in spatial 3D in these glasses, which means you could vibecode experiences on them.

Think of the Aura as a pair of super-powered Xreal glasses with a better viewing area and an included processor puck. Xreal’s existing line of other display glasses float microOLED displays via a USB-C tether in a similar way. The Aura glasses have similar Xreal controls on the arm, but they’re modified to Android XR interface controls that are a little different. There are volume buttons, a button that changes the dimming level of the electrochromic lenses from clear to nearly full black, and a top button that brings up the home app grid when clicked once, or rolls out live Gemini when held.

There’s also an array of three built-in cameras that handle room tracking, visual awareness for Gemini and hand tracking. Two are in the corners of the frame, one’s above the nose. Xreal’s other glasses, for the most part, are camera-free unless you buy a snap-in module.

The prism-like lenses that reflect the microOLED displays from the top of the frame are flatter like the Xreal One Pro line, and prescription lens inserts snap in the same way – I used the ones from the Pro and they work fine.

Industry observers note that speakers are embedded in the arms, pumping out audio that sounds as good as Xreal’s other glasses. Fine for games and movies, but you might prefer earbuds for isolation and privacy.

Industry observers note that the display’s field of view, at 70 degrees, is bigger than Xreal’s largest size on the One Pro (57 degrees), and it feels like a larger projected display in front of me. The resolution is still just the same as Xreal’s other recent glasses, but things still look crisp, even for reading.

Sizewise, they’re like many other tethered glasses: bulkier and weirder than normal glasses, but a lot lighter than any VR headset. Three sets of nosepads are included for fit adjustment, and the arms bend at several angles, too.

In a recent development, I’m writing this review now in the Aura, at my dining room table, with a separately sold folding wireless keyboard made by Xreal. The keyboard performance in Chrome and Docs is fantastic. The trackpad, not quite as much. But I really feel like I can write on this.

Navigating Chrome and work apps also is possible: Logged into my office’s Google-based system, I’ve been handling emails and chats and little needs fine.

In a recent development, getting files on and off the Aura is the trick. I’m Mac- and iPhone-based, so I’d need to put things in Drive and Google apps to triangulate more easily.

But what’s also impressive is that I can plug in other devices, including my MacBook, and use the Aura as a virtual screen. I can float my MacBook and the Aura apps side by side.

According to recent reports, here’s the catch: My MacBook keyboard only controls my Mac, and the Aura’s keyboard and hand tracking only work for the Aura apps. So I need two keyboards side by side to multitask.

Recent findings suggest that to be clear, no one’s handled handing off of peripherals between connected devices well, not even Apple. But it points to the biggest thorn in Aura’s device-bridging dreams.

Industry observers note that connecting to lots of other devices via the Xreal Aura is a wild experience. You just need to cable in with a DisplayPort-ready USB-C cable to do it. The second cable included in the Aura box won’t do it; oddly, that’s just a charging cable.

Right now, I’m connecting the latest iPad Mini and casting a second display up in the air. I’m typing on a Logitech keyboard and mouse paired to the iPad, because the Xreal inputs won’t control it. But the point, mainly, is that it connects. And Gemini can still give me feedback on what it sees on my iPad’s virtual screen.

Whatever you plug in can cast a virtual monitor in-glasses: MacBook, iPhone, Steam Deck. Shockingly, even a Nintendo Switch 2 works, something Xreal’s team has been excited about. There are caveats: The Switch 2 goes into docked mode and needs the Joy-Cons detached, and only certain higher-fidelity DisplayPort USB-C cables work. I played Ocarina of Time on the Aura’s screen and it looked great, but the framerate was a bit lower than what I get on the Switch 2 normally. But again, I can ask Gemini questions about the game as I’m playing, and it sees and gives me answers, provided I’ve clicked a little on-screen toggle to share my screen with Gemini’s visual mode.

In a recent development, bridging between devices is exciting to me, but it’s really just limited to treating the input like a video-in that pops on a second screen in-glasses. Meta’s VR Glasses will do this too. It’s a great bonus addition, but having to cable both my glasses and my other device to the Aura puck means too many cables for my comfort.

The glasses also work on their own without the puck, defaulting to standard Xreal glasses firmware and controls. That’s great if I want to use them with my laptop/phone/etc on the fly without needing the Aura puck, at least.

Sources indicate that turning on Gemini Live with a press of the top button on Aura’s glasses means you can have Gemini in your life constantly, if you wish. Toggle a little upward-arrow icon to share your camera feed and on-screen apps, and suddenly Gemini can also describe YouTube videos for you, or read what you’re writing, or tell you what’s in your hand.

It’s an all-seeing assistant that can even work with Google’s circle-to-search, although that feature’s hard to trigger (press and hold my fingers and click to bring it up, it seems). But I can then circle something in front of me, capture it, and Google AI search brings up results instantly (a book I’m holding, a freezeframe on TV or something on one of my virtual screens).

It has been revealed that this is a step beyond the type of live, camera-enabled AI modes in smart glasses. It can see the world via its cameras and all the virtual apps that get conjured.

In a recent development, that being said, it’s hit or miss. The video feed quality isn’t great, so the AI can’t always read things at a distance. But I wore the Xreal Aura across my kitchen, making coffee while floating a YouTube window in front of me and talking to Gemini, and it felt like some sort of cyberpunk future I’d only experienced in bits and pieces before. It’s something you could walk around with, technically, but not a device I’d wear all day (nor is it designed to be).

Sources indicate that xreal promises four hours of battery life on the Aura, which is more than most VR headset claims of 2 to 3 hours. In practice, it feels good. I can use it for several hours and still have a fair amount of battery left, which seems promising. I’ve only been testing for a week, though.

Sources indicate that but the processor puck can overheat. The puck has venting fans that can pump very warm air, and it’s not meant to be put in a pocket, even though that’s exactly what I want to do with it when I’m wearing Aura on my face. Instead, there’s an included belt clip. I tried putting it in my pocket, and pretty soon I got an overheating warning pop-up message. Don’t put it in your pocket.

I don’t think I’ve ever been as fascinated and borderline overwhelmed as I have with the Xreal Aura. It points in all sorts of directions for where phones, AI assistants and glasses can be heading. Could this little tweener device be my portable workstation eventually?

Right now it’s more of an experiment with fascinating side benefits. It can’t replace a standard VR headset, though parts come close.

The Xreal Aura tested even my upcoming-device tolerance, although I see amazing glimmers. It’s coming with me on my next trips for sure, and I’ll let you know what happens when I do. But it’s not yet the true fusion solution I’m waiting for. And yet, I’m still convinced that the concept, remixed and refined, will be.
