Malaria mosquito bites aren’t one quick jab but five separate moves under the skin

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Under the skin, a malaria mosquito bite runs in five steps, and the researchers who filmed it say the first two are the ones to stop.

Sources indicate that A mosquito bite feels like a single stab. Beneath the surface, however, it’s a sequence. A malaria mosquito pierces the skin in a fraction of a second.

In a recent development, then she spends about 20 seconds pushing a flexible needle in and out, searching for a blood vessel. Only then does she start to drink.

Researchers who filmed and recorded that process on a living human arm counted five separate steps, and the mosquito’s legs and mouthparts move in time with each one.

The experiments were done in Christopher Potter’s lab in the Solomon H. Snyder Department of Neuroscience at the Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine.

Joanna Konopka, who is at the Johns Hopkins Malaria Research Institute, and Danny Poinapen, who also works at Western University’s Robarts Research Institute in Canada, led the work.

Every bite happened on the right forearm of one volunteer, and the mosquitoes were lab-raised Anopheles coluzzii, a malaria carrier from sub-Saharan Africa.

Recent findings suggest that in an interview with Earth.com, Konopka was asked whether any of the five steps could be interrupted to stop a bite or an infection. Her answer named the first two.

“The first two stages of the bite (skin piercing and blood vessel searching) are most crucial for disease transmission, because this is when the pathogen is most likely to be introduced via mosquito saliva,” she said.

The volunteer wore a small, clear acrylic box strapped to her forearm with sports wristbands. It left a window of skin about an inch (2.5 centimeters) square.

One female mosquito at a time was released onto that patch while a high-speed camera recorded at up to 250 frames per second. A tracking program followed the legs, the mouthparts, and the two feelers beside them, which are called palps.

What happens below the surface can’t be filmed, so the team recorded it electrically instead. Each mosquito was glued at the back to a gold wire finer than a human hair while the volunteer held an electrode.

The moment the needle broke the skin, the circuit closed, and every movement underneath demonstrated up as a change in voltage.

“We were fascinated by the coordinated and synchronized movements that female mosquitoes exhibit above and below our skin when they are biting us,” Poinapen told Earth.com.

“And some of these movements occur as fast as an eye blink yet they are finely coordinated!”.

Five stages stood out in the voltage traces: piercing the skin, searching for a vessel, drinking blood, pulling the mouthparts back, and withdrawing them.

In a recent development, A full bite took about 160 seconds on average. Roughly 128 of those seconds went to drinking and about 21 to the search for a vessel.

Sources indicate that above the skin, the body kept pace. As the mosquito pushes the needle deeper, her front legs bend and lift off the skin.

When she pulls back, the legs straighten and press down, and the bend begins a fraction of a second before the mouthpart moves.

A mosquito repeated that push and pull about three times before settling into a steadier search. Her palps changed with each stage too. During the hunt for a vessel they swung side to side above the skin, 10 to 25 times a second.

According to recent reports, the moment blood began to flow, the palps stopped swinging and started a faint up-and-down vibration. The team suspects that vibration matches the pumps in the mosquito’s head that draw blood in.

The team ran the same recordings on Aedes aegypti, the mosquito that spreads yellow fever and dengue.

The two groups split about 150 million years ago, and the traces highlighted it. The malaria mosquito stabbed its needle in abruptly, while Aedes worked it in gradually, with a steady rhythm.

Industry observers note that once inside, Anopheles found a vessel about 17 seconds sooner, with shorter and more targeted probing. Even so, both finished a meal in about the same time, roughly 160 seconds for Anopheles and 165 for Aedes.

According to recent reports, the volunteer reported that every Anopheles bite was noticeable whereas the Aedes bites were painless. Whether that holds for anyone else is still an open question, and Earth.com asked the researchers whether the abrupt stab is why malaria mosquito bites seem to hurt more.

“This is not an established phenomenon – different people can have different experiences and reactions to the bites of these two mosquitoes,” Konopka and Poinapen said.

Industry observers note that they added that the different piercing patterns might relate to when each mosquito is active. Malaria mosquitoes bite mainly at dusk and at night, while yellow fever mosquitoes bite mostly in the daytime.

Mosquitoes taste and smell with their legs and mouthparts as well as their antennae. To test how much the bite depends on that, the team bred Anopheles that lacked one of two genes.

Whole families of taste and smell receptors need those genes in order to work.

One gene, called IR76b, is active in nerve cells of the legs, the mouth tip, and the needle itself, the three parts that touch the skin. The other, orco, is active in the mouth tip but not in the legs or the needle.

Sources indicate that mosquitoes missing both copies of IR76b mostly didn’t bite at all. More than half made no attempt in 20 minutes on the volunteer’s arm. Of the 14 tested, six probed and only four managed a partial meal.

Mosquitoes missing orco still bit, some of them a little later than normal, and 98 percent of their probes ended in a blood meal.

Industry observers note that mutants that did bite kept the same timing through all five steps. The one difference was in the drinking itself, which ran in a more regular, repetitive pattern than in normal mosquitoes.

“We found that some malaria mosquitoes with impaired sense of taste and smell had difficulty initiating and completing bites,” the two researchers wrote.

“This indicates that mosquitoes heavily rely on their ability to flavor us during the biting process.”.

All of this rests on one volunteer’s arm and on lab strains of each species. So the team can’t yet say how much a bite changes on a different person’s skin, or with wild mosquitoes.

According to recent reports, the mosquitoes with no working IR76b never fed while wired up, so there are no recordings of their bites. A third family of taste receptors couldn’t be switched off at all, because no shared gene controls it.

According to recent reports, the biggest gap is under the skin. One possibility the researchers raise is that the needle itself senses chemical cues on the way to a blood vessel, and that mosquitoes without IR76b can no longer aim it.

Following the needle’s movements inside the skin, and finding those cues, is the work the team states is needed next. The same setup, they wrote, could also test how repellents, skin microbes, and different hosts change a bite.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.
