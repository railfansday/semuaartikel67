Hackers help themselves to 1Password's source code and customer data

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

An intruder planted malicious code in backend services, but the biz insists passwords are safe.

1Password is reassuring customers that their vaults remain secure after an attacker breached its systems and stole account information and private source code.

The Canadian security biz disclosed this week that an attacker had compromised an employee's corporate GitHub account, made off with private source code, and deployed malicious code to its backend services to siphon off customer information.

1Password, which began investigating the intrusion on November 19, hasn't remarked how many customers were affected, but confirmed that the stolen data includes names and email addresses used to sign in to the service, account identifiers, timestamps showing when people created and last accessed their accounts, and various pieces of non-secret account metadata.

Recent findings suggest that the firm insists the information that customers actually pay it to protect was beyond the intruder's reach. "At no time were customer vault contents accessed by, or available to, the attacker," 1Password noted.

Arguably more troubling is the revelation that the intruder gained access to 1Password's continuous integration and continuous delivery (CI/CD) infrastructure, which houses credentials used to keep its products running. The company states it has rotated or revoked potentially exposed credentials and that neither its apps nor its browser extensions were affected.

1Password hasn't explained how access to an employee's GitHub account enabled the attacker to deploy malicious code to its backend services, nor has it detailed the extent of the attacker's access to its software build and distribution infrastructure. The organization did not respond to The Register's questions.

The miscreant also got their hands on tokens embedded in emails sent to some customers as part of account invitations, provisioning, and recovery processes. These tokens aren't enough to access an account on their own, according to 1Password, as owners must also authenticate via single sign-on or enter a verification code sent to their registered email address.

In a recent development, the company indicates it specifically notified the small number of owners with affected provisioning or recovery processes underway. It found no internal evidence of account takeovers using the exposed provisioning tokens.

Still, the breach has prompted the password management biz to beef up those verification codes, replacing the six-digit numeric variety with ten-character alphanumeric codes to make brute-forcing them considerably more difficult.

Industry observers note that 1Password has booted the intruder out of its GitHub environment, revoked the stolen token, and rolled back the affected backend services to clean versions of the code. It's since shored up security around its development infrastructure and employees' devices, and brought in outside security specialists to review its handiwork.

The incident recalls the first stage of LastPass's 2022 breach, when an attacker compromised its development environment and stole source code. Information obtained in that intrusion helped enable a second attack that exposed customer vault backups, including encrypted passwords.

Industry observers note that the UK Information Commission's Office slapped LastPass with a £1.2 million ($1.6 million) fine last December over the incident, which compromised the personal information of up to 1.6 million UK users. Among the regulator's gripes was that LastPass had fallen short of the higher standard of security expected of a organization entrusted with people's passwords.

Customers aren't being asked to change their account passwords or Secret Keys, neither of which 1Password stores, although the company is warning owners to be on the lookout for phishing attempts.

A list of email addresses belonging to people known to use a particular password manager is a handy thing for a phisher to have, especially when said password manager has just given them a plausible pretext for getting in touch. ®.
