A 50-year-old theory about evolution may have been wrong all along

For more than 50 years, scientists have debated a provocative idea about evolution: Could organisms protect their most important genes by making them less likely to mutate? First proposed in 1972, the hypothesis challenges the traditional understanding of how genetic changes arise. Now, fresh research from the University of Bath suggests that the evidence supporting this idea has been misleading.

According to recent reports, in a study published in PLOS Biology, Dr. Sofia Radrizzani and Professor Laurence D Hurst of the University of Bath's Centre for Evolution examined the largest collection of spontaneous genetic mutations assembled to date. Their analysis focused on two microorganisms: the bacterium Escherichia coli (E. coli) and the single-celled fungus baker's yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae).

The findings challenge the claim that natural selection can reduce mutation rates in genes essential for survival. Although this theory has repeatedly attracted scientific attention since its introduction, including a renewed debate in 2023, the researchers say their results undermine its central premise.

"The claim that mutation is slower for crucial genes is a triumph of hype over substance," noted Dr. Radrizzani, who co-wrote the upcoming paper as part of her PhD. "It's a really old suggestion and is reinvented in various forms routinely.".

How Mutations and Natural Selection Drive Evolution.

In a recent development, the conventional explanation of evolution involves two main processes. Genetic mutations first introduce variation among organisms. Natural selection then influences which of those variations become more common, favoring traits that improve survival and reproduction. This process can be compared to a sieve that separates different kinds of material, allowing some genetic changes to persist while others disappear.

A familiar example comes from Darwin's finches, which Charles Darwin first encountered on the Galápagos Islands in 1835. These birds have different beak shapes that help them feed on different foods. Random mutations contribute to variations in beak size and shape, and when certain food sources become more plentiful, birds with better-suited beaks are more likely to survive and reproduce. Over many generations, these advantageous traits can spread through the population.

However, an alternative hypothesis suggests that mutations may not occur as randomly as the conventional model assumes. According to this idea, organisms have evolved ways to reduce mutations in their most critical genes (such as those involved in replicating DNA or maintaining basic cellular machinery). Meanwhile, genes in which changes might provide useful adaptations (such as those in which mutations give bacteria resistance to antibiotics, or change beak dimensions) could experience higher mutation rates.

In a recent development, at first glance, protecting essential genes seems like an obvious evolutionary advantage. Mutations affecting these genes can disrupt vital biological processes, cause genetic diseases, or even kill an organism. If such changes are so dangerous, why wouldn't natural selection favor mechanisms that prevent them?

The problem, researchers explain, is that mutations in individual genes are already relatively rare. Reducing those rates even further would provide only a tiny additional benefit, making it difficult for natural selection to favor such a protective system.

The Mystery of Disappearing Genetic Mutations.

Another problem lies in how scientists have measured mutations in earlier research. Important genes may appear to mutate less frequently not because they are better protected, but because harmful mutations in those genes are promptly eliminated from populations.

Industry observers note that professor Hurst mentioned: "We'd expect natural selection to rapidly remove mutations when they occur in more important genes, because these mutations are more likely to be harmful to the organism. This can create the illusion that important genes have fewer mutations when, in reality, some mutations have simply disappeared before we have a chance to analyze them - what you can think of as 'ghost mutations'.".

This distinction is crucial. When scientists examine the genetic changes remaining in a population, they may miss mutations that occurred but were subsequently removed by natural selection. As a result, the apparent stability of essential genes could reflect the disappearance of damaging mutations rather than a lower likelihood of those mutations occurring in the first place.

A Major Test of the 50-Year-Old Evolutionary Theory.

According to recent reports, to investigate the question more directly, the University of Bath team analyzed data from experiments designed to record mutations while minimizing the influence of natural selection. These experiments, known as mutation accumulation studies, allowed researchers to examine genetic changes in E. coli and baker's yeast without the usual strong filtering effects of selection.

It has been revealed that the scientists also performed three independent tests to determine whether natural selection might still have significantly distorted the data. None revealed evidence of substantial bias.

According to recent reports, dr. Radrizzani said: "This looks like the largest and cleanest sample of mutations assembled to date.".

With this extensive dataset, the researchers investigated whether genes that are more important for survival actually experience fewer mutations.

They assessed gene importance using experiments in which individual genes were removed from organisms. Some genes are indispensable, meaning that cells die without them. Others can be deleted with little or no noticeable effect.

If the protective mutation hypothesis were correct, the most essential genes should have shown consistently lower mutation rates. Instead, the researchers found no such relationship in either microorganism.

Professor Hurst stated: "Analyze the data any which way and we found nothing. In neither species did these key genes have a lower mutation rate.".

What Really Determines How Often Genes Mutate?

The team also examined whether other measures of gene importance could explain differences in mutation rates. These included the amount of protein produced by each gene and how strongly natural selection had shaped genes over thousands of years.

Once again, the results failed to support the idea that more notable genes receive special protection.

Dr. Radrizzani said: "We found that genes producing more abundant proteins don't have lower mutation rates. Nor does the way genes evolve over thousands of years predict how frequently they mutate.".

It has been revealed that that does not mean every gene has exactly the same chance of mutating. The researchers identified features of genome structure and DNA replication that influence mutation frequency. For example, genes copied later during DNA replication tend to accumulate mutations more frequently than those copied earlier.

These findings indicate that mutation rates can vary across the genome, but the differences do not appear to reflect a system that specifically shields essential genes from genetic changes.

Recent findings suggest that professor Hurst said: "While we see known causes of variation in mutation rates, we found no evidence for the effects that have been proposed as a challenge to the conventional model. None predicted which genes would have high or low mutation rates, and there are certainly no grounds for a shift in paradigm.".

The research therefore provides upcoming support for the conventional distinction between mutation and natural selection. Although mutations do not occur at identical rates everywhere in the genome, the evidence from bacteria and yeast offers no indication that evolution systematically protects its most notable genes by reducing how often they mutate.
