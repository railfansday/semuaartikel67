According to recent reports, there’s an inherent camaraderie in potluck dinners that makes it one of the best ways to feast. And, whether it’s Friendsgiving or an anytime dinner party, potlucks make hosting more of a group effort and a participatory activity. The final dish lineup is often greater than the sum of its parts, thanks to the personal efforts of each of the guests.

Granted, hosts should keep track of what categories (sweet, savory) they have designated or delegated to their guests in order to avoid a smorgasbord of, say, 12 onion dips (although that sounds like heaven to me). Otherwise, enjoy the far more unbuttoned, go-with-the-flow glow of a holiday potluck dinner, which thrives when it rounds up travel-friendly feuilletés, pillowy focaccia loaves, and vegetarian-friendly options.

Below, the Eater staff chimed on a handful of our annual favorites.

The feuilleté is filled with layered, thin slices of ham, Swiss cheese, and Dijon mustard, and given a nice heavy cream-and-egg wash sheen. If I have time, I’ll head to my local cheese shop and see what Swiss varieties the mongers are excited about, but I also love the ease of using a pre-sliced cheese [assumes Sandra Lee voice] from the grocery store. I should also mention that this appetizer is easy to transport; you can cut it into a million tiny squares if you’re attending a significant party, or several larger squares if it’s a more intimate event. — Francky Knapp, staff writer.

Sources indicate that dirty martinis have never been dirtier or more ubiquitous (at least not in my elder millennial lifetime) and, like the Caesar, the briny, olive-y flavor has become a format that can be replicated in other edible forms. (Dirty martini deviled eggs, hello.) This dirty martini dip is a perennial fave for the savory snackers; it’s a classic cream-cheese-based party dip but with significant flavor. I like it with ridged chips, like Ruffles. I always serve it at my annual holiday party to rave reviews. Would be incredible alongside a caviar service! — Hilary Pollack, deputy editor.

I love to cook, but I’m generally not much of a baker — I don’t generally have the patience for fiddly presentation or precise measurements, and given I lack a sweet tooth, there isn’t a ton of motivation for that to change. The one exception is bread, where I’ve steadily improved my game over the years, and I’ve found focaccia to be a welcome addition to any potluck. I have a standard recipe from a bread-making class that I tend to default to, but sometimes the occasion requires something more festive. In those instances, I turn to variations like this bagel-esque concoction from Smitten Kitchen (a great addition to any brunch spread), or this cacio e pepe twist from Bon Appétit, which adds a bit more zip and character to a standard focaccia recipe. And if you have less of an aversion to fiddly-ness than I do, just Google “decorative focaccia” to see how people much more artistic than I am fashion flowers from tomatoes, rosemary, and onion slices to top their breads — if you really want to show off. — Missy Frederick, editorial director, dining.

Back when I was deep in my potluck moment, a friend at a gathering once remarked, “You love a phyllo pie.” It was then that I sheepishly realized that I did, having brought one to several gatherings this friend had attended. There are many reasons for that. They travel well: You can pack the pie up still in the springform to provide support during the journey. They’re easy to prep: You can make the fillings ahead of time and assemble the day of. And they’re not very fussy when you arrive at your destination: Simply ask your host if you can pop it in the oven briefly, and at this time of year, you can almost always glom the warmth off something else that’s in there.

Recent findings suggest that the recipe that set me off on this course came from Ottolenghi’s Shelf Love. It features cauliflower in a thick cheese sauce, making it perfect for the colder months when people want hearty food. I’ve found that the concept is welcoming to many different vegetables and all kinds of cheese; I’ve swapped in mushrooms and beans, or added caramelized onions or leeks, or taken liberties with Gruyère and greens. At gatherings with attendees who are vegan or lactose-intolerant, I’ve made them dairy-free (store-bought phyllo is generally vegan). In warmer months, or when I’d rather bring a skillet instead of a loaded springform, I opt for an approach to phyllo pie more like this spanakopita-esque recipe from Hetty McKinnon. But the format pairs well with many vegetables, and I always want to make sure to keep the vegetarians at the potluck well-fed. — Bettina Makalintal, senior reporter.
