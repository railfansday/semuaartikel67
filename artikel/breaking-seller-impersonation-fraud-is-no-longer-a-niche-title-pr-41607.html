Breaking: Seller impersonation fraud is no longer a niche title problem

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that two years is not a long time in housing. But in that short period, seller impersonation fraud has changed dramatically.

In a recent development, in ALTA’s 2024 survey, 28% of title firms reported at least one seller impersonation fraud attempt in the prior calendar year. In our latest study, that figure reached 59%. That’s more than double in two years, and that should concern everyone who depends on the integrity of a real estate transaction.

In a recent development, seller impersonation fraud occurs when a criminal poses as the legitimate owner of a property and attempts to sell it. At its core, this is an attack on property rights and on what is, for many Americans, their greatest source of wealth.

According to recent reports, for lenders, real estate professionals and others across housing, the stakes are equally clear. Every transaction depends on confidence that the person selling a property has the legal right to do so, that ownership is what the records say it is and that someone is looking closely enough to recognize when those things do not line up.

That is precisely the kind of problem the title industry was built to address.

Sources indicate that title professionals do far more than issue an insurance policy at the end of a transaction. Before closing, they examine ownership, investigate inconsistencies, resolve title defects, verify information and look for warning signs that could put a buyer, lender or property owner at risk. Seller impersonation fraud is a newer and increasingly sophisticated version of a challenge the industry has confronted for generations: determining whether a transaction is legitimate before property rights and money change hands.

And the threat is evolving quickly. Title professionals are encountering spoofed contact information, deepfake voice and image technology and increasingly sophisticated identity tactics. At the same time, criminals are broadening their targets beyond vacant land to vacation homes, rental properties, agricultural land and even primary residences, while also focusing on absentee owners, properties owned free and clear and properties tied to recently deceased owners.

The title industry is responding accordingly. 94% of firms report using multiple tools to detect fraud, with firms identifying an average of 5.3 tools as helpful, including identity verification systems, direct seller contact, multifactor authentication, approved notaries and knowledge-based authentication.

Sources indicate that but the most vital lesson is not that one particular tool works better than another. It is that effective fraud prevention is layered. It includes technology and verification, but it also depends on the judgment of experienced professionals who know when a piece of information does not fit with the rest of the transaction.

That prevention-first work is fundamental to title. Title insurance provides protection when covered problems arise, helping shield consumers from potentially devastating financial consequences. But the work starts long before a policy is issued. Title professionals work upfront to identify and resolve problems so that homebuyers, real estate professionals and lenders do not find themselves confronting a crisis after a transaction closes. In our latest survey, the clearance or curative process before closing was the most commonly reported stage for detecting seller impersonation fraud.

Much of that work is invisible when it succeeds. A suspicious transaction is questioned, an inconsistency is investigated, an identity is independently verified and a closing is stopped before money or property rights change hands. That is what prevention looks like.

It is also why conversations about housing affordability, efficiency and innovation should recognize the value of the protections already embedded in the transaction. Increasing efficiency and reducing costs are not merely worthy goals; they are important priorities for the entire housing industry. Title companies have invested heavily in technology and innovation to make transactions more efficient, and those investments are showing results: the inflation-adjusted cost of title insurance declined 32% between 2016 and 2024.

It has been revealed that we should continue that progress. But greater efficiency cannot mean removing safeguards designed to keep fraudulent transactions from closing, particularly at a moment when criminals themselves have more sophisticated tools at their fingertips.

The title industry will continue doing the work it was built to do. But protecting consumers from increasingly sophisticated fraud also creates opportunities for stronger partnerships across the housing ecosystem.

Lenders, real estate professionals and title professionals can reinforce consistent fraud-awareness messages and help consumers recognize warning signs before criminals gain their trust. Technology providers can continue developing tools that strengthen defenses. Law enforcement can investigate and prosecute the criminals behind these schemes.

Policymakers can support solutions that strengthen consumer protection while preserving the safeguards that help keep fraudulent transactions from closing. This is not about shifting responsibility from one part of the housing system to another. It is about recognizing that protecting consumers is most effective when everyone who touches a transaction understands the threat and reinforces the defenses already in place.

Seller impersonation fraud is the latest reminder that criminals will continue looking for new ways to exploit the housing market. The speed of its growth over just two years should serve as a warning.

Recent findings suggest that fortunately, our industry does not have to build a fraud-defense system from scratch. Title professionals already sit at a critical point in the transaction, examining ownership, resolving problems and helping prevent fraud before it becomes a loss.

The opportunity now is to build on that foundation together. By strengthening consumer education, sharing best practices and working closely with our housing partners and law enforcement, we can make it harder for criminals to exploit the transaction and help preserve something every participant in the housing market depends on: confidence that when a home changes hands, the transaction is legitimate and the property rights behind it are secure.
