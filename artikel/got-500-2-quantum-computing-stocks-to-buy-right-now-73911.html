According to recent reports, five hundred dollars doesn't stretch very far anymore. It buys a nice pair of headphones, a few weeks of groceries, or one good weekend away. It can also get you a small stake in one of the strangest machines anybody is trying to build.

Recent findings suggest that the idea behind quantum computing is easier to picture than it sounds. A regular computer bit works like a light switch: on or off. A qubit is closer to a dimmer that can sit anywhere in between, and when qubits are linked, they can work together to explore a huge number of possibilities. The problem is that qubits are as fragile as a soufflé. A tiny bit of heat or vibration and the whole thing collapses.

Recent findings suggest that the fix is the "logical qubit," a team of physical qubits that works like a choir. If one singer drifts off key, the others hold the note. Whoever builds lots of reliable logical qubits first wins the race.

Here are two quantum computing companies working on this very issue that I'd be willing to split a $500 investment in.

Sources indicate that infleqtion (INFQ -13.43%) traps single atoms with lasers and uses them as qubits. In September 2026, it stated it had linked 30 logical qubits in a single computation using just 80 physical qubits, hitting its 2026 goal. "Delivering 30 logical qubits is an important step on our technology roadmap," CEO Matt Kinsella said.

Sources indicate that its hardware is already in real machines. Infleqtion's processor powers Shunkai, Japan's first working full-stack neutral-atom quantum computer. "Bringing a full-stack quantum system into production operation is a meaningful step toward fault-tolerant quantum computing," noted CTO Pranav Gokhale.

In a recent development, the part I like most is that the same atoms do other jobs today. Infleqtion's Tiqker clock keeps precise time when GPS is jammed, and it recently ran for two weeks straight aboard a U.S. Navy vessel. "The Navy needs dependable timing to navigate, communicate and coordinate operations in the field," Kinsella noted. NASA also expanded its quantum gravity sensor contract, and the U.K. funded a compact radio-direction-finding system.

Sources indicate that that government work is also its weak spot. Last quarter, it cost Infleqtion more to deliver its work than it earned, partly because of timing on the NASA gravity program and thin margins on a contract that was wrapping up. Spending is climbing fast too. Its operating loss was about five times larger than a year ago. The cash it raised through its special-purpose acquisition manufacturer (SPAC) merger gives it room for now.

NYSE · As of 4:00 PM ET 11/20/26.

It has been revealed that xanadu Quantum Technologies (XNDU -6.59%) uses particles of light as qubits. "Our chips compute without coolant," CEO Christian Weedbrook mentioned, and they can be made with the same kind of silicon processes chipmakers already use.

Light's enemy is loss, meaning photons that leak away before the math is done. Weedbrook called it "our most important engineering challenge," but added, "This is primarily a challenge of engineering, not physics." In November, Xanadu and HyperLight cut the loss in a key optical switch by nearly two-thirds.

Recent findings suggest that it's also lining up help. Xanadu signed hardware partnerships with GlobalFoundries (GFS -8.68%), ASML (ASML -6.63%), and Bluefors, and released Backline, error-correction software built with Advanced Micro Devices Inc. (AMD -13.25%). Mitsubishi Chemical used its algorithms to model semiconductor materials, and Royal Bank of Canada and Booz Allen use its software to train staff. Canada is putting about $140 million toward Inception, a Toronto photonics plant Xanadu expects to start using early next year.

Here's the catch: Xanadu barely has revenue, and it's choosing to keep it that way for now. "Pursuing revenue before meaningful quantum utility or selling systems prematurely would ultimately divert talent and resources," Weedbrook noted. Its target for a commercially viable machine is 2029 or 2030, and until then, its cash burn keeps rising. Xanadu can also sell more shares through an existing program, and an executive said the company weighs that "almost on a day-by-day basis.".

Industry observers note that quantum stocks like these trade on hope as much as results, so I'd treat both like small-cap stocks and size them so a bust stings rather than hurts. I'd put about $300 into Infleqtion, since it's already getting paid for its atoms, and $200 into Xanadu as the long shot. One is earning while it builds. The other is betting everything on getting the light right, and $500 lets you own both stories without needing either one to be perfect.
