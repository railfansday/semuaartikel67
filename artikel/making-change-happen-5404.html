According to recent reports, so, you realize that the way you have been going about things doesn’t work. You might not like the way you behave or your experience of relationships. You might not like the way you feel or how your emotions impact your decisions. And you know that if you don’t turn things around, you are going to keep repeating the same cycle. But people often struggle to change even when they genuinely want to.

I cringe when I hear people say “That’s just the way I am” while at the same time complaining about the state of their relationships. Some people use that statement to avoid responsibility. They believe their personality characteristics are fixed or have repeatedly failed in their efforts to foster change within themselves. So, rather than putting effort into changing themselves, many people prefer that others would simply come to their senses and accommodate them.

You can always work on changing the environment, and this is usually what people try first. They try to convince, persuade, nudge, and manipulate others… or they shut themselves off interpersonally, go into denial, or resort to blame and attack. But these efforts rarely result in lasting positive outcomes, and people stay happy only as long as the environment is cooperating.

Here is my recommended outline for lasting change and understanding why change can be so hard. I recommend taking these steps sequentially, as they build on each other in a cumulative fashion.

It has been revealed that if you want to change yourself, you first need to consider what “self” you are referring to. Changing your entire being may seem insurmountable and lead you to give up before you even start. But many psychological theorists view the self as multifaceted. In other words, we are all groupings of associated parts, and we can use our more developed parts to help shape and coach the less developed ones that we are trying to change.

Sometimes change means de-emphasizing one previously dominant part of your personality and strengthening a part that was previously dormant.

People are often ambivalent about change and might actually get something out of maintaining their negative behaviors.

Before you lay aside your own ambivalence, let’s see what you actually get out of the behavior or experience you say you want to let go of. What will you lose if you change? What are the factors arguing against it?

In a recent development, become conscious of your own resistance. Before evoking your commitment to a newly introduced course of action, it might help to first write down all of the positive things you get out of it. For example, what if you want to cut down on your drinking? Drinking might help you gain social acceptance and feel less lonely. It might help you relax and escape your anxiety and internal demons. Or it might simply help you escape boredom and give you something to do. These are all reasonable goals, and before you lay down the booze, you might want to consider how you are going to meet them.

Industry observers note that but then you must ask yourself, what would need to change first before the behavior can change? Problematic behaviors are often not consciously chosen but, rather, come about through how we regulate our emotions— for example, putting too much pressure on an ambivalent romantic partner, talking too much about your insecurities, or getting aggravated and shutting down when interactions with a relationship partner get touchy.

In these examples, the root cause is often difficulty controlling your emotions.

Sometimes, your rational brain knows what it wants to do, but your emotional brain just won’t cooperate.

You should learn about how people with your personality style regulate anxiety. For example, a person with an avoidant attachment style will most likely regulate anxiety by withdrawing, shutting down, or dominating. A person with an anxious attachment style is most likely to regulate emotions by pressing too hard for reassurance or oversharing.

Learn about your personality and attachment style… then you will have an easier time changing your behavior. In other words, your conscious brain will understand what your emotional brain is doing and how to keep it from taking over.

Sometimes you just need an attitude adjustment. In the preceding paragraphs, I outlined how subconscious processes, like regulating emotions, influence behavior. But sometimes, behavior comes from the more conscious parts of your mind. In other words, your emotions follow your thoughts instead of the other way around. Attitudes are consciously held statements that you tell yourself about the way things are. You can change your attitudes by watching your self-talk and what you say to yourself in your head. Don’t expect to have angry, intolerant thoughts and be able to pull off controlling your emotions and acting loving.

If you want to change a part of yourself, it often helps to have another part supervise the process and give you supportive feedback. Take the example of inner child work. I’ve spoken about this previously in my posts. Although I’m not specifically recommending it here, it serves as a case in point.

According to recent reports, in this approach, you learn to view your less mature, emotional, or wounded aspects of self as the child you once were. Often, that child will have gone through some difficult times that forced him/her to learn to regulate emotions and view the world in ways that don’t serve you well in adulthood. To heal this part of ourselves, we practice seeing it as a separate entity (child). The rational/adult part of our brain then gets to serve as the caring and supportive adult caregiver who can contain and advise the more emotional and less controlled part.

Recent findings suggest that sometimes when you make positive changes, the immediate result might seem negative. For example, if you quit drinking, all of your drinking buddies might get uncomfortable hanging out with you. In short, you might lose access to your old social support system and access to romantic partners if you are single. Addressing the social support issue is one of the main reasons people consider 12-step meetings. In this case, even though the short-term impact of not drinking could seem negative, the long-term impact is likely to be healthier friends and a more sustainable, healthy lifestyle.

Similarly, if you change some of your behaviors within your family system, like caretaking others, controlling, or being in the problem or “sick role,” the short-term outcome might not feel positive. Families (and work/friend systems) usually are in some sort of emotional balance (called homeostasis). When one person changes their role in the system, it throws the system out of balance, and everyone else has to adjust their behaviors or roles to accommodate the change. Some people in the system might not like the change or the disequilibrium it creates, and they may act out in an attempt to pull you back into your role. So, the short-term impact might be to feel more cut off or alienated from family members, but the long-term impact might be healthier long-term relationships that work for you.

Industry observers note that to summarize: The suggestions I put forward in this post just touch the surface of what really would encompass a book. But I believe these are some of the most actionable principles available to get you started. And, above all, be sure to give yourself some grace and self-compassion… and this alone will do wonders to help you make change happen.
