Here are some patterns to observe to discern whether your child’s stomach pain is associated with anxiety or an underlying medical condition.

Industry observers note that Q: “My child has anxiety and often complains of tummy aches. How do I know if the stomachaches are related to anxiety or a medical condition? How do I know when to see a doctor?”.

Sources indicate that as parents, it’s tricky to untangle common complaints about stomachaches from chronic anxiety, an underlying medical problem, or even school avoidance. Here are a few ways to tell the difference, and clear signs that it’s time to seek medical help.

According to recent reports, understand the mind-body connection. Our gut and brain are in constant conversation. When a child feels overwhelmed, scared, or worried, their body will respond with stomach pain, nausea, constipation or diarrhea, and a generalized discomfort. These symptoms are not imagined or exaggerated; they are physical reactions to emotional stress.

It has been revealed that observe timing and patterns. When are stomachaches happening? Stomach pain related to anxiety trends to show up during specific times: before and around school, tests, social events, and bedtime. Does your child’s stomachache dissipate after these types of events, or when they are comforted or distracted?

Are your child’s stomach pains taking place at night? Are they making it difficult for your child to eat? Are they losing weight? Is your child experiencing chronic diarrhea or do they have blood in their stool? Are they vomiting? These symptoms are more indicative of an underlying medical condition, especially if they become worse over time.

It has been revealed that keep a log. Track when your child has stomach pains to help you discern whether it’s due to anxiety or an underlying medical condition. Occasional stomachaches that happen during stressful times are common.

However, if your child complains of a stomachache several times each week for more than a month, consult with your pediatrician or seek a gastroenterologist.

And remember: You don’t have to choose between taking anxiety seriously and taking physical symptoms seriously. Both deserve attention. By watching for patterns, listening carefully, and seeking help when needed, you’re doing exactly what your child needs: letting them know that any pain they experience matters.
