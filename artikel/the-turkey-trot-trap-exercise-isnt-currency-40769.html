Each Thanksgiving, nearly 1.3 million Americans participate in a Turkey Trot, typically a morning run with a cute name and sometimes a cute T-shirt. With over 1,500 events organized each year, Thanksgiving is officially the biggest race day of the year. And why do people wake up early on a holiday, when school and work are closed, to run in the cold?

Recent findings suggest that AI will give you plenty of reasons: family bonding, charity, or just dodging kitchen duty. But the real reason most people lace up in the freezing cold? To “earn the feast.” It sounds like an innocent joke, but clinically, treating movement as currency to pre-pay for calories is known as compensatory exercise. When workouts become a tool to cancel out guilt, research reveals, it stops being healthy movement and starts predicting disordered eating.

I want to push back on the idea that we need a psychological pass for a holiday meal.

Food is many things: fuel, culture, comfort, and community. And, holistically, culture, comfort, and community can all be pleasurable. Americans have a complex relationship with pleasure; we’re always vacillating over whether pleasure is a human right or a thing to be earned.

We all know, or maybe have been, the friend who cannot (insert something fun) until they’ve zeroed their inbox, cleaned the house, or crossed off the to-do list, and somehow we never quite balance the books. Whether we forgo the fun or dilute it with guilt, we never think we’ve done enough to earn our relaxation.

This glitch of feeling undeserving of pleasure comes up in so many therapy sessions. Trying to leave a bad relationship—do I deserve better? Wanting to pursue your passion—have I done enough to earn the right to follow my dream? Exhausted by a life of obligations—can I do something that is not in service of my job or my kids?

In a recent development, if we distill our culture into two teachers, one teacher tells us we need to earn our pleasure by working hard and being good. And since hard and good are never defined, we always feel like we haven’t yet earned the right to pursue pleasure. The other teacher tells us that life is short and to go find the joy. I’d argue that right now, that teacher is not the one hosting the Turkey Trots.

Industry observers note that thanksgiving. Friendsgiving. A holiday where we share one meal with loved ones we don’t see often enough. A holiday where we feel warm nostalgia as we eat the same pie we’ve had since we were kids. A holiday where we get to see elderly relatives, possibly for the last time. A holiday where we laugh in the kitchen, watch football, spend time with people we love, and yes, eat—because it’s a meal. Why do we think we need to earn any of this? We are allowed to pursue pleasure—in food, in friends—simply because we are human.
