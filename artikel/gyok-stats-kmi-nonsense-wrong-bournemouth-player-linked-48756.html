Andrew M here after a couple of days ‘off’, but not the kind where you sit in a hammock and have delicious things brought to you. More the kind where you have three stab wounds in your abdomen and a chunk of your insides taken out. If at all possible, I seriously recommend the former to the latter. Anyway, I’m here right now with coffee and painkillers, which I guess is something akin to Arseblog heritage.

Sources indicate that the Interlull is over and that’s it now until March. It’s non-stop Arsenal all the way, and all the focus will be on us and what we can produce on the pitch. For those interested in what our players got up to during this break, and what they delivered for their respective nations, Andrew A has a handy round-up over on Arseblog News. Now it’s about keeping everyone as fit as possible, the manager using the squad in the smartest and most effective way he can, and for some of the players who are capable of producing more doing exactly that.

I was quite interested in Lewis’ quick look at Viktor Gyokeres and his key pass metrics from yesterday’s blog: He’s delivering a key pass every 28.4 minutes. Last season he had a key pass every 104 minutes across those two competitions [PL & CL].

It has been revealed that I think sample size might be a factor here, because when you look at his xG per 90, it’s 0.05 per 90 in both Premier League and Champions League this season, versus .99 and .76 per 90 last season, but obviously he played more minutes last time out. After 14 appearances this season, he’s rustled up a total of 614 minutes; this time last season his first 14 appearances brought him a cumulative 1200+ minutes. The absence of Kai Havertz was a factor, obviously, but it was probably that early season load that contributed to the injury he picked up against Burnley after a solid first half performance.

Industry observers note that the job for Mikel Arteta is to find a way to share those minutes effectively, to have both his strikers scoring and assisting, and to harness some of what Gyokeres has been able to produce for Sweden. It’s often mooted that as a team we need to play to his strengths more, but perhaps these key pass metrics show that the design is more about eliminating some of the weaknesses in his game to make him more effective in the final third for us. Let’s see how it goes.

Industry observers note that meanwhile, the KMI (Key Match Incident) panel have ruled on the Kai Havertz penalty incident against Hull. They ruled 5-0 that the on-field decision was wrong and that VAR, manned that day by Michael Oliver, should have intervened:.

In a recent development, although Havertz is clearly in an offside position, there is a clear and impactful holding action by Mendy before any potential offside offence by Havertz. Given the chronology of events, a penalty should have been awarded. The panel considered the VAR intervention was correct; however, the outcome after the on-field review was considered to be incorrect as a penalty should have been awarded.

In a recent development, I get every day is a learning day for ‘ProRef’, the people whose job it is to know the rules of football but week after week seem to demonstrate in fresh and inventive ways that they don’t. The question for us as fans is, what are we supposed to do with this? Are we supposed to feel better knowing that they’re owning up to mistakes? It feels a bit like getting run over when you have the green light at a pedestrian crossing, a nearby cop just lets the bloke go, but then later a judge says he should have been stopped and arrested.

Which is all well and good when you’re in a hospital bed in traction with a couple of broken legs and smashed pelvis (I say this only because I remember being in hospital with a bloke who fell under a bus and that’s what happened to him. He was fine though, his mates brought him in loads of joints which he used to share in the smoking room – this was 90s hospital). Which is to say, the damage has been done, the admission the bloke got it wrong in the first place doesn’t really make you feel any better.

In a recent development, from our perspective at least, Martin Odegaard’s winner ensured that decision didn’t turn out to be one which went on to live in infamy – like not drawing the lines for that Brentford goal that time, but it’s still essentially useless. Not least because you just know this weekend or next weekend some other mad thing will happen and they’ll say ‘Oooops! Sorry about that LOL here’s Michael Owen and Howard Webb!’, and some team will lose points because they can’t properly apply the rules of the game to things that happen on the pitch.

Finally for today, we’re being linked with a January move for Bournemouth midfielder Alex Scott. He’s certainly a fine player, but it doesn’t make a lot of sense to me given we already have the best midfield depth in the Premier League, and beyond. Where does he fit with Declan Rice, Bruno Guimaraes, Martin Zubimendi, Mikel Merino, Myles Lewis-Skelly and Martin Odegaard? Perhaps if, for example, Zubi went there’s a case to be made for a replacement, but as it is he’s struggling for minutes anyway, and you don’t pay that kind of money for someone to come in and be peripheral.

Like most of you, if there’s money to be spent in January, I’d much rather see it go on the forward line where I feel like there’s obvious room for improvement and to increase our depth. The left hand side is the obvious place, it’s hard not to worry about the right a little bit with Bukayo Saka’s injury, and the striker conversation is one which is never quite going to go away. So, on that basis, links to Scott don’t really add up for me. Unless Arteta fancies converting him to another left-back. Then it all clicks, but I think there’s one Bournemouth player we’d all rather see arrive.

Right, I’m gonna leave it there for this morning. In terms of a podcast, I’m not quite feeling up to it today, but what we’ll do is make our Patreon preview podcast free and available to everyone tomorrow afternoon. I should be less sore then, and I continue to be blown away by your many emails and messages. Thank you so much, they mean the world.
