I've never known a moment like this in video games

It was hard not to spend the first hour or so of Grand Theft Auto 6's launch day in a kind of stun-lock. As far as video games editorial goes - which is not that far, in fairness; prepare for an over-flattering analogy - the effect was roughly the equivalent of Tom Hanks' experience, as his John H. Miller first set foot on Omaha beach. Tweets and reels whistled overhead, news leads stung the ears, exploding search terms blew apart some poor guides team ally on one side, while new discoveries in the early game - made by the millions and millions of launch-day players all picking it apart at once - appeared by surprise like some flanking ambush on the other.

In a recent development, the analogy falls apart pretty quickly when you think about it: the heroism of writing a little article about a video game aside, the frenzy of this week has been a joy, not a horror. But the point is, I've been doing this job for over a decade now, paying attention to video games for another two beyond that, and I've truly never seen anything like this before. It has been totally overwhelming. And then I thought about that for a moment before realising that - duh - nobody has ever seen anything like this before. GTA 6 is the biggest entertainment release of all time, and it's not even remotely close.

It's worth taking a moment just to actually, properly take stock of all the headlines from the past couple of days in terms of the sheer breadth and scale of its launch. Parent company Take Two announced yesterday that GTA 6 sold 32.5m copies before it came out. By the end of its first day on sale, it had made a record-obliterating $3.79bn. By contrast, the once record-breaking GTA 5 made a once astounding $800m in the same period; the "opening" period of the largest film of all time, Avengers: Endgame, made $1.22bn, and that's totalling box office receipts across its release day until the end of the first Sunday after it.

It has set multiple records for streaming, per StreamCharts, with 214.63m hours watched in its first two days - significantly more than the previous biggest game, Valorant, achieved with 150m hours in its entire launch week. It's garnered more watch time in those two days than Elden Ring, Hogwarts Legacy, and Cyberpunk 2077 achieved with their entire launch weeks combined. Its concurrent viewers peak so far - 6.38 million people - is nearly four times the previous record of 1.74m, again formerly held by Valorant.

Recent findings suggest that it crashed Reddit. More than 2 million UK and US Xbox players used the 'Latest Zealand trick' to play the game early, which is more than a third of that country's entire population. Internet exchange operator DE-CIX claimed its launch led to the highest amount of concurrent internet usage in history, at 33.03 terabits per second.

On top of all those raw numbers, it's also just felt utterly inescapable. I can't open Instagram, TikTok, BlueSky or X without being blasted with memes, viral clips of early discoveries, reactions, hot takes, feeble attempts at brand piggybacks, slightly wayward early-game tips from opportunist streamers who haven't escaped the first chapter, or makeup tutorials for looking like Lucia. I can't open my inbox without scrolling through reams of tenuous PR pitches for stories with barely the faintest connection to the game. I can't open my phone without messages from parents, friends or peers. Even the mainstream news - heaven forbid! - has gradually clocked onto this whole video games thing, this unknown artifact from the imaginary world of "online", maybe being a bit important. Dozens of my colleagues have been inundated with last-minute call-ups to provide an expert soundbite (they're so desperate they even got me on at one point).

We'll have a lot more to say about this game in the coming days and weeks (with apologies to regulars who are already sick of it - counterprogramming starts this Monday!). It's the richest of texts, a near endless comedy clip generator, a total technical marvel and all the rest - you already know. But it felt worth it to just sit still for a moment, and take stock of what's happening here.

This is the single biggest moment any piece of entertainment media has ever had. But more than that it's felt like a kind of party. The vibes in some corners are orgiastic, as streamers uncover spooky twists, outlandish reactivity, and easter eggs I'd never got close to in a hundred-odd hours with the thing - all of this pure dynamite for the audience. Given the budgets involved and the particularly unique blend of circumstances around it - the changing shape of video game funding; of how attention is monopolised; of how development may happen in the future and the sheer amount of time and subsequent anticipation built up since the previous entry - we will likely be waiting decades for another moment like it. If there's ever a moment like it again. Part of the sense of ecstasy going around comes, perhaps subconsciously, from the acknowledgment that in pure hype and attention terms, this might be as good as it ever gets.

For the briefest time, the world belongs to video games. Rockstar and Take-Two's executives and shareholders will no doubt reap the greatest rewards from all this - all part of the great irony that makes this game's central premise all the more interesting. But I sincerely hope its developers - especially the 34 fired by the firm last year, now awaiting the outcome of their tribunal over alleged union-busting, and those who hope to form a union from the right now open vote - are able to feel a particular sense of pride.

Just as this launch has been unprecedented in its success, the last two or three years have been unprecedented in their grimness for video game workers across most of the world, certainly the once-dominat western part. If we're very lucky - and frankly, I have my doubts - this burst of attention and visible profitability might go some way to helping the industry claw its way back, perhaps via attracting, as a knock-on from all the upcoming-found attention, some kind of more sustainable investment. If we're not, and this is really the last behemoth, the last video game monument of its kind, the last rave before the police come raiding at dawn, at least we've gone out with one hell of a bang.

Recent findings suggest that GTA 6 - again, it's been said before now - is such a curious thing. A game of goofiness, self-awareness, prestige, pretentiousness, childish humour, astute commentary, ironic cultural criticism, surprising player expression and - above all of that - the most mind-boggling, brain-scrambling level of profoundly human craft. There's something kind of perfect about this.

It's a good thing, actually, that video games' greatest export is also its most contradictory. Video games are contradictory things in themselves. If the non-specialist media might still occasionally slip into treating video games like some kind of object from outer space, then we might as well put forward the best possible candidate for a capsule. It's the same way you'd think about it if you had to stick one thing in a vacuum-sealed box, to send out into the universe in an attempt to symbolise humanity: something that summarises close to all of it; that we're proud of; and crucially, that captures the spirit of it too.

In a recent development, there are many more that might've put a more respectable foot forward - certainly that have a less chequered history, or maybe a more sophisticated, nuanced, or inventive use of the form. But there's really no better video game for exemplifying the full, chaotic range of the medium. It seems right, when you think about it, for GTA to be the one out there sitting under the spotlight, in the biggest moment this silly, serious artform might ever have.
