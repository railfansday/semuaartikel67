According to recent reports, the S&P 500 index (^GSPC +0.33%) is one of the most popular stock market indexes in the world. Roughly $16 trillion is invested globally in index funds and ETFs tracking the S&P 500. The Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO +0.27%) alone has $1.7 trillion in assets.

Charlie Munger, the longtime business partner of Warren Buffett, once claimed that "95% of people have no chance of beating the S&P 500 Index." But I'm not sure that advice is true in today's market. And there's one glaring reason why.

In a recent development, most investors believe that the S&P 500 is a diversified basket of stocks that captures the valuation of the entire U.S. economy spanning a broad range of industries and sectors. That's true, but only up to a point.

Recent findings suggest that during the dot-com bubble, technology stocks accounted for roughly one-third of the index. When the bubble burst, S&P 500 investors learned they weren't as diversified as they had believed.

The same proved true during the financial crisis, when financial stocks comprised roughly one-quarter of the entire index. When that bubble burst, the S&P 500's supposed diversification benefits proved less reliable than initially thought.

In a recent development, today, nearly half of the S&P 500's value is tied up in either AI stocks or companies that supply the AI industry. The math isn't perfect, as many AI stocks engage in many other business activities besides AI. But the lofty valuations of these companies are very much tied to their AI efforts. If the AI story stumbles, nearly half of the S&P 500 could see their valuations compress sharply.

"Artificial intelligence concentration is increasing, both across the economy and in investor portfolios," research from Franklin Templeton warns. "[I]nvestors are now considering how to best mitigate concentration risk without sacrificing the gains from AI, which include economywide productivity growth and potentially superior investment returns.".

Industry observers note that in almost all cases, sticking with an index fund tracking the S&P 500 is prudent, even during market peaks. If you had purchased shares of an S&P 500 index fund at the peak of every bubble in history, you'd still be sitting on a healthy long-term profit. That's the power of long-term thinking.

According to recent reports, the matter today is that the S&P 500 no longer functions like it did in the past. In past bubbles, peak industry concentrations ranged from 25% to 35%. Today's AI concentration hovers around 45%. The decline of a single industry, therefore, could drag down your entire portfolio.

Recent findings suggest that many long-term, risk-tolerant investors may do well sticking with their traditional index fund exposure. Risk-averse investors such as retirees and those on a fixed income, however, should reassess just how "diversified" their portfolios are. The S&P 500 remains a great index to track long term. But it's no longer the no-brainer investment it used to be.
