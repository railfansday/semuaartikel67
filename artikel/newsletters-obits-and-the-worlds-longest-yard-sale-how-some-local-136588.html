Big gainers included W42ST, the Point/Plover Metro Wire, Grice Connect (“In the rural south, death is a large social driver”), and the Philomath News. Here’s our quarterly ranking of the top 25 local for-profit news sites in the United States.

It’s time for our latest look at which newsrooms are having success increasing their web traffic. We bring you four of these each quarter, looking at four different market segments: local newspapers, nonprofit news outlets, public media stations, and for-profit local news sites. Rankings are based on estimates from Similarweb.

Today’s edition looks at local for-profit news sites. (Eligible sites for this ranking are for-profit members of the trade group LION Publishers — LION standing for Local, Independent, Online News — so these are mostly digital-born newsrooms, not newspapers or TV stations.) The rankings for Q3 feature many of the same sites that performed well in our last edition. This time around, we’ve got details from local news sites in Latest York, Kentucky, Oklahoma, Nevada, Georgia, and Oregon. Below that are the Top 25 rankings for July, August, and September.

W42ST In New York’s Hell’s Kitchen, W42ST has been covering neighborhood news since 2014. Its traffic jumped 84% from June to July. Publisher Phil O’Brien noted that boost is attributable to one day’s traffic to a single story: this story on the deaths, four days apart, of Jeffrey Newman and Jayson Conner, a couple who ran Backpacks for the Street, a charity that offered supplies to those living on the city’s streets. W42ST published its story on July 11; a few hours later, The New York Times ran its own obituary, and thousands of people who read that piece went to Google to find out more. Visits on July 12 were roughly 10 times a normal Sunday on W42ST. “A spike like this is lovely, but it doesn’t pay the bills,” O’Brien told me over email. He noted that the average reader of that story who came from search was engaged for 26 seconds; readers who came to it from W42ST’s own newsletter averaged more than three and a half minutes. “A reader from across the country who spends 26 seconds on an obituary is welcome, but they’re not who we’re building for.” O’Brien was writing to me from England, where he’s currently trying to bring W42ST’s hyperlocal model to the town of Southport (O’Brien was born in Liverpool). Stand Up For Southport is a six-year-old site that received a government Local News Fund grant to expand operations, and O’Brien is spending a few months there to offer his W42ST-bred expertise. The new daily newsletter they’ve launched earned more than 1,000 subs in the first week. It’s a nice reversal from just four years ago, when O’Brien had his American visa renewal application rejected because immigration officials declared W42ST was a “marginal” business. He spent the next five months running the site from London. “The idea is a hyperlocal model that can travel,” he said. Point/Plover Metro Wire Wisconsin’s Point/Plover Metro Wire describes itself as the only locally owned and operated daily news source in Portage County, and the county is the only place it covers. “We do this mainly because nobody else here does, even if it does limit our audience,” said publisher Brandi Makuski. The site had one of the largest leaps in July traffic of any in our rankings, up 114% from the month before. Makuski said high-performing stories in July included a proposed ban on homelessness, a stabbing homicide related to an eviction process, and an increase in stormwater rates for residents. “The extensive follow-up coverage on these and other stories has really helped expand our readership, and kind of reconnect in readers’ minds the importance of community journalism,” she said. Makuski said that about 63% of the site’s traffic comes from Facebook (“as much as I hate to say this…social media is the bane of my existence”). She describes much of her work as “public education” to convince businesses and institutions that a Facebook-first approach for getting the word out comes with risk. “We’re making headway and gaining new customers each time, but it’s grueling piecemeal work,” she said. “We just do the work, and we’re pretty old-school,” she said. “The scanner is on 24/7; we meet with municipal and industry leaders in person regularly; we constantly cultivate relationships and new sources; and, most importantly, we don’t sit on our asses. We spend about 75 percent of our workday, every day, out in the community, stumbling on most of our news in the field.” Grice Connect Statesboro, Georgia, is a town of about 33,000, perhaps best known to Allman Brothers fans. The husband-and-wife team of DeWayne and Lori Grice have run Grice Connect since 2019. Their traffic was up about 77% from June to July. DeWayne Grice told me that Facebook and Google were both up notably. July was also the first full month the site had a full-time sports reporter, a Report for America-funded position. But Grice also credited its obituaries: “In the rural south, death is a large social driver.” During the COVID pandemic, he noticed that some deaths were going unrecorded in the local newspaper’s obit section; the paper charged $150 and up to run one, and they were behind a paywall. Grice Connect launched free obituaries, sponsored by a local hospice and in collaboration with five local funeral homes. Obits drove more than 44,000 views in July and were a “primary pillar” of the site. Philomath News The Philomath News was founded in 2020 by publisher Brad Fuqua, two months after he’d been laid off by the Corvallis Gazette-Times when it closed its weekly paper in suburban Philomath. Similarweb stats showed a 52% increase in the site’s traffic in September, though Fuqua tells me it was October when traffic really jumped. Interestingly, the site’s top referrer in September was NewsBreak, not Google. “Google came back in October, but September showed me how much it helps to have a second big source of traffic when search dips,” he said. In a town as small as Philomath (pop. 5,500), Fuqua has had to figure out ways to drive some traffic from elsewhere in Oregon. He does so with his weekly writeups of the Oregon State Police logs, which he called “consistently my best-performing content” beyond the homepage and which attract readers from Portland and beyond. But it’s the local stories that are the reason the site exists. “People here have stuck with us since we launched in 2020, and that loyalty is what makes a one-person local news operation possible,” he said. Oklahoma City Free Press, LINK nky, This Is Reno In Oklahoma’s capital, traffic to the Free Press in July was up 68% from June. Editor-in-chief Brett Fieldcamp attributed that to an unusually high number of homicides that month (“we report, track, and number every homicide that is logged in OKC throughout the year”). But some of the site’s biggest individual traffic drivers were actually republished press releases from local governments and organizations. “If there’s any insight to glean or to communicate to your readers,” he told me, “it’s that curating and running press releases from trusted departments and local partners can be a far bigger driver of traffic at times than most people might realize, and also a great way to stay engaged with the community you serve.” The Northern Kentucky site LINK nky saw its traffic jump 81% in July, which CEO Lacy Starling attributed to two stories. A routine unbylined piece on the World’s Longest Yard Sale — which “spans approximately 690 miles across six states” — pulled in 18,000 unique visitors, 10% of the site’s total traffic that month. Another piece on a fitness instructor who survived a “widow-maker” heart attack “must have been shared into a group or a site somewhere,” because it drove another 10,000-plus pageviews. Nevada’s This Is Reno had a strong August, up 71% from July. Publisher Bob Conrad attributed that to a frequent cause of local traffic spikes, a natural disaster — in their case, a series of wildfires, culminating in the devastating Hawk Fire.
