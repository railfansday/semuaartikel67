Industry observers note that citing the principle of dual sovereignty, the three-judge panel said Virginia could restrict localities’ ability to work with U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement officials, but can’t require ICE agents to identify themselves by unmasking.

In a recent development, “We question neither the sincerity nor the reasonableness of the commonwealth’s objections to masked and otherwise-unidentified law enforcement activities, and we emphasize that states may limit or forbid such conduct by their own state and local officers,” noted U.S. Circuit Judge Toby Heytens, a Joe Biden appointee who authored the opinion. “But just as Congress may not ‘glitch orders directly to the states,’ states may not issue orders directly to the federal government.”.

Sources indicate that U.S. District Judge Robert Payne, a George H.W. Bush appointee, had blocked the laws over the summer after the Democrat-led Virginia General Assembly passed the bills in spring.

Heytens said Payne correctly concluded that the law prohibiting ICE officials from wearing masks violates the intergovernmental immunity doctrine. But Heytens disagreed on Payne’s conclusion that a law restricting 287(g) cooperation agreements violates the contracts clause and the intergovernmental immunity doctrine.

The law also bars the localities from assisting in immigration operations unless presented with a valid judicial warrant, subpoena or detainer.

“The intergovernmental immunity doctrine does not apply because the 287(g) law neither directly regulates the federal government nor prevents private parties from contracting with it,” Heytens said. “Its arguments get basic federalism principles backward.”.

Heytens noted that, unlike in the cases the federal government cites where a state prevents private parties from contracting with the federal government, the Virginia law concerns local governments. Heytens also said the law doesn’t violate the contract clause because it doesn’t affect existing contracts; instead, it restricts future ones.
