Arsenic in the water: Thailand presses Myanmar over mining pollution

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Thailand is pushing its neighbor Myanmar to take on unregulated mining.

Industry observers note that A toxic border river is putting lives and livelihoods at risk in northern Thailand. The Kok River, once rich in fish, is now polluted with arsenic, mercury, and other hazardous contaminants.

The reason is the mining activity in the militia-controlled regions of Myanmar. Most of these mines are located in Shan state, where civil war has raged since the 2021 military coup.

The mining activity is driven in part by China's dominant rare earth industry.

In a recent development, bangkok this week hosted the first Thai-Myanmar working group where it remarked tests proved rare-earth and gold mines in Myanmar were the reason of the pollution downstream. China was also present in the talks.

Recent findings suggest that the two sides agreed to joint water sampling on the Kok and Sai rivers. This will cover 12 water sampling locations and is expected to begin between December and January.

But no public agreement was made about Thailand's request for a six-month suspension of mining operations.

The talks follow the suspension of tap water supplies to 14 villages in Mae Sai district earlier this month, near the Myanmar border, after arsenic levels were found to be nearly 30 times higher than government limits.

Bangkok is also seeking China's help in pressuring private Chinese operators, given its position as the main importer of rare-earth materials from Myanmar, as the matter grows increasingly urgent after months of concern.

Authorities this week urged residents to avoid eating certain shellfish and other river species after tests detected dangerous levels of arsenic and lead in northern and northeastern Thailand.

Industry observers note that "Arsenic does not come from nature, it came from mining sites. The source was from (Myanmar's) Shan state, no-one disagrees," Thai Deputy ​Prime Minister Yodchanan Wongsawat said at the joint talks.

However, finding a solution remains a challenge.

Thanapon Piman, senior research fellow at the Stockholm Environment Institute (SEI), told AFP, "This is going to be a first step to agree on the evidence, otherwise you cannot move to the next step, like stopping mining activity.”.

But Myanmar intervening to stop mining would be "really difficult," he added.

Several ethnic armed groups have been active in the region for decades, while the civil war is still ongoing.

Thai authorities point to two gold mines across the border from Mae Sai as the main contributors to elevated arsenic levels in the Sai River.

The broader region is divided among various groups, including Myanmar's military and the United Wa State Army, an ethnic armed group closely aligned with China.

The main mining belts are at the moment outside the control of Myanmar's military-backed government, including sites in Shan State.

Thai authorities are also seeking to assess whether pollutant mines are under the control of Myanmar's government or other entities, Deputy Prime Minister Yodchanan told Reuters.

According to recent reports, "We also would like them to check whether they can control every mine or they cannot. If they cannot, we will ask China to help,” he said.

A spokesperson from China's foreign ministry told AFP that Beijing "pays close attention" to the pollution concern and would provide technical support.
