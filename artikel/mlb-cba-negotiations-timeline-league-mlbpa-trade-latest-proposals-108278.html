As the 2026 Major League Baseball offseason continues to unfold, something vitally notable to the future of the sport quietly hums in the background: the ongoing negotiations over a new collective bargaining agreement.

It has been revealed that the collective bargaining agreement, or CBA (or Basic Agreement, as it's known in a formal sense), is the negotiated accord between players and clubs -- i.e., the team owners and commissioner Rob Manfred, who represents their interests -- that governs the working relationship between the two parties, typically for a period of five years. It covers things as mundane as players' meal allowances and travel protocols and as vital as minimum salaries, the structure of free agency, revenue-sharing specifics, and roster sizes. Every CBA can be distilled down to a fight over money, or, more specifically, how the league's revenues will be divided between players and team owners.

According to recent reports, the first CBA covered the 1968 and 1969 seasons and was the first in professional sports history. That was the handiwork of Marvin Miller, the pioneering head of the Players Association (i.e., the players union) and belated Hall of Famer. It was Miller's organizing skills, foresight, and training as a union economist that allowed him to make the Players Association a viable and effective labor organization. Every CBA negotiation from 1972 through 1994-95 resulted in some form and scale of labor stoppage, whether a players' strike or an owner-implemented lockout. After the cancellation of the 1994 World Series because of the worst labor stoppage in the sport's history, a long run of peace followed. That peace lasted until the prior CBA negotiations, which resulted in a 99-day lockout forced by team owners and delayed the start of the 2022 season.

The current CBA negotiations have been underway for some time, and not surprisingly, the two sides remain far apart on several key issues. The biggest fight will be over the owners' renewed desire to implement a salary cap, which the union continues to view as a non-starter. The push for a cap has less to do with the notions of competitive balance and more to do with the ownership's internal belief that being the only non-salary-capped league among the major North American sports is harming franchise values. The cap, though, is but one of many pressing issues on which players and owners must find congruity.

Moving forward, the process will be uncertain and rife with posturing and pointed public comments from both sides. The hope, though, is that a newly introduced CBA will be agreed to before the middle of March 2027, at which point there's a high risk of having the 2027 season delayed or even abbreviated.

It has been revealed that to keep you informed of the twists and turns along the way to a new CBA, we've provided a detailed timeline of negotiations. That timeline will illuminate the vital and finer points of the talks and, along the way, explain the implications of the various proposals coming from each side. The hope, of course, is that all of it culminates in a timely agreement that permits a fully intact and on-time 2027 season.

In earlier iterations of negotiations, MLB proposed a salary-escrow system like the ones found in the NBA and NHL. This would mean a percentage of player salaries is withheld in case league revenue projections fall short for a given year. If that's the case, then that money in escrow goes back to the league to cover the revenue shortfalls. If revenues are higher than projected, that excess is shared with the players. The MLBPA has pointedly opposed the escrow system on the grounds that it undermines the notion of guaranteed player salaries. In its latest statement on the negotiations, MLB withdrew the escrow proposal. "For months, union leadership has attacked the escrow provision used in other sports and mischaracterized the concern as the end of guaranteed contracts," MLB spokesperson Glen Caplin mentioned in a statement. "MLB took another step today by withdrawing our escrow proposal, removing any doubt that every single guaranteed player contract will remain fully guaranteed in the newly introduced salary cap and floor system.".

It has been revealed that to date, the league has tied every individual proposal to the players' acceptance of a cap-and-floor system. The players through their union have historically shown no willingness to discuss a cap on payrolls.

MLB Players Association executive director Bruce Meyer stated MLB is not serious about preventing the lockout and has "wasted months of time" trying to court public opinion when the two sides could have been having substantive negotiations.

"At the end of the day, this is going to come down to the bargaining table," Meyer told the Boston Globe. "And we have been for months, since May, looking for a serious partner in negotiations, and the league has insisted since May that they won't negotiate over anything economic outside of our agreement to a salary cap. So I'd say the more the league is focused on things other than bargaining with us in good faith, I would say that's counterproductive.".

Meyer also mentioned the MLBPA has put forth proposals intended to promote competitive balance and correct the game's revenue disparity, but the owners are focused on a salary cap to "increase their profits and franchise values." He added that the two sides have made progress on non-economic matters, such as drug testing and gambling policies.

To allow for a "smoother transition" to a salary cap/floor system, MLB proposed delaying its implementation until 2028. That would give teams time to prepare and get their payrolls in order, and allow for a full offseason heading into the system. The current competitive balance tax and revenue sharing system would remain in place in 2027.

In a recent development, "We recognize that clubs will need a full offseason to prepare to operate within the cap and floor system. Therefore, with this offseason already underway, we proposed today to begin the cap and floor system in 2028," MLB spokesman Glen Caplin said in a statement. "This proposal would allow a smoother transition into the cap and floor system.".

In a recent development, MLB also upped its salary cap/floor proposal to $258.3 million and $180.2 million, respectively. Back in June, the league proposed a $245.3 million cap and $171.2 million floor. It states it is willing to discuss further phase-in scenarios for a salary cap/floor system.

In its latest proposal, MLB proposed reducing the regular season from 162 games to 154 games, and adding a standard off day to every week. The league also proposed extending the Division Series from a best-of-five to a best-of-seven, and allowing higher seeded teams to select their opponents in the Wild Card Series and Division Series. As with every MLB proposal to date, the 154-game schedule and changes to the postseason format are contingent on the MLBPA accepting a salary car/floor system.

If labor strife lops off 60 or more games from the 2027 MLB season, then the consequences could be significant. The Athletic recently reported that if the forthcoming lockout extends into late May or so and costs at least 60 games, then most of the league's national broadcast and streaming contracts would be extended for another year, through 2029. That's significant because commissioner Rob Manfred hopes to bundle and centralize team streaming rights and share those revenues equally, and the current timeline would theoretically allow that to happen at the same time the national contracts are up for renegotiation. Tacking on another year to those national contracts would push back the significant windfall that owners and the league are expecting from that latest media rights structure. As such, this is a heavy incentive, at least on the league side, to not let any labor stoppage drag into June.
