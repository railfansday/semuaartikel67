Intelligent Speed Limiter Tech giant President: We Don’t Want Our Devices in Every Vehicle in America

Industry observers note that michael Travars, president of active roadway safety technology company LifeSafer, has worked for decades to save lives on the road. From ignition interlocks to intelligent speed limiters, Travars has spent his career piloting technology that reduces injuries and deaths in cars. But even this highly safety-conscious executive doesn’t believe in a statewide or federal speed limiter mandate.

Industry observers note that intelligent speed assistance devices have become a hot topic of discussion among municipal governments and car enthusiasts alike in recent years. The European Union mandated that all new cars feature passive ISA systems, which acoustically alert drivers when they exceed the speed limit, in 2024. In the United States, states like New York and cities like Washington, D.C. are piloting programs that require so-called “super speeders” (who have been found guilty of extreme speeding) to install active ISA systems, which prevent lead-footed drivers from exceeding the limits altogether. LifeSafer is working closely with these municipalities to hone the technology—but Travars’s vision of expansion is somewhat limited.

“We don't want this in every car. We actually don't want the European mandate of ISA in every single vehicle. There's a freedom of the road culture here. I'm a big believer in that,” Travars noted to Road & Track. “We think a lot about the ignition interlock programs. The ignition interlock program reinforces that you don't drive drunk. It doesn't let you drive drunk. The theory behind those programs is that it creates a habit. You can't drive with alcohol. At the end of the program, the ignition interlock device comes out, and they go back to normal driving programs.”.

Using the ISA device is fairly simple. A small screen is mounted on the dashboard, wired through the vehicle’s OBDII port, and operates with a GPS database of roadway speed limits. The device prevents the vehicle from exceeding the posted limit, with a programmable margin of a few miles per hour over mentioned limit. Travars said the devices cost around $6 per day to operate. The device can be overridden, and it includes an RPM-limiting feature that softens typical acceleration.

It has been revealed that “There's a tuning process. Actually, if you've done race car driving, it's a lot like tuning a race car, where you connect configuration tools. You actually put the car in neutral. You push the gas pedal all the way down. It measures the rate of acceleration,” Travars explained. “It is a calibration or a tuning process to do the install. And it's more than just OEM or model-specific. It's actually car-specific. A worn-down accelerator pedal has different movement than a modern accelerator, so it is absolutely a car-by-car process.”.

LifeSafer ISA right now has three primary customers: teens, fleets, and super speeders. Instead of merely monitoring teenage drivers through apps like Life360, some parents are taking action to limit the ability of their newly minted drivers to speed. Fleet operators enjoy similar benefits, including a theoretical increase in efficiency on speed-limited vehicles. But the future of the technology, arguably, is in providing compliance devices for governments.

Virginia has little tolerance for speeding, and the commonwealth was the first state to join forces with LifeSafer earlier this year. As of July 2026, certain reckless drivers are required to install an intelligent speed assistance device under the administration of the Commission on Virginia Alcohol Safety Action Program. And Virginia isn’t the only state in conversation with LifeSafer, either; Travars remarked seven states—Hawaii, Illinois, Arizona, Maryland, Upcoming York, Virginia, and Georgia—have implemented super speeders laws, and around 17 states in total have reached out to the safety technology company.

According to recent reports, but Travars mentioned he believes the state and city level should be the ceiling for these devices. While the safety technology industry would benefit from federal regulatory goalposts, Travars explained, the implementation of speed limiters is better suited to state-by-state needs.

According to recent reports, “I would love to see federal minimum guidelines as to, what the device must do, how many miles it must be accurate for, a certification process; those kinds of things belong at the federal level. At the state level, what kind of program do you want? How long do you want it for? Who goes in it? Let Maryland do Maryland,” Travars said.

Sources indicate that when asked why enthusiasts shouldn’t worry about ISA programs, Travars explained that driving culture in the United States differs greatly from Europe or other markets where widespread ISA programs have been implemented. These devices aren’t meant to limit drivers who have proven themselves to be responsible. Instead, LifeSafer wants these devices to be a temporary stop on the road to safer driving habits.

“Active ISA devices are targeted programs specifically for drivers who need them. These are inexperienced drivers or drivers who have shown that they have a speeding habit that needs to be broken. Period. We don't want ISA in all vehicles. We don't want this to be a mandate in all cars. The goal is to take people who are unsafe drivers, make them safer, and then take them out of the program and restore their right to drive,” Travars said. “These aren't traps. These aren't the [devices] where they report to insurance companies to increase your rates. The data is not sold to third parties ... I think these programs should bar the right to sell that data. These are not trackers. These are not Significant Brother. These are meant to serve a specific purpose, which is to reinforce safe driving behavior long enough that it becomes a habit. Full stop.”.
