“A Southern Storybook Brought to Life.” Inside South Carolina’s Luxurious Lowcountry 20,000-Acre Montage Palmetto Bluff.

I’m traveling down a very narrow two-way road in a tinted Chevrolet Suburban when the chauffeur stops abruptly. He points out two alligators sunbathing on the rocks in the May River on our right. That is my first sighting of an alligator or any reptile in over 15 years—except for a strangely cute banana boa constrictor in my high school science class. I’m no fan of them.

That gator pair, and a few more, dot the river, alongside the long road leading up to Montage Palmetto Bluff, a five-star resort in South Carolina’s Lowcountry.

The sandy dirt road, shadowed by live oak trees draped in Spanish moss—a signature of the Lowcountry landscape—eventually opens onto the resort’s central property: a sprawling white mansion called the Main Inn.

According to recent reports, after the driver helps unload my bags from the truck, resort staff greet me and lead me inside across the wide porch, a hallmark of any traditional Southern residence. The dark wooden floors creak under my feet, the same kind of creaking that reminds me of walking into the comfort of my grandma’s house.

Montage Palmetto Bluff is a 20,000-acre property tucked between Hilton Head Island and Savannah, Georgia. It has over 200 guest rooms, suites, cottages, and other residences, called Village Homes, Moreland Village Homes, and Signature Residences. (I stayed in one of the signature residences.).

The property also offers ample outdoor and wellness activities, including a shooting club, golf course, a 13,000-square-foot spa, a lawn and racquet club, and over 20 miles of walking and biking trails.

For folks who want to throw a lavish, intimate event or wedding, Palmetto Bluff features two beautiful chapels: the May River Chapel and the Somerset Chapel. The latter is where Justin Bieber and Hailey Baldwin held their wedding ceremony in 2019. Through the church’s arched windows, you’ll get magnificent views of the sunset.

The resort is approximately a 30-minute boat ride from Daufuskie Island, a remote, bridge-free barrier island between Hilton Head Island and Savannah, Georgia. It has white-sand beaches, one paved road on the less-developed side of the island, and a rich Gullah Geechee culture. The Gullah Geechee people are descendants of enslaved West Africans who were forced to work on plantations along the southeastern coast of the United States.

Sources indicate that despite the resort’s beauty, it sits atop a dark history. In the Antebellum Era, Palmetto Bluff was home to several plantations where enslaved people grew lucrative crops, including indigo, cotton, rice, corn, beans, and sweet potatoes.

In a recent development, “We believe it is key first and foremost to acknowledge the suffering endured by the enslaved individuals who lived and labored on this land,” Meghan Lamb, senior manager of marketing and public relations at Montage Palmetto Bluff, tells me. “These stories deserve to be shared openly and thoughtfully, both to honor those who came before us and to ensure their experiences, contributions, and enduring legacy are never forgotten,” she adds.

Lamb, who walked my fellow housemates and me through much of the property and, while sharing the Bluff’s rich history, also had an excellent eye for spotting gators.

As a Black person whose ancestors were enslaved on southern plantations, hearing about the history here was a painful reminder, but a lack of acknowledgment would have been even more so.

During the Civil War, Union troops captured the land, and many white families and enslaved people fled the area. Wealthy bankers and businesspeople later purchased the property, and the land changed hands several times. A banker named Richard T. Wilson bought the property in 1901, expanded it, had a large mansion built on the grounds, and gave the estate its current name: “Palmetto Bluff.” The mansion burned to the ground in a 1926 fire; the ruins remain preserved on the grounds today.

Palmetto Bluff is now owned by a private equity firm that specializes in resort management, Henderson Park and South Street Partners.

I stayed in one of the signature residences, houses available for rent for a period of time, with three others for four days and three nights.

The Signature Five Bedroom Lagoon Residence 1003 is a mansion with high ceilings and luxurious bathrooms, plus a fireplace, a porch, a separate cottage, and a large communal dining table nestled in the center of the home.

I chose the bedroom in the back left corner of the house; it had warm, yellow lighting, deep mahogany-colored walls, a king-sized bed, a lush en-suite bathroom, and some of the softest sheets I’ve laid my head on. Despite a packed schedule, I rested easy each night.

You can view room rates and book your stay at a signature residence here.

According to recent reports, on my first night, we were welcomed with a Southern-style buffet in our signature residence. The menu was astounding: a traditional Lowcountry boil with shrimp, corn, potatoes, and sausage, plus fluffy biscuits, mac and cheese, collard greens, and peach cobbler for dessert.

Recent findings suggest that there are also several places to dine across the property. Guests can indulge in fine Southern cuisine at the Octagon or at the elegant River House, which is where I ordered my first Wagyu steak. Brunch is served at Buffalos, and premium cocktails are available at Hush, a speakeasy. Along the lagoon waterway that runs through the resort is Fore & Aft, which serves delicious Mexican fare. Flame is a 1950s-vintage pizza truck you can easily walk up to and grab a slice in the evenings, Wednesday through Sunday. Buzz is a cozy cafe (I got a toasty ham-and-cheese croissant one morning that didn’t disappoint), and Melt is the Bluff’s ice cream parlor. The property even has its own gas station with a small grocery store.

Industry observers note that for a fun treat, you can make your own s’mores at the massive fire pit behind the Main Inn every night at 9 P.M. I did, and it brought me back to when I used to make them as a kid by the campfire in my parents’ backyard.

According to recent reports, as I mentioned—my schedule was packed, but I managed to fit in a few outdoor activities. The 20-plus miles of trails on Palmetto Bluff offer scenic walking and e-biking tours.

I had never been on an e-bike before, so I was pumped for this chance to do so on some beautiful trails. I liked that I could switch between regular biking and an electric boost to reach nearly 20 miles per hour. The combination of speed, wind in my hair, and stunning trail views was exhilarating, so much so that I even made the very risky decision to FaceTime my girlfriend with one hand to show her how much fun I was having.

Andrew Baldino, the public relations and marketing coordinator at Montage Palmetto Bluff, led the e-biking tour and pointed out spots most people tend to miss. One of those, he said, is River Road. The appeal? Sweeping live oaks, long Spanish moss, and extensive lush greenery, he tells me.

The Golden Hour E-Bike Excursion lasts for one hour and 15 minutes and costs $90 per person. You can book your slot here.

Recent findings suggest that on the second day of my stay, I took a paint-and-sip class at my signature residence with renowned artist Amiri Farris, known for incorporating Gullah Geechee imagery into his work. We painted a blue crab because they’re often found in South Carolina estuaries, tidal creeks, salt marshes, and rivers.

On the third day, I embarked on the Daufuskie Island History Excursion tour. Because the tour lasts about four hours, you’ll need to book it early in the morning. My tour started at 7:30 A.M. It was one of the highlights of my trip. I saw dolphins along the way and learned about the island’s rich history on a golf-cart tour of the island’s untouched parts after hopping off the boat. Our captain, who steered the boat and led the golf-cart tour, made the experience even more worthwhile as he’s developed deep relationships with people on the island and shared stories about the island’s characters, bringing a humorous, jovial vibe to the trip.

Recent findings suggest that private tours for up to six people cost $895, tours for up to 12 people cost $1,195, and tours for up to 16 people cost $1,295. You can book them here.

I left my trip feeling nourished, calm, and more knowledgeable about the Lowcountry than before I arrived.

Lamb put it beautifully when I asked what about the Bluff makes visitors want to return—and even buy property on the resort: “It’s a Southern storybook brought to life.”.
