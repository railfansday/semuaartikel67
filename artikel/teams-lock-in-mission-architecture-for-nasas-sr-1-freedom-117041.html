📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

NASA completed a major review for Space Reactor-1 (SR-1) Freedom and locked in key elements of the spacecraft, which is targeted to launch in 2028 and demonstrate nuclear electric propulsion in deep space for the first time.

Experts from across the agency, the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE), and industry met at NASA’s Glenn Research Center in Cleveland for a major SR-1 mission design review in October. Several key decisions were made, propelling the mission from an early concept toward a defined spacecraft design.

Recent findings suggest that “SR-1 marks a pivotal step in how we travel through the solar system,” remarked Steve Sinacore, SR-1 Freedom program director. “This mission will be the first freight run on the railroad of the solar system, and the decisions we’re making now are laying the tracks for every nuclear-powered spacecraft that follows.”.

Industry observers note that planned for launch in late 2028, SR-1 will use both nuclear and solar power to deliver SkyFall, a capsule carrying three next-generation helicopters, to Mars. Once deployed, the helicopters will study Martian geology and climate history, explore new terrain, and search for signs of subsurface ice.

In a recent development, as NASA prepares for more ambitious exploration missions, future spacecraft and surface operations will require significantly more power than current systems can provide, particularly in environments where sunlight is limited or unavailable. SR-1 will demonstrate how fission power can enable long-duration missions, pave the way for reliable surface power systems to support NASA’s future Moon Base, and enable exploration deeper into the solar system.

The SR-1 Freedom spacecraft design has evolved since NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman first introduced the concept in March at the agency’s Ignition event, where leadership shared aims to develop a fission-powered spacecraft in time for the next Mars launch window.

One significant change is to use a shorter boom than the one shown in early illustrations. The boom separates the reactor from the spacecraft’s main body, known as the Power and Propulsion Element. The shorter boom will reduce weight, simplify assembly, and help streamline the spacecraft’s design.

It has been revealed that NASA and DOE experts also determined that improvements in shielding and spacecraft design will allow the reactor to operate closer to the rest of the spacecraft. This will simplify integration while maintaining the radiation protection needed for spacecraft electronics and the SkyFall payload.

According to recent reports, teams are continuing to evaluate other key design features, including the number of radiator panels on SR-1, output power levels of its nuclear power module, and how long its reactor will operate. These decisions will help shape the final spacecraft design and ensure it meets mission objectives.

Industry observers note that in addition, the team is modifying the spacecraft’s Power and Propulsion Element by updating its communications system for the journey to Mars. SR-1 Freedom will use larger X-band antenna dishes to send data across deep space back to Earth. Further maturation of the spacecraft looks will focus on reducing risk, simplifying the design, and staying on schedule.

Industry observers note that NASA awarded a $39.3 million contract to Johns Hopkins University’s Applied Physics Laboratory to develop the heat rejection system for the spacecraft’s nuclear power module. The system will remove excess heat generated by the spacecraft’s nuclear power system, allowing it to operate safely and efficiently. The lab is working with the tech giant Advanced Cooling Technologies as a key strategic commercial partner for providing the critical heat pipe and composite radiator technologies that will enable the system to meet the demanding mission requirements.

Awarded under the Aerospace Research, Development, and Engineering Support II contract, the contract action ensures the Applied Physics Laboratory team will design, build, test, and evaluate the system, along with producing the hardware needed for flight. The heat rejection system is a key part of demonstrating nuclear electric propulsion in space and will help lay the groundwork and industrial production foundation for future high-power nuclear systems used in deep space exploration.

Along with the spacecraft design, teams have defined the mission timeline and planned operations.

Recent findings suggest that after launch and once the spacecraft has left Earth orbit, SR-1 Freedom will activate and gradually increase reactor power. The reactor system will operate for an initial period of time before shutting down. During this period, the spacecraft will collect and send performance data back to Earth. Solar arrays will then provide power as it cruises to Mars.

After delivering SkyFall to Martian orbit, SR-1 Freedom will restart its fission reactor, collecting additional data on the spacecraft’s performance in deep space.

“By refining the design and advancing this architecture, we’re not just building a spacecraft, we’re establishing the foundation for a fresh era of reliable, efficient transport beyond Earth orbit,” Sinacore mentioned. “What we learn from SR-1 Freedom will shape the future of exploration for decades to come.”.

According to recent reports, with key design decisions advancing and teams across NASA, DOE, and industry working together, SR-1 Freedom is moving toward its 2028 launch goal. The mission will demonstrate new capabilities for nuclear-powered transportation and help establish the foundation for future exploration beyond Earth.
