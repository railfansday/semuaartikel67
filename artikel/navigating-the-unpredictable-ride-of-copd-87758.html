Recent findings suggest that HOOVES THUNDER AGAINST packed dirt as the bull bucks to the left and the rider leans right, tightening his grip on the rope. Just as he finds his balance, another sudden buck or turn can change the course of the ride.

The unexpected twists and turns of a bull ride may feel familiar to people living with inadequately controlled chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Not knowing when breathing challenges or a flare-up—a period when breathing becomes more difficult than usual that can last for several days—might occur can make everyday life feel unpredictable.

It has been revealed that mark H., 60, has experienced the challenges that can come with COPD since he was diagnosed in 2010. “COPD isn’t easy to navigate, and it can be a frightening condition to face,” he states. “I tried to ignore my diagnosis early on because I didn’t want to believe it, and I didn’t want to think about how it might affect my life. But ignoring it didn’t stop my COPD from making itself known. Shortness of breath was really impacting my day to day.”.

With support and clinical intervention, Mark was able to better manage his COPD and get back to doing more of the things he loves. Now, he wants others living with COPD to know help is available to manage their condition.

COPD IS A complex and progressive disease that can make breathing more difficult over time.

COPD can be caused by inflammation in the airways that causes damage to parts of your lungs. Patients with COPD may also have chronic bronchitis and/or emphysema.

Industry observers note that people with COPD can experience persistent cough, excessive mucus production, wheezing, chest tightness, and shortness of breath. Understanding COPD is important, and recognizing when the disease may not be adequately controlled is critical. Living with uncertainty around flare-ups can carry an emotional weight as well, notes Corinne Young, nurse practitioner, pulmonary disease specialist, who is the Founder/Executive Director of the Association of Pulmonary Advanced Practice Providers.

It has been revealed that flare-ups, also known as exacerbations, are the worsening of COPD symptoms over several days. They can happen to anyone with COPD. During a flare-up, difficulty breathing, wheezing, and coughing can intensify and take longer to resolve. Even one flare-up can lead to permanent lung damage.

Young, who in addition to seeing patients, competes in team roping at rodeos, knows the importance of paying attention to changing conditions—a perspective she brings to helping patients recognize when their COPD may be inadequately controlled.

Recent findings suggest that “The biggest predictor of a flare-up is a previous flare-up, and they start to get closer and closer together,” Young explains. For example, someone may experience a flare-up or exacerbation once every two years, then once a year, then every six months, then every three. “Inflammatory responses in the body drive these exacerbations, and that may lower lung function,” Young adds. That decline can increase the risk of additional flare-ups, perpetuating the cycle.

Industry observers note that AS MARK’S COPD became more difficult to manage, it increasingly interfered with the life he was used to living. Shortness of breath and frequent flare-ups made it harder to work, travel, and do as many of the things he enjoyed. Eventually, he made the difficult decision to retire early from his job as a field service engineer.

Even after stepping away from work, Mark continued to struggle with his COPD. “I remember feeling like, ‘Well, I guess this is my life now,’” he notes.

IN THE FALL of 2024, Mark underwent a pulmonary function test, bracing for bad news. The results confirmed that his current treatment plan was no longer adequately managing his COPD, prompting a discussion with his pulmonologist about what to do next. His doctor explained that Mark’s high eosinophil count could be a sign of type 2 inflammation. With this information, Mark and his doctor explored other treatment options, including Dupixent® (dupilumab).

Dupixent is a prescription medicine used with other medicines for the maintenance treatment of adults with inadequately controlled COPD and a high number of blood eosinophils (a type of white blood cell that may contribute to your COPD). Dupixent is used to reduce the number of flare-ups and can improve your breathing.

Dupixent is not used to relieve sudden breathing problems and will not replace an inhaled rescue medicine.

Do not use if you are allergic to dupilumab or to any of the ingredients in Dupixent.

Dupixent can cause serious side effects, such as allergic reactions, including skin reactions, that can sometimes be severe, eye problems, and joint aches and pain.

Please see additional Crucial Safety Information below.

Recent findings suggest that mark noticed a difference after adding Dupixent. “I could breathe better,” he states. Now, Mark feels less winded, and he’s doing more of the things he loves. “I’m really grateful that I can enjoy my hobbies more without constantly thinking about my COPD,” he adds.

In a recent development, for Mark, better breathing started with an honest conversation. Young encourages anyone living with COPD to be open with their healthcare provider, who can explain the full range of treatment options available. “It’s not just inhalers anymore—there are a lot of options,” she notes.

Like bull riders who stay alert to changing conditions, people living with COPD can watch for signs that their disease may be inadequately controlled and talk with their doctor about their treatment options. With the right support, they may be able to enjoy more of the moments that matter to them—including, for some, time spent in the rodeo stands.

Before using DUPIXENT, tell your healthcare provider about all your medical conditions, including if you:.

Tell your healthcare provider about all the medicines you take, including prescription and over-the-counter medicines, vitamins, and herbal supplements.

Especially tell your healthcare provider if you are taking oral, topical, or inhaled corticosteroid medicines or have COPD and also have asthma. Do not change or stop your other medicines, including corticosteroid medicine or other asthma medicine, without talking to your healthcare provider. This may cause other symptoms that were controlled by those medicines to come back.

DUPIXENT can cause serious side effects, including:.

The most common side effects in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease include injection site reactions, common cold symptoms (nasopharyngitis), high count of a certain white blood cell (eosinophilia), viral infection, back pain, inflammation inside the nose (rhinitis), diarrhea, stomach problems (gastritis), joint pain (arthralgia), toothache, headache, and urinary tract infection.

Tell your healthcare provider if you have any side effect that bothers you or that does not go away. These are not all the possible side effects of DUPIXENT.
