Industry News: There's a growing 'property tax revolt' as homeowners face rising costs. How it could impact your bill

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Affordable housing was a key midterm election issue, and as Americans wrestle with rising home costs, there's a growing movement to reduce or limit property taxes.

Amid soaring home values, some states want to cap how much local governments can collect from homeowners. Experts have described the trend as a "property tax revolt.".

During the Nov. 3 midterm election, Florida voters approved Amendment 3, which increases the portion of primary residences exempt from property tax for non-school levies and lowers the cap on yearly assessments for certain properties.

North Carolina voters approved an amendment to require the state legislature to enact limits on property tax increases, while voters in Oklahoma and Wyoming rejected other property tax measures.

In Ohio, an amendment to abolish property taxes didn't have enough signatures to make the November ballot, but supporters plan to try again in 2027.

Despite the "mixed bag" on midterm ballot initiative results, "I think we'll continue to see property tax reforms," said Matthew Chase, CEO of the National Association of Counties, or NACo, a nonprofit organization that represents county governments.

Property taxes are levied as a percentage of a home's assessed value, based on its estimated market rate.

Recent findings suggest that some municipalities use a millage rate — with one mill equaling $1 of tax per $1,000 of taxable assessed value — to calculate property tax bills. That rate can adjust up or down based on changes in home values and the local government's budget needs.

In some cases, property tax bills can be bigger due to higher assessments, even when local governments lower the millage rate, experts say.

Property taxes vary widely throughout the U.S., with yearly bills ranging from less than $250 in some rural counties to more than $10,000 in areas near large cities, according to 2026 data from the Tax Foundation, a nonpartisan, nonprofit research organization.

Nationwide, the property tax cut movement has been "driven by affordability," according to NACo's Chase. As "home values have gone up, property taxes have gone up with it," he noted.

In 2020, the median sales price for U.S. single-family homes was about $300,000, compared with roughly $419,000 in 2025, according to the National Association of Realtors.

According to recent reports, last year, the median property tax bill for owner-occupied homes was around $3,300, the U.S. Census Bureau's American Community Survey reported.

Higher property tax bills have been a burden, experts say, as homeowners have wrestled with rising costs for groceries, gasoline and other living expenses. That's sparked more demand for property tax relief.

From January 2020 to June 2026, state lawmakers enacted at least 168 property tax measures — including exemptions, credits, refunds and more — across 40 states and the District of Columbia, according to an October report from NACo.

Property tax cut proponents argue local governments should rein in spending. But other experts say the relief will cause revenue shortfalls that impact local schools, police and fire services, roads and other infrastructure.

"There's no realistic way to replace property taxes in most states," noted Jared Walczak, a senior fellow at the Tax Foundation.

Typically, property taxes are a more stable revenue source compared to income or sales taxes, which can decline during an economic downturn as consumers earn and spend less, experts say.

Reliance on the tax varies, but property taxes are the single largest local levy revenue source for most local governments, accounting for about 28% of local funding nationally in 2024, according to the Lincoln Institute of Land Policy, an independent, nonpartisan foundation and think tank.

"It's easy to savor property tax cuts in abstract," Walczak said. "It can often be much more challenging to define a replacement system that anyone actually likes.".

In Flagler County, located in Northeast Florida, Sheriff Rick Staly mentioned in an email that it's "too early to know" how Florida's recently passed Amendment 3 will impact his department's local law enforcement.

It has been revealed that "I pray we do not return to a pre-2017 level ... of an underfunded, understaffed and being a reactive only agency," he wrote.

An October Tax Foundation analysis estimated Florida's Amendment 3 could cost local governments $4.9 billion in the first year, $8.7 billion in the second year and $11.8 billion annually by tax year 2031.

Many local governments also face rising costs for employees, public services and infrastructure repairs, Chase with NACo noted.

For example, some New York counties are paying more for rock salt this winter, which is essential to clear roads during snowstorms, he noted.

"The challenge is ... the counties can't stop providing the services, and so the money's going to come from somewhere," Chase stated.
