Another 3-2 win in Spain brings the lure of a golden latest hyper-future – but it did not pan out that way last time.

I may have the body of a weak and feeble German, but I have the heart and stomach of a manager of England. And I think it foul scorn that Spain should dare to invade the borders of my realm. Or, for that matter, any one of 24 qualified teams for Euro 2028, because at the end of the day there are no easy games at this level, I’ve always mentioned that.

The words, there, of Thomas Tuchel, England’s Faerie King, speaking to TalkSport shortly after the glorious 3-2 defeat of the Spanish possession armada at the Riyadh Air Metropolitano Stadium in Madrid.

Recent findings suggest that OK, Tuchel didn’t technically say any of this. He was instead wry and spiky. He made a joke about people moaning about his team on the radio, which just goes to show he’s a good lad and one of us (disclaimer: when he wins). But this is England. And beating Spain can do some strange things to you.

Not just because Spain are the defining team of the past 20 years, six-time international trophy winners and current world and European champions. But because Spanish football culture is the global template for tone, texture and tactics, the ultimate source of the Football Association’s slightly dog‑eared footballing blueprint.

Beating Spain is like beating your super-smart but slightly controlling younger brother. It’s getting one over on that other way of playing, punitive high technique, death by possession, patience over urgency. Even better, Tuchel’s team did so in Madrid with a performance based around the hard‑running physicality that English football sees as part of its deep sporting soul.

As such it will be tempting to see this Nations League victory as a springboard, a Rubicon crossed, a shattering of the Iberian ceiling. With some justification; England have the talent to win a home European Championship two years from now. But it feels just a little bit easier to visualise now.

In a recent development, there was something upcoming in seeing Spain not just outmuscled but outplayed in the first half, reduced to whinging about fouls, unable to take the ball away. Not only because England’s pressing was smart and relentless but because of the players’ ability to keep possession in tight spaces.

It is also true that a quick glance back at recent history tells us exactly how much of a golden dawn a win such as this can be. Which is: zero, zilch, nothing. No golden dawn at all.

In reality there are no Rubicon moments, no decisive levellings-up, only staging posts and warning signs, even in victory. This is the most crucial point about a night such as this. It won’t tell you where you’ll end up. But it may tell you how to keep heading the right way, if you’re ready to learn from recent history.

It has been revealed that as it happens, recent history is in the room with us right now, clearing its throat, rattling its chains, trying to shift the Ouija-board dial. Here is an interesting fact. Spain have only twice conceded three goals at home in a competitive men’s international. The second time was on Sunday night, when England won 3-2 in Madrid after going 3-0 up. And the first time was 15 October 2018 when England beat Spain 3-2 in Seville after going 3-0 up.

The parallels between those games are numerically improbable. At the time of win No 1 Gareth Southgate had been in permanent charge for 684 days and one narrow World Cup semi-final defeat. Spain was his 29th game. At the time of win No 2 Tuchel had been in charge for 683 days and another narrow World Cup semi-final defeat. Spain was Tuchel’s 28th game.

That Southgate moment was greeted with understandable optimism. You know the kind of language used because it’s out here now, too. New dawns. Statement victories. Rise of a Golden Generation. Not just a benchmark but a springboard as well, an astonishing feat of carpentry if true.

So was it that? Answer: no. In fact Seville 2018 was arguably the moment Southgate-ism reached a stylistic ceiling. England played a fast-paced game of counterattack that night. The team was an attacking mix, a midfield of Winks‑Barkley‑Dier, with Sterling‑Kane‑Rashford in front. England scored 27 goals in six games the following autumn, starting most with a back four and a double pivot, ending 2019 with a 6-0, a 7-0 and a 4-0. This felt like something new.

The Covid break pretty much killed that mini-era. Or rather, it was allowed to kill it, because coaches have choices too. England went 42 weeks without a game. By November 2020 they were losing 2-0 in Belgium with a back five and a deep defensive midfield.

Sources indicate that the Euros had already been postponed for a year. In the summer of 2021 England started the final against Italy with another back five plus Declan Rice and Kalvin Phillips, and congealed into a kind of footballing rice pudding as the game reached its crisis point.

Industry observers note that here is an unprovable counterfactual. If football had rolled on, if England had not had an empty year to look down and get scared, they might have retained enough momentum to go harder against Italy in that final and win it.

But then, the Southgate era was gruelling for many of those players. There is a separate study to be made of the way so many key early Garethistas disappeared or entered a slow decline. That October 2018 team turned out to be a pop‑up thing of shifting parts, evidence of the complex, zigzagging nature of team building.

Industry observers note that the would-be golden generation included Ben Chilwell, Eric Dier, Joe Gomez, Ross Barkley, Harry Winks and Marcus Rashford. Nathaniel Chalobah made his only appearance as a sub in added time, and still has the second shortest England career, after Martin Kelly.

Recent findings suggest that rather than a golden dawn of ultimate glory, England entered a more talent-appropriate era of losing late-stage games against good opponents. If anything the Spain victory added to the ambient pressure around those tournaments, helping to create the sense it is now unthinkable for England to lose, because we have an image of them as winners, and English winning is the best winning, the only real winning.

In a recent development, the lesson remains the same now, the need to avoid both portents of doom and the lure of a golden new hyper-future. In this sense Tuchel is encouragingly restless both in defeat and victory. Even his new-season touchline look – tailored, sombre, all black – has an agreeably priestly, hair‑shirt energy; less Elizabeth I, author of the introductory quote above, more Martin Luther on his way to a business meeting in an out‑of‑town spa hotel.

Tuchel’s best quality is his obsession with analysis and objectivity. On Sunday night he was already talking about the need to improve. This can be an obstacle at times (see: process-based Argentina substitutes). But it is also a rejection of complacency, ornament, frippery. This England are better than their 2018 equivalent. That team had seven players from Leicester and Arsenal. This one has key parts from Real Madrid, Barcelona and Bayern Munich, including the current Ballon d’Or winner and a £300m midfield tripod.

According to recent reports, the balance of the attack in Madrid was good, not just the most seductively talented players but the best parts for the initiative. Anthony Gordon is an interesting figure in this respect, an intelligent, culturally adaptable, tactically aware footballer, whose game is nonetheless basically running up and down. Elite teams seem to have a place for this role now. Flank-scurry energy node. Right-sided gallop unit. Lewis Hall was also very good again. Any team looks better with a genuine left‑footed full-back, like hanging a picture straight on the wall.

Mainly what Tuchel did in Spain was to select a team with no indulgences, full commitment to a tailored gameplan, no celebrity passengers. And that style is not fixed. See also: Trent Alexander-Arnold against Croatia, and some excellent passes against a deep block even on a middling night. Stick that behind your ear as well, Thomas.

It has been revealed that teams are hugely complex things. Tuchel had to get to know his players. The idea you can simply bolt on an elite manager and expect instant progress was always a bit silly. No dips or lulls, no adaptation. This might work if you come from the same cultural system. But plonking on a brain from someone else’s body is still a way of looking for shortcuts, the managerial equivalent of sticking it in the mixer and hoping for the best. There you go. Give us a final fix on 75 years of inherited domestic culture.

Tuchel is now a little further along with all this. He even did the backs-to-the-wall thing better, waiting to the last moment to press the defensive emergency button, the sight of Dan Burn rumbling out a signal to both teams that this really is the end of the night, like turning on all the lights and getting the DJ to play Don’t Look Back in Anger.

Sunday night was a wonderful game. It was England’s most complete performance under Tuchel. But the lesson hasn’t changed. Trust the process, not the results. Keep doing the ruthless, adaptive things that got you there. England will at some point have to beat the best. And the Nations League is a very useful tool. Win it next summer and taking that final step starts to look a whole lot less of an Arthurian fever dream.
