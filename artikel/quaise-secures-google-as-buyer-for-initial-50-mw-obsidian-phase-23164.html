Quaise Secures Google as Buyer for Initial 50 MW Obsidian Phase

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that quaise Energy announced on November 17, 2026 that Google had agreed to buy 50 megawatts of superhot geothermal electricity from Project Obsidian in Central Oregon. Deliveries are targeted to begin as early as 2030, supplying Google’s existing data centers in The Dalles and communities in the region.

Recent findings suggest that the electricity is intended to be always-on and carbon-free once the plant is operating. The contract gives Quaise a buyer for its initial commercial-scale development and gives Google a prospective source of firm clean generation for an established data-center base.

According to recent reports, obsidian’s first development phase will use conventional drilling to reach superhot conditions at commercial scale. Quaise expects the second phase to be the first in the world to use millimeter-wave drilling, a technique designed to access those conditions beyond the limited locations suitable for conventional geothermal.

The Project Obsidian outline describes a non-contact process that uses high-power microwaves to break down rock. The equipment is designed to connect to existing drilling rigs and make access to superhot heat economical.

Industry observers note that obsidian is designed around underground temperatures of 300–500°C. Quaise estimates that superhot geothermal can produce up to 10 times more clean power per well than traditional geothermal, which relies on naturally occurring hot-water reservoirs that rarely exceed 400°F.

Industry observers note that the project roadmap lists 50 MW in Phase I, 250 MW in Phase II and more than 1 GW in Phase III. Google’s agreement covers that first phase; the later capacities are part of Quaise’s expansion blueprint, not additional purchases under this contract.

The planned start date applies to purchases from the first phase, while the introduction of millimeter-wave drilling and the larger buildout remain development objectives. Technology proven at Obsidian is expected by Quaise to lower development costs for later U.S. superhot geothermal projects.

Google opened its first owned-and-operated data center in The Dalles in 2006 and now operates three data-center campuses there, along with an Oregon Cloud region and a Portland office. The announcement puts Google’s investment in Oregon at more than $7 billion over the past 20 years.

It has been revealed that the Quaise agreement extends a much larger procurement effort. The release counts more than 240 agreements signed from 2010 through 2025 to buy nearly 35 GW of new clean energy. Within North America, it lists 148 projects representing more than 29 GW.

Google indicates it pays for all electricity used by its data centers and covers infrastructure needs directly driven by its growth.

Recent findings suggest that quaise projects 200 to 400 construction jobs and more than 50 permanent engineering and operations jobs at Obsidian. Associated grid upgrades are expected to strengthen local reliability, while royalties from electricity sales are intended to provide lasting economic benefits to the region.

The larger significance for data-center power now rests on execution: the initial development must establish commercial superhot production, and the millimeter-wave approach must prove capable of supporting the much larger expansion Quaise has mapped out.
