Tech Report: The U.S. Let Volvo Keep Selling Cars. Polestar Got A No. What About Mercedes?

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that the Volvo EX-90 and Polestar 3 roll off the same assembly line in South Carolina. They share much of their hardware and, according to Polestar, run the same software. However, when each manufacturer asked Washington for permission to keep selling them in America, they got different answers.

It has been revealed that now Congress is considering a tougher law aimed at keeping Chinese cars out of the U.S., one that could reintroduce headaches for Volvo and put another major automaker in a tough position. Congress is close to a deal on legislation that would ban cars from automakers with more than 15% Chinese ownership stake, as Bloomberg reported. This would be a tough pill for Mercedes-Benz, as around 20% of the organization's stock is owned by Chinese investors.

But the publication reported that Republican and Democratic lawmakers are nearing a deal that could spare Mercedes. They want to send a bill to the president by the end of the year, but its terms have not yet been finalized.

Volvo EX90 assembly at the Volvo Ridgeville factory in South Carolina.

It has been revealed that the Volvo and Polestar decisions came under a Commerce Department rule finalized in 2025 aimed at controlling connectivity and self-driving technology. Washington’s concern is that a foreign adversary could use those systems to collect data on Americans or even remotely control vehicles. The latest restrictions on certain Chinese- or Russian-linked software take effect for the 2027 model year. Hardware restrictions will follow in 2030 and apply to both gas and electric cars, including ones assembled on U.S. soil.

Chinese giant Geely has a majority stake in Volvo Cars, so the Swedish automaker had to apply for specific authorization to keep importing and selling connected cars. The Department of Commerce granted it. Volvo told InsideEVs it still has to meet the same software requirements as every other automaker. The Swedish foreign trade minister confirmed his government worked closely with Volvo to achieve compliance.

Polestar is another Sweden-based automaker that is controlled by Geely. It applied and was denied authorization to keep selling cars from model year 2027 onward. So why was Polestar axed despite Volvo being saved? Polestar told its dealers it had asked the Department of Commerce that very question and had yet to get an explanation, pointing to the common software and the shared South Carolina production line.

It has been revealed that even though Polestar is a Volvo spinoff and also has a Swedish connection, the country’s trade minister stated Polestar didn’t want the same assistance as Volvo. That’s one difference we know between their applications, but the U.S. government hasn’t explicitly said why things shook out this way, and Polestar did not appeal the decision.

Mercedes-Benz EQS SUV production in Alabama.

Industry observers note that the proposed Connected Vehicle Security Act would write existing restrictions into federal law and extend the U.S. sales ban to automakers with more than 15% Chinese ownership, even if they build their cars in America.

According to recent reports, china’s state-owned BAIC Group owns 9.98% of Mercedes-Benz. Geely founder Li Shufu holds another 9.69% through an investment firm. Taken together, that’s just shy of 20%, and over the new proposed threshold.

For reference, Geely owns 79% of Volvo and Geely founder Li Shufu reported beneficial ownership of 60.5% of Polestar’s shares in a September 2026 SEC filing.

That doesn't mean Mercedes will be locked out of the market, though, even if the bill passes as currently written.

Industry observers note that “The U.S. is an extremely notable market for us, and we're not going to do anything to jeopardize our success in the past and our success in the future,” Jason Hoff, CEO of Mercedes-Benz North America, remarked during a media roundtable with InsideEVs last week.

It has been revealed that when asked whether the company had a plan for its Chinese shareholders to divest, Hoff declined to comment on specific conversations Mercedes has with shareholders. He was more direct about the legislation itself.

“You know, we always have and always will support anything that protects the national security of the United States. So we've never opposed this bill, but you know, in the end, we'll also take whatever steps we need to comply with it if it does get passed," Hoff mentioned.

Mercedes says its Chinese shareholders have no seats on its management board and no say in day-to-day decisions. They could still have voting rights as shareholders. That matters because, according to Bloomberg, lawmakers are considering an exception that would let companies exclude passive investments with no voting rights from the proposed 15% ownership calculation.

It’s still unclear how Mercedes would prove its Chinese stakes qualify. In the meantime, House members have asked Mercedes to provide documents, answer two dozen questions by December 1, and arrange a meeting with its U.S. boss.
