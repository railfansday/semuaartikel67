How did Coinbase stop deepfake Elon Musk’s promise to double your crypto?

🌐 Source: Ambcrypto • 📅 18 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 2 min read (349 words, 📖 Standard)
💡 Key Takeaways
  • Scammers recently used a fake video of Elon Musk to get people to send them crypto.
  • Especially since according to Coinbase, it blocked a big chunk of those transfers before the money could disappear.
  • … and Coinbase stopped $4.7 million from reaching scammers.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Scammers recently used a fake video of Elon Musk to get people to send them crypto. It didn’t go to initiative.

Especially since according to Coinbase, it blocked a big chunk of those transfers before the money could disappear.

… and Coinbase stopped $4.7 million from reaching scammers.

It has been revealed that someone put out a YouTube livestream made to look like a Tesla flying car demo. On screen was an AI-made Elon Musk, and the pitch was to send crypto and get 2x back.

More than half a million people tuned in. The stream flashed wallet addresses and QR codes. So, sending money took just a scan.

Industry observers note that in a blog post, Coinbase laid out what happened. In about two hours, hundreds of its customers tried to send funds to the scammers. Its automated fraud system caught about 91% of that.

The real Musk had nothing to do with any of it.

Here, it’s worth pointing out that Coinbase hasn’t mentioned how much of the remaining 9% actually got through.

Its fraud agent watches outgoing transfers as they happen.

If any transfer looks off or iffy, like a payment to a freshly created wallet or something tied to a “giveaway,” the agent in question steps in. In such a case, the agent opens a chat with the user, explains why the transfer might be risky, and prompts them to think twice before proceeding.

Recent findings suggest that it even checks its own replies before showing them. According to Coinbase, no person was involved at any point. Nobody’s account got hacked either. No passwords leaked. People (some of them long-time owners) chose to hit send because the fake Musk was convincing.

Our systems can see risk signals in real time. They can’t see intent.

That is exactly what makes these scams tough.

Industry observers note that it isn’t the first fake Elon Musk either. A doctored video in 2023 reportedly cost victims around $160K. In June 2024, deepfake giveaways popped up on more than 35 YouTube channels during a Starship launch.
