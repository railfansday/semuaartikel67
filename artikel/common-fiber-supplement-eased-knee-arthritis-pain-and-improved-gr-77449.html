Common fiber supplement eased knee arthritis pain and improved grip strength in six weeks

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A six-week trial found that inulin fiber supplements modestly reduced knee osteoarthritis pain and improved grip strength in some adults.

In a recent development, adults with knee osteoarthritis who took inulin, a fiber found in chicory root, ended a six-week trial with less knee pain than volunteers on a placebo. On a scale from zero to ten, their scores fell 1.11 points more.

Volunteers who took inulin alone also gained grip strength, a moderate effect. Those who followed a digital exercise program, or combined both treatments, did not.

The work was led by the University of Nottingham in England, and its results appeared in February 2026.

Sources indicate that in 2019, about 528 million people worldwide were living with osteoarthritis, according to the World Health Organization. The knee is the joint most often affected, with 365 million cases.

Sources indicate that about 33 million U.S. adults have the condition, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimates. Physical exercise may help relieve pain and improve function, the agency notes.

In the U.K., 5.4 million people have knee osteoarthritis, according to a 2025 report from the charity Arthritis UK.

“Our recent lived experience survey showed that six in ten people are living in pain most or all of the time due to their arthritis,” noted Professor Lucy Donaldson, the charity’s director of research.

Under its former name, Versus Arthritis, the charity was one of the trial’s funders.

Between May 2022 and February 2025, the researchers randomly assigned 136 adults to four groups, and 117 completed all assessments.

Those who finished averaged 67.5 years of age, and 58.1 percent were women.

Sources indicate that one group took 0.7 ounces (20 grams) of inulin powder a day. A second followed Joint Academy, a digital physical therapy program that began with a call to a personal physical therapist.

A third group combined both, and a fourth took maltodextrin, a starch-based powder, as a placebo. For ethical reasons linked to blood sugar, the placebo dose was half the inulin dose.

Sources indicate that volunteers did not know whether they were taking inulin or the placebo, but those assigned to exercise knew it. The staff who measured outcomes were blinded to all treatments.

Inulin’s pain result only just reached statistical significance, with a 95 percent confidence interval spanning 0.04 to 2.18 points.

In a recent development, exercise lowered pain scores by 1.55 points compared with placebo, and the combination by 1.67 points. No added benefit from combining the two emerged, though the sample was too small to reliably detect one.

Sources indicate that on average, both single treatments exceeded the one-point drop that patients typically notice as meaningful. Exercise came close to the two-point threshold for feeling “much better.”.

A third of placebo takers also improved by at least one point, but only 11 percent improved by two points or more.

Industry observers note that inulin made people less sensitive to pressure and to repeated painful stimuli, two measures linked to how the nervous system processes pain. Exercise alone had no such effect.

“This study raises the exciting possibility that a simple dietary change – adding a fibre supplement to your breakfast or yogurt – could meaningfully reduce pain and improve physical function,” stated Dr. Afroditi Kouraki of the University of Nottingham’s School of Medicine, the lead author.

According to recent reports, exercise improved a timed up-and-go test and a 30-second chair-stand test. Inulin alone had no effect on either.

Recent findings suggest that inulin feeds gut bacteria that produce short-chain fatty acids. Only inulin raised blood levels of butyrate, one of those fatty acids, and of GLP-1, a hormone made in the gut.

It has been revealed that within the inulin groups, higher GLP-1 was associated with better grip strength, in an exploratory analysis that needs independent replication. The authors offer no explanation for the combined group’s grip result.

“The link we observed between GLP-1 and grip strength is particularly intriguing and points to a broader gut-muscle-pain axis that warrants further investigation,” said Professor Ana Valdes, the senior author, also of the School of Medicine.

It has been revealed that A 2024 Canadian trial of a similar fiber in adults with knee osteoarthritis and obesity reported gains in grip strength and mobility after six months. Its effect on knee pain was only a trend.

According to recent reports, the short duration may not capture the full effects of exercise, the researchers acknowledged. Expectation effects in the exercise groups cannot be ruled out either.

Dropout reached 21 percent in the two exercise groups, compared with 3.6 percent in the two groups without exercise. To compensate, allocation odds were shifted toward exercise during later recruitment, which may have introduced bias.

A second analysis that included dropouts, with missing scores statistically imputed, gave consistent results. Only the effect of exercise on the up-and-go test lost statistical significance.

In a recent development, of 50 inulin takers who finished, 23 reported minor digestive effects such as wind and bloating. None stopped the supplement or dropped out because of them.

U.K. government guidelines cited by the National Health Service call for 1.1 ounces (30 grams) of fiber a day, so the inulin dose alone supplied two-thirds of that.

Valdes sits on the scientific advisory boards of the nutrition company Chuckling Goat and of OLIPOP, roles declared unrelated to this study.

The study was published in the journal Nutrients.
