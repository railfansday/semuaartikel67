Wizards of the Coast's War Against MTG Proxies Isn't as Bad as It Sounds

Wizards of the Coast, a Hasbro subsidiary and the publisher of popular tabletop games Magic: The Gathering and Dungeons & Dragons, has filed a massive lawsuit against a provider of proxy Magic cards, PrintMTG Enterprise, seeking more than $28.6 million in damages.

It has been revealed that proxies are replicas of Magic cards printed to resemble real items. Although they are not allowed in official tournaments, proxies are still somewhat popular in casual play, especially within the Commander format. Notably, Premodern, a format that includes cards from 1995 to 2003 and is steadily growing in popularity, allows proxy cards in some tournaments.

However, while some Magic players are already concerned about a possible visit from the Pinkertons, things aren't as bad as they seem. Wizards is not suing PrintMTG Enterprise for producing proxy Magic cards, but for marketing their products as proxies "to lend them a veneer of legitimacy while actually plying a business built around the sale of counterfeit cards," the lawsuit reads. The distinction is important: "An honest proxy is a stand-in for a real card that makes no claims to authenticity, by appearance or otherwise." On the other hand, "A counterfeit is made to be mistaken for the real card and to take its place as something owned, traded, or sold.".

Magic has expanded swiftly in the last few years to become a massive business built on the value of its collectibles. The game is also Hasbro's main revenue driver​​​​​​​; in the third quarter of 2026, Magic surpassed $600 million in quarterly revenue for the first time. In a global trading card industry estimated at around $15 billion, Magic ranks second, behind​​​​​​​ the Pokémon Trading Card Game. While Wizards does not sell single Magic cards, the secondary market still drives the firm's product sales. Counterfeits thus hurt the Magic business as a whole, the lawsuit claims. It's not surprising, then, that Wizards isn't going to look the other way when it comes to a counterfeiting network.

However, the lawsuit also makes it clear that the company doesn't have an matter with proxies: "Wizards recognizes that private and noncommercial proxying is a vehicle players use to further engage with the game, not to undermine it." Proxies are important, especially for formats like Vintage or Premodern, where older, expensive cards are very popular. It's understandable that players may not want to sleeve up and shuffle their Black Lotus for a Vintage match at the local game store.
