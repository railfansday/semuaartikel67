According to recent reports, beauty terminology has evolved quite rapidly in recent years—particularly around aging. What was long labeled antiaging has shifted toward more realistic definitions of what happens to skin and the body as we get older. But the latest buzzword, longevity, as ubiquitous as it’s become, doesn’t exactly fill the gap for those seeking ways to keep skin, hair, and the body looking youthful. Hailey Bieber’s recent use of the term “smart aging” just may be the word substitute the category has been missing.

Natalia Guzman, FNP-C, CANS, a board-certified nurse practitioner at Atelier Aesthetix in Beverly Hills, explains that smart aging is a proactive, science-based approach to maintaining healthy, youthful-looking skin while embracing the natural aging process. “Rather than trying to ‘stop’ aging altogether, which is biologically impossible, smart aging focuses on understanding how our skin changes over time and making informed decisions to preserve a healthy, strong, overall youthful appearance,” Guzman tells ELLE.

It has been revealed that from a dermatologic perspective, Dr. Brianna Olamiju, a board-certified dermatologist with Spring Street Dermatology in NYC, offers this explanation: “Smart aging is about being proactive, making informed skin care choices, and supporting the skin’s natural functions over time. It involves protecting the skin from environmental damage, maintaining a healthy skin barrier, stimulating collagen production when appropriate, and addressing concerns such as fine lines, wrinkles, uneven pigmentation, and loss of elasticity,” states Olamiju. Ultimately, she says, smart aging is about helping your skin look and feel its best at every stage of life while embracing the natural aging process.

It has been revealed that guzman emphasizes that aging beautifully isn’t about looking young. “It’s about looking and feeling like the best version of yourself at every stage of life.”.

In a recent development, as skin evolves, collagen production gradually declines, cellular turnover slows, and environmental factors such as UV exposure, pollution, and lifestyle habits contribute to visible signs of aging. Guzman adds that smart aging means addressing these changes strategically through preventative skin care, healthy lifestyle choices, and, when appropriate, evidence-based aesthetic treatments. “I like to think of smart aging as investing in your skin’s future and being proactive rather than waiting until significant changes occur and attempting to reverse them,” Guzman indicates. “A lot of the time, the latter takes much more work, and sometimes it cannot be completely corrected.”.

According to Guzman, the biggest difference between smart aging and traditional antiaging is the philosophy behind it. “For decades, the beauty industry has promoted the idea that aging is something we need to fight, prevent, or erase,” claims Guzman. The term “antiaging” often focuses on correcting visible signs of aging, such as wrinkles, volume loss, pigmentation, and skin laxity.

Both Guzman and Olamiju agree that smart aging takes a more holistic, comprehensive, and realistic approach. Olamiju notes that anti-aging creates the expectation that we should try to look younger indefinitely. “Smart aging shifts the focus toward skin health, prevention, and maintaining long-term skin quality. Rather than viewing wrinkles, fine lines, and changes in skin textureas something that must be eliminated, smart aging encourages us to support the skin as it naturally evolves. It is less about fighting aging and more about aging well,” she explains. This approach, she adds, also emphasizes consistency, prevention, and individualized treatments rather than chasing every new product or cosmetic trend.

According to recent reports, guzman adds that smart aging, instead of fighting age, means working with the skin’s biology to help it function and look its best for as long as possible. “The main difference is that we’re not simply chasing wrinkles. We're creating a long-term strategy that prioritizes skin quality, natural-looking results, and overall skin health,” Guzman emphasizes. “To me, anti-aging is often about correcting what we see today, while smart aging is about preventing and protecting what we’ll see tomorrow.”.

It has been revealed that olamiju and Guzman are completely aligned on the first step in a smart aging routine: wearing sunscreen daily. “The foundation of smart aging starts with a consistent skincare routine and healthy lifestyle habits. Daily broad-spectrum sunscreen with an SPF of at least 30 is arguably the most important step because ultraviolet exposure is one of the leading contributors to premature skin aging, including wrinkles, loss of elasticity, and uneven pigmentation,” claims Olamiju. Guzman adds that UV exposure contributes significantly to collagen breakdown and changes in skin texture, noting that sunscreen is essential even on cloudy days.

Olamiju also recommends incorporating topical antioxidants, such as vitamin C, to help protect against environmental oxidative stress, along with moisturizers containing ingredients like ceramides and hyaluronic acid to support hydration and the skin barrier. “Retinoids, including retinol and prescription tretinoin, can be beneficial for improving fine lines, skin texture, and collagen production, provided they are appropriate for the individual,” Olamiju describes.

In a recent development, once your at-home routine is in place, Guzman recommends strategically incorporating preventive and corrective aesthetic treatments. “Depending on the patient’s age, anatomy, and concerns, treatments such as neuromodulators, collagen-stimulating injectables, microneedling, and energy-based devices may help maintain or improve certain aspects of skin appearance,” notes Guzman. The key, she adds, is choosing the right treatment for the right patient rather than following every new aesthetic trend. Olamiju includes chemical peels in a very similar list of in-office treatments, depending, of course, on an individual’s concerns, skin type, and goals.

In a recent development, finally, the experts agree: don’t underestimate lifestyle. Adequate sleep, regular exercise, balanced nutrition rich in antioxidants and healthy fats, avoiding smoking, managing chronic stress, and limiting excessive alcohol consumption all contribute to overall health and can influence how our skin looks and functions.

The best time to start is earlier than most people think, Olamiju shares. Ideally in your 20s, when prevention can have a meaningful long-term impact. “This does not mean that someone in their 20s needs an elaborate skincare routine or aggressive cosmetic treatments. Rather, it is an opportunity to establish healthy habits, including daily sunscreen use, gentle cleansing, and consistent moisturizing,” says Olamiju.

Industry observers note that guzman advises starting a smart aging routine before you feel like you need to correct something, but it’s also never too late to begin. “I encourage patients to think of smart aging as something that evolves with them rather than a routine that begins at a specific birthday,” she adds, offering this decade-by-decade breakdown:.

In your 20s. The priority should be prevention. This means establishing healthy skincare habits, wearing sunscreen consistently, maintaining hydration, and introducing targeted active ingredients when appropriate. At this stage, the goal isn’t to aggressively treat aging. It's to protect the skin and build a strong foundation.

In your 30s. You may begin noticing subtle changes in skin texture, fine lines, pigmentation, or elasticity. This is often a good time to reassess your skin care routine and consider professional treatments based on individual needs. It may also be time to consider a little Botox, energy-based treatments, and biostimulators, all depending on the individual’s needs.

In your 40s and 50s. Changes in collagen, facial volume, skin elasticity, and hormonal influences may become more noticeable. A smart aging approach at this stage may include a combination of medical-grade skin care, collagen-supporting treatments, and continued careful selection of aesthetic procedures.

Industry observers note that in your 60s and beyond. The focus remains on maintaining skin health, supporting the skin barrier, addressing concerns such as dryness and uneven pigmentation, and choosing treatments that respect the skin’s changing needs.

Olamiju adds that even patients who begin prioritizing their skin health later in life can benefit significantly from consistent sun protection and an appropriately tailored skin care regimen.

Guzman states this is one of the most vital conversations we should be having in modern aesthetics. Both experts say smart aging is not a one-size-fits-all concept. They explain that different skin types and skin tones can experience visible aging differently, and understanding those differences is essential to creating a safe and effective treatment plan.

Individuals with lighter skin tones may notice fine lines, redness, and visible sun damage earlier, particularly if they have a history of significant UV exposure. Those with darker skin tones may have more visible concerns related to uneven pigmentation, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, and changesin skin tone. “Melanin provides some natural protection against ultraviolet radiation, but it does not eliminate the risk of sun damage or skin cancer, so daily sunscreen remains essential for all skin tones,” Olamiju warns.

Sources indicate that guzman adds that these pigmentation concerns also become especially important when we introduce aesthetic treatments such as lasers, chemical peels, or microneedling. “Certain procedures and treatment settings carry a greater risk of pigmentation changes in patients with more melanin-rich skin, so proper patient selection, device choice, and clinical expertise are critical,” Guzman notes.

Overall, smart aging should combine science, prevention, consistency, and thoughtful aesthetic care. “Aging is inevitable. How we care for ourselves throughout that process is where we have the opportunity to make a meaningful difference,” Guzman concludes.
