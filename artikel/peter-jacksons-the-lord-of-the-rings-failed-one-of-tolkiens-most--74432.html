Peter Jackson's The Lord Of The Rings Failed One Of Tolkien's Most Dangerous Villains

Peter Jackson's The Lord of the Rings is an absolute masterpiece, a loving tribute to J.R.R. Tolkien's epic novels. They're not perfect, of course; despite their phenomenal length, they cut a huge number of scenes and plot elements. Tom Bombadil is missing, for example, while the final battle against Saruman at the Shire is completely absent. That particular cut has proven deeply controversial, with fans still debating it today.

Sources indicate that oddly, though, that's part of a wider mistake made in The Lord of the Rings. In the books, that Shire battle is the culmination of a story that played out very differently to Tolkien's movies. And, importantly, it means the Peter Jackson films actually failed one specific character—one of Tolkien's most complex villains.

Played by Brad Dourif, Grima Wormtongue was a manipulative adviser to King Théoden. In the books, he's a subtle schemer who'd been given his nickname by his political enemies, who'd learned to fear his Machiavellian cunning. Wormtongue was cruel and ambitious, a servant of Saruman who had proven far more successful than his master anticipated. He'd subtly undermined Théoden's reign, bringing the kingdom of Rohan to the brink of destruction.

Recent findings suggest that the book version of Grima Wormtongue doesn't achieve this through magic. Instead, he's simply the consumate politician, proof that you don't need physical power or sorcerous ability to be a dangerous villain. He reinforced this through the use of poisons, leaving his opponents under his thrall. He may have supposedly been an adviser, but the ambitious Grima Wormtongue had essentially taken over the entire kingdom before he was exposed and defeated by Gandalf the White.

Peter Jackson's The Lord of the Rings took a different approach, though. Here, the scene where Gandalf frees Théoden makes it abundantly clear he was enchanted; he has lost all strength and vitality because he is ensorcelled, unable to exercise free will. More to the point, when Gandalf breaks the spell, it isn't Wormtongue who is sent flying. No, Saruman himself is shocked as his power is broken, because he is the one who has really taken control of Rohan.

The difference is an important one. In Tolkien's books, Grima Wormtongue has a power all of his own, one not even Saruman possesses; he's still a tool, but one who is dangerous enough to operate independently, and who doesn't need the wizard's magic of Saruman to make him more dangerous. In contrast, in the films, he is essentially a channel; Saruman pours his magic through him, using Grime as a way to get to King Théoden. Wormtongue is, oddly, so much more dangerous.

Looking back, I can't help feeling Peter Jackson largely failed Grima Wormtongue. He turned Grima into something so much less than Tolkien intended, making him nothing more than a pawn (albeit one who eventually strikes back). Even his final scenes were rewritten, because Grima's compressed plotline was supposed to end at the Shire. It's one of the few missteps in Jackson's The Lord of the Rings.
