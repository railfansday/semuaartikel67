Industry observers note that there are many perks of being a Women’s Health style writer, and testing comfortable sneakers for work ranks very high on the list. My days are basically a rotation of steps—commutes, errands, long walks in the name of journalism—which means I’ve become extremely picky about what earns a spot on my feet. But time after time, I keep coming back to Hoka, aka the King of Cushion.

Since the brand debuted in 2009, Hoka has perfected the balance between support and comfort (the number one feature in a walking shoe), indicates podiatrist Brad Schaeffer, DPM, foot surgeon and owner of Central Park Sole in New York City.

Industry observers note that hoka has not only asserted itself as a top contender in the sneaker scene, but the brand has also become a style statement, too, making ortho-looking shoes look cool with their plush cushioning. The brand churns out a bunch of styles for all sorts of activities, from running shoes to hiking boots, but Hoka’s walking shoes are some of my favorites. I get that combing through all the specs can get overwhelming, so luckily, I’ve done the legwork for you by testing and walking in Hoka sneaks for the past two years. Plus, I enlisted the help of Schaeffer and other WH editors to get the scoop on the right shoes for you.

After testing nearly every Hoka model, the Clifton 11 reigns supreme for walking. I was also a fan of the previous Clifton 10, and the newest version feels very similar. It is by far the most comfortable model in the series to date, and it’s earned a spot in my shoe rotation for its supportive yet flexible cushioning that gives me a spring in my step.

The first major difference I noticed is how light the Clifton 11 feels. Even after wearing it for several hours at a time, it doesn’t feel bulky or clunky. Which makes sense: Hoka did shave nearly half an ounce off the weight compared to the 10. The toe box is slightly more narrow, too, but I actually preferred the more secure fit because it kept my foot from sliding around without feeling cramped. If you like a little more wiggle room for your toes, I would suggest opting for the wide sizing, though.

Recent findings suggest that despite its lighter weight, it has an ample stack of foam padding throughout the midsole. That weightless yet cushiony feel makes it ideal for my longer walks throughout NYC. The shoe has a mild heel drop (the same as the previous model at 8 mm), which helps distribute pressure evenly across the foot.

Industry observers note that I also found that the foam absorbed impact well on both pavement and the treadmill. My feet—unfortunately plagued by plantar fasciitis—never felt fatigued wearing this shoe. There's technically a rocker shape built into the sole, but it's subtle enough that I barely notice it—instead, my stride just felt smooth and natural whether I was walking or running. I’m also a fan of the durable rubber outsole that gives plenty of traction. I never feel like I’m going to slip while wearing these, even when walking down slippery subway stairs.

Industry observers note that so far, the Clifton 11—which was debuted alongside the upcoming Clifton Pro— does a great job of keeping my feet comfy and supported. It’s a great shoe for anyone in need of a comfortable, everyday walking sneaker that can also handle the occasional jog (unlike the Clifton Pro, which is geared more toward high-speed running).

Industry observers note that hoka is known for its incredibly cushioned sneakers, but extreme cushioning is not always the best for keeping feet stable. In recent years, Hoka has leveled up their offering with more stability options, but the right pick depends on how your foot moves. If your stride is balanced, go for a neutral shoe like the Clifton or Bondi. These models provide cushioning and support without changing your natural gait.

If your foot tends to roll inward (aka overpronation) or outwards (supination), a stability Hoka shoe like the Arahi or Gaviota features extra structure along the arch and heel to keep your feet aligned and locked in. This lowers your risk of aches and injury.

Recent findings suggest that A popular Hoka sneaker, like the Bondi or Clifton, has super-thick cushioning made of EVA foam that is rubber-like and incredibly durable. With each step, it compresses and provides a snappy bounce. Despite the Hokas’ bulky look, they’re all relatively lightweight at under 10 ounces—hefty enough to keep your foot locked in, but light enough not to bog you down. Plus, several Hoka styles feature a "rocker bottom," which refers to a little lift under the heel and toe so those two parts of the shoe don’t touch the ground when standing still, similar to the base of a rocking chair. That shape helps you to walk from heel to toe, which means you exert less energy to propel your strides.

Some Hoka sneakers are designed for specific arch types: flat, neutral, and high. For example, the Gaviota is designed with flat feet in mind; the Clifton is designed for neutral arches; and the Bondi is suitable for high arches. If you’re unsure of your arch type, a simple test is to dip your foot into water and step onto a piece of paper. If you see your entire foot, it’s flat, while seeing half means it’s neutral. Seeing barely anything means you have a high arch.

Sources indicate that A heel-to-toe drop, sometimes called a midsole drop, is the difference in height between the heel and forefoot (toe area). Schaeffer claims to look for a walking shoe with a heel-to-toe drop that puts your foot in a neutral position, which will be different for everyone’s unique foot and step pattern.
