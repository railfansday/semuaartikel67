At least it’s less disruptive than an internet shutdown.

At 08:30 on Thursday morning every powered-on mobile phone in South Korea blared out an Extreme Emergency Alert tone for 10.5 seconds – but the nation was at peace, with no major natural disasters occurring.

In a recent development, the extreme emergency South Korea hoped to avoid was cheating in the annual College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT), an exam that over half a million people sit in the hope of securing admission to their preferred university. This year, around a third of those applicants are repeating the test after their previous effort didn’t get them the result they wanted.

It has been revealed that CSAT involves five separate examinations staged in a single day. Participants can only bring pencils to the test. Smartphones or any sort of tech is strictly forbidden.

Which is why Korean authorities used the national alert system on the day of CSAT, because if the 40dB tone sounded in an exam centre, it could indicate a student had deliberately or inadvertently tried to bring a smartphone – perhaps as an exam aide.

Korean media report the 08:30 alert turned up nine phones.

Authorities will send a second alert tone at an unspecified time later in the day to again smoke out any surreptitious smartphones.

It’s not possible to opt out of the alerts, one reason a concerned netizen has used the Android concern tracker to suggest sending alerts to detect exam cheats could be an abuse of the emergency alert system that Google might want to do something about.

South Korea sends a lot of emergency alerts, for matters such as helping to find missing persons or to warn of dangerous weather. It’s not uncommon to receive several in a single day. Authorities also gave advance notice of the CSAT alerts, which will not, therefore, cause panic on the streets of Seoul or Busan.

In a recent development, CSAT day is, however, a serious occasion. Some businesses open late so test-takers can get to their exams without having to battle peak hour traffic.

Those measures are arguably less disruptive than the widespread internet outages that some states in India impose on major exam days.

It has been revealed that an alleged leak of one Indian exam paper saw the nation’s tech regulator ban messaging service Telegram in the country and eventually sparked a youth protest movement that Newly introduced Delhi tried to suppress with more deliberate internet outages. ®.
