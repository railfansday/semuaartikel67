People struggle with life's inevitable challenges, traumas large and small, and the chaos of our times everywhere. Throughout Gina Vild’s career, most recently as an associate dean at Harvard Medical School, she has been fascinated by what psychology and neuroscience tell us about our capacity to thrive in the face of adversity. She has also watched people confront profound hardship, heal over time, and thrive. Vild has navigated her own adversity of loss early in life and profound betrayal. She shows this in her recent book, Buoyant.

GV: No one escapes life’s inevitable losses, disappointments, and unexpected turns. When confronted with adversity, some people rail against the darkness while others think, "Barn's burned down, now I can see the moon?” Buoyant people cultivate a mindset that enables them to adapt, heal, and grow. Embracing acceptance and forgiveness.

LM: If adversity is unavoidable, how do we become who we were?

According to recent reports, GV: That may not happen. By moving through the difficulties life hurls and mining them for lessons, we often emerge changed and wiser. We are butterflies. Only by leaving the comfort of the chrysalis can it spread its wings and fly. We can discover our most beautiful lives by embracing transformation and remaining open to all possibilities.

LM: If we can't restore an old life, how do we build a fresh one?

GV: Each person navigates their pain differently; no single roadmap or certainty will get us there, but common threads exist.

Buoyant people know they have a choice. They recognize they cannot control what happens to them, but they can choose how they will respond. They can remain trapped in the past and carry sorrow like a heavy backpack. Or choose to look forward, embrace hope as a discipline, take purposeful action, manage unruly emotions, and reach for connection.

Buoyant people accept and grieve what is forever lost. Grief is love’s necessary counterpoint. When we choose to love deeply, we are accepting the possibility of profound sorrow. When we can see grief as a healing balm, we pay tribute not only to what we have lost, but to the immeasurable gift of having loved deeply.

Buoyant people find the courage to embrace the unfamiliar and accept the risk of not knowing where the path will lead. Detours and zigzags litter the path, but also opportunities to discover unimaginable possibilities. The Journal of Traumatic Stress reports that reaching for new possibilities is a hallmark of posttraumatic growth. As the poet John O'Donohue reminds us: “Awaken your spirit to adventure. Hold nothing back, learn to find ease in risk.”.

LM: How do we keep the past from controlling our future?

GV: We cannot change the past, but we can stop letting it replay in a mind loop. Our thoughts hold extraordinary power over every aspect of our lives, and learning to manage them can change life.

Even a fleeting thought can spark a decision, a habit, or a change in direction. When our thoughts hold us back, one of the most empowering things we can do is learn to change the channel. With practice, we can teach ourselves to observe the thoughts whispering in our ear and catch them before they land. We see destructive thought loops and flip the script. We may not control which thoughts arise, but we can choose which ones deserve attention.

LM: Can gratitude erase suffering?

GV: When hardship strikes, it’s like a single, overwhelming black dot on the screen of our life. It dominates our field of vision, blotting out all else. To ease the sorrow and regain perspective, pull back, widen the aperture, and let the light in. That light comes from gratitude. Gratitude is not a platitude; it's a catalyst for mental toughness, physical health, and greater happiness.

LM: How do we become present in life, and not just get through it?

Industry observers note that GV: Mindfulness, the practice of renewing our minds and tending to our bodies, returns us to who we are at our essence. By stripping away worry, fear, obligation, and distraction, mindfulness anchors us in the present moment, enhancing mental clarity and emotional stability. This can come through guided meditation, journaling, yoga, knitting, music, time in nature, or enjoying a pet. When we are fully present, we don't just move through our days.

LM: How do we release resentment without excusing wrongdoing?

GV: Through forgiveness, we release resentment over a past hurt and permit ourselves to move from pain to a mindset where we can flourish. We can choose peace and healing over bitterness and blame. An international study in BMJ Public Health underscores the restorative power of forgiveness; practicing it can ease depression and anxiety. Yet some struggle with forgiveness, equating it with absolution. But forgiveness doesn’t condone past harm.

Industry observers note that nelson Mandela understood this. After 27 years in prison, he walked toward the gate upon his release and had a revelation: Liberation required the release of bitterness and hatred. If he carried them through the prison gates, he would still be in prison.

LM: How do we take responsibility for our own healing?

GV: We cannot control the past, but we can shape the future. We can strengthen resilience. Decades of research, including a BMJ Open review, confirms that resilience can be bolstered in response to adverse events, setbacks, and disappointments. As the Buddha reminds us, “Every life has a measure of sorrow, and sometimes this is what awakens us.” We can choose this awakening by using proven practices, seeking support, and taking purposeful steps toward healing. In doing so, we mine our sadness for wisdom and discover strengths we never knew we possessed.
