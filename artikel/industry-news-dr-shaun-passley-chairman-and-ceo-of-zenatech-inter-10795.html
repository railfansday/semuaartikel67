📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Dr. Shaun Passley is Chairman and CEO of ZenaTech, bringing a background in enterprise software development and technology entrepreneurship to autonomous drone systems. He founded Epazz in 2000 and established ZenaTech in 2017, building on his software experience to connect aircraft, AI analytics, and business workflows. His work focuses on making drone technology practical for organizations that need better ways to collect data, automate fieldwork, and manage complex operations.

Recent findings suggest that zenaTech develops AI-powered drone, Drone as a Service, enterprise software, and quantum technology solutions. Through ZenaDrone and its service operations, the company combines aircraft design, data capture, and analytics for applications including surveying, inspections, agriculture, and warehouse inventory management. In this interview, Passley discusses autonomous navigation, the commercial model for drone services, and the technical and operational work required to bring coordinated drone fleets into everyday business workflows.

It has been revealed that you founded Epazz in 2000 and went on to establish ZenaTech in 2017, transitioning from enterprise software into AI-powered autonomous drones. What inspired this shift, and what opportunities did you identify that traditional drone manufacturers were overlooking?

According to recent reports, it started with a software test in Ireland. We were developing supply chain software for agriculture and brought a third-party drone to test it for plant identification. The drone was cumbersome to set up and struggled with the rugged terrain, constant rain, and changes in altitude. It didn’t have the sensors needed to adapt to those conditions, so we decided to build our own.

We also saw agriculture, especially spot spraying, was an obvious opportunity. There is a lot more that can be done with drones in data collection, pick harvesting, killing weeds and generally in improving yields. That’s the type of technology you will start to see commercialized heavily in the next three to five years. Much of that work is still labor-intensive. We saw an opportunity to build the hardware and software together and offer it as an accessible and cost-effective service.

Recent findings suggest that our background in enterprise software gave us an advantage. We understood the need and had the skills to connect drone hardware with software that could process data and automate workflows. As we began looking at industries like land surveying, infrastructure inspection, and warehouse logistics, we saw significant opportunities to modernize processes that still relied heavily on manual labor and outdated under digitized systems.

We also recognized early that collecting aerial data was only part of the solution. Businesses need technology that can process that information and turn it into actionable insights. That led us to developing and manufacturing our own drone hardware through ZenaDrone, integrated with AI analytics and enterprise software. We saw an opportunity to build an integrated platform and delivery mechanism that can automate entire workflows, rather than simply manufacture drones. This led to establishing our Drone as a Service business, currently a growing global brand, providing these turnkey, on-site services in a host of sectors.

Recent findings suggest that artificial intelligence is becoming increasingly central to drone technology. How does ZenaTech integrate AI and machine learning into its drone platforms, and what capabilities distinguish an AI-powered autonomous drone from a conventional drone operating on preprogrammed flight paths?

Traditional drones operating on preprogrammed flight paths follow a defined set of instructions. AI-powered autonomous drones can use onboard sensors, computer vision, and machine learning to interpret their surroundings and respond to changing conditions.

According to recent reports, take our IQ Nano indoor drone. With onboard computer vision and obstacle avoidance sensors, it’s designed to operate inside warehouses without GPS. It can navigate around obstacles, scan inventory barcodes, and upload data into inventory management systems. The broader opportunity is enabling drones to collect, interpret, and act on information with greater independence.

We are integrating AI into our drone platforms to support capabilities such as obstacle avoidance, autonomous navigation, predictive analytics, and automated backend data processing across our platform.

ZenaTech has embraced a Drone as a Service model to make autonomous drone technology more accessible. What are the biggest barriers preventing businesses from adopting AI-powered drones, and how does your Drone as a Service approach address challenges such as cost, technical expertise, and operational complexity?

It has been revealed that for most business customers, their focus isn’t on drone ownership. They care more about completing the job faster, safely, and at a competitive cost.

Sources indicate that the biggest barriers for businesses are the upfront investment, technical expertise, and operational requirements involved in managing commercial drone fleets. Organizations need to deal with trained pilots, recurring maintenance, regulatory compliance, and systems to process the data once it’s been collected.

Our Drone as a Service model addresses these challenges by providing turnkey drone-powered services on a subscription or usage basis. We manage the aircraft, pilots, compliance, data capture, and analytics. Customers get access to drone technology and its benefits automating the task without having to manage the operations themselves.

This makes it easier for businesses to adopt autonomous technology while focusing on the services and outcomes they need. It also allows us to introduce automation into industries that might struggle with the upfront cost or technical requirements.

It has been revealed that zenaTech recently reported 316% year-over-year revenue growth in Q2 2026, with DaaS accounting for the vast majority of revenue. How much of this growth reflects increasing customer demand for AI-powered automation, and what indicators do you use to measure the actual productivity improvements delivered to customers?

Recent findings suggest that acquisitions and then integrating our drones into the workflows for services such as surveys, mapping and inspections have been major drivers of ZenaTech growth. Our goal is to make the businesses we acquire more productive and create margin growth and leverage across our growing network of almost 45 global DaaS locations.

Sources indicate that our growth reflects a combination of strategic acquisitions, expanding demand for drone-powered services, and the operational efficiencies we’re introducing across our Drone as a Service network. In the second quarter of 2026, revenue increased 316% year over year, with Drone as a Service accounting for approximately 93% of total revenue. More recently, we reported average organic revenue growth of 27.1% across approximately 20 Drone as a Service locations acquired in 2025, with some locations growing by more than 40%.

Sources indicate that we measure productivity through practical outcomes: how quickly teams can complete fieldwork, how fast customers receive data, and how many additional projects existing teams can handle. Across our Drone as a Service operations, we survey more than 125 active projects per week. We have also reported internal estimates of delivering data 60% faster and reducing project costs by approximately half compared with traditional ground-based survey methods. Our priority is to increase operational capacity while improving efficiency and safety.

Acquisitions have become a major component of ZenaTech’s expansion strategy, particularly in surveying and engineering services. How do you integrate AI-powered drone technology into these established businesses, and what opportunities exist to automate workflows that have traditionally required significant manual labor?

We acquire established surveying, geospatial mapping and inspection businesses with experienced and licensed professionals, and existing revenue and customer base, then we introduce our drone technology to improve how they operate.

Recent findings suggest that regarding efficiency, in most cases, traditional surveys require crews to spend days collecting measurements often across challenging terrain. Drones equipped with photogrammetry and LiDAR capabilities can collect precise spatial data more efficiently and reduce the need for manual fieldwork. We also train licensed professionals, including surveyors and inspectors, to operate our autonomous systems. They already understand the work and have the expertise to do it well. All we do is give them better tools so they can get better results, faster and safer.

Beyond surveying, we’re expanding into services such as power washing for business exteriors and public infrastructure, inventory management for large warehouses, and construction progress monitoring for AI data centers and commercial building projects. We also offer end-to-end data management services to help customers access accurate site data more efficiently, streamline project workflows, and reduce project delays.

In a recent development, autonomous drones operating in complex environments must process large amounts of sensor and visual data while making decisions in real time. What are the most significant technical challenges involved in achieving reliable autonomy, and how is ZenaTech approaching areas such as computer vision, obstacle avoidance, and edge AI processing?

Sources indicate that one of our key priorities is advancing drones’ ability to accurately interpret their surroundings, process data in real time, and operate reliably across a wide range of dynamic environments. We’ve encountered this firsthand with our IQ Nano indoor drone. During an early warehouse pilot, dim lighting and reflective packaging made it difficult to accurately read inventory barcodes.

We addressed this challenge by training the AI vision system to recognize glare and distortion, analyze images from multiple angles, and identify the clearest image for barcode recognition.

We also developed an AI-enabled camera system with integrated LED illumination to improve data capture in low-light environments. Our drone platforms incorporate onboard AI, computer vision, and advanced sensors to support autonomous navigation, obstacle avoidance, and real-time data collection and analysis. These capabilities are particularly notable in GPS-denied environments, where drones must navigate without relying on satellite positioning.

Sources indicate that another challenge is determining which decisions drones can safely make autonomously and when human intervention is needed. Drones can already follow programmed routes, while more advanced systems can adjust their paths as conditions change. Wider adoption depends on demonstrating that those decisions are reliable, remain within defined operating limits, and meet regulatory requirements. The FAA permits autonomous flight under Part 107, while requiring the remote pilot to retain the ability to direct the aircraft. The next step is to strengthen decision-making in unexpected situations while maintaining appropriate human oversight. Operators need confidence in both what the system can do and how it responds when something goes wrong.

Agriculture represents a significant opportunity for AI-powered drones, particularly in precision spraying, crop monitoring, and resource optimization. How are ZenaTech’s platforms evolving to support these applications, and how close are we to agricultural drones that can independently identify crop conditions and take corrective actions with minimal human intervention?

I think agriculture will become significantly more automated over the next three to five years.

It’s a significant opportunity for AI-powered drones, particularly as farmers and growers look for ways to reduce costs, optimize resources, and ultimately improve yields. At ZenaTech, we’re advancing precision agriculture solutions through our ZenaDrone platforms, which combine autonomous flight, advanced imaging, and AI analytics to support crop health monitoring, early detection of plant stress, and more targeted agricultural treatments.

In September, we announced the initial prototype and the start of the testing phase for our IQ Octo drone. This platform is specifically designed for precision agriculture imaging, AI analytics, and includes autonomous spraying and seeding. More recently, we started developing a mobile drone operations trailer to support automated, multi-drone on-site agricultural services. The mobile trailer enables drones to land and automatically recharge batteries, refill drone spray tanks with liquid agricultural inputs and replenish seed hoppers, reducing downtime between flights and operating with minimal human intervention. Our goal is to advance our development to bring these capabilities into agricultural workflows to help monitor crop conditions, support targeted treatments, and optimize manual labor requirements.

Sources indicate that zenaTech has unveiled research into quantum-enabled navigation systems designed to support drone operations in GPS-denied environments. What role do you envision quantum technologies playing alongside AI in autonomous navigation, and what technical milestones must be achieved before these capabilities become commercially practical?

Sources indicate that GPS signals can be jammed and spoofed, particularly in defense environments. We need drones that can navigate reliably when satellite positioning is not available.

We see quantum technologies as a complement to AI, combining quantum-based sensing with AI-driven path correction to enable more accurate navigation without relying on satellite signals.

At ZenaTech, we’ve advanced our quantum navigation system into the testing phase at our facility in Sharjah, UAE, starting with IQ Nano in GPS-denied environments. We plan to expand field testing to the ZenaDrone 1000 and other drone platforms. Our current focus is on validating accuracy and reliability under real-world conditions. Our long-term objective is to give autonomous drones another reliable way to navigate when satellite positioning fails.

These are important steps toward integrating quantum-enabled navigation into our broader autonomous drone portfolio for defense and commercial applications.

In a recent development, defense and government applications are becoming an increasingly crucial area of development for autonomous drone systems. How does ZenaTech approach the balance between AI-driven autonomy and human oversight, particularly in sensitive applications involving surveillance, threat detection, and autonomous decision-making?

AI-driven autonomy has the potential to improve situational awareness, operational efficiency, and safety in defense and government applications. However, I believe human operators must retain the ability to override an autonomous system. That means having a kill switch to stop or safely terminate the mission if something goes wrong.

Sources indicate that zenaTech is developing autonomous systems for intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance, cargo transport, and counter-UAS operations. Our integrated counter-UAS system is being designed to combine AI-powered threat detection and tracking with autonomous swarm coordination, allowing a single operator to oversee multiple interceptor drones. Our focus is on using AI to process information, support faster decision-making, and reduce personnel exposure to hazardous environments while developing systems that can operate reliably in complex defense settings.

Industry observers note that looking ahead over the next three to five years, how do you envision the convergence of AI, autonomous robotics, and enterprise software transforming the industries ZenaTech serves? Do you anticipate a future where coordinated fleets of intelligent drones operate as autonomous agents, managing entire workflows rather than executing individual tasks?

Recent findings suggest that it’s already happening. Much of the underlying technology exists today. You could have robots that automatically insert information into the company’s ERP solution, and the ERP solution could tell the robot to perform a certain number of tasks on some type of schedule set by a human being.

Industry observers note that when it comes to our drone platforms, we’re moving toward a future where autonomous drones become integrated into everyday business operations, managing complex workflows with less manual intervention. ZenaTech is already seeing how combining drone hardware, AI analytics, and enterprise software can transform traditional labor-intensive industries. Our Drone as a Service model is helping bring these capabilities into surveying and inspection operations, while our research and development initiatives are advancing technologies such as drone swarms and next-generation AI autonomy.

Sources indicate that the next phase of growth for the drone industry will come from making safe, large-scale operations more practical. U.S. regulatory progress, particularly around flying beyond the pilot’s visual line of sight, is helping open that opportunity. Combined with advances in autonomy and aircraft reliability, this can allow drones to cover larger areas more efficiently, bringing greater value to customers in surveying, infrastructure inspection, and agriculture.

In a recent development, over the next three to five years, I’m expecting to see coordinated fleets of intelligent drones perform simultaneous and connected tasks, from collecting and analyzing data to supporting operational decisions in real time. Our long-term vision is to build a vertically integrated company and AI autonomy platform through our ZenaDrone drone platforms, and Drone as a Service business. Our goal is to enable organizations all over the world to manage entire workflows more efficiently, allowing skilled professionals to oversee operations and decisions with advanced data and focus on higher-value work.
