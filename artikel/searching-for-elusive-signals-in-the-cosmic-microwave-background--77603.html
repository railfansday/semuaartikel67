In a recent development, as the oldest observable light in the cosmos, the Cosmic Microwave Background is a fundamental signal that has a lot to teach us about the Universe. It dates back to the recombination epoch, when photons could first travel freely through the cosmos. The CMB is like a snapshot of the state of energy and matter about 380,000 years after the Major Bang.

It has been revealed that but the CMB has also changed over time with the expansion of the Universe. As the Universe has expanded, the CMB has been stretched into microwave wavelengths that we can't see. That's why, despite containing the overwhelming majority of the Universe's photons, we can't see it without the help of telescopes.

Recent findings suggest that two space telescopes have played important roles in mapping the CMB: the Wilkinson Microwave Anisotropy Probee (WMAP), and the Planck satellite. They were both up above the obscuring effect of Earth's atmosphere, which is the main obstacle to observing the CMB. The CMB is nearly uniform, but not quite. It has tiny irregularities that cosmologist have mapped in increasing detail. These small temperature inhomogeneities can reveal a lot about the Universe and the Major Bang.

According to recent reports, but there's also another way of studying the CMB. The South Pole Telescope is a 10-meter radio telescope in Antarctica that takes advantage of a small atmospheric window to observe part of the CMB. It's also in a designated "radio-quiet zone." It's credited with discovering a newly introduced feature of the CMB called the B-mode polarization. Gravitational lensing by intervening matter like galaxy clusters created this B-mode polarization. That proved that the CMB can hold evidence of gravitatonal effects.

That was a significant deal because the polarization had been predicted decades earlier. The CMB's polarization is almost perfectly uniform. But almost isn't completely, and there are tiny variations in the B-mode polarity. In this case, these tiny changes were created by intervening matter.
