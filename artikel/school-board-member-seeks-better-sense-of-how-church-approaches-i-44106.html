📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

This happened when the board was deciding whether to allow a long-term lease of space on weekends to a church, pursuant to a policy that allowed such leases for community groups generally. And, a federal court held Friday, this violated the First Amendment. Some excerpts from the long opinion in Pines Church v. Hermon School Dep't, by Chief Judge Lance Walker (D. Me.):.

In a recent development, plaintiff Matt Gioia moved his family from Colorado to Maine in the summer of 2020 to start a new church. Gioia was born in Waterville, Maine, and lived in Guilford, Maine until he graduated high school and joined the military at seventeen years old. Initially, Gioia's church—The Pines Church—gathered at his home. In March of 2021, The Pines Church began holding Sunday morning services at a movie theater in Orono, Maine, pursuant to a month-to-month lease arrangement. As the Church grew, Gioia began to look for a new space that could accommodate a larger group.

Gioia sought space from the Hermon School Department, which generally opens up its facilities to various groups:.

This policy provides that "[s]chool facilities should be made available for appropriate community use when such facilities are not required for their primary purposes: the instruction of students and related school activities." The policy distinguishes between "occasional or short-term use of school facilities," which "shall be authorized by the school principal," and "long-term rental or lease," which "shall be authorized by the Hermon School Committee upon the recommendation of the Superintendent of Schools.".

But the Hermon School Committee (which consisted of "Committee Chair Jesse Keith, Vice Chair Karen Shorey, Stephanie Oiler, Haily Keezer, and Christopher McLaughlin") denied the Church's request for a year-long or six-month lease, allowing only a month-to-month lease (which appeared to be inadequate for the church, given its need for a stable meeting location). Here's part of the summary of the facts, based on the bench trial that the court held:.

The next day [after a hearing], a former committee member emailed Keith and Shorey to share her belief "that it is not in our communities [sic] best interest to lease one of our school facilities to any religious organization," warning that "[t]here is a quiet movement in our country by some conservative religious groups who are actively working to soften the boundaries between church/state, starting with our schools" and that granting the Church a lease would lead to "a slippery slope." She wondered how the Hermon School Department would have to respond to a similar request from "an Imam who wants to open a mosque," in light of the "precedent" set by a lease to The Pines Church.

Her email concluded with unintended irony: "There's a reason why the founding fathers wrote the constitution the way they did." Keith and Shorey denied at trial that this email influenced their decision-making with respect to the Church's lease request, which I find implausible in light of the trial record….

McLaughlin reached out to [HSD Superintendent Micah] Grant and explained that he "had a few additional questions" for Gioia:.

McLaughlin "wanted to get a better sense of how [T]he Pines Church approaches issues of diversity, equity, and inclusion around their messaging around some key issues relevant to marginalized communities." Those questions requested information about the Church's views on same-sex marriage, "[a]ccess to safe and affordable abortion," "[a]ccess to gender affirming medical care," "[c]onversion therapy for LGBTQIA+ individuals (youth and adults)," and "[i]nclusive sexual education and access to birth control for youth." ….

It has been revealed that [At a later meeting, High School Principal Brian] Walsh took the podium and explained that he had several reservations about the Church's request. He first explained that it would be difficult to find the staff to clean the building on Sunday afternoons. But apart from this, he continued:.

In a recent development, [W]e've never had a long-term lease, at least over my twenty-one years here, and I think there's a number of reasons we don't do that. There's a lot of variables we just don't know. No question our students should come first, our program should come first.

We also don't know or have the sense of how this will be promoted, to [the student representative's] question. If you put Hermon High School's name with a church or another organization with different beliefs than the school has, I see that as problematic. But again, that's where I sit and that's where I stand. Doesn't mean this organization won't do a great job, doesn't mean they won't support, hopefully, our mission as well. I just don't know, there are too many variables with that and again we have not—we have never done that.

It has been revealed that what I would not want is for students to feel like a group organization that we are renting to, their vision or their mission is now ours. And there's a blurred line when it comes to that. I have nothing against this church, this reverend, or anything, we've just never said yes to something like this, so probably why it's in your hands, because you guys get to make that decision. I'm looking at the facility and some other variables, so that's kind of—that's where I come from….

[At a later meeting,] Oiler then made a motion for a month-to-month lease, citing concerns about the number of people and cleaning the space afterwards, which she was careful to emphasize had nothing to do with the organization. Keezer asked if they could ask Gioia about how the Church kept its current space clean. Oiler curiously protested that this was not about the Church (when in fact it was ostensibly about the Church and its janitorial readiness) but any organization, then adding the less than persuasive point that if fifteen people got the stomach bug at the same time, someone would have to clean it up. {At trial, Oiler testified that she had in mind a specific incident that occurred at Hermon Middle School when one of her children was a student there.}.

Industry observers note that given the shortage of custodial staffing, she explained, she was concerned about the number of people who would be coming in and out of the building. She suggested that the Committee investigate its current rental arrangements, to make sure those organizations were paying what they should considering the costs associated with hosting them. What this had to do with the Church's application remains a mystery….

According to recent reports, the Free Speech Clause prohibits restrictions on access to limited or designated public fora that "discriminate against speech on the basis of viewpoint," including on the basis that the viewpoint in question is a religious one. In other words, if a public school's facilities are available for public use, a religious organization cannot be denied access to those facilities because it is a religious organization. Similarly, the Free Exercise Clause "bars even 'subtle departures from neutrality' on matters of religion," and therefore prohibits "denying a generally available benefit solely on account of religious identity," unless such denial is "justified … by a state interest 'of the highest order.'" ….

By virtue of Policy KG, the Hermon School Department made its facilities available "for appropriate community use when such facilities are not required for their primary purposes: the instruction of students and related school activities," including for "long-term rental or lease." Having done so, the School Department could not then reject the Church's lease application because it is a religious organization or because of disagreement with its religious viewpoints, without violating the First Amendment, as Plaintiffs contend has occurred….

The court concluded that the Department indeed violate the Church's First Amendment rights, by discriminating against it based on its "religious viewpoint or out of animus against that viewpoint":.

I start with the question of whether a majority or significant block of committee members demonstrated bad motive.

I find without any difficulty that McLaughlin exhibited bad motive. McLaughlin's intrusive questions expressed viewpoint related concerns towards Plaintiffs, and he was the only member not to vote in favor of any lease term, including the month-to-month arrangement. Although his email itemizing political and cultural questions occurred early in the process, before the Committee received any advice from counsel, and even though he testified that he harbored no ill will toward Plaintiffs after gaining more information about them, it strikes me as very likely that McLaughlin lacked objectivity when it came to the question of leasing school space to the Church.

Sources indicate that it appears that he based his decision directly on the Plaintiffs' religious beliefs and its associated political and culture ethics that flow from those beliefs, and the religious nature of the proposed use. McLaughlin would have approached a lease request differently had it been presented by an organization that shared his personal beliefs.

I also find that Oiler was unable to overcome concerns over constitutionally permissible school-church association, notwithstanding the legal advice the Committee received, and Grant repeatedly emphasized. Though less openly hostile than McLaughlin, Oiler's attitude toward the Church was anything but neutral…. [I]t struck me during her testimony, and I find, that Oiler's chief concern was that the tenant would be a Church. This was made only more obvious by protesting far too much that this was not about the Church but instead fear, for example, of mass vomiting epidemics and the platoon of janitorial assistance that would be required to mop it up.

All of her other concerns about space and the like were definitively answered by Grant as not remotely problematic. I find based on the trial record that her motive was to avoid the School's association with the Church for its religious views. The First Amendment is designed to avoid discriminatory treatment of religious organizations in their enjoyment of the benefits of public facilities based on their religious beliefs or speech. For this reason, I find that Oiler, like McLaughlin, voted as she did based on "bad motive.".

I find that Plaintiffs have proved that bad motive prevented Shorey from approaching the lease question fairly. Shorey, at her deposition, suggested that she believed McLaughlin's culture war email was appropriate, even though she later sought to characterize it as inappropriate at trial.

It may be that Shorey believes that a School Committee should have the discretion to question any organization about how its values align with what she sees as the school community's values, to the extent a school community has its own conflicting body of values that can be reduced to a catechesis—perhaps it does. But support for a process that involves examining a prospective tenant's views on religious problems, simply because the prospective tenant is a church, tends rather starkly to highlight the questioner's lack of neutrality toward religion….

Although I find that a majority of voting members exhibited bad motive which would itself be sufficient to end the inquiry, my findings should be considered also in context of the discussion during the deliberative process, which included rather openly anti-religious organization sentiment expressed by Walsh at least as he served as a voice of the school community and the discomfort it might experience especially if the beliefs of the School, whatever those might be, and the Church were at loggerheads. Even if the record had not established a majority of voting members acting out of bad motive, it would still have constituted a significant bloc of legislators and "circumstances suggesting the probable complicity of others," as well as "evidence indicating that the legislators bowed to an impermissible community animus.".

Recall that Walsh passed along student concerns about the Church qua church using the School and when asked if any spoke supportively of the idea, Walsh reported that none had. Also recall the email received by Keith Shorey from a former committee member. That former member insisted that this was all a Trojan horse coordinated by conservative Christian groups to blur the line between school and religion. What would the Committee do, the former committee member queried, if an Imam were to set up shop at Hermon High School next?

The standard is one of likelihood, and on that basis, I find that at least three of the five decision makers acted under the influence of bad motive and in so doing violated the Plaintiffs' rights under the First Amendment….

Industry observers note that defendant points to the majority vote in favor of a month-to-month use arrangement as proof of the lack of bias on the part of its committee members. I agree with the Church that this was not persuasive evidence of a lack of bad motive but to the contrary, is evidence of bad motive. The Committee knew the Church required a more fixed arrangement before uprooting itself from its then current location than a month-to-month arrangement provides. This appears to me to have been an offer designed to be rejected not one born of wholesome support for the Free Exercise Clause….

Bethany Onishenko and Robert H. Tyler (Tyler Law LLP), Erin Mersino and Julianne E. Fleischer (Advocates for Faith and Freedom), Joel L. Oster (Oster Law Firm), Wenonah Wirick (Conley & Wirick, P.A.) represent plaintiffs.
