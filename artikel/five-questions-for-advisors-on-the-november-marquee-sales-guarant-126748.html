Five Questions for Advisors on the November Marquee Sales: Guarantees, Spotty Bidding, and a $190 M. Van Gogh

It’s Thanksgiving week and, for those of us who live and breathe the art market, that means only the briefest of breaks between the all-vital November auction season and the year’s grand finale at Art Basel Miami Beach. But before we turn to Miami, a quick post-mortem on the marquee sales just passed.

Perhaps more than in any other industry, the health of the art market is in the eye of the beholder. Do you measure it by overall totals? By sell-through rate? By the number of bidders, or the number of guaranteed lots? By the ratio of hammer prices to estimates? Each figure tells a slightly different story, leaving plenty for our resident market whisperers to debate. But before we turn this week’s column over to the advisers, let’s set out some basic figures.

Industry observers note that according to data from ARTDAI, the November evening sales at Christie’s, Sotheby’s, and Phillips generated $2.03 billion, a modest 7.5 percent increase over 2025, but the highest total since 2022. Sotheby’s led the pack with $1.16 billion, clearing $1 billion and taking more than half of the three houses’ combined total for the second straight year. (Its share rose to 57 percent from 53 percent. Christie’s share dropped to 38 percent from 43 percent, while Phillips’s ticked up to 5 percent from 4 percent, pushed by the $44.6 million Modigliani sculpture.).

According to recent reports, and yes, the $384 million gap between Sotheby’s and Christie’s is roughly what the Blaquier-Arrieta collection brought in on its own: $412 million on just 12 lots. Winning those big consignments really does matter.

Sources indicate that but for all those gaudy totals, there was a definite sense watching the week’s sales that bidding was uneven, thin, and at times absent entirely. Blame fickle collectors, sure, but it seems the auction houses pushed their estimates to the limit. Per ARTDAI, hammer prices across the various-owner sales in November 2025 came in 17 percent above the low estimates and at roughly 80 percent of the high estimates. This season, hammer prices for the same sales (i.e. excluding the Blaquier-Arrieta single-owner sale) were flat against the low estimates and just 69 percent of the highs. As Sotheby’s CEO Charles Stewart noted after the Blaquier-Arrieta sale last week, “Our estimates weren’t shy.”.

In a recent development, but does an aggressive auction house make for a weak market? Or is this the same market we’ve seen for the last few cycles—one that is choosy, blue-chip and provenance-driven, and competitive for the right objects—just approached differently by the specialists? That’s what we asked the advisers, along with their takes on everything from the spread of guarantees to that $190 million van Gogh.

Answers have been edited for clarity and concision.

According to recent reports, evan Beard, Beard & Co: Our having to repeatedly chase Impressionist and Modern Day Sale works well above the high estimates with a fairly low hit rate tells us that the “flight to safety” is more than just a tired cliché. Even second- and third-tier Impressionists performed very well.

According to recent reports, liz Sterling, Sterling | Teel Capital: The strong result for the Jeff Koons Bob-Tail [at Sotheby’s] was a pleasant reminder that no matter how highly orchestrated evening sales have become, there are still surprises. Equally, the results for the small Robert Ryman and Robert Rauschenberg from the Agnes Gund collection clearly demonstrate that there is still connoisseurship in the market.

Recent findings suggest that nina del Rio, del Rio | Byers: I have always believed the market is driven by the number of participants; more bidders = higher prices, more volatility, and less predictability. The current moment feels more predictable, safe, and tempered—which would signal fewer bidders. Estimates were full, reflecting strong performance over the past few seasons, and the buyers are still there for the majority of material, which is European Impressionism and modernism and American postwar abstraction, but there was less competition and flatter prices as a result.

Sources indicate that however, I was surprised by the sober bidding on the truly rare material, which represented a singular (once-in-a-lifetime) opportunity for buyers. Monday evening after the Sotheby’s The Now and Contemporary auction, I told ARTnews I was excited for the Blaquier sale on Wednesday because of the rarity and quality of the works; well, I was wrong that rarity would translate into bidder competition and runaway prices. The fact is, each work was priced at the top end of the artists’ (very mature) markets—leaving little room for price discovery, and bidding was thin and tempered as a result. Did buyers perceive these prices as the very top end of the market? Yes, I think so, which meant little to no competition for great works.

Maria Brito: Believe it or not, the moment that caught my attention happened in Stockholm, with the sale of Hilma af Klint’s Ser III No. 5. It hammered at 108 million Swedish kronor against a high estimate of 8 million, an extraordinary 13.5 times the estimate! Here is an artist whose importance to the history of abstraction has been celebrated by major museums for years, but whose market barely existed because so few of her abstract paintings ever became available. And suddenly collectors were fighting to pay nearly $13 million for one.

Industry observers note that we saw something similar with Aaron Douglas’s Harriet Tubman in New York, which sparked a seven-minute bidding war involving eight bidders and sold for $14.48 million against a high estimate of $3.5 million. I think this tells us something fascinating about today’s market. There is enormous capital available for historically significant, genuinely rare works, particularly by artists whose contributions have been undervalued. Collectors are willing to reconsider established price hierarchies, and when they really want something, they’re not shy about spending.

Allan Schwartzman, Schwartzman&: For me, there were several standout points: the fever for hot young artists at auction has passed; Robert Rauschenberg remains the most crucial innovator of the last 75 years, and yet his market remains undeservedly quiet; and behind that, Gilbert & George are a crazy bargain; even though their work is photographic, they are unique and should be commanding the prices of the great painters of their generation.

According to recent reports, erica Samuels, Samuels Creative: Beyond prices, this week highlighted a distinct mood across the field. There is a strong sense of collegiality—advisors, dealers, and auction houses are actively working together to ensure success for their clients amid a generally sideways market. The market relies as much on goodwill as it does on capital, and both were evident this week. Despite broader tech and financial shifts, such as the Anthropic IPO, this remains a human-driven market centered on human-made art.

Sources indicate that danielle Forest Brandi, Beaumont Nathan: There were a few revealing moments for us, but among them was the spectacular result for Monet’s La Barque. The price of $6.9 million was, on the one hand, a surprising result for a work that measured only 2 by 3 inches, but on the other hand, a good case study for how collectors are behaving at the top end of the market. Looking at that alone, it’s perhaps an irrational result, but when you think about the rarity of the object, the story, and how good that picture will look on someone’s wall, i.e., styled alongside their Chinese porcelain collection and Nakashima furniture, it starts to make sense. That’s how collectors are thinking right now; it’s the beauty of the art market and what makes collecting different from other pursuits.

In a recent development, evan Beard: Two key themes drove the high third-party guarantee rate this season: (1) Much of the material came from single-owner sales that carried house guarantees—Blaquier, Baum, Clive Davis, etc.—so the houses had to put the risk off on third parties. (2) Consignor expectations were elevated coming off a strong spring season, which pushed estimates to a level that the houses felt more comfortable locking in guarantees rather than risk a lot going unsold.

According to recent reports, guarantees are fundamental at this point. They’re a behavior tool to get an early bid from a buyer and a behavioral tool to give comfort to a consignor. Both sides still have the chance at upside. Guarantees are an crucial lubricant.

Nina del Rio: Estimates were high across both auction houses, reflecting the past six years of consistent demand and solid performance—especially for European modernism and postwar American abstraction. The auction houses agree to full estimates to compete for consignments. My guess is that Christie’s was less willing to offer house guarantees which were not backed by irrevocable bids at the start of the consignment-getting season. And Sotheby’s had a bit more faith that they would find an IB [irrevocable bid] later in the process. In brief, I believe Sotheby’s appetite for risk—and possibly their confidence in the market—was a bit stronger this season.

Industry observers note that maria Brito: Sotheby’s just revealed its biggest-ever week of fine art sales at $1.31 billion, which is extraordinary. But let’s look underneath those numbers. Almost a third of that total came from just 12 paintings in the Blaquier-Arrieta collection, all guaranteed, and the majority hammered below their low estimates. Sotheby’s guaranteed 77 percent of its evening lots compared with 47 percent at Christie’s, suggesting a much more aggressive approach to securing major consignments. Guarantees are essential to attracting exceptional property, and third-party guarantors can absorb much of the risk. But a guaranteed sale tells us little about competitive demand. What interests me is how many collectors were willing to bid beyond the guaranteed price. The money is clearly there. The willingness to compete at any price is another matter.

Allan Schwartzman: The numbers speak for themselves; I don’t have any great insights here.

It has been revealed that erica Samuels: Each house has a different appetite for risk and adapts its strategy based on the specific estate or group of works. So I think comparing things side by side is tricky because it’s not apples to apples. While guaranteeing lots significantly affects outcome variations, there is clear demand right now for artists like Warhol (Flowers, Soup Can), Celmins, and Albers in postwar, as well as Tanguy, Kandinsky, and Monet in the modern.

Recent findings suggest that danielle Forest Brandi: The IB market is relatively saturated at the moment, and there is a lot of competition. To be in the game, you need to be quick, decisive, and on the inside track. Collectors are clearly enjoying the ability to gain a competitive advantage through the IB mechanism, and it shapes behavior enormously. We find that collectors who don’t get the IB early on may not bid without it. That remarked, this season highlighted us that when the guarantees are set too high, it can take the energy out of the room and make it harder for works to inspire competitive bidding.

Evan Beard: Elevated estimates repel potential bids. Blaquier estimates crowded out potential bids. Most works get auctioned three times in a season: the auction between the houses for the material; the auction among potential guarantors; and, finally, the auction among the general public. The first two auctions erode the margin for the houses. Keeping estimates low while winning consignments is always a tricky dance.

It has been revealed that liz Sterling: I think a lot of works found their market this season regardless of where the presale estimate was set. Where presale estimates were overly aggressive, the auction houses worked hard to match reserves to bids and get works sold. Conversely, works that were conservatively estimated, such as the Aaron Douglas and Edward Hopper at Sotheby’s, saw competition and achieved prices well in excess of their presale estimates.

Nina del Rio: The markets for the high-profile works this season, which were largely European Impressionist and modern, and American Abstract Expressionist, are all relatively mature, and the top end of the artists’ prices have largely been realized. Couple this with full estimates (which the auction houses had to offer sellers to win the business) and it leaves little room for price discovery, even for the exceedingly rare Blaquier van Gogh. I believe this affected competition for the van Gogh, the entire Blaquier sale, and several other highlights across all the auction houses. However, I can’t help but wonder whether the last-minute claim on the van Gogh took other bidders out of the mix? Just a thought … and certainly not unique to me.

In a recent development, maria Brito: I think we’re reading too much into the number of bidders. In 2022, when the global art market reached its second-highest level in history, Orchard with Cypresses from the Paul Allen collection sold for $117.2 million with exactly three bidders. This week’s van Gogh also attracted three bidders, but fetched $190.4 million, more than 60 percent above the previous record. I consider Châtaigniers en fleurs a substantially better painting. Yes, it hammered at $165 million, below Sotheby’s $180 million expectation, but that doesn’t make the result disappointing. We also cannot ignore the Nazi-era provenance controversy, settled barely a day before the auction. Whether it deterred potential bidders is impossible to know. At this level of the market, buyers have extraordinary financial resources, but they still have their own ideas about value.

Sources indicate that allan Schwartzman: It brought a huge price. There were few bids because the auction houses sometimes have to stretch to get headline material. Many works that are competitively offered sell on few bids because the auction houses have already conducted an auction between themselves to get those works. This does not necessarily reflect a thin market.

Erica Samuels: I enjoy the philosophical roundabout of auction house estimates. There are different categories: the attractive, come-hither estimate; the punchy estimate; the estimate right in line with the market. It’s pretty nuanced, and you have to be careful about reading too much into them. An estimate is not a price tag. It’s a range, a suggestion. And to a certain extent, there’s a part of the auction world you only understand if you’re out there seeing a lot of things. Some artworks couldn’t sell privately, so they come to auction to try a broader audience. They may not find a buyer at any estimate.

In a recent development, but let’s be honest, it’s quite thin at the top. $190 million is a lot of money for any buyer to spend. No wonder it sold to a very seasoned professional.

Danielle Forest Brandi: The discrepancy between headline prices and estimates was a defining feature of the week. In recent years, the houses drove down estimates in an effort to revive competition, but it seems that pricing has crept back up. The thinness we saw with bidding was more indicative of these ambitious estimates than the market overall.

Evan Beard: From 2020 to 2023, we were in a pretty intense risk-on speculation cycle where emerging contemporary was hot, so a lot of that material got flipped to auction. We’re in a risk-off cycle now. So very few emerging contemporary lots need to go to auction because they are easily accessible privately. The pendulum will swing back, albeit for a upcoming group of hot young artists.

Nina del Rio: There was a lot of American postwar and contemporary material on the market last week, but few really great, major examples in the auctions. Rothko, de Kooning, Richter, Frankenthaler, and Stella (among others) were well represented with fully priced seven- and eight-figure examples, but nothing outstanding or market-making vs. the great European material on offer. (It is worth noting that women artists from the same postwar period performed better, but were still uneven—Kusama and Agnes Martin in particular.).

Industry observers note that de Kooning and Rothko are interesting case studies. While de Kooning was well represented, especially by the 1975 oil from the Hedreen collection at Sotheby’s and the Whitney’s de Kooning at Christie’s, both were fully estimated, and neither had the potential to push the artist’s market. Four Rothko paintings on paper were offered last week; one very good example sold well above estimate, and the other three examples, which were arguably not as strong, sold below estimate or were unsold.

Maria Brito: The contemporary market is suffering from serious fatigue. There are simply too many artists, too much work being produced, and too much material circulating. Collectors have become desensitized and increasingly skeptical, particularly after watching so many artists’ prices rise dramatically and then collapse.

Compare that with Impressionism and modern art, where the universe of historically established artists is considerably smaller and the supply of important works is finite. There’s a level of scarcity and historical validation that gives buyers confidence. Contemporary art is a much more crowded field, and there’s simply not enough demand to support everyone at the prices we’ve become accustomed to seeing.

Recent findings suggest that of course, exceptional artists and exceptional works will continue to command extraordinary prices, but collectors are increasingly questioning what’s genuinely worth pursuing. And I think that’s healthy.

Allan Schwartzman: The fever for hot young artists at auction is over for now. This is for several reasons: prices for most young artists for whom there was hysterical (read: speculative) buying at auction could not and did not live up to such lofty numbers; a fresh generation of artists has not emerged for which the market has the same speculative appetite (and that’s a good thing); and sometimes the market for young artists is very different at auction than at the galleries that represent artists and develop those markets.

According to recent reports, erica Samuels: Collectors are looking for historical depth far more than in previous seasons. A new wave of younger collectors is definitely entering the market. They are educated, critical thinkers who ask great questions, which makes for an exciting path forward. They want collections that move across generations of artists, not just one moment.

Sources indicate that danielle Forest Brandi: With some exceptions, the contemporary offering across both houses this season felt a bit tired in [the] view [of myself and my colleagues at Beaumont Nathan], especially in comparison to the Impressionist and modern. The depth and cultural authority of blue-chip modernism seems to offer an assuring sense of solidity and substance that is perhaps universally needed right now. Of course, tastes change, the market moves on, and in time the contemporary world will come back as that market continues to recalibrate. But in the meantime, it seems to us that collectors are more interested in the past than the present.

Evan Beard: Anyone who tells you that Gen Z is driving the art market in any meaningful way is either misinformed or trying to sell you a dream. The market is driven by a group of private equity, hedge fund, real estate, and tech entrepreneurs in their 50s and 60s. It’s the usual suspects. And each new year a few new massive buyers enter the fray. New wealth grows up and ultimately buys art. But young collectors are not a major driver at auction at the moment. How many Gen Z collectors are on the ARTnews Top 200 collectors list? Or the Forbes 400 list? Or any list? Their day is coming, though. We’ve got some young risers we’re working with.

Nina del Rio: I would be guessing if I had an answer to this question. Here’s what I know: buyer access to great quality primary market material, while still difficult and often reserved for museums, is less difficult than in the past. I believe the younger collectors are buying great material from galleries (as they should). There is less great material by living artists at auction, and the speculation in that auction market has quieted considerably.

It has been revealed that maria Brito: I didn’t see clear evidence of a major generational shift this week, but the reality is that we rarely know who the ultimate buyers are. So much of the bidding happens through advisors, dealers, and telephone representatives. The auction houses know who’s behind those paddles. The rest of us are largely guessing. There’s certainly growing participation from younger collectors, but we should distinguish between buying a $20,000 print and bidding $100 million on a painting. Younger wealth is entering the market, but I haven’t seen compelling evidence from this week’s sales that it has fundamentally changed who’s buying at the very top.

Allan Schwartzman: I was not in the room to observe live bidding.

Recent findings suggest that danielle Forest Brandi: Impressionism was clearly the dominant category during the November sales, across different levels of the market. We are seeing a growing generation of collectors who are engaged with this area and other historical categories, seeking established blue-chip names or, the inverse, overlooked artists in history like the female Surrealists. Prices across many historical areas of the market have come down from where they once were, making accessible entry points for newer collectors who want to buy things that have substance and will hold up over time.
