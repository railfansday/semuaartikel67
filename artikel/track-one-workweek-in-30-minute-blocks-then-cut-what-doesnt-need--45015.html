Track One Workweek in 30-Minute Blocks – Then Cut What Doesn’t Need You

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a recent development, keep a log of one normal workweek, written every half hour, to see where your hours go before you try to fit more in. Then hand off or drop the work that doesn’t serve your top priorities, and book calendar time for the work that does, while your week still has open hours.

When long days still leave you behind on your top priorities, staying later only adds hours of the same mix of work.

Recent findings suggest that you win time back by dropping tasks, not by doing all of them faster, and memory is a poor guide to which tasks to drop. Looking back on a week, you’ll remember the long meetings and the significant deadline, not the five-minute favors, status emails, and file searches that filled the time in between.

So log the week as it happens. Pick a week without a holiday or a deadline crunch, and every 30 minutes write down what you did in the half hour before, interruptions included. A notebook works, or use Clockify, a time tracker whose free strategy times each task and breaks the week down in a report.

On Friday, sort every entry into three groups: work only you can do, work someone else could do, and work nobody needs. Hand the second group to a teammate or a direct report, or automate it with an email template or a scheduled report. Stop doing the third, after you check with your manager that no one relies on it.

In a recent development, take a manager whose log demonstrates nine hours a week in status meetings, including two hour-long weekly check-ins where people only share updates. She replaces those two hours of meetings with a shared written update that takes each person 15 minutes a week to post and read, so she and every attendee get an hour and 45 minutes back each week.

Before you leave on Friday, book time on next week’s calendar for your top two or three priorities, so latest meetings get scheduled around those blocks.
