Latest Update: Sotheby’s, Blaquier-Arrieta Family Reach Deal With Fischer Heirs, Clearing Way for $180 M. Van Gogh Sale

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sotheby’s has reached an agreement with the heirs of a Jewish publisher who challenged the sale of a $180 million Vincent van Gogh painting last week, clearing the way for the picture to go under the hammer Wednesday night.

The auction house noted Tuesday that the Blaquier-Arrieta family, which has owned van Gogh’s Châtaigniers en fleurs (1890) since 1975, reached a settlement with all the legal descendants of Hedwig and Samuel Fischer. The terms weren’t disclosed, but Sotheby’s confirmed to ARTnews that the agreement resolves the ownership claims and that the painting’s buyer will receive clear title. The deal brings an end to a messy public brawl that erupted last Thursday in the pages of the New York Times, just six days before the painting was due to lead Sotheby’s $450 million sale of the Blaquier-Arrieta collection.

Industry observers note that as the Times first reported, the Fischer heirs argued that their family had been forced to sell the painting in Amsterdam in 1936 because of financial pressures brought on by Nazi persecution. Their Berlin lawyer, Gunnar Schnabel, demanded Sotheby’s withdraw the work from sale unless it could establish that the transaction had been voluntary.

In a recent development, sotheby’s disputed that account. As ARTnews reported on Sunday the auction house noted records from the archives of the art dealer Paul Cassirer highlighted that the painting sold for 68,000 Swiss francs, or approximately $22,000, in June 1936. Sotheby’s maintained that this was a fair price for a van Gogh at the time, and that the picture had been in Amsterdam for nearly three years before its sale.

Industry observers note that the timing of the claim raised eyebrows among several art-market professionals who spoke to ARTnews under the condition of anonymity. Some saw it as a pressure tactic, arriving when Sotheby’s had little time to respond and plenty to lose if the picture was withdrawn. The auction house had guaranteed the work for an undisclosed sum and spent months promoting it as the star of one of the season’s biggest sales.

Judd Grossman, a New York art lawyer who isn’t involved in the dispute, warned that recent changes to the HEAR Act, which governs Nazi-era restitution claims, could encourage more of what he called “gamesmanship by claimants waiting to the very last minute to come forward before seeking their slice of the pie.” (As part of a separate ongoing lawsuit, lawyers for the Republic of Austria, the Albertina Museum, and the Leopold Museum Foundation have labeled the updated HEAR Act unconstitutional.).

Whether the Fischer heirs received any financial compensation under the agreement is unclear. Nor does the settlement establish whether the painting was sold under duress in 1936. In Tuesday’s announcement, Sotheby’s stated the agreement recognized both the painting’s complicated history and the persecution suffered by the Fischer family under the Nazis.

The Fischer heirs welcomed the settlement, thanking Sotheby’s and the Blaquier-Arrieta family for what they called a “trusting and fair negotiation” that acknowledged their family’s suffering and honored Gisela Bermann Fischer’s long history of pursuing restitution.

Industry observers note that bermann Fischer, who died in 2014, had previously pursued restitution for artworks lost by her family during the Nazi era. In 2007, she declined to oppose the sale of a Paul Cezanne work that had passed through the same Amsterdam dealer, while expressly reserving her right to challenge other sales.

The Blaquier-Arrieta family also described the settlement positively, saying in a statement it looked forward to “an extraordinary result and to passing along this masterpiece to a new generation of collectors.”.
