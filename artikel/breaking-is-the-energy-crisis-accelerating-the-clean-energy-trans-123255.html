Breaking: Is the Energy Crisis Accelerating the Clean Energy Transition? 4 Takeaways from the IEA’s World Energy Outlook 2026

Energy expert Nick Wayth unpacks findings from this year’s report—and what needs to happen next.

Industry observers note that the International Energy Agency has published its World Energy Outlook 2026 amid an unprecedented global energy crisis that is reshaping how countries think about energy security. The report finds that the push to reduce dependence on imported fossil fuels is accelerating electrification and the shift toward domestic energy sources, including renewables. Yet electricity demand is surging, grids are struggling to keep pace, and concerns about energy security are also giving coal a longer life in some countries. The IEA warns that the pursuit of energy security alone won’t deliver the emissions reductions needed to meet global climate goals.

What do these findings mean for the energy transition, and where should policymakers focus their efforts? Nick Wayth, global director of the WRI Polsky Center for the Global Energy Transition, explains four key takeaways from this year’s report—and what needs to happen next.

In a recent development, Q: How close will current and proposed policies get us to the COP31 pledge to raise electricity’s share of final energy consumption to 35% by 2035? What changes are needed to bridge the gap?

The crisis in the Strait of Hormuz has reinforced the case for electrification as a way for countries to diversify their power sources and reduce dependence on imports, improving energy security and affordability. While the world’s reliance on fossil fuels hasn’t changed substantially, there’s been a real shift in how countries think about the risks of that dependence—and the benefits of electrification. But progress is too slow. The IEA projects that under current and proposed policies, electricity will account for only 29% of final energy consumption by 2035. That’s only half the increase needed to reach the COP31 target of 35%.

Recent findings suggest that much of the technology is already available. The IEA estimates that nearly half of fossil fuel demand in road transport and more than a third of demand for low- and medium-temperature industrial heat could be electrified at a competitive cost today. The challenge is creating the conditions for businesses and households to make that switch.

To reach the target, countries need to develop detailed aims for shifting transport, heating, and heavy industry toward electricity and invest in transmission and distribution. Governments can help keep their industries competitive by making it more attractive for businesses to switch from gas to affordable electricity. Some countries are doing this successfully already. Germany, for example, has reduced electricity taxes for manufacturers and has subsidized grid charges, helping lower electricity costs for businesses.

Recent findings suggest that Q: The IEA projects rapid growth in electricity demand from both air conditioning and data centers. How do the challenges posed by these two sources of demand differ?

Sources indicate that energy demand from AI and data centers has dominated public attention this year, but by 2035 the global growth in electricity demand from cooling is forecast to be about 70% greater than from data centers. Notably, the IEA finds that global demand for cooling buildings is overtaking demand for heating them for the first time in history.

Energy demand from data centers is concentrated in the U.S., China, and the EU. By contrast, demand for cooling is widespread, especially in emerging and developing economies where much of the population is vulnerable to extreme heat, an increasing threat to human life.

Under current and proposed government policies, cooling is projected to account for 12% of global electricity consumption by 2035. But during periods of peak demand, when millions of air conditioners are running at once, it could reach around 30% of the electricity needed. Electricity grids must be able to cope with these spikes in demand, even if they occur only occasionally. Without better planning and more efficient cooling, countries could face costly grid upgrades—or risk power outages during extreme heat.

Industry observers note that this is why policymakers and utilities need to anticipate future electricity demand and blueprint investments in grid infrastructure accordingly. But building more isn’t the only solution. Governments and utilities should also invest in energy storage and other grid flexibility measures that enable them to adapt to fluctuating supply and demand as needed.

It has been revealed that Q: The energy crisis is accelerating clean electrification in some countries but also encouraging greater reliance on fossil fuels in others. What does the IEA’s outlook tell us about the future of oil, coal, and gas—and the risks of investing in fresh fossil fuel infrastructure?

The report shows that global oil demand is expected to level off by 2030 under current and proposed policies. The slowdown in China’s oil demand, driven largely by electric vehicle adoption, is expected to continue. Other fast-growing economies, such as India, despite electrifying transport, are nonetheless projected to increase their demand for oil.

Recent findings suggest that the current energy crisis is reinforcing these differences in strategy. Some countries are investing in electrification to reduce dependence on imported fossil fuels, while others are turning to domestic fossil fuels.

The energy crisis has given coal demand a short-term boost, and the IEA now expects coal use in 2035 to be higher than it projected just a year ago. Even so, under current and proposed government policies, global coal demand is expected to fall by 14% between 2025 and 2035, driven largely by a decline in coal-fired power generation, particularly in China. But coal use in industry is projected to fall by just 5%, highlighting the challenge of reducing dependence on the fuel.

Countries need to be cautious about investing in infrastructure that may not be needed. The IEA estimates that about 400 billion cubic meters (bcm) of fresh liquefied natural gas (LNG) export capacity is under construction and could come online by 2035. Yet under current and stated government policies, export capacity could significantly exceed demand in 2035 by70 bcm. That surplus raises the risk of underused infrastructure and investments that fail to pay off.

The IEA estimates that the world will need at least 25 million kilometers of new power lines over the next decade to meet growing electricity demand and connect new sources of power, including renewables. Another 15 million kilometers of existing lines will need to be upgraded or replaced. Both will require substantial investment.

The biggest challenge is delivering these changes fast enough. Major grid projects can take 5 to15 years to strategy, secure permits for, and complete—far longer than many renewable energy projects. Governments need to speed up permitting and grid connections, improve coordination between utilities and developers, and encourage investment before demand materializes. One encouraging example is the EU’s recent provisional agreement to streamline permitting for grid projects.

Equally crucial is to invest in ways to use existing assets more efficiently. Grid-enhancing technologies can speed up connections by allowing existing power lines to carry more electricity, reducing the need to build new infrastructure. And increasingly, governments and utilities are seeing the importance of making grids more flexible—building in capability and strategies to balance supply with demand in real time. Brazil, for example, pays large electricity consumers to temporarily reduce consumption when the grid is under strain. Businesses that fail to deliver the agreed reductions can lose payments or face suspension from programs. By combining energy storage, demand-response programs, smart grid controls, distributed energy generation, and other flexibility strategies, energy planners can meet growing demands and increase resilience while reducing the need to build more grid infrastructure.
