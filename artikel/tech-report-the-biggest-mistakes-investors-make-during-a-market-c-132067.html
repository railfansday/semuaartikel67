Sources indicate that when the stock market starts falling, investors face a difficult test. A 10% decline can rapidly become 20%. Headlines grow increasingly negative, and even experienced investors begin to question their decisions.

It has been revealed that but market crashes also create opportunities. Some excellent businesses become available at prices investors could only dream of during a bull market. So why do many investors fail to take advantage?

Often, it comes down to three mistakes: trying to predict the bottom, selling because of fear, and buying cheap stocks without understanding why they are cheap.

Recent findings suggest that one of the biggest mistakes investors make is waiting for the perfect moment to buy. They want the market to fall further before investing. Then, when prices decline again, they become convinced that an even bigger crash is coming.

Eventually, the market recovers, and they miss the opportunity altogether.

The problem is that nobody knows where the bottom is until it has already passed. Consider an investor who wants to buy shares in a company trading at $100. The stock falls to $80, but the investor waits for $60. It then rebounds to $95.

The investor was right that the stock had become cheaper, but waiting for the perfect price meant missing the opportunity.

Industry observers note that A better approach is to focus on valuation rather than market timing. Decide what a business is worth and identify the price at which it offers an attractive potential return. If the shares reach that level, consider buying a portion of your intended position. If the price falls further and the investment case remains intact, you can consider adding more.

This approach, often called buying in stages, removes the pressure to get the timing exactly right.

Watching a portfolio lose value is uncomfortable. When a stock falls 30%, investors naturally wonder whether they should sell before things get worse. Unfortunately, fear can make a temporary decline feel like a permanent disaster.

The important question is not how much the share price has fallen. It is whether the business has deteriorated.

Industry observers note that imagine owning shares in Amazon (AMZN -3.12%). During a recession, consumers may spend less, businesses may become more cautious, and technology stocks may fall sharply. But before selling, examine what is happening inside the firm.

It has been revealed that is AWS still attracting customers? Is Amazon maintaining its competitive position? Are consumers still buying things online? If the long-term investment case remains intact, a falling share price alone may not justify selling.

Of course, this does not mean holding every stock through every downturn. Sometimes a falling share price reflects genuine problems: Customers are leaving, competitors are gaining ground, debt is becoming difficult to manage, or management has made poor decisions.

So what to do? Whenever a stock you own falls sharply, revisit your original reasons for buying it. Compare the latest business performance with your expectations.

If the investment thesis is broken, consider selling. If the business remains healthy and the valuation is more attractive, holding or adding may make sense.

A market crash can make almost everything look like a bargain. But some stocks fall for good reasons.

A manufacturer might trade at just 5 times earnings, while its competitors trade at 20 times. At first glance, it looks cheap. However, what if its sales are declining, its debt is rising, and customers are moving to competitors?

The low valuation may be justified because the business is losing its ability to generate profits. This is known as a value trap: A stock looks inexpensive, but its underlying business continues to deteriorate.

According to recent reports, in short, don't buy a stock just because it has fallen 50%. Understand why it fell, examine its financial health, and decide whether the problem is temporary or structural.

Nobody knows exactly when the next market crash will happen, how far prices will fall, or when the recovery will begin. Trying to predict all three is an unnecessary burden.

Instead, investors should focus on what they can control: understanding the businesses they own, knowing their value, and maintaining sufficient financial flexibility to capitalize on opportunities.

A market crash can be frightening, but it does not have to be destructive. The biggest winners are not necessarily investors who buy at the absolute bottom. They are often those who avoid panic, recognize genuine bargains, and have the patience to let good businesses compound over time.
