Chris Van Hollen and Chuck Schumer represent conflicting visions on concern that has become ‘litmus test’ for Democratic voters.

The race to lead the Democratic Party’s incoming Senate majority has become the latest battlefield between two conflicting visions for how the US should relate to Israel – a once unquestioned ally whose collapsing standing among the US public has become a defining feature of American politics.

Industry observers note that chuck Schumer, the veteran Latest York Senator and current Democratic minority leader, is one of Israel’s most reliable supporters in Congress. As he views it, according to an interview he gave last year, his “job” is to “keep the left pro-Israel”.

Sources indicate that on the other side is Chris Van Hollen, the Maryland senator and challenger to Schumer for Senate leadership, who in recent years emerged as a steadfast advocate for Palestinian rights in the Senate. He supports sanctions, voted to block sales of weapons to Israel, and advocates for the families of US citizens killed by Israeli soldiers and settlers, as well as against the detention of Palestinian children by the Israeli military.

In a recent development, the two represent two opposing sides of the Democratic party’s approach to Israel at a time when a majority of Democrats hold unfavorable views of it. Van Hollen has repeatedly visited the occupied West Bank. In January 2024 he traveled with Oregon’s senator Jeff Merkley to the Rafah border crossing between Egypt and Gaza, where they denounced Israel’s restrictions on humanitarian aid.

While he has in recent years criticized Israeli prime minister Benjamin Netanyahu and called him a “major obstacle” to peace, Schumer has been an unflinching supporter of Israel throughout his political career, repeatedly referring to himself as “Shomer Yisrael” or “guardian of Israel”. Earlier this year, he said he views it as part of his job to fight for “all the aid that Israel needs”.

It has been revealed that he has opposed the Iran nuclear deal, the boycott, divestment and sanctions movement, and a UN resolution affirming that Israeli settlements in occupied territory are illegal, and supported the US Embassy move to Jerusalem. During the war in Gaza, he has repeatedly voted against measures seeking to restrict military aid and weapons sales to Israel. He did, however, vote in favor of a failed resolution that would have required the state department to produce a report on Israeli human rights abuses in the West Bank, and has supported efforts to impose sanctions on individual settlers.

Schumer’s office did not respond to a request for comment. In a statement, Van Hollen’s office mentioned that “the Israeli government has become increasingly hardline in its approach, diminishing the possibility of a two-state solution, including through its indiscriminate attacks on Gaza and its complicity in the escalating violence in the West Bank”.

“The senator has urged the United States to use its leverage to push the government of Israel to comply with US policy and law.”.

While the Senate leader has ample discretion to set the party’s legislative priorities and move bills to the floor for votes, he would not necessarily be able to influence the outcome of specific votes – or foreign policy – especially for the remainder of Trump’s term.

It has been revealed that van Hollen has stressed that his challenge to Schumer is about delivering the “change from the status quo” that drove historic numbers of US voters to elect Democrats earlier this month. He has suggested he has the votes of 15 colleagues, but so far, only five have publicly pledged to back him including Michigan’s newly elected Abdul El-Sayed, an outspoken critic of Israel, Merkley, Vermont’s Bernie Sanders, Minnesota’s Peggy Flanagan and Michigan’s Elissa Slotkin. Several others have criticized Schumer and called for change but not committed to a vote.

While he is clearly the underdog in the race, Van Hollen insists he has a “path” to win. Whether or not he succeeds in his bid to oust Schumer, the race has significantly raised his national profile as an advocate for Palestinian rights.

Sources indicate that outside the Senate, advocacy groups’ endorsements are largely following divides on Israel. Bend the Arc, a progressive Jewish group, noted that “the midterm elections proved that a majority of Americans are hungry for change and American Jews are no exception”. The Institute for Middle Eastern Understanding’s Policy Project cited Schumer’s record as the only current Democratic senator to vote against the Iran nuclear deal as “paving the way” for the current war in Iran.

“Senator Van Hollen is committed to rights-respecting policy and understands that accountability is necessary to comprehensively challenge impunity,” mentioned Brad Parker, associate director of policy at the Center for Constitutional Rights.

“The recent electoral lesson over the past year is that incumbents who ignore the overwhelming and shifting electorate demands to end US complicity in Israeli crimes operate at their own peril.”.

Last week, dozens of protesters with the Sunrise Movement, a youth climate organization, were arrested outside Schumer’s Washington office, where they called on the senator to “step up or step aside”.

It has been revealed that but Schumer – a titan of the party and the highest-ranking Jewish elected official in US history – has much of the political establishment behind him. He also has the backing of major pro-Israel groups.

In a recent development, “Jewish Americans have been a critical component of a winning Democratic coalition, including in the 2026 midterms,” said Halie Soifer, the Jewish Democratic Council of America’s CEO, which endorsed him. “We’re confident that he’ll lead Democrats to deliver for the American people in a way that is consistent with the values of the Jewish community in the 120th Congress.”.

On election night, before Van Hollen announced his challenge, Aipac also issued a statement congratulating Schumer and incoming House speaker Hakeem Jeffries for their caucuses’ wins.

It has been revealed that despite significant shifts in rhetoric, Democratic officials have moved more slowly than their base. Earlier this year, 103 House Democrats voted to slash military aid to Israel – an unprecedented rebuke, but still less than half the party’s caucus (only one Republican voted in favor). The Senate has been even more cautious.

While he has tried to focus on other complications since announcing his challenge, Van Hollen did reference his differences with Schumer on Israel – most notably when he accused him of having “joined with Aipac and crypto” to try to defeat Michigan’s El-Sayed. Schumer favored El-Sayed’s Aipac-backed opponent, Haley Stevens, during the primary, but he supported El-Sayed in the November general election. In an interview on Fox News on Sunday, Van Hollen spoke about growing settler violence in the West Bank, and the Israeli’s government complicity in it. “I don’t think American taxpayer dollars should be going to that purpose,” he said.

“When I first spoke out about Gaza and what I thought – the bad conduct in Gaza – I was a pretty lonely voice, frankly,” Van Hollen also remarked in an interview last year.

In a recent development, when he first ran for Senate a decade ago, Van Hollen was backed by some Aipac-aligned donors and regularly voted with most centrist Democrats on matters relating to Israel. But he has also had a longstanding interest in foreign policy, human rights, and the Middle East, and in his decade in the Senate he has frequently invoked the “Leahy law”, which demands accountability for abuses by US-supported foreign militaries – including Israel’s.

His criticism of Israel has grown more forceful over time. Following the 2022 West Bank killing of the Palestinian American journalist Shireen Abu Akleh, by an Israeli sniper, Van Hollen repeatedly met with her family and led the congressional response to the killing, aggressively pressing the Biden administration for accountability. (To this day, there has been none, and despite Van Hollen’s repeated efforts, an official report on Abu Akleh’s killing remains classified). Van Hollen’s office remarked that Abu Akleh’s killing “underscored for him the impunity with which the Israeli government was increasingly acting with regard to the West Bank”.

In recent years, he has also championed the families of other US citizens killed by settlers and soldiers – including Rachel Corrie, Ayşenur Ezgi Eygi, and Sayfollah Musallet. He also met with Mahmoud Khalil, the Columbia University student that has become the face of the Trump administration’s crackdown on pro-Palestinian speech, shortly after Khalil was released from ICE detention. (When he was arrested, by contrast, Schumer issued a statement saying that he “abhor[s]” Khalil’s views, though he added that if the Trump administration could not prove Khalil had committed a crime, his speech was protected by the US constitution.).

Recent findings suggest that the contest is the latest proxy war over the future of the US-Israel relationship. It played an outsized role in mayoral elections in New York and Los Angeles, and defined heated Democratic primaries. It is dividing Republicans, and turning groups like Aipac into a liability for candidates.

Kidus Girma, campaign director for the Sunrise Movement, told the Guardian that while the speaker majority vote was about whether the Senate would respond to voters’ calls for change from tackling climate action to getting money out of politics, support for Palestine had become “a litmus test problem for Democratic voters”.
