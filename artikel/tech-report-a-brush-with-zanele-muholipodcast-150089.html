Industry observers note that muholi was born in 1972 in Umlazi, Durban, South Africa and lives today in Cape Town. They are a self-described visual activist, who, since the mid-2000s, have used photography to document the lives of LGBTQ+ people, initially in South Africa, where they were born.

Zanele Muholi, Diana Cristina Nascimento Ramos, Salvador, 2025, from Faces and Phases, ed. by Renée Mussai (MACK, 2026).

Courtesy of the artist and MACK.

They have since expanded their project around the world and work in discrete, often ongoing series that have a common aim: to respond to violence and discrimination against queer and trans communities, to reflect their defiance and resistance, and to expand their visibility in visual culture in South Africa in particular but also far beyond.

In a recent development, zanele Muholi, Ashara Ekundayo, Venice, 2026, from Faces and Phases, ed. by Renée Mussai (MACK, 2026).

Courtesy of the artist and MACK.

Sources indicate that the people they depict have, since the start, been far more than sitters or subjects; they are collaborators, participants who help define the way they are represented. A crucial factor is that Muholi turns the lens onto themself. “When I position myself in my own frame,” they have noted. “I do so on my own terms attempting to articulate my own way of seeing and wanting to be seen.”.

Industry observers note that zanele Muholi, Christine Madonsela, Daveyton, Gauteng, 2017, from Faces and Phases, ed. by Renée Mussai (MACK, 2026).

Courtesy of the artist and MACK.

And whether it is their unflinching and tender portraits of their collaborators or their imaginative and improvised self-portraits Muholi’s photographs are always beautiful objects, with a fastidious attention to the properties and possibilities of their medium.

In a recent development, zanele Muholi, Mantedieng ‘Mantis’ Mamabolo-Cunningham, Parktown, Johannesburg, 2018, from Faces and Phases, ed. by Renée Mussai (MACK, 2026).

Courtesy of the artist and MACK.

In a recent development, they discuss the influence of their mother, who was a maker herself, and was a profound influence on one of their most notable works, Somnyama Ngonyama (2012). They reflect on the enormous impact of photographers including Rotimi Fani-Kayode, Lola Flash and Joan E. Biren, writers including Audre Lorde, and musicians including Brenda Fassie. Plus, they answer our usual questions, giving insight into their daily practices and the rhythms of their work, and reflecting on the ultimate question: what is art for?
