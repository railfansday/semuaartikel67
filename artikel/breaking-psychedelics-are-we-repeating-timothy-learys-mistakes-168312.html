Posted November 27, 2026 | Reviewed by Tyler Woods.

Today's psychedelic renaissance traces back to Timothy Leary, the colorful Harvard psychologist whose slogan, “Turn on, tune in, drop out,” became a prescription for the counterculture.

Today, in an era of ubiquitous artificial intelligence (AI) and brain studies, looking to the 1960s for answers seems almost quaint. But history has its uses, especially if we seem determined to repeat it.

Professors Leary and Alpert led the Harvard Psilocybin Project, highlighting potential therapeutic and mystical applications of psychedelics like psilocybin for treating addiction, anxiety, and depression.

According to recent reports, but advocacy increasingly overshadowed research. Leary's Concord Prison Experiment suggested psilocybin reduced criminal recidivism. Later evaluations—like a 34-year follow-up of the Concord Prison Experiment—found claims of lasting treatment effects were erroneous.

By 1963, Harvard dismissed Leary. He was obsessed with personal intoxication and circumventing both student safeguards and academic caution. His counterculture advocacy helped trigger a strong cultural backlash and subsequent psychedelic criminalization.

Today’s psychedelic trials avoid Leary’s mistakes by emphasizing strict screening, clinical oversight, and controlled settings.

And modern psychiatry has great hopes for psychedelics. Yet psychedelic trials face distinctive challenges. For example, patients know when they receive the active drug, and their expectations influence outcomes. Therapists also may be true believers.

Industry observers note that meanwhile, the RAND Psychedelics Survey estimates that roughly 11 million American adults used psilocybin in the past year. But support is greatest for FDA approval. Approval for psilocybin among military veterans was only 24% for general legal use but 54% for FDA-approved therapy. Yet passing legislation, opening psychedelic centers, and obtaining FDA approval are very different accomplishments.

Oregon voters approved the measure, and psilocybin centers opened in 2023. Adults 21 years and older (including out-of-state visitors) may access services with no medical diagnosis, prescription, or referral.

Unlike a retail cannabis dispensary, clients can’t buy takeout mushrooms. Instead, services require a mandatory preparation session, on-site administration of up to 50 mg of psilocybin under the supervision of a state-licensed facilitator, and an optional follow-up integration session.

Sources indicate that an August 2026 JAMA Network Open study of 346 participants at 24 licensed centers by P. Todd Korthuis, MD, and colleagues offers a landmark real-world dataset and cautionary tale for policymakers.

Participants described improvements in depression, anxiety, PTSD symptoms, well-being, and life satisfaction. But there was no control group, randomization, or representative patient population.

Also, 4 participants (1.2%), psychedelic novices, experienced serious behavioral adverse reactions requiring emergency care.

However, 3 of the 4 participants who experienced serious adverse reactions nevertheless described their experience as highly meaningful.

Sources indicate that meaningful? Certainly. Therapeutic? Not necessarily.

In a recent development, A separate WUSTL mystery-shopper study found that centers took screening seriously. I previously reviewed this study in detail.

It has been revealed that although legally framed around general personal growth and wellness, Oregon data show the vast majority of clients seek services for specific mental health struggles. According to the OPS 2025 Year in Review, the top reported reasons for seeking psilocybin were anxiety, depression, PTSD, and other trauma.

Three months from treatment, 4.5% of participants reported persistent/worsening depression, 1.9% reported thoughts of death or suicide, and 8.7% reported symptoms suggesting possible hallucinogen-persisting perception disorder (HPPD).

A recent NPR report highlighted severe, lingering psychological destabilization in some patients following psychedelic clinical trials. Yet post-psychedelic risks, ranging from panic attacks to suicidal ideation and dissociation, are frequently overlooked.

Psychiatrist and neuroscientist David Rabin wrote in the Los Angeles Times that roughly half his clinical time is spent helping patients recover from challenging drug experiences. Some patients are frightened or disconnected. Others believe they discovered universal truths. As Rabin put it, “Certainty is a hell of a drug.”.

Sources indicate that patients may suffer from persistent anxiety, perceptual disturbances, psychiatric deterioration, or destabilization of their understanding of themselves and reality. Prior screening before taking psychedelics cannot accurately identify everyone at risk.

It has been revealed that in addiction medicine, we learned long ago that successful detoxification isn’t the same as recovery from addiction. Similarly, completing a psychedelic session and not experiencing an emergency isn’t itself evidence of long-term safety.

Two upcoming neuroscience studies deserve attention.

In a recent development, gudmundsen and colleagues, reporting in Nature Communications, found distinct metabolic effects of psilocybin, LSD, and 2C-B in rats, with modest, drug-dependent changes detectable one week later. Only some changes were shared across all three drugs. A healthy person who takes 2C-B and one who takes psilocybin don’t run the same risks.

In Science Advances, Delgado-Sallent and colleagues in BU's Department of Psychological and Brain Sciences reported that psilocybin and ketamine altered decision-making strategies and orbitofrontal activity in mice. At selected doses, decisions became slower but more accurate, suggesting that psychedelics change brain-wide configurations and local dynamics to promote more deliberate, flexible, and sensory-driven decisions.

Industry observers note that neither study establishes human brain injury, and neither indicates lasting clinical benefits. But there are known and unknown risks with psychedelics. Treating severe illness justifies accepting some risk and uncertainty.

Industry observers note that psychedelics appear to change the brain. But who needs their brain changed?

Opening a window for change doesn't determine what’s actually learned in the experience. In a clinical setting, therapy shapes that window. At a party, a retreat, or with a charismatic guide, something else may climb through that window of opportunity; for example, psychedelics may unmask psychosis or mania in someone who looked and felt fine.

Insights may feel valid without being true.

Psychedelics increase emotional openness, suggestibility, and trust. These characteristics may facilitate therapy. Alternatively, sometimes they facilitate exploitation.

Undue influence and misconduct are potential problems. People in a highly suggestible state are easier to exploit.

A 2024 JAMA Network Open consensus statement led by Amy McGuire addressed informed consent, physical touch, professional boundaries, training, and accountability.

Consent to take a psychedelic is not a blank check, as far as consent is concerned.

Industry observers note that physical touch is ethically fraught in conventional psychotherapy. Add psychedelic intoxication, disorientation, heightened suggestibility, and a patient asking to be held—what could possibly go wrong?

In a recent development, patients need explicit boundaries. They also need the ongoing ability to withdraw consent, and independent channels to report misconduct.

The therapist’s good intentions are not a safeguarding protocol.

When I began studying addiction, it was considered a moral failure.

Sources indicate that changing neurotransmission is relatively easy. Producing sustained recovery is considerably harder.

Neuroplasticity is an opportunity, not a clinical outcome. Addiction itself demonstrates that experience-dependent brain plasticity is not invariably beneficial.

Consequently, so far, claims that one psychedelic session can replace years of psychotherapy exceed available evidence.

Sources indicate that six decades ago, Leary popularized psychedelic experiences before demonstrating therapeutic value. Today we know better.

Recent findings suggest that the psychedelic trip ends. The biological and psychological consequences may not. Also, if a subsequent glitch arises, who responds? Who provides treatment? Who pays? This seems unclear.

Treatment-resistant depression, trauma, PTSD, GAD, and addiction warrant innovative treatments. In contrast, existential uncertainty isn’t a condition requiring pharmacological intervention.

Turn on the science. Tune out the hype. And don't drop the patient.
