The United States has lost its measles elimination status, and has now exceeded 5,400 cases and 10 total measles-associated deaths in 2026.

The status determination from the Pan American Health Organization, which is part of the World Health Organization, was made late last week and first reported by the Wall Street Journal before being confirmed by other outlets. The public health body had planned to make the announcement public on Tuesday.

The U.S. had notched elimination status, reflecting no continuous spread of the disease for at least 12 months, in 2000. Regaining it similarly requires the country to stop continuous spread for 12 months. PAHO recommends countries that have lost elimination status submit and enact a corrective action plan that includes immunization and surveillance.

Sources indicate that the decision landed shortly after the country hit 5,412 confirmed measles cases, per a Nov. 13 upgrade from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. The federal public health body also lists 591 hospitalizations (11% rate) and six deaths—though it has been hesitant to outright confirm states' reports of measles-associated deaths, of which there have now been 10.

Health groups and public health professionals have pointed to lagging vaccination rates and checked endorsement of routine childhood vaccines from current leadership at the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, namely Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., as fueling the country's outbreaks.

The Wisconsin Department of Health Services on Thursday reported its first measles-outbreak associated death since 1990.

According to recent reports, the deceased was an infant whose case was among many in the southwestern part of the state, for whom the coroner listed measles as immediate cause of death. As of Nov. 3, the department has reported 431 cases across 13 counties in 2026, the highest number of cases in over 35 years. All but three of those are among individuals who were not vaccinated, and nine have involved hospitalizations.

According to recent reports, "Any death of a child is a tragedy and even more so when it is related to a vaccine-preventable disease," Kirsten Johnson, secretary of the Wisconsin Department of Health Services, said in the announcement. "Measles can cause serious illness and death, and it is important for all Wisconsinites to evaluate their risk and take steps to protect themselves and their family.".

Sources indicate that as of Oct. 29, the Centers for Disease Control reports 4,852 confirmed cases of measles this year across the country, far outpacing 2025's total of 2,289. Of these, 511 have been hospitalized (versus 244 in 2025) and three deaths have been recognized—though health officials in Pennsylvania alone remarked they have confirmed five measles-associated deaths. The CDC has said its death count may change after it completes work to develop a new standardized case definition for "deaths due to measles.".

The Latest York State Department of Health on Monday afternoon reported its first measles-associated death in 30 years.

Recent findings suggest that the state has counted 210 total cases this year as of Nov. 3. The reported death was a child in the northern Lewis County, according to the department. No information was given about the child's specific illness or the role measles infection may have played in their death.

According to recent reports, sixteen confirmed cases of measles have been reported in Lewis County as of Tuesday, the department said. Many of New York's cases are located among upstate counties, and just eight are in New York City, according to the department.

In a statement, Newly introduced York Governor Kathy Hochul—who on Oct. 5 declared a Disaster Emergency in response to rising measles case rates—expressed her sympathies to the child's family and told residents that "vaccination remains our best protection against this dangerous disease.".

"This is New York’s first measles-related death in 30 years, a devastating reminder of what’s at stake when Washington deliberately erodes decades of progress in protecting Americans from preventable illnesses," the statement reads, echoing criticisms of federal health leadership voiced by Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro regarding measles-associated deaths in his own state.

A spokesperson for the Department of Health and Human Services told Fierce Healthcare that it had deployed epidemiologists and other experts to New York after receiving a formal request for support. The support is presently scheduled to run through Nov. 13, and includes on-the-ground "surveillance, data analysis, strategic planning and other outbreak response support," they noted.

Of the roughly $100 million in additional measles and vaccine-preventable diseases funding the department has provided via the CDC since August, nearly $2.4 million has gone directly to Newly introduced York State and $2.1 million to New York City, they stated.

In a recent development, "Secretary Kennedy and CDC Director Dr. Erica Schwartz continue to make clear that the MMR vaccine is the most effective way to prevent measles," they added.

Industry observers note that the U.S. is in the midst of its worst measles outbreak in decades, with more than 4,000 cases reported to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention as of early October and a sixth measles-associated death disclosed by local Pennsylvania officials on Monday.

The vaccine-preventable disease was declared eliminated in the U.S. back in 2000—meaning that there was no endemic spread of the disease—but has resurfaced in force during recent years as declining childhood vaccination rates have pushed most states below the 95% coverage bar for school-age herd immunity.

The CDC had counted 2,289 cases across the entirety of 2025, which had been the country’s highest total since 1990, and several public health officials have warned for months that number would likely be surpassed based on the scale and frequency of early-year cases.

Measles-associated deaths have also accompanied the higher case counts, though just how many is a subject of dispute. While three deaths were confirmed in all of 2025, this year logged its first in late August with Pennsylvania, which hosts more than a quarter of the total cases, now reporting six measles-associated deaths as of Oct. 13. The CDC, however, lists two deaths alongside a note that its count is subject to change, and has promised more broadly to develop a upcoming, standardized case definition to be used across all reporting states.

That discrepancy is an example of friction between federal health leadership and the broader public health community that experts say has contributed to this year’s outbreaks.

Recent findings suggest that pennsylvania’s initial death announcements in August were contested by Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., who accused Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro of withholding sufficient information for the CDC to confirm the deaths and using his press conference announcing them as “gaslighting the public” for political purposes.

During an ensuing social media feud, the governor and the secretary described a heated phone call in which Shapiro (at the time) rejected federal assistance and accused Kennedy of fueling the outbreaks with only lukewarm endorsement of the MMR vaccine and a broader concurrent campaign to scale back other routine childhood vaccinations.

Numerous state public health officials and experts have lobbed similar criticisms of Kennedy, who is affiliated with anti-vaccine groups, and federal health agencies under his purview.

The CDC, for its part, has reportedly infused at least $100 million of extra public health funding to states for immunization programs and surveillance, and leading officials including Kennedy and new CDC Director Erica Schwartz, M.D., have described the MMR vaccine as safe and effective when asked directly. Still, the agency has not released the type of broader vaccine messaging and awareness campaigns that it would have in years past, critics are quick to note.

Sources indicate that pennsylvania isn’t alone in raising alarms over rising measles cases. Newly introduced York Governor Kathy Hochul declared a State Disaster Emergency in early October, the first such declaration in recent years.
