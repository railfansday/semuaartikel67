Sources indicate that over the long haul, the U.S. stock market is a wealth-creating machine. And this is exemplified by the performance of the benchmark S&P 500 (^GSPC +0.81%), which has risen by a whopping 246% over the last decade. That represents a compound annual growth rate of 13.2%, noticeably above the index's historical average of 10%.

Much recent growth has been driven by a brand-upcoming industry called generative artificial intelligence (AI), which has seen America's tech giants pour billions into compute and memory hardware for data centers.

That said, equity prices don't go up every single year. And history highlights that periods of abnormally high growth usually end with drawdowns as excitement fades and Wall Street gets a more realistic view of what the future will bring. Let's explore some historical examples to help investors understand what comes next and figure out strategies to stay ahead.

By now, you have probably heard that the cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings (CAPE) ratio is near record highs. This is a stock market valuation metric that compares the index's current prices to average inflation-adjusted earnings over the past 10 years. It smooths out the effects of short-term fluctuations, giving investors a clearer view of what the market is worth today compared with other periods in history.

Over the last 100 years, the S&P 500's CAPE ratio has spiked three main times. The first was in late 1929, when it hit 32.6 right before the stock market crash that led to the Great Depression. The second time was during the dot-com bubble in late 1999, when it hit 44.2 (the index declined by 9.1% and 11.9% in the following two years).

With a CAPE ratio of around 40, the third major spike is underway. And it is being driven by many of the same factors that sparked the dot-com bubble over two decades ago. Generative AI is promising to transform the way people live and do business. And Wall Street is pouring money into the opportunity, even though its long-term cash flow and profit-generating potential are far from established.

Industry observers note that unlike the dot-com bubble, which was led by unprofitable internet stocks, the generative AI boom is dominated by some of the world's largest and most profitable technology companies. This includes names like Nvidia and Micron, which help supply the compute and memory chips hyperscalers use to run and train large language models (LLMs).

These companies enjoy some level of safety due to their strong economic moats and high profits. These can be used to fund shareholder goodies like buybacks, which can support stock price growth. That noted, they aren't without their risks.

In a recent development, research firm Gartner has joined a growing chorus of analysts getting nervous about the AI industry. They point to the alarming gap between what hyperscalers are spending on AI infrastructure and the actual revenue generated on the consumer side of the opportunity, and expect an eye-popping $3.5 trillion impairment charge as these assets are written down to better reflect their actual value-creating potential.

Such a huge reversal in market optimism could have severe impacts on the S&P 500's performance, especially given that the top five tech giants now account for 30% of the index's total value.

Over the long term, the S&P 500 has always bounced back from corrections and crashes. That means investors who already own the index shouldn't panic sell because of the recent uncertainty. That said, the market has become unusually exposed to the vulnerable AI industry, and it may make sense to boost diversification by allocating capital to other, less exposed industries.
