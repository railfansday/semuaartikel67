Te Araroa: supermarkets are scary

Our rest day and a half in Ruakaka had been amazing. My new friends surprised me with a birthday cake (complete with sparkler candles) and we watched Lord of the Rings while it absolutely poured outside. That movie is so much more relatable when you’re thru-hiking. Someone remarked “my elevensies is an OSM bar” and Sven asked why they couldn’t hitch to Mordor. The washing machine ran non-stop and I washed my two pairs of socks and three pairs of undies about six times.

Industry observers note that but it was time to move on, those of us not taking a double zero packed up in the morning and started walking toward the beach, where we’d walk a few kilometres before a road-walk into Waipu where we’d been told we could get a free coffee. I also needed more oats and a small resupply so this was a much-needed stop. After about five minutes on the road Sven drove past us in a van, waving and yelling and I knew I should have waited for him that morning. Annabel and I decided that the road sucked so we managed to get a hitch with a nice lady into Waipu, where she dropped us off for our free coffee.

The best part of the coffee shop.

According to recent reports, sven turned up not too long after that, bemoaning that he was no longer ultralight because he had to buy a whole significant roll of K tape (nevermind that his pack weighed 20kg or 44lbs before this purchase). Back with my hitching buddy, we made quick work of getting a lift from Waipu to have a look at Waipu cove and beach and then up the worst of the hard-paved road to our next campsite. After only a couple hours of walking we had arrived, and this is where I started to think that maybe hitching was no longer for me. Or at least such big sections. I had gotten caught up in the fun of zooming past our friends laughing. I decided, sitting at camp at 1:30pm, that I wouldn’t hitch so much anymore. I wanted to earn this thing and not hitch it away.

Industry observers note that because we were so early to camp, Sven and I thought maybe we could continue to the next camp spot and cut out another day. But I knew that Charlie, Sinèad and Annabel were not far behind us and I was also really enjoying spending time with them. I also liked the vibe of a upcoming hiker who I’d met at the camp, Anica. So, unfortunately, this is where the dynamic hitching-duo split up. I stated bye to Sven and watched him disappear down the trail. I’d be lying if I said I didn’t miss my friend, but this was the right decision.

I pitched my tent and the girls arrived, then we all sat in the shelter chatting and watching storms roll past us. It was a sort of spooky experience but luckily none of them passed directly over us.

The next morning we packed up early and walked down the hill from our campsite.

According to recent reports, our aim was to get to a supermarket – the first significant one we’d seen in a while. But first we’d walk down from our camp, along the coast (with amazing views!) and through Mangawhai heads.

Black sand beach at Mangawhai heads.

We made it to Mangawhai heads by lunch and all I could think about was cheese and sundried tomatoes, so it was a real stroke of luck that the first shops we came across had a sandwich featuring exactly those ingredients. We ate lunch and then walked a few kilometres to Fresh World to do a four day resupply. I felt a bit ill from the cheese and I think that saved me from buying too much food.

I promise there are sun-dried tomatoes in there.

It’s funny resupplying on a trail day. The shop is incredibly overwhelming and tiring, and you just sort of wander the aisles hoping you’ve picked up enough stuff. We also bought a whole lot of fruits and vegetables because our bodies were starving for nutrients and fresh foods. Coming out of the shop, we spent about an hour sorting through our groceries, removing the packaging and jamming them into our food bags and then sitting on the ground drinking kombucha. We fielded several sets of questions about our walk from supermarket shoppers and staff and joked that we were like celebrities.

By the time we made it to our trail host’s place we were still an hour too early so we laid out our tents to dry on a patch of grass by the road and started stretching. In hindsight this was a very weird thing to do in a suburb and is absolutely what prompted our hosts to come out to the street early and tell us to move into their much more private garden. The trail hosts at kilometre 463 have a great set up! A cold shower, water, more charging ports than you can imagine, a major, mostly flat garden and a little cabin that sleeps two. Sinèad and I snagged beds in the cabin and then we all took turns in the very non-private shower (which doesn’t really phase any of us anymore).

I’m still thinking about this bed.

For dinner we had hot chips from the takeaways down the road and all of our fruit and vegetables. It felt so good to be there, even if we were all exhausted from the supermarket trip. I fell asleep in 0.02 seconds this night, content to be in a comfy bed and full of food.

After my glorious sleep in the cabin, I woke ready for the day. Today we’d walk the length of Pakiri beach, cross an estuary and then climb a big hill to some more trail hosts.

Anica and Charlie on Pakiri beach.

It has been revealed that by this point we were all over walking on beaches, we’d all done over 100km of beach in the first four days of Te Araroa and many more since. So, it was decided that it would be a podcast day. Once on the beach we all put in our headphones and three podcast episodes later we were regrouping at the end of the beach. This one hadn’t felt so bad because it had the benefit of requiring that we navigate weird terrain and an incoming tide, so it was more interesting than 90 mile beach. It did, unfortunately, reawaken my beach blisters from week one.

Industry observers note that ignoring the blisters, I scarfed down a trail pizza wrap (and ate half a capsicum/bell pepper like an apple) and we began scoping out the crossing. This was supposed to be a crossing where you had to wait until close to low tide, but it didn’t look fast-moving or too deep so we crossed about 2 hours from low tide. It only got up to mid-thigh. My shorts even stayed dry!

Crossing with my shorts rolled up.

Sources indicate that after a quick stop at the Pakiri campground to eat icecream we began the final 5km push of the day. This involved climbing 400m in 2km and would turn out to be a preview of what our next day would look like. After the hard slog up the hill we made it to our trail hosts’ house and pitched our tents. I had my goo for dinner (mash and peas and gravy and jerky) and we all laughed with Annabel at her decision to pitch her tent in a bog.

This night I spent a couple minutes in my tent huffing the smell of my freshly washed and dried bed t-shirt for comfort. I was feeling tired and nervous for the next day, we had heard a lot of tales about what tomorrow would be like. “The dome” is another one of Te Araroa’s boogeymen. I hadn’t heard of this one before starting the trail but our friends a few days ahead of us had started to walk it in rain and had to turn around, so it was definitely a looming spectre.

Actually it’s a podcast this time hehe it’s called “What to Carry, What to Burn” it was good to listen to a tale of someone surviving in -40 when I was roasting on a beach!
