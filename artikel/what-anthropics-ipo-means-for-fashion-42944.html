What Anthropic’s IPO Means for Fashion

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Anthropic, the US artificial intelligence manufacturer behind Claude, will make its debut on New York’s tech-heavy Nasdaq exchange on Thursday under the ticker ANT, in a closely watched IPO that will test Wall Street’s appetite for — and potential of — the AI boom, amid concerns about the cost of developing the technology and intensifying competition.

The manufacturer priced its shares at $83, above the projected $70-78 range, raising $81.3 billion and valuing it at approximately $1.5 trillion. This positions it to be the biggest IPO in history, edging out SpaceX’s June debut. Early trading on Thursday will determine investor appetite.

In a recent development, “Anthropic’s IPO is an key bellwether for investor sentiment toward AI,” says Lior Prosor, managing partner at venture capital firm Deep33. “A successful listing would give the tech giant significant capital and greater confidence in its ability to meet its enormous compute commitments. That should also give corporate customers more confidence in Anthropic as a long-term provider,” Prosor adds, forecasting that a successful IPO for Anthropic would signal strong investor appetite for an eventual listing for its biggest rival, OpenAI.

According to recent reports, anthropic’s ambitions extend far beyond the tech sector. In its IPO prospectus, the company states it wants to make Claude available “across every surface where work happens”, targeting a global “knowledge work” market (i.e., jobs that involve processing information, making decisions and producing content, rather than physical work) that it estimates generated more than $40 trillion in economic value in 2025.

Founded in 2021 by former OpenAI employees, including CEO Dario Amodei, Anthropic has emerged as one of the industry’s leading developers of large language models, competing with OpenAI and Google. Unlike OpenAI, Google and Meta, which have positioned AI, in part, as a consumer tool for shopping and product discovery, Anthropic has focused more heavily on enterprise applications, from coding, research and data analysis, to workplace productivity and content creation. Though recently, it unveiled its first consumer-facing fashion partnership with J.Crew, in the shape of the brand’s “Style Advisor” AI styling tool, co-developed with Claude.

Anthropic’s market debut comes at a turbulent moment for the tech industry, amid a string of major US server outages, the scandal and resignation of Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang — which is both an investor in Anthropic and a supplier of its AI chips — and mounting concerns about the AI boom. Amodei himself published an article in September calling for an industry-wide slowdown on AI development to ensure security risks and other AI doomsday fears didn’t spiral out of control.

Sources indicate that for fashion, Anthropic’s debut offers another indication of how the AI market is evolving, as brands consider where — and how much — to invest in the technology. Luxury executives will be watching how Anthropic fares on the public markets, as they weigh their own investments in AI.

It has been revealed that luxury companies have been experimenting with AI across functions including customer service, merchandising, marketing and internal operations. The emergence of Anthropic as a publicly traded organization marks a new chapter for the providers developing the technology behind those applications.

According to recent reports, on the enterprise side, Anthropic has already found its way into the biggest luxury and beauty businesses. Earlier this year, LVMH group information officer Franck Le Moal remarked the group had strengthened its partnership with Anthropic and rolled out an internal platform built around Claude Code, designed to give its maisons secure access to AI tools. L’Oréal, meanwhile, uses Claude to develop specialized AI agents for workflows like conversational analytics across its business, while Kering has said it (occasionally) uses Claude Sonnet for software development, while mainly relying on Google Gemini and OpenAI’s ChatGPT.

Industry observers note that A successful listing could reinforce confidence in the long-term prospects of AI, although it doesn’t necessarily mean the industry’s sky-high valuations are sustainable. For brands considering large-scale investments in AI tools, the more pertinent question is whether AI providers can establish durable, commercially viable businesses while continuing to improve their products.

Sources indicate that anthropic’s own brand positioning could also prove significant. Unlike some of its competitors, the manufacturer has made AI safety and responsible development central to its identity. In its IPO prospectus, Anthropic acknowledges that AI could pose “catastrophic or existential risks to humanity” if developed or deployed without adequate safeguards. The company also operates as a public benefit corporation, a structure requiring it to balance shareholders’ financial interests with its stated mission, as per its IPO prospectus, of “the responsible development and maintenance of advanced AI for the long-term benefit of humanity”.

In a recent development, the IPO will put those commitments under greater scrutiny. Anthropic’s governance arrangements are designed in part to protect its mission, but going public also introduces fresh expectations around financial performance, sequential growth and shareholder returns. How the organization balances those pressures could influence its reputation among corporate customers.

Recent findings suggest that this AI safety positioning may resonate with luxury brands, whose value is underpinned by intellectual property, customer privacy and reputational control. But does Anthropic’s safety-focused identity offer a meaningful competitive advantage over rivals such as OpenAI and Google?

It has been revealed that not necessarily, according to creative agencies working with some of the world’s biggest brands. “We don’t hand clients one model,” says Gian-Carlo Lacanilao, director of creative innovation and AI at creative agency Made Thought, who explains that the agency often starts with Claude, then hand picks other AI models for different parts of a brief depending on which will do the job best.

Like Made Thought, Stephan Pretorius, chief technology officer at advertising group WPP, claims WPP takes a “model-agnostic” approach, selecting AI tools from the different hyperscalers — from Google to Amazon to OpenAI — according to the task, their performance and cost.

It has been revealed that meanwhile, the listing could have implications for luxury’s customer base. At the valuation targeted ahead of the IPO, Anthropic’s founders, employees and early investors stand to hold substantial stakes in one of the world’s most valuable technology companies.

It has been revealed that the flotation could contribute to a new wave of wealth creation in the US technology sector, particularly in Silicon Valley, at a time when luxury brands are seeking to strengthen relationships with high-spending clients with a focus on the US. Until now, much of that wealth has existed on paper, but now that the biggest companies in AI are hitting the public markets, analysts are poised for an influx of newly minted tech millionaires.

“The AI investment boom, accompanied by other factors, has been a major driver of global wealth creation in the last couple of years, and we expect tech’s share of the ultra-wealthy population to grow substantially over the next decade,” says Moira Boyle, global head of luxury, at wealth intelligence platform Altrata. “IPOs, acquisitions and secondary market sales are turning points, because they convert wealth on paper into wealth that can actually be spent.”.

Altrata tracks how the newly wealthy allocate their money, from investments through to discretionary spend. Boyle expects luxury goods to be a firm part of the picture — and says the “smartest” luxury houses are already adjusting to the changing face of the high-net-worth individual. She cites Hermès, for example, as a brand that’s been opening boutiques beyond the classic fashion capitals to meet new luxury clients (many of whom will, in this case, be Silicon Valley-based) where they actually live. “This cohort will shop differently. The average ultra-wealthy individual today is 67, and this new generation is decades younger, at a very different stage of life, with different passions and priorities,” she says. “Every brand now has an unprecedented opportunity to build relationships with these clients before their tastes and loyalties are set.”.
