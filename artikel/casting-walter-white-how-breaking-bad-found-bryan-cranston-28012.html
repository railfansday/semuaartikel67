Casting Walter White: How ‘Breaking Bad’ Found Bryan Cranston

In a recent development, while the Breaking Bad universe would eventually expand to include dozens of characters, the pilot was actually a tidy affair. At its center was Walter White, whose journey would require an actor of extraordinary depth and nuance. It’s not an exaggeration to say that the success or failure of Breaking Bad would rest primarily on his sagging shoulders.

According to recent reports, SHARON BIALY, casting director: I don’t know if Bryan knows this, but when Sherry [Thomas] and I went in to meet with Vince to try to get the job as casting directors—­our “audition”—­we literally mentioned, “You may think we’re crazy. We hope you don’t fire us before you hire us. But what about Bryan Cranston?” And he said, “By golly, that’s who I want. I worked with him on The X-­ Files.”.

In a recent development, sherry and I did a lot of theater at the time. We were doing Aaron Sorkin’s new play [The Farnsworth Invention], and Bryan came in and read. We were blown away. He was so talented on Malcolm in the Middle, obviously, but this was live theater with a lot of language. Anyway, Bryan didn’t get the role, but his audition was incredible. And I never forgot it.

VINCE GILLIGAN, creator: Bryan was the only guy I was thinking of when I wrote the script. I had written an episode of The X-­ Files called “Drive,” which came out in 1998, and I was lucky enough to hire him to play the bad guy.

According to recent reports, we were really struggling to cast this role because we needed a guy who could be really scary, someone who could make us feel like our hero, Agent Mulder, could get his head blown off at any second. But also, he had to be kind of an asshole. [Throughout the episode] he’s ranting about the government. He’s ranting about stuff like, “Agent Mulder— is that a Jewish name?”.

He’s a thoroughly unlikable guy. And yet you had to feel bad for him, for what happens to him at the end of the episode. You had to feel sympathy.

We only had a day or two left before production was going to start. We were going to have to settle on somebody. We had plenty of actors come in who were scary as all get out, but they weren’t necessarily likable. And then this guy walks in the door: Bryan Cranston. Never heard of him before. And he nails it in the audition. As he left, we all stood up and shook his hand, and then I looked to the other producers and to Rick Millikan, our casting director, and I stated, “Off to wardrobe!” He was that good.

When I came up with the idea of Breaking Bad, I thought, This is a guy who, again, is going to be very unlikable. He’s going to do a lot of unlikable things, and the audience still has to sympathize with him. Who would be good for this role? I know! That guy, Bryan Cranston. And I wrote it for him. I never really had anyone else in mind.

In a recent development, MELISSA BERNSTEIN, co-executive producer: He was in an episode of X-­Files, playing this racist, and somehow, over the course of the episode, you go from loathing him to feeling badly that he doesn’t make it. He just was able to make someone so unsavory be human. Vince also remarked, “And he’s so great with comedy. I just feel it in my bones that he could bring those chops to drama—­his sense of timing, the way he embodies those choices physically. He’s perfect.”.

BRYAN CRANSTON: I instruct young actors all the time that without a healthy dose of luck, you will not have a career. And this [episode of The X-­ Files] was a lucky break for me.

Recent findings suggest that I had just gotten back from shooting this little movie that I wrote and directed, called Last Chance. I spent all of our money on it, and by the end I was like, Wow, what are we doing on this film? But we rolled the dice on it, and now I was back in town and got a call from my agent about an audition. I mentioned, “Whatever it is, let me know, because I really could use the money.” It just so happened that my look at the time, from this movie that I did, was a perfect look for this guy [on The X-­Files]. So, I went in, and I got the job—­and I was grateful for it.

In a recent development, it’s only in retrospect that I realized Vince Gilligan wanted to see me [to discuss Breaking Bad] was because I had played this reprehensible character that, despite his actions, still needed the audience to root for his life. Despite the despicable person in the back seat, David Du­chovny’s humanity would not allow him to just pull over and say, “Fuck it, die. You’re a horrible human being. I just want you to die.” He couldn’t do it. That’s really, in a nutshell, what Vince Gilligan does.

I knew intuitively that coming off seven years of a comedy, I needed to look for a drama. If I didn’t, I could have been typecast. If you want to expand on the opportunities that may present themselves, you need to take control. I was offered a couple of comedy pilots that I turned down, and I was perfectly willing to wait. Then Breaking Bad came into my lap.

Sources indicate that I read it . . . and I couldn’t believe it. I was stunned. I thought, This is perhaps the best hour­-long screenplay that I’ve ever read. I felt tremendous empathy for this character. This poor guy who is just struggling. And I was the same age as Walter White—­I was fifty.

But reading the pilot, I didn’t know where it was going. You have this fish­ out­ of­ water teacher, cooking crystal meth? How could that possibly be sustained year after year after year? How can someone who knows nothing about that world survive? And Vince explained that he wanted to turn Mr. Chips into Scarface.

According to recent reports, MARK JOHNSON, executive producer: Obviously, some people came up for Walter White who were names, but we never got around to entertaining them seriously. It was Vince’s idea to cast him. And despite the fact that there wasn’t a unanimous wave of approval, there wasn’t the kind of pushback that you’d probably have today, in large part because I don’t think the expectation was that we were going to get any real name, any significant name.

Sources indicate that MELISSA BERNSTEIN: AMC had found Jon Hamm for Mad Men, and they sort of got to reintroduce him to the business. I think they thought we would do something similar with Bryan.

It has been revealed that CHARLIE COLLIER, president, general manager, AMC: I was never in the room when they were debating the cast with Vince and Mark. That would have been the programming group. And then they would come back to us and say, “They want that father from Malcolm in the Middle.” And we’d be like, “Really? He’s going to be the linchpin for this whole ambitious project?”.

CHRISTINA WAYNE, senior VP, scripted programming, AMC: When Vince suggested Bryan, we all kind of noted, “That guy from Malcolm in the Middle?” And then he started showing us a bunch of tape of Bryan in other things, and I was, like, Oh my God—­he’s a total chameleon.

Recent findings suggest that JAMIE ERLICHT, co­-president, Sony Pictures Television: It’s funny. This is one of those great Hollywood stories that has been told so many times. You sit on the sidelines and watch, and it takes on a life of its own. That life is a little different than the truth, sure, but it’s a better story.

The story is that Zack [Van Amburg] and I heard “Bryan Cranston,” and we were really unsure, and we tried to convince Vince that, No, that’s not the way to go. That’s part of a story about how despite all the forces against him, Bryan Cranston became Walter White.

Sources indicate that it’s a great story. I love that story. I mean, I don’t love that story.

Sources indicate that the truth is, the show was going forward. We weren’t worried about the writing because Vince was knocking it out of the park. The directing? We weren’t worried about the directing. The casting was going to be the next critical element. If you don’t have it—­ well, it’s funny to think about it now. With ninety­ nine point nine percent of other actors in that role, that show is just a well­ written good show that doesn’t become the legacy show that it’s become. And so Vince brought up Bryan Cranston.

I had worked with Bryan on King of Queens. He was the wacky neighbor, a guest star. I was a mid­ level executive, and I was in charge of that show. I always thought he brought a lot of comedic value, just like he did on Seinfeld, and obviously Malcolm in the Middle.

So, when the name Bryan Cranston came up, I was like, Hmmm, that’s interesting. I’ve never seen him do the more dramatic stuff. Do you think he can do it?

Vince was very passionate. He said he had worked with him on X-­Files, and that’s where he really saw what he was capable of on the drama side. We said, “Great, would you send us the X-­Files episode?” Which he did. I remember it was the next day. Zack and I were watching it. We looked at each other and said, “It’s a little bit of a leap of faith, but Vince is passionate, and Bryan is truly talented. Sure, we’re in.” The process took all of thirty­ six hours.

That version of the story, the true version of the story, is not as sexy as the one where Bryan jumped through thirty­ nine hoops and almost didn’t get cast. But that’s how it happened.

In a recent development, ZACK VAN AMBURG, co­-president, Sony Pictures Television: Remember, at this point, Michael Chiklis had gone from doing comedy to being a badass antihero (in The Shield). So, on one hand, you worry: Wait, are we just pulling a page out of the same playbook? Let’s take another sitcom guy and have him do a dark drama.

Recent findings suggest that I think Vince was really clear on why he wanted Bryan. There was a likability factor that was undeniable. There was also a sort of sympathy that Bryan was able to elicit. And Vince also noted, “No, wait a second—­ this guy’s also a dramatic actor.”.

While some of this stuff gets lost in the haze of time—­ and the internet has a way of transforming rumors into something disguised as fact—­ it’s fair to say that Bryan was always the first choice for the role of Walter White. Regardless of what you may have heard, no one else was offered the job. Not Matthew Broderick, not John Cusack. No one. But you can’t fault anyone for doing their due diligence.

According to recent reports, SHARON BIALY: The network likes names. They want to talk about other names. We did that. We did a couple of auditions and meetings. We just met recently with an actor on something else, and he reminded me that he was in the waiting room when Bryan walked out, and he turned to the other three actors and said, “We should all just go home. It’s over. That guy’s going to get it.”.

VINCE GILLIGAN: We saw a lot of really good actors, several of them now famous names. They never dissuaded me that Bryan was the guy. And luckily, Bryan remarked yes, because he was up at the same time for a pilot for Fox that was going to pay him . . . well, my understanding is, substantially more money. Like a doctor show. Thank goodness he went with us instead.
