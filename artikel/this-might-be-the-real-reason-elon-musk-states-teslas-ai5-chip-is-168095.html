This might be the real reason Elon Musk states Tesla’s AI5 chip is going to Optimus

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Elon Musk says Tesla’s cars don’t need the next-generation AI5 chip, so it’s going to Optimus first. A new report from Taiwan’s chip supply chain gives a more practical reason: Tesla won’t have enough AI5 chips for its cars until 2028.

According to Commercial Times, Tesla’s chip design partner Global Unichip (GUC) is giving its limited TSMC 3nm capacity for 2027 to Google and Microsoft data center chips first. That leaves Tesla with a relatively small AI5 allocation.

Last Sunday, a Tesla owner asked Musk when AI5 would be in latest cars. His answer: “It’s going to Optimus. Cars will remain at AI4. That’s enough to solve superhuman driving.”.

As we reported this week, FSD v15 is basically AI4’s last shot. Tesla engineers are already squeezing the last few percent of utilization out of the chip, and the AI4.5 refresh is mostly a move to LPDDR5X memory. Musk mentioned Tesla cut AI4.5 from 32GB to 24GB of RAM because “RAM is super expensive,” and that AI4 itself now costs twice as much due to memory and storage price hikes.

It’s quite a change in aims. In June 2024, Musk mentioned AI5 would be in vehicles in the second half of 2025. Tesla only taped out AI5 in April, and Musk had already started describing its focus as Optimus and Tesla’s supercomputer clusters.

Sources indicate that the Commercial Times report, published today, describes GUC as the custom ASIC design partner on Tesla’s AI5 project. GUC is the design services house whose largest shareholder is TSMC, and it works mostly on data center silicon.

Recent findings suggest that industry sources told the paper that GUC’s 2027 allocation of TSMC’s N3 capacity is much larger than this year’s. But with AI compute demand exploding, it still can’t serve every big customer at once.

So GUC is prioritizing North American cloud projects, including Google’s Axion 2 and Axion 3 server processors and Microsoft’s in-house chips. Those orders carry high margins and good visibility. Tesla’s robot and car chip doesn’t make the front of the line.

The result, according to the report, is that Tesla’s initial 2027 wafer quota can’t support annual shipments for a fleet of millions of cars. The supply chain expects that limited AI5 volume to go first to Optimus, which needs a lot of compute but ships in small numbers, and only expects large-scale adoption in cars after N3 capacity expands further in 2028.

Sources indicate that TSMC is adding 3nm capacity, but not fast. Its upcoming 3nm fab in Tainan is scheduled for volume production in the first half of 2027, and its second Arizona fab, which Musk has noted will produce AI5, in the second half of 2027.

Tesla does have a second source. Samsung is building its own version of AI5 on 2nm at its Taylor, Texas fab, but it expects volume production of Tesla’s AI chips there in the second half of 2027.

Now look at the numbers. Tesla delivered roughly 1.6 million vehicles last year. Every one of them needs a self-driving computer.

Optimus is a different story. Tesla now plans to put AI5 in Optimus around April 2027 and push external Optimus deliveries to late 2027. Even an aggressive robot ramp next year is measured in thousands of units, not millions.

If your 2027 AI5 supply is a fraction of what a car program needs, Optimus is the only product Tesla has that can absorb it.

Industry observers note that musk’s version is that cars don’t need AI5 because AI4 is “enough to solve superhuman driving.” The supply chain’s version is that Tesla can’t get enough AI5 chips to put in cars anyway. Both can be true at the same time, but only one of them explains the timing.

Sources indicate that A video stream is fundamentally unstructured data. It takes enormous computing power to process those pixels, identify objects, determine their positions, and then derive motion vectors for direction and speed. And the better you want to get at interpreting increasingly complex real-world situations, the more demanding the computational requirements become. Hardware gets faster, of course, and algorithms get better.

But you’re fighting an increasingly difficult scaling problem.

Sources indicate that most other major players take a different approach. They combine cameras with LiDAR and radar, which provide actual physical measurements rather than forcing the system to infer everything from pixels. Radar directly measures range and relative velocity, while LiDAR provides precise 3D spatial data. Both still require processing, but they give the system a much more structured starting point.

It has been revealed that tesla is betting almost everything on one approach: throw more computing power and AI at the problem. That’s a pretty expensive race to run, and I don’t see how they can win it on those terms alone. Think of the famous chessboard-and-rice-grains analogy. If the chessboard represents Tesla’s autonomous driving challenge, and each square doubles the computational power required to make the next leap, Tesla hasn’t even made it through the first row before the numbers start getting out of hand. And there’s an entire chessboard still to go.

It has been revealed that look at how Tesla has been handling silicon this fall. It cut AI4.5 RAM. It cut AI5 RAM from 144GB to 72GB, then bumped it to 96GB a day later. It says AI4 costs double what it used to. That’s a company rationing chips and memory, not one choosing to leave a 12x compute upgrade on the shelf because it isn’t needed.

And if AI4 really were plenty, Tesla engineers wouldn’t be talking about squeezing out the last few percent of the chip for v15.

So here’s how we read it. Tesla had to pick where its scarce AI5 chips go in 2027, and it picked the low-volume product. “AI4 is enough” is the story that makes that decision sound like a choice. And it’s a story we heard about HW3, Tesla’s previous self-driving computer.
