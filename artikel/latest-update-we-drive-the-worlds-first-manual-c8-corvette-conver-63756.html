Latest Update: We Drive the World’s First Manual C8 Corvette Conversion

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The first manual C8-gen Chevrolet Corvette is running and driving only a year after Tremec revealed its new TR-9060 six-speed manual transaxle, and Road & Track just got a chance to take it for a spin.

The car in question, built by Peitz Performance in Texas, is actually a race car, and it’s still in the early prototype phase. In other words, my test is not representative of what a polished and optimized road car will feel like. But it was enough to prove that the concept works.

The basics are relatively straightforward. Tremec engineered this latest six-speed transaxle to fit lots of different cars, from the C8 Corvette to McLarens and more. It’s for the aftermarket to play around with, as Chevy has made it clear the C8 won’t be getting a manual from the factory. Alex Peitz, the owner of Peitz Performance, had an existing relationship with Tremec, which is how he managed to get his hands on one of these transaxles before regular production of them begins. (Tremec indicates it’s targeting July 2027 for initial sales to distributors.).

Industry observers note that already neck-deep in C8 builds, Peitz used his experience and—most importantly—3-D scans of the car. This head start helped him map out how he’d install the transmission, shift cables, clutch, and shifter before he even had the parts in hand. A heavily modified Cadillac CT4-V Blackwing clutch pedal is squeezed into the pedal box. There was some fear that the new gearbox would interfere with the exhaust system, but that ended up not being an problem once it was installed. Of all the problems to solve, though, making the C8’s complicated and advanced electrical architecture talk nicely with the manual was the toughest challenge. It’s one Peitz admits he is still sorting out.

It has been revealed that on top of being version one of the manual C8 Corvette, Peitz’s car is a fully caged and built race car, with all the temperamental nature common to the breed. Unfortunately, when I arrived to drive it, the power steering and ABS had recently left the chat. With massive slicks mounted both front and rear, that made steering the car one hell of an arm workout. On top of that, the fixed bucket seat was, well, fixed. Peitz has longer legs than I do, and I was only just barely able to get the extremely heavy race clutch to the floor with the toe of my shoe extended as far as I could.

The good news is that the transmission itself seems to work without matter. Driving the C8, I felt instantly familiar with where each gear should be. There was no vagueness or sloppiness to be found. That said, what was missing was the affirmative thud I was expecting as each gear was fully selected. The weight of the throw action was oddly light for such a powerful and torquey car, although not offensively so. But these are preferences that should hopefully be tweaked later as the aftermarket fine-tunes the action of the gearchanges with bushings and shifter design.

According to recent reports, one thing every manualized C8 is set to struggle with is shifter placement. The car’s center console was never designed with a manual transmission in mind. It is too tall, and that presents a problem for ergonomics, as the upcoming shifter sits inherently higher than where you expect to find it. Depending on an individual driver’s height or arm length, it may work fine, but my arm felt far too high in the air as I grabbed each gear. A shorter shifter might help, but only by making the problem less noticeable as opposed to fixing it entirely.

Rev matching was remarkably easy, although the pedal placement meant I wasn’t able to heel-and-toe. Auto rev matching will be possible, Peitz indicates, and while the demonstrator doesn’t have it now, it will be an option for those who want to convert their cars in the future. And this early pedal arrangement will surely evolve as Peitz and other shops work up more polished solutions.

One of the promising aspects that should not change is the gearing. I tested the car at Michigan’s newest racetrack, Motorsports Gateway, and found myself constantly shifting thanks to the relative shortness of the ratios. This is definitely a good thing; you choose a manual for the pleasure of changing gears. Porsche 718 owners know the curse of especially long gears well—if you can go over 80 mph in second gear, you end up rarely actually needing to shift on a twisting road. That won’t be the case for a C8 with the Tremec, because like with the eight-speed Tremec dual-clutch in the factory C8, you are going to stay busy stirring the pot.

Sources indicate that beyond that, at this stage, I can’t say too much about how a roadgoing C8 manual will be, but Peitz gets credit for doing the hard yards of proving it is indeed possible to get three pedals into the mid-engined Corvette. Tremec says it looks on installing its transmission in a test car of its own next year, and that will be a bone-stock production C8. We’ll be champing at the bit to get behind the wheel of that one when it ultimately happens. But Peitz Performance deserves the credit for getting here first. The manual Corvette is back!
