Tesla toughens FSD driver monitoring in Europe while loosening it in the US

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Tesla is rolling out stricter driver monitoring for its “Full Self-Driving” system in Europe, with earlier warnings, a full strike for drivers who look away during a manoeuvre, and a hands-on-wheel requirement above 130 km/h (81 mph).

In a recent development, in the US, Tesla has spent the last year going the other way. Its CEO told owners they can text and drive on FSD, and recent US builds were tuned to nag drivers less.

Sources indicate that on November 16, the Dutch vehicle authority RDW unveiled that it signed off on a upcoming enhancement to Tesla Assisted Driving, the name Tesla now uses for FSD (Supervised) in Europe. It covers HW4 vehicles and goes out over the air.

According to the RDW, drivers now get attention warnings earlier. If a driver isn’t paying attention while the system performs a manoeuvre, they get a “full strike.” The regulator indicates the patch also adds measures to prevent misuse of the system, but it didn’t spell out what they are.

Recent findings suggest that there’s a speed component too. Above 130 km/h (where legal), the driver has to keep their hands on the wheel. And setting a maximum speed above what the system displays now requires the driver to confirm it by scrolling the wheel up, with the speed limit sign pulsing red when the car goes over.

It has been revealed that the RDW indicates it tested the revision on its own track and on public roads and found no adverse or unintended effects. Ten other countries have adopted the Dutch approval so far, with Finland now on the list, while EU-wide approval is still waiting on a vote at the TCMV, the committee of member states.

This comes two weeks after Tesla cut the system’s speed margin from 50% to 10% over the limit in Europe and dropped the “Full Self-Driving” name there. Germany had made both a condition of supporting EU approval.

Now look at how Tesla handles the same software at home.

At Tesla’s shareholder meeting last November, Musk complained that the car was “a little strict about keeping your eyes on the road” and noted Tesla would “allow you to text and drive essentially” within a month or two. A month later, an owner asked whether FSD v14.2.1 allowed texting. Musk’s answer: “Depending on context of surrounding traffic, yes.”.

It has been revealed that texting while driving is illegal in 49 states. FSD is still a Level 2 system, and Tesla’s own fine print notes the driver is responsible at all times.

It has been revealed that in May, Musk confirmed that FSD v14.3.3 was built to nag drivers less. Tesla did add better eye-gaze tracking and eyewear handling in a June build, to be fair. But the pitch to owners hasn’t changed: fewer nags is sold as a feature.

In a recent development, and US drivers have been allowed to go hands-free on FSD at any speed since Tesla moved to cabin-camera monitoring in 2024. There’s no 130 km/h rule here.

NHTSA noticed. In its engineering analysis of FSD crashes in low-visibility conditions, the agency asked Tesla to justify a list of its marketing claims, and it specifically cited Musk’s texting post. In September, Rep. Raja Krishnamoorthi asked NHTSA to look into Tesla’s driver monitoring after dozens of videos appeared to show drivers asleep on Autopilot and FSD.

Industry observers note that the results are on the road. Last month, a driver told the CHP he was asleep on FSD when his Tesla drove through a freeway closure and hit a patrol car, seriously injuring two officers. Owners are also filming themselves defeating Tesla’s driver monitoring and posting it for clout.

Both markets get the same cabin camera and the same HW4 computer.

In a recent development, but in Europe, a type-approval authority tests every patch before it ships and can refuse it. In the US, nobody pre-approves driver-assist software. Tesla self-certifies, and NHTSA only steps in after complaints and crashes pile up, which is how you end up with a CEO publicly telling owners to text while the regulator is still writing letters about it.

Industry observers note that this enhancement proves Tesla can build stricter driver monitoring whenever a regulator demands it. The code exists. It’s running on HW4 cars in 11 European countries right now.

That's because Musk is buddies with the current federal government that would regulate his not-full-self-driving system. Whereas Europe doesn't kowtow to his whims. We'll see what happens in 2029 with a latest administration.

According to recent reports, that makes the US approach hard to defend. Tesla’s argument for relaxing supervision is that FSD is now good enough. But if that were true, it would be showing the RDW data proving looser monitoring is safe, not adding earlier warnings and harsher strikes to get approved. It’s doing the opposite in Europe because it has to, and the opposite of that in the US because it can.

Sources indicate that we’ve been saying it for a while: FSD gets more dangerous as it gets better, because people trust it more and watch the road less. The answer is monitoring that tightens as the system improves. Europe is forcing exactly that. In the US, Tesla is loosening it, encouraging illegal phone use from the top, and leaving drivers with 100% of the liability when it goes wrong.
