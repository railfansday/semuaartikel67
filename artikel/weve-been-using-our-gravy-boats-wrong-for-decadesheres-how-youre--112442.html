We’ve Been Using Our Gravy Boats Wrong for Decades—Here’s How You’re Supposed to Use Them

That little spout on your gravy boat may not be what you think it is.

According to recent reports, I have a particular weakness for antique dishes. Most weekends I can be found happily perusing the shelves at thrift and antique stores, where an occasional beautiful piece makes up for an awful lot of cracked mugs and mismatched dinnerware. The gravy boat I use every Thanksgiving was one of those finds. I love it—even though I’ve never been particularly fond of pouring from it. Gravy has an unfortunate tendency to run down the spout rather than into the intended destination—a minor inconvenience of an otherwise lovely piece.

Then I came across an Instagram reel from Steven Moore that made me question something I’ve been doing my entire life. Moore, a British antiques expert, explains that what most of us recognize as a pouring spout on a gravy boat may actually have been intended as a place to rest a small ladle. Excuse me? I’d spent years admiring and collecting old dishware, and somehow this particular bit of history had escaped me. More importantly, I was beginning to suspect that my antique boat had been unfairly blamed for an awful lot of spilled gravy.

Ellie Martin Cliffe For Taste Of Home.

Industry observers note that according to Moore, traditional gravy boats were often intended to be used with small ladles, their handles resting nearly in the notch we’ve come to think of as a spout. In the sort of formal household familiar to anyone who’s watched The Gilded Age, a footman would have served the gravy or sauce by the spoonful rather than by tipping the entire boat over someone’s plate. It’s a rather elegant arrangement, and one that makes considerably more sense to me than trying to coax gravy out of a vessel that seems determined to spill all over the tablecloth.

Sources indicate that moore also offers an explanation for how we got into the habit of pouring. He says that around the middle of the 20th century, ceramic manufacturers began doing away with matching ladles to save money. Without the ladles, people started using the boats as little pitchers, and eventually the pouring method became familiar practice.

Industry observers note that of course, some gravy boats really were designed for pouring, and the presence of a spout doesn’t necessarily mean you’ve misunderstood the entire object. Both methods have a history, although I find Moore’s explanation fascinating. A piece of dishware I’ve used for years suddenly makes much more sense, and I rather like the possibility that I might accidentally stumble upon its matching ladle one day while I’m out treasure hunting.

Industry observers note that for gravy boats intended to be used with a ladle, the boat stays on the table while guests serve themselves by the spoonful. A small sauce or gravy ladle works perfectly well, even if it wasn’t made to match the boat, and its handle can rest against the spout if the shape allows.

In a recent development, this is especially useful for gravy boats with attached saucers, which can be awkward to tip without sending accumulated drips onto the table. As Etiquetteer notes, ladling sauce is traditional, but pouring is perfectly acceptable when there’s no ladle.

And if your gravy boat pours beautifully, by all means carry on. Mine has never been especially cooperative, and after all these years, I’m rather pleased to discover that there may be a good explanation as to why.
