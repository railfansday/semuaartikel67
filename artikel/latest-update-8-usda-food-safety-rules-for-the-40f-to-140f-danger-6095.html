Latest Update: 8 USDA food safety rules for the 40°F to 140°F danger zone

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that food left out more than 2 hours, or 1 hour above 90°F, is risky. Eight USDA rules cover timing, temperatures and handwashing.

It has been revealed that most people decide whether a dish left on the counter is still good by looking at it and giving it a sniff. That check offers no protection against the germs that make people sick, because they grow across the whole range of temperatures between the refrigerator and the oven. A dish that has come out of the oven but has not yet gone into the refrigerator falls somewhere in that range. These bacteria also generally cause no change in how food tastes, smells or looks, according to FSIS. A dish can pass every test a nose or an eye can give it and still make someone ill. The checks that work are the ones made with a clock and a thermometer: how long the food has been out, and how hot or cold it is.

According to recent reports, the risk reveals up less in the refrigerator than at the long holiday meal or the buffet, where dishes sit out for hours and nobody keeps track of the time. Once food reaches the home, keeping it safe becomes the cook's job. "Food safety doesn’t stop at the processing establishments that produce the food you eat," Dr. Mindy Brashears, USDA Under Secretary for Food Safety, said. That job lasts as long as the gathering does, and the CDC advises checking food temperatures regularly throughout an event. Each safeguard is a check that someone has to remember to make. None of them is built into the food.

The Food Safety and Inspection Service, part of the USDA, laid out the federal government's rules for keeping food out of the "danger zone" of 40°F to 140°F. Those rules range from how long a dish can safely sit out to how to reheat leftovers and why clean hands matter. Most of them work the same way. They keep food at one edge of that range or the other, limit how long it spends inside it, or heat it back out. One rule has nothing to do with temperature, because germs also reach food on people's hands, no matter how carefully a cook watches the thermometer. Together, the rules add up to a short set of habits: glancing at the clock, reaching for the thermometer, and stopping at the sink.

Industry observers note that A dish left on the table can become unsafe within the length of an ordinary meal. "Bacteria grow most rapidly in the range of temperatures between 40 °F and 140 °F, doubling in number in as little as 20 minutes," the USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service said. That is about as long as a conversation over dessert. The phrase "as little as" describes the fastest case, not a rate every dish will follow. A safe habit still has to initiative around that fastest case, because nobody at the table knows how swiftly the germs in their own dish are growing. The growth also builds on itself. Each stretch of time out adds latest germs on top of the ones that grew before it, so a few extra minutes cost more than they seem to.

The fast doubling applies to germs that can make people sick, not only to food that is going stale. That turns a forgotten platter from a question of taste into a question of health.

Recent findings suggest that nobody can watch germs multiply inside a casserole, but a cook can move the dish out of the danger zone by chilling it or heating it. Because the growth compounds, how soon food leaves the zone matters as much as whether it leaves at all.

Every minute a dish spends between 40°F and 140°F counts against it.

The safety check for food on a buffet table is a clock, and food out of the refrigerator has a firm limit of 2 hours, according to the Food Safety and Inspection Service. The USDA sets the same two-hour limit in its latest advice for spring holiday meals. A host will meet that deadline at a weeknight dinner, an office potluck and a holiday table alike. This makes the decision at the table a yes-or-no one. A dish is either within its two hours or past them, and the rule leaves no room for a guess that it is probably still fine. Only a host who noted when the food came out can apply the limit, so the habit starts the moment a dish reaches the table.

The two-hour limit is the general rule, but hot weather shortens it. A host serving food on a day hotter than 90°F has just 1 hour before the food must go back into the cold. A picnic table or a backyard cookout in summer sun often gets that hot without anyone treating the day as unusual, so the stricter limit applies more often than people expect. The USDA advises that eggs meant for eating can stay at room temperature for two hours. Under the hot-weather rule, the same eggs served at a sweltering outdoor spread would have only one hour. Taking the food outside, not anything about the food itself, shortens the deadline, and a host who remembers only "two hours" will go past it.

Anyone serving food outdoors in summer should plan around the shorter of the two limits.

According to recent reports, A cooked dish still needs protecting after the cook turns off the burner, because the rule covers hot food that isn't served right away, the stretch most cooks treat as downtime. The USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service says that such food should be kept at 140°F or higher, for example with warming equipment. A cook can't count on the heat a pan carries out of the oven, and a casserole cooling under foil on the counter falls short of the rule. The 140°F line is the top edge of the danger zone, and a dish that slips a few degrees below it is back in the range where bacteria grow fastest. The CDC uses the 40°F to 140°F range as the danger zone for Clostridium perfringens, a germ that causes food poisoning. Holding food hot adds no safety to a dish. It keeps a properly cooked dish from losing the safety the cook already gave it.

According to recent reports, meeting the rule takes no specialist gear. Chafing dishes, steam tables that have been preheated, warming trays and slow cookers can all keep food at that temperature, according to the Food Safety and Inspection Service. Once a host hands the job of holding the heat to one of these devices, the device itself needs checking. With a warmer set too low or a thermometer that reads wrong, the food can cool below 140°F while everything on the table looks in order. The CDC advises food service workers to check that their equipment and thermometers work.

A switched-on warming tray is no proof that the food is hot. The proof is a reading from a thermometer someone has checked.

Recent findings suggest that the cold side of a holiday spread looks like the safe side, which makes it easy to forget. A bowl of potato salad or a shrimp platter set on ice seems protected for as long as the meal lasts. A cold dish counts as safe only while its temperature holds at 40°F or colder, and resting it in a bed of ice is one way a host can keep it there, according to the Food Safety and Inspection Service. That temperature is the bottom edge of the danger zone, and a cold dish that warms a few degrees on the table has moved into the range where germs grow. As the meal goes on and the ice melts, the food can warm past that line without any guest noticing.

Industry observers note that A host can also limit how much cold food sits out at once, instead of relying on the ice alone. The USDA advises setting out smaller portions and replenishing them as needed. The less food on the table at a time, the less of it can warm into the zone before guests eat it or the host returns it to the fridge. Cold holding becomes a job that lasts the whole meal, with the host returning to the refrigerator for fresh portions until the last guest is served.

Recent findings suggest that keeping food cold starts days before the meal, with the frozen turkey. The USDA states leaving a turkey out on the counter to thaw is unsafe, and a bird thawing in the refrigerator needs 24 hours for each 4 to 5 pounds of its weight. On the counter, the bird would spend hours well above the 40°F line that the cold rule exists to hold. Safe thawing is therefore a scheduling job as much as a temperature one, and a cook who takes a holiday turkey out of the freezer the night before has already run out of time to thaw it safely.

Cold food stays safe through the calendar and the refill as much as through the ice bucket.

Recent findings suggest that cooking is the step where a cook heats food past the danger zone on purpose, and the rule behind it sets two separate checks. The oven should be no cooler than 325°F, and the meat or poultry itself has to reach a safe internal temperature, according to the Food Safety and Inspection Service. A properly set oven is only the starting condition. The Food Safety and Inspection Service gives 145°F as the target for a roast, chop or steak of beef, pork or lamb. A cook takes that reading from the middle of the meat, and it sits far below the oven setting, so a hot oven tells a cook only that the roast is still cooking. The meat is done when its center reaches the target, not when the oven has been hot for long enough.

A cook who keeps a single "safe" number in mind is following a rule that doesn't exist. The Food Safety and Inspection Service sets ground beef's safe temperature at 160°F, higher than the target for a whole cut, and poultry's at 165°F. A steak and a burger therefore call for different readings, and chicken and turkey need the most heat of the three. Remembering only one number causes trouble either way. A cook who uses the whole-cut figure for everything leaves a burger undercooked, and one who uses the poultry figure for everything dries out a roast. The thermometer has to be read against the right target for the food in hand.

Reaching the target protects the meat only until the cook takes it off the heat. The Food Safety and Inspection Service warns that cooked food should never go back onto the platter that held it raw. A cook who carries burgers back from the grill on the plate that brought them out can put the germs that cooking was meant to kill back onto the finished meat.

Cooking is a job finished at the plate, not at the thermometer reading.

Leftovers get no latest deadline when the meal ends. The Food Safety and Inspection Service indicates cooked food should go into the refrigerator within two hours. A dish's time on the buffet counts toward that limit as much as the minutes it waits after dessert. At a long gathering where guests graze all afternoon, a dish set out at the start can use up its two hours before anyone thinks to pack it away. By the time the last guest leaves, much of the time for clearing the table is already gone.

It has been revealed that getting leftovers into the fridge on time is only part of the job, because cooling them properly matters just as much. "One of the most common causes of foodborne illness is improper cooling of cooked foods," the Food Safety and Inspection Service remarked. The danger lies in the cooling step itself, not only in whether anyone remembers to refrigerate the food. The agency pairs that warning with an instruction to pack leftovers in shallow containers. The choice of container is part of the safety rule rather than a matter of tidiness, so a host has to think about how food goes into the fridge as well as when.

Recent findings suggest that hosts can shrink the leftover job before there are any leftovers. For a game-day spread, FSIS recommends serving the food in smaller batches, one round for each half. With less food sitting out at once, less of it can pass the two-hour limit before cleanup. The host also has less food to cool in a hurry at the end.

A host who decides how much to set out, and when, has settled the size of the leftover job long before anyone clears the plates.

Sources indicate that reheating is the step meant to kill the bacteria in leftovers, not just slow their growth, and its test is a temperature taken inside the food. The Food Safety and Inspection Service says that a dish of leftovers is ready when steam rises from it or when its middle registers 165°F. FSIS advises taking that reading with a food thermometer. The target covers the inside of the food, so a hot oven or a sizzling crust doesn't show that a dish is safe, and a plate too hot to touch can still fall short. The probe belongs at the center, the last part of a dish to get hot.

It has been revealed that steaming is the easier test, and most people will reach for it, but it comes with no number attached. The thermometer reading is the measured standard for leftovers. A cook who goes by steam is still following the guidance, but a cook who uses a thermometer has a firmer answer.

Sources indicate that the bigger trap is a dish that is hot in one spot and cool in another. Federal advice on microwaving aims to heat food evenly, and FSIS recommends checking the temperature in several places. One reading can land on a hot spot and miss a cooler pocket elsewhere in the dish. In practice, the 165°F rule applies to the coolest part of the food, not the first spot the thermometer touches.

In a recent development, liquids get a simpler test that a cook can see in the pot. FSIS states sauces, soups and gravies should be brought to a rolling boil. The reheating check also decides whether food can be saved, because leftovers thawed in the microwave can go back in the freezer once they have been heated to 165°F.

After that last temperature check, the remaining safeguard is the cook's own hands.

Every other safeguard against the danger zone comes down to a reading on a clock or a thermometer. Handwashing comes down to whether a cook stops at the sink at all. Washing with soap and warm water for at least 20 seconds helps get germs off a cook's hands, according to the Food Safety and Inspection Service. A wash that takes well under a minute and no special equipment is easy to do and just as easy to skip. Much of the risk lies in the washes that never happen.

Skipping the wash is a serious lapse, because hands are how food workers usually contaminate food. In outbreaks traced to food that restaurant workers contaminated, the workers had spread germs from their hands to the food in nine of ten, according to the CDC. The short scrub the USDA recommends is the step meant to block that route. The moment that matters is when a cook moves from raw meat to the next task without stopping at the sink.

According to recent reports, even in restaurant kitchens, where handling food is the job, most of the washes that should happen don't. Restaurant workers do about nine tasks an hour that should send them to the sink, the CDC reports, but they wash only about two or three times an hour. Handwashing is meant to interrupt cooking again and again through every hour, and in a busy kitchen that interruption is the first thing to go.

It has been revealed that conditions in the kitchen, not a stricter rule, get people washing more. The CDC finds that restaurant workers wash more often during slower stretches, at restaurants that train their staff in food safety, and in kitchens with several hand sinks or a sink in plain view. Those conditions make the USDA's 20-second instruction easier to follow. Anyone feeding a crowd at home can apply the same lesson by clearing the sink, setting out soap and putting both where people can see them.

Clean hands depend as much on where the sink is as on anyone's willpower.
