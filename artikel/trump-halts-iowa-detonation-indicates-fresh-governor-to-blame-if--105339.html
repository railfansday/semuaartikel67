Trump halts Iowa detonation, indicates fresh governor to blame if it blows accidentally

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

President Trump took to social media Monday afternoon to announce that he has ordered the US military to halt looks to detonate 68 deteriorating barrels of lead azide at an Army ammunition plant in Middletown, Iowa. Trump claimed that the fate of the unstable, highly explosive material is now in the hands of a federal court and Rob Sand, Iowa’s governor-elect, who won’t be sworn into office until January.

Sources indicate that the Army had planned to detonate the barrels on November 20, but delayed the blast on Thursday amid public outcry and a legal challenge from environmental groups. The explosion will launch a plume of toxic gases and neurotoxic lead that could reach up to 100 miles away from the Iowa facility, the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant (IAAAP).

Iowa residents and officials said they learned of the detonation only days beforehand. The environmental groups, led by Earthjustice, claimed in an emergency court filing that the Army had not fully considered safer options for disposing of the lead azide before planning the detonation.

It has been revealed that late Thursday, a federal appeals court granted Earthjustice’s request to put a hold on the Army’s permit from the Environmental Protection Agency, which allowed for the controlled detonation to be carried out. Around the same time, Acting Secretary of the Army Adam Telle mentioned the detonation would be postponed until Monday so the military could communicate with Iowans about the situation and looks.

Documents released by the Army last week mentioned that the military first learned in March that the lead azide had started to degrade and crystallize. Lead azide is used in detonators for munitions, but in crystallized form it becomes highly unstable and sensitive to physical movement or friction. The IAAAP is said to contain around 9,700 pounds (4.4 tons) of lead azide in barrels held in earth-covered containers.

Sources indicate that in late March, the Army awarded a contract to MuniRem Environmental, a Georgia-based firm that uses chemicals to neutralize munitions to safely dispose of the lead azide, according to a New York Times report. MuniRem uses a sulfur-based solution that reportedly breaks down explosives and immobilizes toxic chemicals, eliminating the need for detonation. The company had proposed spraying the inside of the earth-covered containers housing the barrels as well as the barrel surfaces to neutralize risks of spontaneous explosions.

Sources indicate that but two months later, the Army canceled the contract without explanation. An Army contracting officer told MuniRem in an email at the time that the cancellation “was not based on deficiencies” in MuniRem’s plans, the Times reported. The firm’s founder said MuniRem filed public records requests to get more information on why the contract was scrapped, but it has not received a response.

According to recent reports, now, the Army claims that the lead azide has only degraded further since March and that it cannot move the barrels or even open their lids without risking a catastrophic explosion. Further, it claims that winter temperatures in the coming days increase the risk of spontaneous detonation.

“As the temperatures drop, the risk of an uncontrolled explosion increases because the formed crystals can break more easily in freezing temperatures,” lawyers for the Trump administration wrote in a court filing over the weekend. “If the crystals break, this could cause a chain reaction detonation that could cause an uncontrolled dispersal of lead azide into the environment.”.

The Army claims that the only choice now is between a controlled, mitigated explosion or an uncontrolled, unmitigated explosion. In court filings this weekend, government lawyers argued that the Army does not need the now-suspended EPA permit to move forward with the detonation, because the Army has determined that the lead azide poses an “immediate threat to human health, public safety, property, or the environment.”.

According to recent reports, last Thursday, Telle remarked that the delay until Monday was the “latest possible moment” for a controlled detonation before temperatures dropped dangerously low. But the military subsequently delayed the detonation again until Tuesday and then pushed it back to Wednesday.

Sources indicate that in his social media post Monday, Trump noted he thought carrying out the controlled detonation was the “proper course.” But he said he was making the decision the responsibility of the federal appeals court and Governor-elect Rob Sand, a Democrat. If the lead azide spontaneously detonates on their watch, Trump claimed, they will be fully to blame.

“If he or the Courts do not agree, we will have no choice but to not proceed with the controlled removal of these hazardous materials, and be forced to hold the incoming Governor responsible for any and all death or destruction caused by this very unfortunate, and longterm, situation,” Trump wrote.

In a social media post Sunday evening, Sand called for answers on how the situation was able to become so dangerous, specifically raising the question of why MuniRem’s contract was canceled.

“Iowans deserve transparency over how we got here, then answers and accountability to stop this from ever happening again,” he wrote.
