The real lesson businesses should learn from video games (it’s more than ‘gamification’)

It has been revealed that in the early 2000s I (Bing) found myself crouched in an office cubicle frantically scribbling down a few things I thought the video game industry could teach a burgeoning online bookstore. I was initially reluctant when my friend, the legendary venture capitalist John Doerr, asked me to take a meeting in Seattle with a buzzy, young startup CEO. I was a midcareer video game executive and this particular founder had already been named Time’s 1999 person of the year. Terrified to look a fool, I ducked into a cubicle at the Electronic Arts office in Seattle and outlined an idea for an online customer loyalty program based on the strategy that had turned Madden NFL into a forever franchise by giving gamers reasons to come back year after year. I practically stuffed the one-pager in Jeff Bezos’s hand the first time we met over coffee. He laughed, but indulged me and unfurled the page. I started my career in video games as a marketing chief, and I was still in the habit of writing headline copy for all my one-pagers. Across the top of this one I wrote: Amazon Prime. Jeff would say that Prime’s success was a good surprise, but when you know video games, when you understand the magical ways that they tap into our intrinsic motivations and make us want to come back again and again, you understand that game design thinking has the potential to increase retention and engagement in almost any walk of life. After Amazon Prime worked so well, I brought the lessons of video games to all kinds of consumer industries, from entertainment products like Spotify to financial products like Better Mortgage to educational products like Coursera and Duolingo and even weight loss apps like My Fitness Pal. A bad rap I used to call the craft of applying video game design to consumer products “video gamification,” but these days gamification has a bad rap. Some of the smartest business minds I know brush it off as a short-lived 2010s trend that didn’t pan out. I’ve heard countless product teams complain that the points and leaderboard systems they build into their user interfaces didn’t make a dent in any of their retention metrics. Worse still, I have friends and peers who recoil when they hear the word “gamify,” immediately assuming someone is trying to manipulate their time and attention. Gamification has taken on such a negative connotation that I’ve stopped using the word altogether. The thing is, the so-called “gamification” trend that failed hard in the 2010s was based on a fundamental misunderstanding of what makes successful games work. Product teams that couldn’t figure out how to boost traffic to their app would probably be surprised to learn that successful games rarely rely on competition to motivate players. In fact, in video games, cooperation is a far more important mechanism than competition. Connection, mastery, productivity If you’re going to channel the motivational magic of a great game, you need to understand what actually goes into crafting one. The top reasons that players come back to a game are, in order of importance, social connection, personal mastery, and productivity. Most business execs I meet assume that productivity measures like money and rankings are the most important motivational forces, but the video game industry has found for decades that social relationships and the satisfaction of self-improvement far outweigh them. In the early days of the Facebook juggernaut game Farmville, its developer, Zynga, found that the notifications that invited friends to play cooperatively garnered 30 times the clicks that the competitive, brag-based notifications did. In games, a two-way social interaction is the top predictor of whether or not somebody comes back tomorrow. Nongamers find this surprising, but they shouldn’t! We’re human beings, ever motivated to build communities and deepen our relationships with one another. We come back to the games and products that best align with that. One of the great social features of the business networking platform Linkedin is “Endorsements,” where users are invited to publicly vouch for their peers and strengthen their professional relationships. Cooperative features like this give people a reason to return to the résumé sharing platform regularly, even when they aren’t searching for jobs. Personal mastery and self-improvement are also intrinsic motivators to every gamer. Mechanics that give people a framework to measure and track self-improvement, like experience points and level-ups, create an invigorating sense of progress with each session. Scores are important in games, but more so as a way to rate individual progression instead of rank it against others. Productivity measures like time saved and money earned also motivate us, especially when they’re paired with our social and mastery priorities. We like earning money, sure, but we love earning money doing things we already wanted to do, and we might forgo it when it comes in direct conflict with our social relationships and sense of self-worth. More of what they want Amazon Prime works because it gives its customers more of what they already want. Free and fast shipping offers an incredible productivity proposition, especially in terms of time saved, and individualized customer profiles help us form identities around ourselves as consumers. Amazon knows that one of the biggest predictors of whether a Prime user renews is how often they use their subscription benefits, but pushing members to make purchases they otherwise wouldn’t risks undermining long-term loyalty. So Amazon treats Prime like a forever game franchise, continually improving the experience and layering on upcoming benefits like Prime Video, Amazon Music, and prescription savings. Each gives members another reason to use their subscription in ways that already align with their interests. When I gut check a user experience to see if it’s aligned with my own wants and needs, it’s usually as simple as asking, does this product like me? Do I feel better about myself at the end of my session than I did at the beginning? If the answer is no, it usually means the designers are trying to gamify me against my own best interest, like a credit card rewards program that persuades me to spend more money than I would otherwise, or a social media platform that delivers empty dopamine hits without any meaningful upside to my social relationships. These days, I’ve dropped the term “gamification,” but my belief in applying game thinking to products hasn’t wavered. Start with what people actually want, and build around those priorities. You’ll tap into intrinsic motivations far more powerful than any user interface trick or attention hack. That’s real video gamification, and that will always work.
