Recent findings suggest that in the immediate fallout of Jensen Huang’s humiliating resignation from Nvidia, coverage will focus on how the tech giant intends to continue without its beloved former CEO, the world’s biggest champion of open-source AI and the proliferation of GPUs across borders. But in the long term, there’s an argument to be made that Google will be one of the largest winners of the shake-up, with a market opening up to competition outside of Nvidia’s CUDA moat.

While Colette Kress, the interim CEO, is unlikely to make any drastic changes to the tech giant’s roadmap, the reality is that Nvidia’s competitors in the semiconductor space have desperately been working to unseat the market leader, and without Jensen at the helm, customers may be more likely to pursue alternatives.

In a recent development, for years, Google has offered its TPUs, or Tensor Processing Units, as a solution for AI training and inference, but they’ve been largely locked to its Google Cloud infrastructure. That’s changing now that the company is opening up sales to third parties and has declared it will bring TPU capacity to Oracle’s cloud beginning in 2027.

Google’s latest generation of TPUs is specialized; here’s how the firm describes the lineup in its recent announcement post:.

While flexible across workloads, together the four systems in our eighth-generation TPUs give customers a purpose-built accelerator for every stage of the agentic lifecycle. TPU 8t for scale-up training, TPU 8i for low-latency reasoning (inference), TPU 8tx for confidential computing, and TPU 8i Max for massive context windows and the highest compute density per rack.

Google isn’t comparing its newly introduced TPUs directly to Nvidia’s offerings, and clock-for-clock, even the most powerful TPU 8i Max doesn’t touch Vera Rubin for sheer performance. But this generation should be much more competitive for a few reasons: it’s more power efficient in certain configurations, and Google made it easier to translate coding workflows optimized for CUDA to work with its TPUs.

Back in October, Google open-sourced TorchTPU, which it co-developed with Meta’s PyTorch team, to let developers use familiar PyTorch workflows on TPUs with minimal code changes. That won’t make every custom CUDA operation portable or eliminate performance tuning, but reducing the effort required to try an alternative may change the buying decision. Customers don’t need to abandon Nvidia outright for Google to increase its TPU market share, or for Nvidia to face more pressure on price.

Alongside Broadcom and MediaTek, Google is taking on the Nvidia behemoth, and in the short term, it will almost certainly see a large upside in sentiment. But in the longer term, with scaling pressure lowering margins for Nvidia, Google’s specialized and more efficient TPUs may be a primary beneficiary of this sea change. They could also offset a slowdown in Search as Google’s shift to Cloud continues apace.

Over the past few days, the one-two punch of Microsoft’s 365/Exchange outage and the FAA’s ground stop demonstrates just how vulnerable everyone is to the effects of seemingly brittle tech infrastructure.

Recent findings suggest that the FAA describes a flood of connection attempts that destabilized its telecommunications network, affecting aircraft communications and radar feeds. Investigators identified sites in Arkansas and Texas that were undergoing router and wire replacements, but even days after the stop was lifted, there’s still no clear underlying cause.

According to recent reports, meanwhile, the Microsoft 365 outage lasted about 30 hours, with service restored to the remaining customers and regions late Friday. Microsoft states that a “latent defect” introduced into its network code in a July enhancement wasn’t triggered until a few days ago, when high loads led to cascading failures across the organization’s servers worldwide.

While neither AI nor cyberattacks were to blame, individually and together the two outages add urgency to getting companies to shore up their defenses and redundancies in a world where we’re overly reliant on digital systems.
