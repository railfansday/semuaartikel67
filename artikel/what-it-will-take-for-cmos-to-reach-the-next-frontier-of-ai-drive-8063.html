What it will take for CMOs to reach the next frontier of AI-driven growth

In a recent development, CMOs at the largest enterprise brands across consumer goods, financial services, B2B and more are all struggling with the same thing: more AI technology has not translated into more business growth. They’re stuck chasing efficiency gains, funding tools that optimize tasks inside functional silos, running pilots that go nowhere and delegating AI decisions to IT teams.

But a few CMOs are doing it differently. Eric Schwartz, CMO of The Clorox Company, told me his marketers are building AI systems that produce highly optimized product content. They’re encoding brand voice, compliance guardrails and seasonal context into agents that work across every brand in their portfolio. Their goal isn’t to save time on doing what human writers can already do. Instead, they want to codify, scale and orchestrate the expertise that increases relevance, engagement and conversion with consumers. That’s a growth play.

Industry observers note that this is an operating-model decision that the CMO has to own: give marketers the strategic, system-building work and shift execution to AI agents. That takes much more than a chatbot built for a solo work experience.

It has been revealed that “I tell everyone that tries to pitch me about AI capabilities that the last thing I need is a 14th chatbot to save our marketers time,” Schwartz noted. “What I need is interoperable functionality.”.

In a recent development, schwartz isn’t alone. A vanguard of marketers are charting the frontier of AI-native marketing. To do this requires three fundamental shifts.

Employees are becoming more efficient with AI, but report that leaders aren’t asking them to reinvest the productivity gains into strategic work; they’re just expected to produce more output. Boston Consulting Group found that 66% of frontline employees receive limited or no guidance on using saved time, and more than half don’t reinvest it into strategic work.

Measuring AI success solely on efficiency invites efficiency’s reward: a smaller budget.

In a recent development, for example, one CMO’s AI-powered content system is saving the company 500 hours a month. But the company’s CFO saw the dashboard and asked: “If marketing can do this with 500 fewer hours, why does the budget stay the same?” The dashboard was supposed to prove value. Instead, it made the case for a cut.

A marketing team at a regulated financial services firm chose a different approach. The team was losing 360 hours a week to legal review bottlenecks, manual multi-channel content creation and constant product updates across 800-plus references. The team built a system with compliance encoded into the workflow and distribution automated across 25 channels. The result: 360 hours freed up weekly, 90% less editing time and zero agency spend.

The marketing team reinvested those hours into producing an award-winning ad campaign, not producing more of the same content. In a category where most consumers can’t name three financial lenders, breaking through the sameness drove brand recall, pipeline and market share. The system handled the production, but the humans did the work that won.

It has been revealed that the AI opportunity isn’t time saved. Frontier CMOs reinvest that time in architecture, curation and orchestration: designing agentic workflows, encoding brand voice and steering the system to drive growth. The humans who know best what makes their brand different turn saved time into a real advantage.

According to recent reports, historically, marketing and revenue have operated on separate tracks. In B2B, marketing fills the top of the funnel and hands off to sales. In consumer brands, marketing creates content while commerce manages the shelf. In financial services, brand marketing and the advisors who own the relationship rarely connect. In most organizations, no single team owns the moments that decide the sale.

Sixty-seven percent of B2B buyers now prefer a rep-free experience, according to data from Gartner. Consumers and bank customers are doing their own research too. In financial services, buyers are asking AI tools for recommendations before they ever talk to an advisor. These buyers aren’t waiting for a handoff.At the frontier, there are no handoffs. AI agents carry customer data, market signals and brand and compliance context between marketing and revenue teams in real time. Context doesn’t die at a cross-functional boundary. It compounds.

Schwartz described what this looks like at Clorox. The organization’s media ecosystem spans retail media, e-commerce and traditional retail, and the goal is to connect performance back to sales — or to the signals that point to sales. Most marketing organizations stop at creating and deploying campaigns. Schwartz wants that signal flowing back to show where to lean in and scale and to shape what gets created next. By closing that loop, a media budget compounds into growth instead of disappearing.

A CMO recently told me his organization has 40 active pilots, all marked green. Everything worked in the lab, but nothing survived contact with the organization. IT loved the tools, but marketing couldn’t use them.

This happens when AI adoption is delegated to IT. The CMO sponsors the budget but doesn’t own the transformation. In Writer’s 2026 survey, more than half of CMOs (52.3%) reported “significant knowledge gaps” about AI. Gartner predicts that by 2027, lack of AI literacy will rank among the top three reasons CMOs are replaced.

According to recent reports, at the frontier, the CMO owns the entire marketing AI agenda: selecting tools, redesigning workflows and deciding what should remain human-led. That means making AI adoption a cultural shift, not a software rollout. And that can’t be delegated.

Schwartz is an example of what this looks like in practice. He made AI adoption his own rather than delegating it to an innovation team or task force.

The CMOs making progress are changing how the work gets done, moving from traditional marketing operations toward a new model of driving growth with AI. They aren’t simply doing the same work with AI layered on top, but reimagining foundational structures, systems and strategies for a new era of marketing. The frontier isn’t a place a CMO arrives at.
