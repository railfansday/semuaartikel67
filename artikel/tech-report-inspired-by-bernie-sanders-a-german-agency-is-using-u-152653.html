Red, red, green — those are the party colors of the left-leaning coalition recently formed in the German state of Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania. Now a Berlin-based agency wants to see the same alliance between the center-left Social Democrats, the socialist Left Party, and environmentalist Greens win the 2029 national elections.

Industry observers note that the Goodforces wants to help make that a reality with their campaign neunund20 (twenty-nine), taking its cue, in part, from the American left. The agency was set up in response to the rise of the far right across Europe.

Co-founder Patrick Haermeyer told DW that the campaign's aim is "to give people hope and an alternative narrative to: Things are getting worse and worse." He worked as an online organizer for the election team of Bernie Sanders in the US Democratic presidential primaries in 2016 and cites Sanders' "not me, us" campaign as an inspiration.

It has been revealed that neunund20 currently has two strategies: It provides digital space for short-form videos and social media content on political and social topics on Instagram, Tiktok, and Bluesky. At the same time, it tries to connect content creators, activists, NGOs, and political initiatives around shared "progressive" issues.

Users with a particular interest can click to get a link giving them access to archives of relevant social media material that they can then reshare. That feature is modelled on the campaign of Newly introduced York's left-wing mayor Zohran Mamdani.

And there are other projects. The Goodforces was recently involved in a tactical voting initiative in the September elections in the state of Saxony-Anhalt, intended to keep the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) from winning an absolute majority. Haermeyer says physical campaigns of that kind are necessary to provide essential "interim victories" en route to the next parliamentary elections.

The Greens and the SPD, the two parties recommended by the app created by the organization, made it over the 5% hurdle and into parliament. The help that they received was declared by each party in the form of a €40,000 ($43,900) donation.

The agency sees itself as a kind of Super PAC, modeled on political organizations in the United States that collect money to carry out campaigns without directly supporting a single party or a special candidate.

For Christoph Bieber, political scientist at the University of Duisburg-Essen, the Goodforces is an interesting new type of player on Germany's political scene because it operates outside the country's political parties.

Recent findings suggest that but many of those parties are eyeing the development with disquiet. The far-left, populist, immigration skeptical Alliance for Social Justice and Economic Reason (BSW) criticized the Goodforces' intervention in Saxony-Anhalt, telling the newspaper FAZ that it was an "infantilizing, and perhaps even antidemocratic action." The neoliberal Free Democrats (FDP) called the tool "wrong and intentionally misleading." The FDP failed to clear the threshold, while the BSW only just squeaked in. The AfD gained 43% of the vote and its candidate may soon become Germany's first far-right state premier since the Nazi era.

Haermeyer set up the agency in 2024 along with Peter Jelinek, and both men previously worked for Green Party European Parliament member Michael Bloss. Ahead of that year's European elections, the EU's Green Deal began to come under increasing attack.

That year also ended with the collapse of Germany's first federal coalition between the SPD, Greens, and FDP, after much infighting and attacks from right-wing quarters.

According to recent reports, "We noticed that more and more successes and freedoms that we had fought for were being reversed: Things like freedom of movement, border checks, and other things that were coming more and more under fire," Haermeyer told DW. "And we noticed that it was primarily democratic and progressive forces that were being drawn into defensive battles.".

The AfD, founded in 2013, already in the age of social media, has successfully targeted younger voters with in its online campaigns. But the older parties, in particular, have struggled. The Goodforces saw a need for support in the online environment and is now seeking to plug this gap.

The social media accounts of the campaign neunund20 contain posts on domestic German topics, such as the current negotiations to form a red-red-green coalition government in Berlin, and the country's fresh military service law. They also highlight "wins" in Spain in the protests for affordable housing and for US Democrats in the midterms.

Haermeyer stresses that the issues the agency highlights are determined together with a wide range of grassroots organizations and activists. "The campaign is not agreed in conjunction with the SPD, the Left Party, and the Greens," he stated.

Agency funding comes from commissions and small donations. Haermeyer notes that contributions generally range from $20 to $50.

Bieber sees the emergence of non-party campaigning, long an accepted practice in the US, as a bid to compensate for the shortcomings of traditional parties.

"It is an omission of the parties," he said. "For years they have been saying we have to modernize, we have to become more open and get away from rigid membership structures. There are legal regulations governing the organization of political parties in Germany, but perhaps it could be more flexible and open.".

In a recent development, the center-left and center-right significant-tent parties have been in decline for decades. Membership of the SPD dropped from 943,000 in 1990 to some 348,000 at the end of 2025. Chancellor Friedrich Merz's Christian Democratic Union (CDU) has seen a fall from 750,000 to under 357,000.

Party membership is particularly low in former communist eastern Germany. Centrist parties have also struggled to attract younger voters. A report by the Deutsche Kinder und Jugendstiftung (German Children and Youth Foundation) highlighted that only 20% of young people felt their concerns were taken seriously in the 2025 German elections and that trust in parties was low.

In the United States, campaign financing comes predominantly from donations, and lobby groups can buy influence by contributing unlimited amounts to super PACs. In Germany, party funding comes, in part, from taxpayers and membership fees, and donations are capped. However, Bieber warns that whereas there are strict rules regarding the disclosure of donors to US Super PACs, these do not exist in Germany.

Sources indicate that the political scientist does not believe that US-style campaigning is going to take hold in Germany, but he does see potential dangers: "This could be a door opener to make others consider whether they want to go in this direction in the field of campaigning. And he added: "If it becomes rife, it could lead to a further devaluation of parties and their structures.".
