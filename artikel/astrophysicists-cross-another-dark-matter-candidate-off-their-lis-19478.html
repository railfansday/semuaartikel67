Astrophysicists Cross Another Dark Matter Candidate Off Their List: Stupendously Large Black Holes

If cosmologists ever identify what dark matter actually is, it may be because of a process of elimination.

According to recent reports, there are many potential candidates for dark matter, including WIMPs and axions, which are both hypothetical particles. But one of the most intriguing candidates are primordial black holes (PBHs) that formed in the early Universe. They're hypothetical too, but are massive objects, so their presence should be detectable in a way that particles aren't.

Industry observers note that all dark matter candidates have their supporters and their dectractors, and PBHs are no different. But PBHs have fairly well-defined search strategies that should detect them if they exist. If they exist, then cosmologists should find evidence of them in the cosmic microwave background (CMB).

The CMB is relic radiation from the Massive Bang itself, the oldest detectable light that exists. It was released when the Universe was only about 380,000 years old. For the most part, the CMB appears smooth and uniform, or isotropic. But increasingly powerful detectors let cosmologists examine the CMB for minor fluctuations. Those could show the presence of PBHs, or at least a subset of them called stupendously large black holes, or SLABs. SLABs, if they exist, are truly stupendously massive, with masses up to 10 billion billion solar masses. Yes, that's two billions one after another.

It's titled "Closing the Mass Window for Stupendously Large Black Holes," and the lead author is Christopher Gerlach. Gerlach is a theoretical physicist at Johannes Gutenberg University in Mainz, Germany, and its PRISMA++ Cluster of Excellence.

SLABs, if they're real, formed from overdensities of matter after the Massive Bang. They didn't need to form massive stars, burn their fuel, and explode as supernovae. They skipped that step, and are too massive for that, anyway. Since they're theorized to have formed so early, before massive objects like galaxy clusters, they should be the only things that left such an imprint on the CMB.

âIf these SLABs had existed in large numbers and contained significant amounts of dark matter, they would have left a characteristic imprint on the CMB through their unique perturbations, or, in a word, making it grainy," mentioned study co-author Nicholas Leister, also a JGU physicist.

Industry observers note that previous work has all but eliminated the possibility that regular primordial black holes could account for all dark matter. But what about SLABs? Have they survived the process of dark matter elimination, unlike their less massive cousins?

Recent findings suggest that to find out, the researchers first modelled SLABs and how they would affect the CMB. Then they compared the modelled effects to the actual CMB. The result was clear: the CMB doesn't have the characteristic graininess that SLABs should have imprinted on it.

"In this work, we have shown that the production of stupendously large PBHs would generate large DM isocurvature perturbations, tightly constrained by CMB and BAO data," the authors explain.

Recent findings suggest that the results also counter some other thinking regarding PBHs and dark matter. When the JWST discovered its notorious early massive galaxies, galaxies so massive that they challenged models of galaxy formation, it set the astrophysical community in motion searching for explanations. Astrophysicists cast their net wide, and one explanation they caught concerned PBHs. One 2022 study, titled "Accelerating Early Massive Galaxy Formation with Primordial Black Holes," suggested that PBHs make up a fraction of dark matter and were behind the JWST's early, massive galaxies.

But that's unlikely to be true, according to these results.

"They also challenge scenarios attributing the early massive galaxy candidates observed by James Webb Space Telescope to PBH-induced fluctuations," the researchers write.

A gravitational wave background hum pervades the Universe, sensed by Pulsar Timing Arrays, and some researchers asked if SLABs could be responsible.
