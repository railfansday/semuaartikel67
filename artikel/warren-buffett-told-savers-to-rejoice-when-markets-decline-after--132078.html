In a recent development, at first blush the suggestion is counterintuitive. A falling market is certainly nothing for investors to celebrate.

As has been the case so often since taking the helm at Berkshire Hathaway (BRKA +0.54%)(BRKB +0.37%) back in 1970, however, this bit of advice first dished out by Warren Buffett in his 1997 letter to Berkshire shareholders was eventually vindicated.

In a recent development, granted, it was three years too early. The tech-driven rally underway at the time carried on and even accelerated into the early 2000s. That's when it all unraveled. From that year's peak to 2002's trough, the S&P 500 (^GSPC -0.25%) fell on the order of 40%. Then five years following that multiyear low, the index had reclaimed everything it lost, rewarding those investors with enough forethought to celebrate the opportunity by diving into beaten-down stocks.

It certainly wasn't the first time something like this had happened either.

In a recent development, the irony? By the time the S&P 500 had doubled its 2002 low by mid-2007, the next cyclical headwind -- led by the subprime mortgage meltdown -- was already underway. Less than two years later, the index had lost a little more than half of 2007's peak value. And once again it was destined for a major recovery. Only this time, the S&P 500 would only need four years to recover, and would ultimately go on to quadruple its 2009 low before the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic finally brought the rally to a screeching halt in early 2000.

Industry observers note that even then, though, the pandemic-prompted bear market that pulled the S&P 500 down by one-third of its value would erase that loss before the end of the same year, en route to what would be a bull market worth 90% in less than two years. The 2022 bear market also dialed the index back by more than 20%, but the bull market since then is up more than 100% from the late-2022 bottom.

The point is, every major and minor setback the stock market has suffered so far has eventually been recouped, and then surpassed.

According to recent reports, it's not like Buffett doesn't take his own advice either. He famously bought a stake in then-deeply-discounted Goldman Sachs back in 2008, and locked in a 60% gain on this position in 2020. Berkshire added a bunch of now-privately-held construction materials companies between 2000 and 2002 as well, positioning it for a couple of waves of housing booms that would materialize in the meantime.

It has been revealed that the point is, while corrections and bear markets feel pretty miserable at the time they're happening, Warren Buffett is right -- they're a reason to rejoice, particularly if you missed out on most of the bull market that preceded them. Dips are a second chance to step in at a discounted price regardless of how big or small they are.

Industry observers note that just don't get too cheeky or clever when this window of opportunity opens. No one knows exactly when or where a bottom is going to be made, and plenty of investors have missed out because they were holding out for slightly better prices that never materialized. Any decent dip that offers an entry price you can live with in the long run is more than good enough.
