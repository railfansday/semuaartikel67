Industry News: Office light may throw off the body’s daily rhythm

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a study of day-active rats, an hour of simulated daylight at noon helped restore activity rhythms weakened by fluorescent office lighting.

People spend about 90% of their time indoors, under ceiling lights that are a small fraction as bright as the sky outside. The body reads that light as a biological signal.

In a rodent that keeps human hours, living under standard fluorescent office light weakened the animal’s daily rhythm of activity.

An hour of simulated daylight at noon each day, roughly analogous to a lunchtime walk outdoors, partly restored the rats’ daily activity rhythm.

In a second experiment, more of the male rats that spent six weeks under fluorescent light showed diabetic-range blood sugar than rats under daylight-strength light.

A smaller share of sperm were moving in the fluorescent-light group, about 19% compared with 45% under simulated daylight.

The study is small, with eight animals in the first experiment and 20 in the second, and all of them were male.

According to recent reports, lily Yan, a professor in the Department of Psychology at Michigan State University, led the work. The lead author is Antony Bum Kim of the Department of Architecture at the University of California, Berkeley.

“It is a biological signal that influences circadian organization and, potentially, a range of downstream health outcomes,” Yan said of light.

The body’s internal clock helps regulate daily patterns, known as circadian rhythms, in sleep, appetite, and much else.

Nearly every lab rodent is awake at night, which makes it a poor stand-in for a person whose body clock runs on daylight.

The Nile grass rat, a small African rodent, is active in the daytime, and Yan’s lab has used it for years to study how light affects the brain.

According to recent reports, over each cage, the team installed a programmable light that can imitate the color mix of many sources. Its daylight setting ran at about 5,600 lux, roughly the level that building designers treat as true daylight.

The office setting imitated a standard fluorescent tube at about 150 lux, the brightness of a typical indoor workspace.

Sources indicate that eight male grass rats lived under four lighting schedules in turn, two weeks each. Sensors logged their movement day and night.

Sources indicate that the first schedule was steady daylight from dawn to dusk. The second was daylight that brightened to the same midday peak and faded again, which delivered about half the total light.

Sources indicate that the third was fluorescent office light all day. The fourth was office light with one hour of full daylight at noon.

The rats were most active, and most sharply divided between a busy day and a quiet night, under steady daylight.

Under fluorescent light their activity was lower and their rhythm flatter, with its peak shifted later in the day.

The ramped daylight did not match the steady version even though it reached the same peak.

The authors wrote that the total amount of light over the day, more than its brightest moment, seems to set the strength of the rhythm.

Adding the noon hour of daylight to the office schedule raised the strength of the rhythm again, though only partly.

All eight animals showed a burst of activity during that hour. The authors called it a partial rescue.

A separate group of 20 male grass rats lived for six weeks under either steady daylight or fluorescent light, 10 in each group.

The rats’ body weight, belly fat, and average blood sugar did not differ between the groups.

But five of the 20 animals had blood sugar in a range the lab treats as diabetic, and four of the five had lived under fluorescent light.

Recent findings suggest that in a follow-up with a different way of drawing blood, the pattern held. Four of eight rats under fluorescent light crossed that line, compared with one of seven under daylight.

According to recent reports, the two groups produced the same number of sperm. Under daylight, about 45% of those sperm were moving. Under fluorescent light, about 19% were.

“Factors affecting health are not limited to drugs, diet and exercise,” Kim said. “The light environments we create in our homes, schools and workplaces are also biological exposures.”.

Ten animals per group is a small sample. More rats under fluorescent light crossed the study’s threshold for diabetic-range blood sugar, while average blood sugar was similar in the two groups.

The authors also wrote that blood drawn after euthanasia can read differently, which is why they ran the follow-up.

Every animal was male, so the study states nothing about females. And a grass rat under a programmable lamp is not an office worker with windows, a commute, and a phone.

The authors wrote that the findings support daylight deficiency as a risk factor for diabetes in this animal.

The sperm result is new, they wrote, since earlier work on light and sperm looked at day length rather than light quality.

What remains untested is whether the same shifts happen in people. The authors wrote that the next step is for other groups to repeat the work in grass rats or other day-active animals.

The next question is whether an hour of brighter light at midday could help people who work far from a window maintain a stronger daily rhythm.

The full study was published in the journal Journal of Biological Rhythms.
