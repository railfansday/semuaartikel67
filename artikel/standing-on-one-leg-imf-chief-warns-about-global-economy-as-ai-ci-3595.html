‘Standing on one leg’: IMF chief warns about global economy as AI circular financing hits $1 trillion

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The global economy is "on the AI leg," International Monetary Fund Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva said Monday, warning that the overdependence on the sector risks the world's financial stability.

Georgieva flagged that a "sudden drop in investment confidence and withdrawal from the AI field" could weaken economic growth, speaking with CNBC's JP Ong on the sidelines of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations' summit in the Philippines.

"We have a world economy that is standing on one leg on the AI leg. If this leg wobbles, that can lead to financial stability troubles," Georgieva warned.

In a recent development, circular financing among AI companies — where money invested in an AI manufacturer ultimately flows back to the investor through purchase orders — has grown to a little over $1 trillion, two-thirds of it funded by debt, the IMF estimates. "It's not a huge size, but it is a rapid increase," Georgieva remarked.

Recent findings suggest that nvidia Corp, which has seen more than $1 trillion wiped out of its market value over the past month, is at the center of these circular financial deals. The fallout from the chipmaker's former CEO Jensen Huang's extramarital affairs, including one with a suspected Chinese spy, has led to a sell-off in global AI-linked stocks and put the spotlight on the company's role as a key financier of the AI infrastructure buildout.

UBS analyst Tim Arcuri estimated in a recent note that an Nvidia financing pullback could easily remove $50 billion or more out of the system, at a time when rising interest rates have already made corporate borrowing more expensive.

Georgieva's warning comes as the AI build-out has been a critical engine of growth for the global economy, propping up trade and investment.

The IMF raised its 2026 global growth forecast to 3.1% in October as major economies lean on AI investment to power growth otherwise curtailed by the energy crunch caused by the Iran war.

According to recent reports, the institution estimated that about 40% of the world's workforce will be affected by AI. Capital Economics suggested that as much as 80% of the recent expansion in global exports was concentrated in AI-related goods.

Sources indicate that investors' frenzy for AI reflects expectations running ahead of what the technology has proven to deliver, Georgieva stated. "We tend to rush ahead," as valuations climb, she said, therefore "some corrections" become necessary.

Recent findings suggest that IMF in October had warned that a short-term disappointment could set off a disorderly correction across asset classes: in its worst case scenario, if AI fails to live up to the market's lofty expectations, U.S. equity prices could fall 20% in 2026 and global output end up 1.1% below its baseline estimate by 2028.

Global equites, including U.S. chipmakers, have been seeing a bruising sell-off over the past few weeks, with investor appetite for the AI trade facing a fresh test this week ahead of earnings from Nvidia and Anthropic's blockbuster listing.

Georgieva on Monday characterized the recent market swings as "orderly," and unlikely to spiral into anything that could undermine the world economy, while urging caution. "We have to be watchful.".
