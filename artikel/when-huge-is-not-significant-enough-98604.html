When Huge Is Not Significant Enough

Posted November 23, 2026 | Reviewed by Monica Vilhauer Ph.D.

Fans of the X-Men film franchise, which premiered in the long-ago year of 2000, were treated to the original version of Hugh Jackman’s now-longstanding portrayal of the character Wolverine. Appearing frequently shirtless in the movie, at the time Jackman seemed muscular enough to be super-heroic. He has played Wolverine nine more times in the decades since then, but rather than growing older and more relaxed, Jackman’s physique has seemed to develop in the opposite direction. Bigger, leaner, and more defined, Jackman could stand for an increasingly familiar male ideal.

Like him, many men spend hours in the gym trying to bulk up. They may carefully monitor their diets, timing their protein intake for maximum effect. They may indeed grow larger and more muscular; family, friends, and partners will notice. They may share images of weight-lifting transformations on social media, or even consider anabolic steroids or testosterone injections. Despite it all, some of these men will continue to see themselves as too small, too ill-defined, and not muscular enough.

It has been revealed that this condition is known as muscle dysmorphia, a form of body dysmorphic disorder (BDD) characterized by an unrelenting preoccupation with the idea that one is not muscular or lean enough. Even when a man is objectively well built, he may perceive himself as physically inadequate. It’s not common, but it’s real: a 2025 study of nearly fifteen hundred boys and men between the ages of 15 and 35 (Ganson et al., 2025a) concluded that the disorder likely existed in approximately 2.8% of those surveyed — across demographic groups, not only among weightlifters or athletes. (Indeed, a 2024 study in the Journal of Eating Disorders found higher muscle dysmorphia symptom scores among men who wanted to increase their body weight than among those who wanted to reduce it.).

Recent findings suggest that as I’ve suggested, while physical fitness is usually worth aspiring to, there’s a significant difference between ordinary, healthy fitness and muscle dysmorphia. Even when a man is objectively strong, “cut,” and full of muscles, he may look at himself in the mirror and see only weakness. This preoccupation can then lead to repeated, muscle-seeking behavior, such as checking himself in the mirror, researching new exercise routines, measuring body parts, or seeking new nutritional information. It can also involve excessive weightlifting, spending too much time at the gym, or taking on rigid dietary pursuits that can even become unhealthy or dangerous.

Industry observers note that but as all that exercise produces results, it never seems like enough: getting bigger just feeds back into the perception that they still aren’t significant enough. And when men with the disorder can’t soothe their anxiety by working out, they can feel real emotional distress, as well. The central characteristic of muscle dysmorphia is being absorbed by the perception that one is not muscular enough; it describes men whose daily routine and sense of self are dependent on the pursuit of muscularity, not those who just don’t like missing a workout (Cooper et al., 2020).

According to recent reports, these days, muscle dysmorphia is particularly associated with male body-image concerns, although women can experience it as well. As we’ve seen in Hugh Jackman’s transformation, men in popular culture are expected to be physically strong and imposing, with muscles sharply defined and standing out through their clothing. (So many superheroes!) Women face body image concerns as well, of course, but the pressure on women often focuses on losing weight and appearing thin rather than strong. For men, being muscular can stand in for physical attractiveness, competence, or even dominance.

It’s easy to find examples of these masculine norms on social media nowadays; just as in the past, we’ve been treated to male movie stars whose bodies are designed to show off a great deal of time in the gym; adherence to these norms has been associated with the symptoms of muscle dysmorphia (Grunewald et al., 2022). Research by Ganson and colleagues (2025b) supported this, finding that greater exposure to muscle-oriented social media content was associated with higher levels of probable muscle dysmorphia.

Filters, strategic lighting, and increasingly sophisticated photo-editing tools can offer further enhancement to these already extreme physiques, and sometimes the men in these social-media posts use performance-enhancing drugs, which pushes them still further out of the realm of the realistic. Overall, the fitness content on social media can complicate the distinction between healthy exercise and unhealthy physical enhancement. Psychological vulnerabilities may also play a role: in a 2024 study of men attending gyms, Kandemir et al. found that perfectionism helped explain the relationship between vulnerable narcissism and the risk of muscle dysmorphia.

According to recent reports, muscle dysmorphia can be harmful in a number of ways. Training too hard, even through pain, can lead to injuries — some of which can be lasting. Restricting one’s diet can also have serious consequences for one’s health, such as hormonal disruption (meaning reduced production of testosterone, among other things), reduced bone density, and an increase in the risk of fractures or hypotension (Mountjoy et al., 2023).

The obsession with being muscular can take its toll on one’s social life, too, when men seem to prioritize their nutrition or their workout schedules over their partners’ needs. Daily work obligations can be affected when muscle dysmorphia affects one’s ability to concentrate (Cooper et al., 2020).

Recent findings suggest that and of course there are the serious emotional consequences of feeling ashamed of oneself — like depression, anxiety, or self-hatred — because of body-image-related beliefs. Even when a man’s friends seem to admire their discipline or dedication, or comment in positive ways about the change in his physical condition, these would-be compliments can have a paradoxically damaging effect if they spur a man to try even harder to achieve his unrealistic muscular aspirations, or if they make his beliefs seem more reasonable than they are.

According to recent reports, as I’ve noted before, men may be comparatively less likely to seek help for emotion-related problems than women; when the topic is body-image concerns, they may be less likely still. Some may not even believe they have a problem, as the effort to become (and look) strong seems to be valued by their peers and by the culture overall (Brown et al., 2023).

Recent findings suggest that in therapy, however, it is possible to draw a subtle but meaningful distinction between a realistic, healthy body image and a source of compulsive striving. Irrational or distorted cognitions may be examined; other sources of self-worth can be identified. In other words, the effort to become “big” may also have an idiosyncratic meaning to each individual man, and this meaning stands a chance of being explored and identified in the context of the therapeutic relationship. (Medical intervention may become necessary as well, in cases involving potentially harmful medications or nutritional restriction.).

In the end, muscle dysmorphia creates a slippery slope: trying to be major seems to promise better self-esteem, but the effort only results in further, more extreme beliefs about how big one needs to be to achieve those benefits. The only real way out is to grow in a different direction — to learn that feeling good means accepting yourself as you are, and trying to live in a healthy way without wondering what you still need to change.
