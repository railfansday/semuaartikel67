It has been revealed that those roadside scales do more than weigh semitrucks. Here’s what inspectors check and what happens when a truck pulls into a weigh station.

Recent findings suggest that drivers pass truck weigh stations all the time, but few know everything these stations do or what happens when a truck pulls in. Once a semitruck exits and rejoins the highway, it’s hard to tell what goes on in between.

Learn what happens when a truck pulls in, what inspectors look for, and why some drivers can keep going.

It has been revealed that truck weigh stations help states enforce weight limits and spot safety problems. Trucks can weigh too much overall or carry too much weight on certain axles, putting extra strain on roads and bridges. On the Interstate System, the standard federal limits are 80,000 pounds gross vehicle weight, 20,000 pounds on a single axle, and 34,000 pounds on a tandem axle. Federal bridge weight rules and certain exceptions can also affect how much a truck may legally carry. Limits on other roads can vary.

Industry observers note that rules vary by state. “Some require all commercial motor vehicles to stop, while others require only those above a certain weight to stop,” according to the Federal Highway Administration. “Ultimately, States must certify that they are enforcing their weight limits on certain highways in order not to jeopardize their receipt of Federal highway funds.”.

Industry observers note that drivers who pull in follow signs directing them to a scale, where their trucks are weighed either while moving slowly or at a complete stop. If the weight is within the limits, they may be cleared to leave. Inspectors may still pull a truck aside for a closer look even when its weight is fine, based on screening results, visible defects, or random selection. An overweight truck may also be inspected further.

Inspectors may check a truck’s tires, brakes, lights, and cargo, along with the driver’s records. They may also review credentials and permits, such as registration and fuel-tax paperwork, along with the driver’s hours-of-service logs. “There are eight levels of inspections ranging from the Level I Inspection, which evaluates both the driver and vehicle, to inspection levels with a more specific area of focus, such as Level VI for radioactive materials and Level VIII for electronic inspections,” according to the Commercial Vehicle Safety Alliance.

In a recent development, drivers of overweight trucks may receive a citation or have to move or remove some cargo before leaving. The exact consequences vary by state and violation.

In a recent development, more serious safety problems, like faulty brakes, can put a truck or driver out of service. A truck placed out of service can’t return to the road until the problem is fixed. Drivers placed out of service must wait until their restrictions end.

It has been revealed that some trucks can skip the stop altogether through electronic bypass programs. These programs use safety records and other information to determine whether a driver can continue without entering the station. Drivers still have to follow any signs or signals telling them to stop.
