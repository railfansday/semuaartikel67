Microsoft wants to put a dollar value on AI’s work – but can you trust the math?

Industry observers note that we all know AI can get expensive. But does that expense result in real business value? That’s the trillion-dollar question Microsoft aims to answer with today’s introduction of Agent 365 Business Analytics.

Also: Microsoft indicates its new AI coding model rivals Claude Opus 5.5 at 5% of the cost.

Recent findings suggest that earlier this year, Microsoft introduced Agent 365, its tool for tracking agents the way an HR department would normally track human employees. With Agent 365 Business Analytics, Microsoft is attempting to build an accounting system for AI labor. The system attempts to calculate the economic value of the work performed by agents compared with what the agents cost, and then provide a return on investment estimate.

Recent findings suggest that if it works, this is some very cool stuff. Microsoft is trying to map labor-market economics onto AI usage data to create value estimates.

To do so, it uses real labor-costing information, including data from the US Bureau of Labor Statistics, the O*NET database from the US Department of Labor, and its own LinkedIn Occupational Graph.

This data, along with data gathered from Agent 365, is aggregated into analysis intended to answer:.

The goal is to provide a comprehensive picture of AI spend against value produced.

Sources indicate that software accounting is long established and straightforward. We measure subscription fees, cost per seat, compute costs, burden costs (like floor space and other infrastructure), and more. But AI work doesn’t fit that model, making it very hard to quantify value.

Instead, Agent 365 Business Analytics measures cost both relative to human labor and in terms of technological expense. For example, it might look at a task and determine how much human effort that task would normally take, and then price that effort based on prevailing common labor rates.

According to recent reports, then, it might factor in how much of the task was actually done by a human vs. how much was contributed by an AI, along with the estimated value of the AI’s contribution. Then it mixes in the actual AI processing cost (whether in subscriptions, tokens, on-premises infrastructure, and cloud support).

What comes out is a hybrid labor estimate that combines human contribution, human-replacement savings, AI costs, and then potentially AI savings over human costs. It sounds positively Orwellian. But the bottom line is that companies really haven’t been able to understand the AI bottom line.

According to recent reports, there’s a catch. All these numbers are estimates. In its blog post, Redmond specifically claims, “Assisted hours are a directional estimate of work AI helped complete, not measured time saved or financial return.”.

In other words, what Agent 365 Business Analytics estimates might not actually line up with real-world experience or expenses. It’s also possible that AI work might have been assigned because there was AI to do the work, not that the AI was saving manufacturer labor costs with every token processed.

In other words, Microsoft may be able to tell you the economic value of AI-assisted work, but it can’t demonstrate definitively that the organization saved any real money.

I find it particularly telling that this is from the firm that makes PowerPoint. Because now, with Agent 365 Business Analytics, it’s entirely possible that you could produce economic dashboards that look really, really impressive, but are entirely irrelevant to real life.

Recent findings suggest that that’s not to say the product is useless. But it does go to the idea that you will have to be quite disciplined in how you interpret the results and how much stock you put in them for decision-making. It is one source of possible truth, not the source of absolute truth.

But if it can get in there and manage your plumbing, help you control costs, and be the HR operation for your AI workforce, Microsoft could own a central piece of your business’s operations.
