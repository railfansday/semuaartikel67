After 3 Marathons Without a Strategy, I Finally Followed One. Here’s What Happened in NYC.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The first time I entered the Newly introduced York City Marathon drawing, I got in. I’m not sure what possessed me that day to apply, having never run another race in my life. I figured I’d never get in, so why not? I played soccer growing up, and when the world shut down during COVID-19, running became the easiest way to get out of the house and see my friends. That’s where my running journey began.

Industry observers note that my NYC Marathon training also happened during my senior year of college, which brought its own challenges. While I was excited to run, I also didn’t want to miss out on any of the socializing that happened that year. I did my best to make it to every party, every club soccer game, and anything in between while running as much as possible. I was scared that running was going to infringe on my everyday life and I wouldn’t have time to keep having fun. Despite all of that, I had caught the marathon bug and after finishing the New York City Marathon in 2022, I went on to run the Paris and Chicago Marathons in 2024.

Industry observers note that while training for those first few marathons, I didn’t have any real blueprint, except to not let it interfere with my social calendar. My strategy consisted of building up mileage in order to reach 20 miles about four weeks out from race day. In between, I pieced together training information from influencers on Instagram and Google searches (this was pre-ChatGPT!). Now that I’m a Runner’s World+ member, I have all those resources right at my fingertips.

Recent findings suggest that although I caught the marathon bug almost instantly, I also felt defeated after New York. I knew I could have done better. Between New York City in 2022 and Paris in 2024, I never ran more than 10 miles at a time, but I was running enough that I knew I could do it again if I really wanted. I followed a similar approach for Chicago in 2024: Build up to one long run about a month out, then fill the rest of the weeks with shorter runs and one medium-length run. It wasn’t much of a plan, but it worked for me. My times kept improving, and I broke four hours for the first time in Chicago.

Sources indicate that looking back, I realize that my no-plan approach was partly a way to protect myself. If I never said my goal out loud, I couldn’t feel disappointed if I missed it. Breaking four hours in Chicago changed that. For the first time, I wanted to see what I could do with a real plan.

In a recent development, I was now living in the city I had expected to call home ever since I left college, so I really wanted this race to feel meaningful. For the first time in my marathon career, I downloaded a Runner’s World training plan, the 16-week Break 3:45 plan to be exact, printed it out, and started tracking all of my runs. I wanted to beat my time in Chicago and get as close to 3 hours and 45 minutes as possible.

In a recent development, in the beginning, this felt super daunting. I had never done an interval run in my life nor had I ever intentionally sought out hills to make my runs feel harder than they already did. Nonetheless, I found my old Garmin Forerunner 55 and learned how to upload intervals to run up and down the West Side Highway. When the plan called for hills, I made my way back to the Queensboro Bridge for the first time since 2022, ready to face it with excitement instead of fear. Even while pretending to be excited, the incline is no joke!

It has been revealed that for a while, I really felt like I had gotten the hang of things. I actually started to recognize what different paces felt like during the intervals, instead of sprinting and pulling back and looking at my watch while I ran. And, I could now confidently run over many of the bridges in New York City, notably the Williamsburg and Manhattan, even though they aren’t even part of the marathon course. I wasn’t following the strategy exactly, but I was able to reorganize the days of the week to hit all the workouts.

Industry observers note that this was working! Until it wasn’t.

Near the end of August (roughly week eight of the strategy), it became hot all the time, I was constantly traveling for weekend trips, and I had started to lose the excitement of running. So while I was making sure to hit all of the long runs, the interval training fell off completely. I was a little disappointed in myself because the interval training runs were workouts that made me feel like I was getting stronger each week. I had never done this type of workout before, so I could tell I was getting better each time.

Recent findings suggest that to find the joy of running again, I really started to lean on my friends. Every week, I’d send out a few texts to see who was available and wanted to go for a run with me. I ran the loop in Central Park almost every week and met up with my friends in Brooklyn to run every Friday morning. I started to explore parts of the city I had never been to before, which helped ground me in my training and remember that running is supposed to be fun and is an exciting way to explore the city.

From my previous experience, I knew that running the long runs was the most important thing for me to feel confident on race day. So much of marathon running is mental, so knowing that I could run 20 miles (and have run 26.2 before), was enough for me. So, while I started with a plan, I started reverting back to my tried-and-true method—the just make sure you run the long runs plan.

Sources indicate that outside of the training proposal itself, I leaned on Runner’s World a lot. I didn’t have access to this in the past so I didn’t feel confident about planning for prerun meals or even how to take gels during the race. I also found that being able to read runner stories online made me feel more connected to the community, which helped me mentally with my training.

Proposal or no plan, I’m proud of myself! My official time was 3:57:39, so while I did not achieve my goal of breaking 3:45, I did run a sub-4 race, and following the Runner’s World plan helped me get there. The last time I ran this race, I was a senior in college and thought I had it all figured out. I was going to move to New York City, get a real adult job, and start the life I had been imagining. Similar to my experience following the Runner’s World plan, this is not how everything went. Instead, I moved to a different city after graduating, eventually found my way back to New York, and my life is so much more full than I ever expected because of it. Maybe things aren’t always supposed to go to plan, and that’s a good thing!
