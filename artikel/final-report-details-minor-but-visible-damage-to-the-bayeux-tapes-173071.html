Final Report Details Minor, But Visible Damage to the Bayeux Tapestry Following Trip to the British Museum

A final report released by France’s ministry of culture yesterday details where the Bayeux Tapestry sustained some minor damage following its transfer from Normandy to the British Museum, reports Le Figaro.

While the overall state of the nearly 1000-year-old tapestry was deemed “satisfactory” by the eight conservationists and experts working in France and England, who co-authored the report, they found there were “two zones of fiber breakage,” new folds and tensions, as well as “very small” damage that appeared in areas that were already weakened, prior to the voyage. News of an initial condition report about the tapestry showing some damage made headlines in September, but this is the first time a final copy has been seen by the media. ARTnews has reached out to France’s Ministry of Culture for a copy of the report, which was not shared via press release or posted on its website, as is customary with public announcements.

According to recent reports, in other details, translated from the French news story, experts reportedly observed that “a red wool thread in an embroidered area and another from the linen background cloth have undergone a slight progression.” Additionally, they spotted “slight folds and pockets,” as well as areas of tension in the embroidery and its linen support. For the latter, the tapestry’s horizontal display, in contrast with its previous vertical hang, should help fix the stretching glitch. The report also warns of areas to watch closely, particularly on the tapestry’s return trip to France in July 2027. These include several more pronounced folds, areas of wear, and a broken thread.

Sources indicate that the authors, who conducted their report in July and August, added that it’s not possible to know precisely when the changes occurred within the time span of the tapestry’s transfer from Bayeux to its current display in London. Additionally, they noted that this last check, including additional, highly detailed photographs of the artifact, is the most complete review of the tapestry to date. It was compared with two less thorough assessments made in 2020 and 2025.

“We now know the condition of the tapestry as never before, which will be useful later on,” noted Delphine Christophe, general director of heritage in France, speaking to Le Figaro. She added that the overall assessment, despite the noted damage, proved “exceptional,” given the fragile tapestry’s roughly 230-foot length and 20-inch height.

Industry observers note that the loan of the tapestry was heavily criticized in the months leading up to the handoff, due to risks of harming it. But after three failed attempts to secure a loan, it was ultimately made possible thanks to a carefully controlled ground transportation blueprint that was designed to eliminate almost all vibrations, aided by the political will of French President Emmanuel Macron. The latter was intent on making a strong diplomatic statement of friendship with the UK. In exchange, France will receive the Sutton Hoo archaeological ship burial and the Lewis Chessmen set from the British Museum. However, as Le Monde’s Roxana Azimi pointed out, “the Sutton Hoo treasure does not resonate at all with the French public,” and the French people “are getting objects that are certainly valuable, but not very impressive.”.

In a recent development, as for the Bayeux Tapestry, it is believed to have been made in Canterbury following the coronation of William the Conqueror as king of England in 1066. It tells his side of the Norman Conquest, which was one of the most pivotal moments in English history. Meanwhile, British Museum director Nicolas Cullinan responded frankly to Azimi’s point in Le Monde. “Yes,” he said. “It’s very hard to beat the Bayeux Tapestry.”.
