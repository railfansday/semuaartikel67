Industry News: Theaster Gates is Changing the Future of American Fine China

It all started when Theaster Gates wanted to throw a dinner party. The Chicago-based artist, whose practice blends ceramics, painting, performance, and urban renewal, imagined inviting 200 guests on each of five nights. Central to the project, he envisioned creating all the tableware himself.

Recent findings suggest that “My friends were like, ‘Oh, you should work with Pickard,’ ” Gates recalls, referring to the last remaining producer of porcelain tableware in the US, founded in 1893 and headquartered since 1930 in Antioch, Illinois. When he told Pickard he would need some 10,000 items, the firm admitted such an order would be daunting but invited him to visit.

“I came to the warehouse and realized that the manufacturer had had better days,” Gates says. “I’m wanting to say this with a lot of dignity, but as we moved between tarps and equipment, it became clear that the company was just doing what it could to stay alive.”.

China dishware fills two rolling racks at the Pickard factory in Illinois, which was recently purchased by Theaster Gates.

Gates at the Pickard facility with molds for dishware.

Sources indicate that pickard’s august history of commissions includes White House services for first ladies Laura Bush and Michelle Obama, as well as the official State Department dinnerware used in US embassies around the world. Once an essential element of bridal registries, fine china has waned in popularity, however, along with the tradition of formal entertaining at home. After CEO Andy Pickard Morgan, a member of the fourth generation of the family to run the business, acknowledged that he was ready to call it a day, one thing led to another, and Gates notes, “I am now the owner of Pickard China.”.

Industry observers note that his acquisition might seem out of character. Gates has forged a formidable reputation for himself and his Rebuild Foundation by buying rundown buildings in Chicago’s historically Black South Side, then converting them into thriving arts and education hubs. A onetime drug den is now the Black Cinema House; a century-old savings and loan is now the Stony Island Arts Bank, which mounts exhibitions and safeguards historical collections. Pickard, meanwhile, is located in a quiet, predominantly white village on the Wisconsin border, some 67 miles from his studio on Chicago’s South Side.

A Pickard artisan finishing a plate.

Gates, funnel in hand, surrounded by artifacts of the Pickard process.

Pickard pieces line plywood racks.

But Gates does not see a contradiction. For starters, he notes, his interest in craft transcends racial or ethnic origins; one formative experience was studying with master potters in Japan. Gates notes that while many of his collections relate to Black culture, he also took on the late scholar Robert Bird’s library of films by the Russian director Andrei Tarkovsky and the classical music archive of the LaSalle Quartet, whose core members were Jewish. “I have those because they are rich, and someone passed, and those things needed a newly introduced life,” he explains. “The problem of the archive is not one of Black or white; it’s one of stewardship and imagination.” He has even shown an affinity for luxury goods, having made ceramic vessels for Prada Home in Milan this past April.

Pickard also presents the kind of major, multifaceted problem Gates, who holds a master’s degree in urban planning, indicates “makes me feel alive as an artist,” in this case encompassing craftsmanship and legacy, as well as the value of labor and community. His intends are still developing but include upgrading the facilities, rethinking the product line, and beefing up the staff, which now stands at 14 full-time employees, down from dozens at the firm’s height. Preserving their know-how for posterity is a priority. “I want this really capable team to train a fresh generation of descalers and liners and glazers,” he says. Also definite: The company will continue operating in the commercial sphere, and he will use its massive kilns to fire his own large sculptures, something he’s wanted to do for a decade. “I’m thinking about manufacturing ceramics as an extension of my practice,” he notes.

A Pickard artisan filling molds.

The Pickard logo emblazoned on a wall.

Gates has worked with a variety of materials over the years, including tar paper—a nod to his roofer father—and salvaged fire hoses, which reference Civil Rights Era marchers who were blasted with water by police. Ceramics, though, were the focus of his formal studies and remain at the heart of his practice. “When I was training, I touched porcelain, but I was always a rough stoneware guy,” he indicates. “The more grog and sand and feldspar, the better.”.

Today Gates considers porcelain “an amazing craft” and expects the workshop to become “a playground for artistic promise,” where he, together with staff and visiting artists alike, can “dream about a raw material that could be anything.”.

Setting the table with bone china may no longer be the signifier of position and civility it once was, but Gates believes there’s still room for appreciation, as both an instrument of ritual and a family heirloom. “Legacy objects play a huge role in how we remember our grandparents, our parents, and our past,” he claims.

And Gates’s own party is very much still on the table, so to speak. “Porcelain things are beautiful objects, but I’m drawn as much to the use of the thing as I am to the thing,” he says. “The idea that I could make large works of art with porcelain is exciting, but I’m also excited to be able to host a thousand-person dinner and make all the wares for it.”.
