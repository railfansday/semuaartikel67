Silicon Valley divorce lawyers are seeing surge in outreach from CEOs, founders, and high-net-worth employees who are citing the Nvidia scandal as the reason for their calls.

The revelation of Jensen Huang’s affairs, and Nvidia’s board subsequently firing him from the tech giant he founded, is a “precautionary tale for other CEOs,” remarked one lawyer who has been involved in the high-profile divorces of some of the tech industry’s most famous CEOs.

Although Huang’s board reinstated him on Monday in a controversial move, the roller coaster of events has pushed other CEOs to think about how their assets are connected, and potentially at risk, as a result of their personal relationships. In addition to pursuing prenuptial agreements, many are interested in drafting nondisclosure agreements (NDAs) for their significant others, which is a latest trend, claims Monica Mazzei, a partner in Buchalter’s San Francisco family law office.

“It’s totally changed the landscape,” stated Mazzei. “I’ve never seen this before. This was definitely major news in the family law space.”.

According to recent reports, since the Nvidia scandal broke, three clients have contacted Mazzei with requests to draft NDAs for the significant others of CEOs. They specifically cited Huang in the outreach, mentioned Mazzei, who specializes in representing high-net-worth clients. Most of the requests are coming from the firm, not the CEO themselves, in an effort to protect the business.

“The tech giant is probably telling the CEO, ‘Anyone you need to date needs to sign this because we don’t want you to end up in a Jensen Huang situation,’” Mazzei remarked.

Sources indicate that huang had at least three known affairs, two with internal Nvidia employees, and one extra controversial relationship with Alice Huang (no relation), an executive at the Singapore-based organization Megaspeed. Alice Huang is reportedly suspected by some intelligence officials of being a Chinese spy and of illegally providing Chinese companies access to advanced Nvidia chips. The allegations are that Alice Huang is a “honeypot” who lured Jensen Huang into a relationship to get access to proprietary information.

“Why honeypots work is because when you’re dating someone you’re not thinking about what they might overhear on a phone call, or see on papers lying out at home,” mentioned Mazzei. “They’re just in your life, overhearing a lot.”.

Demand for prenuptial agreements has also surged. Jensen and Lori Huang have been married for 41 years, and reportedly do not have a prenuptial agreement. Jensen Huang is one of the richest people in the world, with a net worth of roughly $137 billion, according to the Bloomberg Billionaires Index. According to California law, Lori stands to come away with half of that in a potential divorce, along with shares in Nvidia.

It remains to be seen if Lori Huang will file for a divorce. If the couple were to decide to split up, Lori and Jensen Huang would most likely agree to a settlement prior to the divorce proceedings becoming public, says Mazzei. Once the terms are finalized, Mazzei reckons, the couple would then go public with the news, as Jeff Bezos and Mackenzie Scott did.

Libby Leffler, founder and CEO of thisfirst.com, an online prenup service, states she’s seen more customers naming tech billionaires and founders as the inspiration for their agreements since the Nvidia scandal broke. Most of Leffler’s clients are founders and early employees at pre-IPO companies, especially in California and Upcoming York. Every time a public figure’s divorce or prenup makes headlines, Leffler’s inbound requests jump, but the naming of tech CEOs this time is different.

“People used to name-check celebs, but now they are name-checking tech CEOs,” Leffler tells Fortune. “What they really mean is that they want to feel as protected as they assume those people are.” The most referenced arrangement is Jeff Bezos’s rumored prenuptial agreement with Lauren Sánchez, who became his second wife in 2025.

“Your relationship and your cap table are more connected than people think, and founders are seeing that play out,” Leffler mentioned.

According to recent reports, mazzei also noted a surge in prenup requests, specifically coming from Anthropic employees. When the firm went public at a $1.5 trillion valuation on Nov. 19—a week after Huang’s ousting—it minted thousands of multimillionaires among the company’s relatively small staff of 5,300. The average fortune of its non-founding staff is roughly $44 million, although nearly 600 pocketed at least $100 million.

Recent findings suggest that mazzei could not link the Anthropic employees’ requests to Nvidia directly, although the Huangs’ lack of a prenup may have spooked them. In general, many of them are on the younger side, and Mazzei noted that among younger generations prenups are becoming more common.
