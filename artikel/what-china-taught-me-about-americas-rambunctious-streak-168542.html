I've long wondered whether a rambunctious toddler is more likely to develop a balanced temperament than a reticent tike. Is a quiet kid more likely to learn boldness than a bold child is to learn when to shut up?

I bring that up because, having just visited China, I've been thinking about the trajectories of our almost opposite nations.

The Chinese are a much more cooperative population than we are. It reveals up even in the fluid dynamics of the society.

I spent a lot of time walking through non-touristy streets. That's not enough to make me an expert, but I did form impressions. People flow around each other more fluidly than they do here. They're not distracted by each other. They don't nod or look each other in the eye. They simply assume each other. There seems to be no stigma in being glued to a phone.

It has been revealed that they're less thirsty for attention. They aren't busy reassuring each other or trying to stand out. They strain less to look and feel exceptional. There were plenty of stylish young people, but not to the extent we see here. Even the ads are less glam.

According to recent reports, it stands to reason. This is a huge, dense urban population whose parents and grandparents, a generation or two ago, were mostly peasants. Their society was shaped by Confucianism 2,500 years ago, a philosophy that is more about etiquette than anything else, at least in its popular form.

Yes, the Chinese have at times gone terribly off the rails. Indonesia also struck me as an extraordinarily tolerant society, yet 60 years ago anti-Communists there slaughtered over a million people. Societies can change. But today China is a mild child. We're the thirsty, intense, fanatical, exceptionalist, rambunctious one.

We distract ourselves with obscene amounts of self-flattery and pandering in commerce and politics. The midterms alone cost 12 billion dollars. All that pandering and flattery doesn't make us wiser. It makes us more anxious and delusional.

We've just about used up the earth's carrying capacity for self-flattering nonsense. Meanwhile, the demand for flattery and pandering is only getting worse.

In China, there's so little of it, at least so far. That could change, as it did in Indonesia after I visited eight years ago, with the rise of Islamic fundamentalism.

Industry observers note that china doesn't go through the motions of national elections. It's one-party rule, with no pandering and a focus on practical welfare that, on my visit, went far beyond what we see here. They proposal to double their train lines by 2035. There's practically no theft in the city center, and the cameras are everywhere, so you'd get caught and punished. China's Gini coefficient, a measure of wealth inequality, is around 35. Ours is about ten points higher.

On my two trips there, I met only one Christian. It's an atheist country, and you can't talk about the supernatural as real. There's none of our jersey-wearing, lifestyle-brand supernaturalist grandstanding and infighting.

Recent findings suggest that people grumble about the lack of freedom of speech, but they can grumble without looking over their shoulders. At Peking University, built on the land of the emperor's Summer Palace and just a few blocks from the government center, a professor told me that, paradoxically, they have more freedom of speech there. In distant cities, you have to attend a Communist Party meeting every two weeks. At the university, you just fill out minutes. There are limits, though. You can't talk casually about Mao on social media, and you can't call him a dictator. They're trying to put the violence of the 1966-76 Cultural Revolution behind them, along with the poverty. In 1976, China's average income was a third of Sub-Saharan Africa's. They've come a long way. Maybe they'll keep going.

If you're not watching China, you should be. Odds are they will surpass us, and Pax Americana will give way to Pax Sinica. Since I study American fanaticism, I could get a distorted view of humankind. China makes me think humankind might pull off some kind of harmony. It fills me with optimism about human potential.

And maybe we'll learn from China's success. Maybe the rambunctious child will learn to chill out. We had better.

Maybe we'll converge on some mix of reticent and rambunctious that makes humankind survivable.
