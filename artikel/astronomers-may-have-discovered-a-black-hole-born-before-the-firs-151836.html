Astronomers may have discovered a black hole born before the first stars

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, on December 18, 2019, astronomers recorded something unusual happening to a star in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a satellite galaxy of the Milky Way. For approximately one hour, the star gradually grew brighter before fading back to its usual appearance. The change was smooth and symmetrical, with no sudden explosion or flare. It looked as though an invisible object had moved between Earth and the star, bending its light in our direction. Afterward, the star returned to normal and revealed no further changes in brightness.

Researchers have named the mysterious object Phoebe, but its true identity remains unknown. The event appears to be an example of gravitational microlensing, a phenomenon predicted by Einstein's general theory of relativity. When a compact object with sufficient mass passes in front of a distant star, its gravity bends the star's light and temporarily magnifies its brightness. This produces a recognizable pattern that differs from the changes caused by variable stars, stellar flares, or passing asteroids.

Astronomers at Swinburne University in Melbourne identified the signal while examining observations from a survey that frequently monitored stars in the Large Magellanic Cloud. The distinctive pattern convinced the team that they had detected a genuine gravitational microlensing event. What remained uncertain was the nature of the invisible object responsible.

Three Possible Explanations for the Mysterious Object.

Researchers have identified three potential explanations for Phoebe. One possibility is a rogue planet that was expelled from its original solar system and now travels alone through the Milky Way. Another is a similar wandering planet located within the Large Magellanic Cloud. If the second explanation proves correct, Phoebe would become the first planet discovered through gravitational microlensing beyond our galaxy.

The third possibility is far more extraordinary. Phoebe could be a primordial black hole, an ancient object that formed in the earliest moments of the universe. Unlike ordinary black holes, which develop when massive stars collapse, primordial black holes could have emerged from extremely dense regions of space during the first fractions of a second after the Major Bang. Such objects would have formed long before the first stars appeared.

A One-Hour Signal Reveals a Surprising Clue.

The unusually short duration of the brightening provides an key clue about Phoebe's mass. Gravitational microlensing events depend partly on how massive the intervening object is. Generally, lighter objects produce shorter events, although their speed and position also influence how long the magnification lasts.

It has been revealed that phoebe's signal lasted approximately 60 minutes, placing it near the detection limit of current astronomical surveys. Using the observed duration and their models of the event, researchers estimated that the object has a mass roughly three times that of the Moon. That would make it considerably less massive than any planet in our solar system and far too small to be a conventional black hole formed by a collapsing star.

Black holes produced through stellar collapse typically have masses of at least about five times that of the Sun. Phoebe's estimated mass falls enormously below that threshold. If the mysterious object really is a black hole, its tiny mass would point toward a primordial origin rather than the death of a star.

Could Phoebe Be a Primordial Black Hole Linked to Dark Matter?

In a recent development, to investigate where Phoebe might belong, the researchers compared the likelihood that the object originated from different populations. These included stars in the Milky Way, stars in the Large Magellanic Cloud, and objects associated with the surrounding dark matter halo.

The results strongly favored the dark matter halo. According to the team's calculations, Phoebe is approximately 100,000 times more likely to belong to this population than to one associated with ordinary stellar matter. This enormous difference makes a potential connection to dark matter particularly intriguing, although it does not establish that Phoebe is a primordial black hole.

Recent findings suggest that if the primordial black hole interpretation is correct, Phoebe could be among the oldest objects ever detected. It may have formed before the first atoms and stars existed, when the universe was still experiencing the extreme conditions that followed the Major Bang.

For roughly 13 billion years, such an object could have traveled unseen through space, leaving almost no trace of its existence. Then, on a single night in December 2019, its gravity may have briefly magnified the light of a distant star, offering astronomers a remarkable glimpse of something born near the very beginning of the universe.
