Industry News: Benefits need to change with the times

🌐 Source: Fastcompany • 📅 16 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 6 min read (1253 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways
  • Over the next five years, AI will change the people companies need and the work they do.
  • Industry observers note that over the next five years, AI will change the people companies need and the work they do.
  • According to recent reports, the change is already underway.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Over the next five years, AI will change the people companies need and the work they do. At the same time, medical costs will keep taking more of the benefits budget. Employers will have to rethink what it means to take care of their people. The change is already underway. Agents write code, run tests, and carry out research assignments. OpenAI reports that an internal model has resolved more than 100 long-standing math problems. For many companies, though, there’s still a gap between giving individuals better tools and changing how the business runs. Agents need access to firm information, permission to act, and a way to get help when something goes wrong. Research co-led by Chicago Fed economist Ezra Karger points to uneven adoption as a reason technical progress takes time to reach the economy. Here’s what I anticipate: By 2028, expect more tasks to be handed to AI, with people checking the results. By 2030, companies will reorganize around that work. By 2032, agents will carry assignments across departments, bringing people in when a decision or approval is needed. WORK CHANGES AT DIFFERENT SPEEDS Software, law, and consulting will move fastest. As coding, research, and drafting take fewer hours, people will focus more on deciding what matters and checking the work. Firms will have to figure out how people learn to become experts when the work that used to teach them has been automated. Stanford research already finds weaker hiring for young workers in AI-exposed jobs, although it does not establish causation. Physical work will change more slowly. Better planning helps a manufacturer, but someone still has to maintain the equipment. Patients still need nurses, and homes still need electricians. Anthropic’s scenarios show how cheaper knowledge work can support demand for hands-on work. MEDICAL COSTS KEEP CLIMBING Meanwhile, healthcare keeps getting more expensive. Aon expects costs to rise 9.5% in 2027 before employers take steps to offset the increase. CMS projects health spending growing faster than GDP through 2034. KFF finds workers’ contributions toward family coverage rose 9% this year. Employers can spend considerably more and still deliver bad news at open enrollment. Employees see a bigger deduction from their paycheck. Explaining that the increase would otherwise have been worse doesn’t make it feel like a better benefit. Routine assignments will take fewer hours, and companies will depend more on people with experience. Losing someone with hard-earned judgment or trusted customer relationships will be harder for a lean team to absorb. Good medical coverage remains essential. But competing on medical alone is becoming a losing proposition. Employers are trying to get medical spending under control through allowances for individual insurance, higher-deductible aims, and medical captives that pool large-claim risk. These approaches change how costs and risks are shared. They don’t stop medical inflation. LIFE NAVIGATION GIVES PEOPLE PRACTICAL HELP Non-medical benefits offer more room to make each dollar count. In MetLife’s survey published earlier this year, 73% of employers called them the most cost-effective way to support employee well-being. One way to put that support into practice is life navigation: someone who helps find care for a parent, sort out a disputed bill, or handle a family emergency. The idea is simple. Give people one place to turn. Someone will do the research, make the calls, and stay with the problem until it’s sorted out. AI can handle routine legwork, but human support still matters when life gets complicated. Leave and PTO, housing assistance, income protection, and financial well-being support helps employers stand out. So does life navigation. People remember who helped when life got hard. That matters when deciding where to work—and whether to stay. Jon Cooper is CEO of Overalls.
