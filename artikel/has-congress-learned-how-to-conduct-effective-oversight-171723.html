Has Congress Learned How To Conduct Effective Oversight?

Industry observers note that democratic leaders have promised that their newly introduced congressional majorities will focus on affordability and accountability. They have also pledged to engage in extensive oversight and investigation of the Trump Administration, and firms and individuals that have operated within the White House's orbit.

It has been revealed that listening to some talk about their intends, such as in this Ezra Klein interview with incoming House Oversight Chair Robert Garcia, I wonder whether House Democrats have learned that Congress's oversight and investigation authority is not as all-encompassing as some might think. The authority to conduct inquiries for legislative purposes is broad, but it has limits. And during the first Trump Administration, the failure of House Democrats to contend with those limits hampered oversight efforts.

It has been revealed that I discuss the legal contours of Congressional oversight and investigatory authority in my latest Civitas Outlook column. In this piece, I explain why Trump v. Mazars is the greater constraint on free-wheeling fishing expeditions than is the Trump immunity decision.

Nothing in the Constitution expressly grants Congress the power to investigate executive branch agencies, let alone the President (or anyone else, for that matter). The authority to conduct oversight is an adjunct to Congress's legislative powers. In the simplest terms, it is often necessary and proper for Congress to secure information and conduct inquiries as it considers legislation and makes appropriation decisions. Or, as the Court explained in Mazars, this "power of inquiry—with process to enforce it—is an essential and appropriate auxiliary to the legislative function." , , ,.

According to recent reports, no justice in Mazars endorsed the then-common view that Congress could demand all information it sought, subject only to the constraints imposed by Executive Privilege. Rather, the justices were unanimous that Congress lacks the power to investigate for investigation's sake, or to conduct oversight for the purpose of public disclosure or inducing prosecution. Congress has no authority to try private citizens in a committee hearing. As the Chief Justice explained in his opinion for the Court, congressional subpoenas are valid only insofar as they are "related to, and in furtherance of, a legitimate task of the Congress." More generally, congressional subpoenas must serve "a valid legislative purpose," and Congress bears the burden of demonstrating this standard is met. Document demands that appear designed more to harass or punish the political opposition than to inform legitimate legislative activity will not be enforced.

Insofar as Congress wishes to investigate the White House or high-ranking officials, its greatest investigatory authority is not the power to inquire for legislative purposes. Rather, it is the power to impeach. In practice, this means some of the greater restraints on Congressional oversight are political, rather than legal.

Industry observers note that while the Court's decision in Mazars focused on the connection between document demands and the legislative process, this is not the only path Congress may take. The Constitution grants Congress the power to enact legislation for various purposes. It also delegates the power to impeach, and these powers are distinct. As Judge Neomi Rao explained when the Mazars case was before the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit, "investigations of impeachable offenses simply are not, and never have been, within Congress's legislative power." They stand on their own bottom.

Sources indicate that this distinction matters, because if Congress wishes to investigate potentially impeachable offenses, it must be clear that is what it is doing so voters can evaluate its actions accordingly. "Allowing Congress to investigate impeachable officials for suspicions of criminality pursuant to the legislative power has serious consequences for the separation of powers because it allows Congress to escape the responsibility and accountability inherent in impeachment proceedings," Judge Rao warned. This wolf must come as a wolf.

In a recent development, and on Trump v. U.S., I take a different view than some other legal commentators:.

Some commentators fear that the Supreme Court's presidential immunity decision in Trump v. United States will hamper otherwise legitimate congressional inquiries. "Congress cannot act on, and courts cannot examine, the President's actions on subjects within his 'conclusive and preclusive' constitutional authority," the Court declared. While this insulates the President from criminal prosecution for actions within the core of executive authority and would accordingly limit the Congress' ability to subpoena related information for legislative oversight purposes, it is no shield in matters of impeachment.

Aggressive Congressional oversight of the executive branch is not upcoming. Nor is the politicization of oversight. Once political parties emerged, it was inevitable that party loyalty would rival (if not overcome) each branch's institutional imperatives. Oversight fights were once more political than legal, but Congress could also defend its prerogatives without invoking the courts. A Congress that legislates and appropriates through regular order has more weapons to deploy and a greater ability to force executive branch acquiescence. Insofar as the next round of oversight fights are resolved in court, it will be yet another sign of Congress's decline.
