Adecco CEO: Only 19% of workers think their bosses get AI’s toll. This is the leadership test of this generation.

🌐 Source: Fortune • 📅 19 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 6 min read (1190 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways
  • Recent findings suggest that only 19% of workers say their leaders are empathetic to the effects of change.
  • According to recent reports, the AI debate is entering a more serious phase.
  • It has been revealed that AI’s productivity prize will not come from rolling out more tools alone.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that only 19% of workers say their leaders are empathetic to the effects of change. That finding, from our latest Global Workforce of the Future research surveying 40,500 workers across 36 countries, should make every leader stop and think.

According to recent reports, the AI debate is entering a more serious phase. The first wave focused on what AI could do. The second focused on massive jobs replacement. What matters now is more pragmatic: which organizations can turn AI into lasting productivity, resilient operating models and better work? How do leaders prepare themselves to accompany their people and organizations to create value?

It has been revealed that AI’s productivity prize will not come from rolling out more tools alone. It will come from redesigning work, skills, governance and operating models. Technology may be the catalyst, but workforce readiness will determine how much value companies actually create.

AI needs to happen with people and not to people. This is the leadership test of this generation.

The evidence gives us reason for optimism, but not complacency. Labor market data tells a more nuanced story than the job apocalypse headlines of earlier this year. AI is not triggering a collapse of human work. It is reshaping how work is organized, performed and governed. Employment remains resilient across most advanced economies – OECD employment rates remain near record highs. Large-scale AI-driven job losses just have not materialized – with impact on tasks rather than jobs. Most companies are still at the beginning of their AI journey, experimenting with tools and trying to understand where value can be created.

According to recent reports, disruption is not insignificant but still far from the scale suggested by some narratives. Entry-level hiring in AI-exposed knowledge work is under real pressure, particularly in software and customer service, but we also see positive signals. Our placement data collected in 23 different markets points to a clear shift in demand: over the past nine months, entry-level placements in AI-exposed functions declined by 2.2% year on year, while demand for highly skilled white-collar profiles rose by 28%. A credible people strategy needs to hold both realities at once: confidence that work is not disappearing at the same time as urgency about how swiftly tasks, skills and career pathways are changing.

Sources indicate that the more important shift is not the replacement of people. It is the redesign of work itself. The organizations that act fastest will turn AI into a lasting competitive advantage.

According to recent reports, history is instructive here. General-purpose technologies like steam power, electricity, and information and communications technology did not transform productivity simply because organizations adopted them. Their value emerged only once businesses redesigned workflows, jobs, skills and management practices around them.

Industry observers note that AI will be no different. Buying technology is relatively easy. Changing how an organization operates is the hard part.

According to recent reports, in terms of work redesign, this requires a shift from workforce management to hybrid workforce orchestration: the discipline of continuously designing, coordinating and evolving work so that people and AI agents contribute where they create the greatest value. Intelligent systems can deliver speed, scale and pattern recognition, while people provide judgment, creativity, trust, accountability and human connection.

The World Economic Forum estimates that 59 out of every 100 workers will need training by 2030. Of those, 29 can be upskilled within their current role, 19 can move into new opportunities with the right support and 11 risk being left behind. That makes the central challenge a strong focus on access to learning while creating robust career pathways. The Adecco Group provides training, upskilling and redeployment services, so we see these transitions up close, every day.

Workforce readiness and cultural resistance are now major barriers to adoption. Overcoming them will be a defining leadership challenge in the years to come.

The stark reality is that many organizations are moving faster with technology than their people’s understanding and confidence, creating a readiness gap that slows transformation and hurts productivity.

Our latest research exposes a striking contradiction at the heart of AI adoption. Three quarters of workers now use AI at work, yet formal training completion has fallen by 10 percentage points in a single year. The workforce is broadly open to change, but organizations are falling short on the conditions that make it possible: only 36% explain to employees how AI creates opportunity for them, and only 39% involve workers directly in job redesign.

Recent findings suggest that the benefits are already becoming unevenly distributed. Workers who have completed AI training are 51 percentage points more likely to feel comfortable working alongside AI, and 96% of those involved in redesigning their roles are willing to adapt to change, versus 29% of those excluded from the process.

Sources indicate that this is the leadership bottleneck in the transition. Just 21% of workers feel concerns about fresh technologies are taken seriously and only 39% feel supported by their manager to adapt.

The implications for the C-suite and investors are significant. AI readiness cannot be measured by technology spend, license numbers or chatbot adoption alone. It must be judged by whether AI is improving productivity, strengthening workforce agility, building trust, increasing skills resilience and helping organizations to redeploy talent as work evolves.

We should therefore stop treating productivity and people as competing objectives. The organizations that succeed will not treat AI primarily as a cost-cutting exercise. They will treat it as an operating model transformation – shaping work around human strengths, involving employees in change, embedding governance and creating continuous systems for learning and mobility.

In a recent development, for investors, this demands a broader definition of AI maturity. Does implementation lead to measurable business impact? Does the organization understand how work is changing? Can it continuously redeploy skills? Can it measure improvements in productivity and work quality, not just headcount reductions? These will increasingly become the leading indicators of long-term resilience.

Leaders need to do three things:.

First, redesign work before automating it. Understand where people create differentiated value and where AI can augment or automate tasks.

It has been revealed that second, build skills around real work and real career opportunities. Training without a pathway to apply those skills will not be enough.

Recent findings suggest that third, involve people in the change. Explain what is changing, listen to concerns and give employees a role in redesigning how their own work gets done.

None of these are technology decisions. They are leadership decisions.

In a recent development, that is why I remain optimistic. The defining capability of the intelligence era will not be artificial intelligence alone. It will be leaders’ ability to orchestrate human and digital intelligence together in ways that make organizations more productive, more resilient and more human. AI is changing how work gets done.

And this, ultimately, is the opportunity in front of us: not simply to deploy AI, but to use it to expand human potential.
