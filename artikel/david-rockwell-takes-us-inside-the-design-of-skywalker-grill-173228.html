David Rockwell Takes Us Inside the Design of Skywalker Grill

Designing a restaurant for George Lucas might seem like an invitation to draw from the filmmaker’s extensive cinematic universe. But when David Rockwell and his team began developing Skywalker Grill, the signature restaurant at the Lucas Museum of Narrative Art in Los Angeles, the conversation went in a different direction.

Sources indicate that rather than referencing the Star Wars franchise or any of Lucas’s other films, the restaurant would take its cues from something more personal: Skywalker Ranch, the filmmaker’s creative retreat in Northern California. Lucas and his wife, Mellody Hobson, envisioned a space that felt warm, intimate, and welcoming, a deliberate contrast to the futuristic architecture of the museum designed by Ma Yansong of MAD Architects.

In the latest episode of Once Upon a Project, Rockwell joins host AJ Paron to discuss how that vision took shape, from the unconventional fabrication techniques behind the restaurant’s sculptural wood interior to the decisions that shaped its relationship with the surrounding museum.

“They wanted to start the conversation around warmth and the kind of warmth of Skywalker Ranch in many facets, not only material palette but also custom furniture,” Rockwell explains.

Recent findings suggest that that direction ultimately led to an interior defined by rich cherry wood, curved banquettes, custom furnishings, and carefully integrated lighting. One of its most striking features is a continuous wood shell that wraps from ceiling to walls, creating an almost cocoon-like environment. According to Rockwell, there isn’t a single right angle in the restaurant.

Achieving that effect was considerably more complicated than it appears. Each cherry wood panel was individually fabricated using a combination of traditional woodworking and technology-enabled techniques. Behind the seamless surfaces, the team also had to accommodate seismic requirements, mechanical systems, lighting, and acoustics.

When Paron asks what proved most challenging during the project, Rockwell points to the engineering behind the wood shell. “If I had to pinpoint one element, it would be developing the system to mount and support the cherry wood panels in such an organic design,” he recalls.

The conversation also explores how the team approached the restaurant’s relationship with the surrounding museum. While the building’s sweeping forms and futuristic character could have encouraged a more theatrical interior, Rockwell and his clients were interested in something subtler.

Industry observers note that instead of incorporating recognizable cinematic imagery, the design borrows from filmmaking’s use of light, movement, and framing. A curved bar directs attention toward panoramic views of Los Angeles, while the lighting was carefully designed to avoid reflections in the expansive glass walls.

Even the furniture placement was considered from an unusually cinematic perspective: Rockwell Group created renderings from every seat in the restaurant. “We’ve never had anyone ask us to look at that from that specific point of view,” Rockwell shares.

Throughout the episode, Rockwell offers a behind-the-scenes look at his collaboration with Lucas and Hobson, who were closely involved in the design process, reviewing mockups, testing furniture, and helping shape the restaurant’s many details.
