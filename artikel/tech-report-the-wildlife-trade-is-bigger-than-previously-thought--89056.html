Tech Report: The wildlife trade is bigger than previously thought, with nearly 80,000 species and counting

A upcoming review finds that global wildlife trade reaches far beyond familiar pets and seafood, and gaps in recordkeeping make its full scale hard to measure.

It has been revealed that ask about the wildlife trade and most people picture smuggled ivory, tiger skins and pangolins. Almost none of the recorded trade is like that.

Most documented wildlife trade is legal, and it includes the fish in the freezer aisle, the orchid on the windowsill, and the gecko in the pet shop window.

A fresh review documented 79,554 species in international wildlife trade, well above the roughly 50,000 previously estimated.

A broader tally that included species people use for subsistence and other purposes reached 107,356.

Even the newly introduced number is likely a considerable underestimate, the authors wrote, because almost no country records what it buys and sells.

Alice Hughes, a conservation scientist at the University of Melbourne, led the review. Her co-authors come from the organizations that track wildlife trade, including TRAFFIC, the IUCN and the UN’s Food and Agriculture Organization.

The count comes from stitching together databases built for other purposes, and the plant half of it rests on records of use rather than records of sale.

In an interview with Earth.com, Hughes said the finding is about ordinary buyers as much as smugglers.

“Almost all of us are part of wildlife trade, and whilst some of that, such as fisheries are managed, sectors like the pet trade are not,” she said.

It has been revealed that legal wildlife trade is worth an estimated $220 billion to $300 billion a year. Smuggled wildlife is worth an estimated $28 billion to $40 billion.

So the legal side is roughly six to 11 times larger than the smuggling that gets the attention.

Sources indicate that “When we think of wildlife trade we tend to think about the illegal trade of elephants, tigers and pangolins. But it’s much more than that,” Hughes stated.

“Wildlife trade includes everything from timber, seafood and medicinal plants to pets, cut flowers, ornamental fish and fungi, and most of it is legal.”.

It has been revealed that legal doesn’t mean harmless. Where researchers have population numbers for a traded species, the populations being collected from are, on average, 50% to 60% smaller than populations left alone.

By one estimate the authors cite, trade has contributed, directly or indirectly, to the global extinction of 294 species.

The team pulled together every trade record it could reach. The largest was the CITES Trade Database, which holds the records of legal trade in protected species going back to 1975.

Alongside it went the IUCN Red List, UN fish and timber records, TRAFFIC’s seizure reports and the US import database.

Beyond the databases, the researchers drew on published surveys of individual animal groups and a world checklist of plants that people use.

Recent findings suggest that animals accounted for 47,685 species, and up to 31,306 of those are vertebrates. That works out to 42% of every vertebrate species science has described.

Put another way, two out of every five kinds of animal with a backbone have been traded somewhere.

For birds, the share is higher still: about 65% of all living bird species have been traded. Plants and fungi added 59,671 species potentially used or traded.

Industry observers note that the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service logs wild animal shipments into and out of the United States in a database called LEMIS, and it periodically releases the records.

Since 2000, more than 21,000 wild animal species have been traded into the US alone.

In a recent development, more than a quarter of all traded animal species appear in that one country’s records and in no other database. For mammals, the figure is 54%.

Much of what the world knows about which animals are traded comes from one country’s import records.

Hughes’s team surveyed 61 countries and searched for “wildlife trade database” in 30 languages. The US system was the only full, public record of trade across groups of animals from any country.

According to recent reports, “The United States is the only country that systematically records and publicly shares trade data across a wide range of taxa. For much of the rest of the world, equivalent data simply does not exist,” Hughes said.

Asked what it would take for other countries to follow, Hughes told Earth.com it starts with accepting that “legal trade is not necessarily sustainable.”.

Without data on trade in other groups, she said, the trade can’t be managed to make sure it is sustainable.

“Trade has changed, the exotic pet trade will continue to grow, and with so many species in trade, that poses a real risk unless we start monitoring it better,” she stated.

The pet trade is where the new counts diverge most from the old global estimates. The gaps are largest for reptiles, invertebrates and plants, which are also the hardest to track.

A lizard or a beetle bought online travels by mail and often isn’t identified to species at the border.

In a recent development, CITES, the treaty that regulates trade in at-risk species, covers about 9.5% of reptile species. The latest review recorded 4,620 reptile species in trade, about 37% of all described reptiles.

Asked which traded species surprised her most, Hughes mentioned it was how rapidly newly described species appear for sale.

According to recent reports, collectors sometimes use the scientific descriptions themselves to find animals to trade, she remarked. She also pointed to “the price people are willing to pay for some, like 100s of dollars for a single isopod (woodlouse).”.

For buyers, Hughes mentioned the place to start is where an animal came from.

Corn snakes and leopard geckos should be captive bred, she noted, and chameleons often come from the wild and don’t survive well in captivity.

“If you are not sure where they come from, just don’t buy them, for every wild caught animal in the shop, many others die in transit,” she said.

Governments have agreed on a set of global biodiversity targets, and one of them covers the impacts of trade.

According to recent reports, its single headline measure is the share of fish stocks being fished at sustainable levels. By the review’s count, that measure reflects about 2% of the species in use and trade.

“While the global biodiversity framework does have a target on trade, based on our estimates it only accounts for around two or three percent of species in trade and misses the other 97 percent,” Hughes mentioned.

The authors wrote that the fix doesn’t need a new system. Almost every country already runs customs checks and animal health checks on imports, and most CITES permits are still paper.

Electronic permits exist, but only about 30 of the 184 CITES countries use them.

If customs offices digitized shipping invoices and matched them to a species list, the authors wrote, they would record much of what is now lost.

The United Kingdom has required an electronic tag on every sheep, and a log of its movements, since 2009, so record-keeping on that scale is already being done.

According to recent reports, seizure records reflect how hard a country enforces its laws as much as how much is smuggled. And the databases don’t agree with one another on which species are traded, which is part of the paper’s point.

Even so, Hughes mentioned the estimate is still an underestimate, because most countries do not collect the data.

It has been revealed that the larger question is whether wild populations can sustain the trade. An IUCN working group has assessed the potential sustainability of trade for 347 species across 117 countries.

For many traded species, researchers still lack the data to tell how much is being taken from the wild and how those populations are faring.

Hughes and her co-authors propose standardizing how trade data are collected and starting at a regional level, with the European Union as a possible first model.

Until that happens, each newly introduced database that opens is likely to add species to the count.

For many species, researchers still can’t tell how trade is affecting wild populations.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.
