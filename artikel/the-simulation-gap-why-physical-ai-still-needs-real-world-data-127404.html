The Simulation Gap: Why Physical AI Still Needs Real-World Data

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that simulation, digital twins, and synthetic data can reduce the time, cost, and risk involved in training robots. That has encouraged work on robotics foundation models intended to operate across tasks and industries. A lot of people in our field now assume that with enough simulation, we won’t need much real-world data at all. I’m less convinced, especially as Physical AI moves from controlled research environments into live operations. Analysts now treat Physical AI as its own branch of AI: systems that sense, reason, and act in the physical world instead of producing text or images.

For testing ideas, probing edge cases, and building training simulations, it is indispensable. My concern is with systems built mostly in simulation. They tend to break when they meet a real site. I’d rather treat simulation as a multiplier. Start from a real recording of a robot in an aisle, then use simulation to vary the lighting, clutter, and people around it. One real run becomes many plausible ones; each still tied to something that happened.

Industry observers note that A model can do well on the data it was trained on and still fail when something changes. That’s overfitting, and for a robot trained in simulation, the training data is the simulator. Researchers who study the reality gap see it as one of the main barriers to moving robots from simulation into the real world. The gap is difficult to measure, but teams relying heavily on simulation need to track how performance changes under production conditions.

In warehouses, you usually see it as a gap between the demo and the deployment. A robot runs well in one building and struggles in the next. One relies on a large seasonal workforce, the other on a stable crew; one stores small, fast-moving SKUs, the other bulky items.

In a simulator, engineers decide what exists. Objects can be labeled, events logged, and individual variables changed without disturbing the rest of the environment. That level of control makes simulation valuable for testing, but live warehouses are far less observable and far harder to isolate.

Industry observers note that robots share the floor with people, equipment, inventory and business processes, and the site itself keeps changing. Worse, a lot of problems only show up at scale, after you’ve already deployed thousands of robots and made the investment. A navigation model behaves until the season turns and, at one particular hour, sunlight lands on a sensor. Someone parks equipment in an aisle that’s always clear; now it’s a bottleneck. You rarely find any of this in benchmark datasets, but it often decides whether a deployment works.

AI is good at picking up patterns like these, such as when and where congestion builds. None of that shows up unless you’re collecting from live operations. Simulation can also teach a model the wrong lessons, and in manipulation it’s even more obvious. Vision models are very good at cheating, and they’ll happily latch onto a texture or render artifact that exists only in the simulator.

In a recent development, “World models,” which learn how a physical environment behaves and responds to a robot’s actions, are one attempt to address this problem. Nature recently took a close look at this work, and researchers see it as a possible bridge between perception, prediction and action. They don’t escape the problem, though. A world model is only as grounded as the real observations behind it. Today’s best world-model platforms still can’t capture every meaningful interaction in a complex physical environment — and a busy warehouse is full of them.

Synthetic data is most useful when it extends real-world datasets rather than standing in for them. It can add hazardous or low-frequency scenarios, such as a blocked main aisle or a forklift crossing a robot’s path. Many of the biggest Physical AI programs now lean on synthetic data to grow their training sets, move faster and show models rare conditions.

The limits come from how it’s made. Behind every synthetic dataset is an engineer deciding what to model, what to leave out, and which scenarios seem likely. Even sensible choices leave gaps because the resulting data can only represent the conditions included in its design.

Many teams now build on the same open models and published architectures, so the algorithm alone rarely separates one firm from another. What differs is the operational data available to train, test, and improve those models. A navigation trace becomes far more useful when you can connect it to what the robot was doing and whether the work is finished on time. Warehouse observations get interesting when they’re linked to inventory, throughput, labor, and performance.

Industry observers note that that’s why scale matters. A fleet spread across many sites sees cold-storage aisles, peak-season surges and night shifts that a single pilot never will. A congestion model tuned to one site’s shift change, for example, tells you little about a site that runs around the clock.

Google’s RT-2 demonstrated that mixing web-scale knowledge with robot data can help a robot handle situations it wasn’t trained on. But many real robotics problems are still specific to one domain. A robot can pick up a box perfectly, and the building can still miss its shipping cutoff.

It has been revealed that does a warehouse robot need to understand the physical world? Not all of it. It needs to know warehouses: inventory flow, labor workflows, congestion, equipment, and the site’s constraints.

Domain expertise determines what the system should optimize and which signals are relevant. In a warehouse, that means evaluating the model against operational measures such as throughput and shipping performance, rather than judging it only by a perception or manipulation benchmark. That knowledge matters more as models get more capable.

Industry observers note that as I recently discussed in an examination of Physical AI and robotics, I expect most of the industry’s near-term value to come from specialized systems in well-defined domains rather than from general-purpose intelligence. A warehouse operator judges a robot by whether orders ship on time and how many labor hours it saves, so those are the numbers a warehouse AI system should be built and judged on.

The decisive test is operational: place the system in a live warehouse and measure whether it performs across sites, shifts, workflows, and changing conditions. When it falls short, real-world data highlights where the model’s assumptions stopped matching the environment.

Dr. Oscar Mendez is Director of AI & Data Science at Locus Robotics, where he leads AI strategy, research, and deployment across a global fleet of more than 22,000 autonomous robots. An award-winning, internationally recognized researcher in Physical AI, robotics, machine learning, and computer vision, he focuses on bringing AI out of the digital world and into operational reality, developing intelligent systems that perceive, reason, and act to deliver greater productivity, adaptability, and operational confidence in large-scale warehouse environments.
