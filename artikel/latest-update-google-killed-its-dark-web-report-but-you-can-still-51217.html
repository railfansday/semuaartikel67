Latest Update: Google Killed Its Dark Web Report, but You Can Still Monitor Data Breaches

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Google’s Dark Web Report used to be the only consumer-focused platform that let people scan major data breaches, dark web marketplaces, and private forums to see if their personal information had ended up in the hands of hackers. But the organization discontinued the service at the start of 2026, with fresh scans stopping on Jan. 15 and all accounts deleted on Feb. 16.

Industry observers note that google notes it sunsetted Dark Web Report because, although the information it provided was generally useful, the service failed to provide “helpful next steps” for owners whose data was found on the dark web. Google promised a fresh service that would provide more actionable intel, but that service has yet to materialize, and there haven’t been any further announcements about it yet.

Recent findings suggest that dark Web Report may not have been perfect, but it was the only early warning system of its kind that was available for personal use and let users run scans free of cost. Now that it’s gone, however, there are plenty of other services that offer the same service at varying costs and levels of effectiveness. Let’s talk through the nature of the insight that Google's reports offered, how it collected that information in the first place, and what options exist in the market to replace it.

Google launched the Dark Web Report in March 2023 as a premium feature available to Google One people, but later extended it for free to anyone with a Google account and moved it to the "Results about you" dashboard.

According to recent reports, you'd set up a monitoring profile with your name, date of birth, physical address, phone number, emails, and Social Security Number if you lived in the U.S. After that, you could head to the dashboard anytime you wanted to run a free scan across dark web forums and marketplaces, publicly disclosed corporate data breaches, and other hidden online spaces to see if any of your personal information had shown up there. It was easy to use—but not a lot of people knew it existed in the first place.

According to recent reports, it’s unclear what prompted the internal decision at Google, but in the absence of concrete next steps, it is reasonable to think the reports could create panic for the uninitiated. There’s no way to remove your information from the dark web once it’s already in circulation there. Still, having an early warning so you could reset 2FA or self-lock your SSN isn’t without merit.

Industry observers note that but perhaps more importantly, most people with Google accounts didn’t know and hadn’t heard of the Dark Web Report’s existence. Google has likely taken it as a hint that users want more active remediation tools than simple alerts, which is why it’s now directing users to the Password Manager, Passkeys, and “Results about you” dashboard instead—though, again, the company hasn't followed through on a direct replacement for Dark Web Report, despite teasing such a feature.

Industry observers note that many of the top dark web scanners draw from the same well of information, scanning against major data breaches and dark web spaces. Several also offer breach notifications that send you email alerts when your details show up in a periodic scan. However, none of that is worth paying for because Have I Been Pwned or Mozilla Monitor cover those features quite well for free. Even the default password managers from Google and Apple come with some ability to monitor leaked credentials.

According to recent reports, there isn’t an easy way to scrub your information from the dark web once it ends up getting leaked. But you can take steps to protect sensitive information or financial data once you know there’s been a leak. If a password has been compromised, reset it immediately. Also, enable 2FA on the affected account for added security. Even better, switch to a passkey if that account supports it. If not, authenticator apps are a safer way to receive 2FA codes than standard SMS.

According to recent reports, if your Social Security number has been leaked, freeze your credit with Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. You can do this by contacting each agency separately. Freezing your credit will prevent unauthorized credit withdrawals and protect your credit score from abuse by attackers. If one of your credit or debit card numbers has ended up in a breach, call your bank to freeze transactions on that card immediately.

According to recent reports, finally, get in the habit of using email aliases instead of sharing your actual email address with every platform and service provider. Addy.io and Proton’s SimpleLogin are both popular services that let you generate temporary email aliases that point to your inbox.
