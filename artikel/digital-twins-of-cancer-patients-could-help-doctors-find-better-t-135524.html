Digital twins of cancer patients could help doctors find better treatments

Digital twins of breast tumors could help doctors predict treatment responses and identify potentially effective alternative drugs.

Recent findings suggest that ask a chatbot-style AI which drug to give a cancer patient, and it may offer an answer without providing a transparent, scientifically grounded explanation. That, the researchers mentioned, has left many doctors skeptical of such tools.

In London, a team took the opposite route: a program built from logic rather than statistics, which a doctor can open up and read.

Run on virtual copies of 31 real tumors, the program predicted whether a patient’s treatment would work 74% of the time.

For 12 of the 13 patients whose incomplete response the model correctly predicted, it identified at least one additional drug that might have helped.

Jasmin Fisher of the UCL Cancer Institute led the work, with Pedro Victori as first author and collaborators at the University of Cambridge and the Cancer Research UK Cambridge Institute.

Every result is a simulation checked against records from a trial that has already ended, and the drugs it suggested were not tested in the patients it picked them for.

“We see our computer model as an aid for existing cancer clinical practice, not an alternative to it,” Fisher remarked.

The cancer in question is triple-negative breast cancer, about 15% of breast cancers.

It has been revealed that its cells lack the three receptors that most breast cancer drugs aim at, so hormone therapies don’t work. About 40% of patients die within five years of diagnosis.

Sources indicate that instead of training on patient data, the team hand-built a map of how the disease works. They read 191 research papers and drew 142 genes, proteins and cell processes as nodes.

Then they linked the nodes with 379 arrows that say which one switches which on or off, and let each node be off, low or fully on.

Industry observers note that the map includes the machinery that repairs broken DNA, which chemotherapy and the drug olaparib attack. It also includes the machinery of cell division, which the chemotherapy drug paclitaxel jams.

Two output nodes sum it up: whether the cancer cells die, and whether they keep multiplying.

Before applying the model to individual tumors, the team checked it against 160 laboratory results from 33 other papers and adjusted it until it reproduced all of them.

According to recent reports, the patients came from PARTNER, a trial run by Jean Abraham’s team in Cambridge. Its patients, all with triple-negative breast cancer, received four chemotherapy drugs before surgery, with or without olaparib.

For 31 of them, the team had full genome sequences of the tumor.

Each patient’s mutations and missing or duplicated genes were loaded into the map to make a digital twin of the tumor.

A patient with a disabled BRCA1 gene, for instance, had that node fixed at zero. Then the twin was given the same treatment the patient had received, and the program reported whether the cells died.

Sources indicate that the predictions matched the real outcomes in 23 of 31 cases. The program was right for 10 of the 14 patients whose cancer disappeared completely, and for 13 of the 17 whose cancer did not.

Only those 23 accurate twins were used for what came next.

Industry observers note that the team then took the 13 twins that resisted treatment and switched off every gene in the map, one at a time. They watched for the switch that tipped the simulated cells into dying.

Twelve of the 13 had one. For 10, the program predicted a complete response, eight with a single added drug and two with a pair.

The most broadly useful target was a protein called ATM, which helps cells repair DNA damage; blocking it was predicted to produce a complete response in seven patients.

The other twins pointed to drugs that have passed phase 2 trials, including inhibitors of Chk1, ATR, MEK, mTOR and Wee1.

Fisher noted the point is to give a doctor a lead, not an order.

“In practice, the treatment option recommended by our model may, under closer scrutiny, be discounted by the clinician – for example, if they view the drug as too toxic,” she stated.

“But it may also lead them to explore, and eventually settle on, a third option which they might otherwise not have arrived at.”.

The one tumor for which the program could not identify an effective additional treatment contained three distinct populations of cancer cells.

Spatial RNA data demonstrated three groups of cells with different gene activity sitting in different parts of the tumor.

Simulated separately, each of the three had targets the whole-tumor model had missed. One of them, a lower-grade carcinoma, was predicted to respond completely to an added drug.

A sample taken after treatment held a fourth group unlike any of the first three, carrying two changes the model flags as resistance.

The team also pulled the 29 genes the program had flagged as targets and tested them as a signature in a public database of breast cancer patients.

Among patients with the basal subtype treated with chemotherapy alone, those carrying mutations in the signature genes lived longer.

Thirty-one patients is a small group, and the authors wrote that it limits how finely they can slice the results.

The model is right about three times in four, which also means it is wrong once in four. The twins that failed were set aside before the drug search began.

The map knows only what 191 papers told it, so it can’t propose a mechanism nobody has published.

It has been revealed that it also ignores the immune cells and other tissue around a tumor, which matter most for immunotherapy. And it models the drugs used in PARTNER rather than the newer antibody-drug conjugates now in use.

It has been revealed that each twin was built from DNA alone. The authors wrote that adding gene activity and protein data would likely sharpen the predictions.

The framework is designed to be updated as a patient’s tumor changes under treatment.

Determining whether the suggested drugs actually benefit patients will require a clinical trial in which the model’s predictions inform treatment decisions.

The full study was published in the journal Cell Reports Medicine.
