Lucia’s Rise: ‘Grand Theft Auto’ Forces Gamers to Grow Up—and Punishes Them With Narrative Consequences If They Refuse

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that for the last 25 years, nearly every noxious gamer stereotype had a home in Grand Theft Auto. Juvenile, sexist, crass, embarrassing—people could accuse the games of being these things because the allegations were, in part, true. Beginning with 2001’s Grand Theft Auto III, the series began giving players the freedom to wreak havoc across satirical renditions of US cities. It was an amazing technical feat that only grew more impressive with each subsequent Grand Theft Auto game—and harder to appreciate the more those cities were filled with dick jokes and sex workers.

Recent findings suggest that this, for the most part, suited Grand Theft Auto’s publisher just fine. What business did the firm have calling itself Rockstar Games if it cared what a bunch of prudes thought?

But with Grand Theft Auto VI, something has changed. And the game’s staggering launch-day revenue—$3.79 billion—suggests that this change was a welcome one.

Rockstar has executed an astonishing about-face in Grand Theft Auto VI, choosing to build a game around a couple depicted with aching sincerity—from their car-boosting meet-cute to the desperate stickups they commit, with the player’s help. Jason Duval (played by Dylan Rourke) and Lucia Caminos (Manni L. Perez) are a modern-day Bonnie and Clyde, out of options and on the run, hoping they can land a score big enough to turn things around.

This earnestness is new for Grand Theft Auto, which historically has had a deep love for pushing buttons. (Its sister series, the Western Red Dead Redemption, has less of a problem with heart-on-the-sleeve storytelling.) Lucia, especially, is new. She’s the first woman you can play as in the series, and by the transitive property, she’s also the first woman that Grand Theft Auto has ever had to take seriously. Doing so changes the entire shape of the series.

Until now, Grand Theft Auto has never really thought much of women. True, the games have never thought much of anyone; Rockstar traffics in a nihilistic comedy that lies somewhere between South Park and a Verhoeven film, a dim view of the United States where everyone sucks. But women, usually, have sucked the most. They’ve been the butts of countless jokes; in the Grand Theft Auto universe, they’ve been nags and drags and sex workers secondary to the men that the games were about. Grand Theft Auto III, a game that barely has a plot, begins with main character Clyde’s girlfriend betraying him after a heist, shooting him and calling him “small time” before running off with the take. It doesn’t get much better from there.

So yes: Lucia is a huge pivot for the series, especially because Grand Theft Auto VI doesn’t trust players with her right away. After a brief prologue in which a desperate stickup goes wrong, Lucia is sent to jail and players begin the game as her boyfriend Jason Duval. In addition to doing odd jobs for a small-time crime boss (Stephen Root) and hanging out with your pal (Bobby Moynihan), players have to choose: Are they going to be a good boyfriend? Rockstar Games know they might be making a substantial ask of their audience, so they slow-roll it. Before Lucia gets out of jail, the game suggests to the player, maybe fix the stairs? Or get a haircut? Buy her a necklace?

In the days since the game came out, many have noted how amusing it is that Grand Theft Auto VI has made caring about your girlfriend one of its first challenges. The game’s “Lucia will appreciate this” notification is its first viral meme, and the source of both incel crashouts (“woke slop … so obvious it was made by women”) and earnest appreciation. (One of the first clips to spread online was of streamer Stable Ronaldo painting Jason and Lucia's bedroom one color before recoiling in horror: What if she hates that shade?).

According to recent reports, lucia is a destabilizing event not just for gamers, but for Grand Theft Auto—a damning yet fascinating example of gaming culture’s priorities on its biggest stage. GTA has always been a series of warring ambitions: violent Rube Goldberg machine, Karl Ove Knausgaard–esque obsession with quotidian details, and thrilling Hollywood blockbuster all in one. These disparate aims are often in conflict with one another, and one way to read the series’ heretofore exclusively male focus is as an attempt to thread this needle. By tossing all of its commentary under the carpet of “satire," Rockstar can also use a veneer of meanness to argue that the games’ misogyny is actually an intentional lambasting, much like everything else in GTA.

Recent findings suggest that but taking a woman seriously, and making her colead with a male character, requires an entire rethinking of what Grand Theft Auto is. “It’s a product of the story we’re trying to tell,” Grand Theft Auto VI codirector Rob Nelson said when asked by The Guardian if this is the first Grand Theft Auto to encourage empathy from players. “We’ve tried for the first time in a GTA to provide mechanisms that encourage it, for those who want to.”.

This is an extremely roundabout way of saying that, in this Grand Theft Auto, Rockstar asks gamers to grow up—and punishes them with narrative consequences if they don’t take the game up on the offer.

The funny thing about Grand Theft Auto VI’s gamified empathy is that, like everything else in a video game, it has to teach the player how to accomplish that goal. The game does this through prompting players to do chores, but also through Jason’s characterization. Throughout the game, Rockstar has to convey what its writers believe a well-adjusted man looks like—or at least what constitutes a man well-adjusted enough to earn the respect of a woman like Lucia, who walks out on him when he tries to exclude her from some of his more dangerous criminal activities.

To that end, there are an abundance of moments in the game where Jason demonstrates the virtues of being respectful and maintaining boundaries, several of which are a departure from GTA series norms. There are no sex workers in the game anymore (though there are strip clubs). While Jason and Lucia can break up depending on the player’s choices, there are no other options for committed or casual romance.

According to recent reports, the money you steal is ostensibly for Jason and Lucia to use together: so they can get away and start over with their young son, who’s right now entrusted to a relative. Casual conversations between strangers players meet on the road make Jason’s respectful nature both a point and a punchline. There’s one man who’s looking for his missing wife after a night of partying. When he starts to brag about her sexual dexterity, Jason interjects to say, “Hey. Let’s draw a line, bud.”.

Recent findings suggest that in some ways, this is all rather embarrassing—though in a different way than the weapons-grade sexism of Grand Theft Autos past. One ought to feel a twinge of shame while marveling at any entertainment franchise for including a woman. Yet Grand Theft Auto VI is being graded on a curve. It has a near-perfect Metacritic score in large part for its depiction of Lucia, and for Jason’s relationship to her. The context speaks to a state of arrested development, both in Grand Theft Auto and in major-budget games writ large.

Industry observers note that none of this changes the fact that Lucia really is one of the most well-realized video game characters in a digital city unlike any other. But perhaps her inclusion is less an apology for GTA’s past, and more of a concession to how out of step the series had become with the culture. Because if you’re meant to have empathy for one character, the natural next question is, “Well, what about everyone else?".

Rockstar’s maniacal pursuit of verisimilitude goes a long way here. The game’s animations are so realistic that in GTA VI, shooting unarmed civilians is legitimately unsettling. It’s also disincentivized by a “criminal profile” rating that will mark your character “irredeemable” if you engage in too much wanton violence. (This is not a “game over”—it merely locks players out of achieving a happy ending unless they restart from before their murder spree.) These sorts of limits have become another reliable source of outrage directed at the game, with rage baiters drumming up controversy over Rockstar’s decision to, for example, not permit players to shoot up a nightclub.

It has been revealed that this is the tricky reality of encouraging empathy for “those who want to,” as Nelson put it. Rockstar has certain intentions, but so do players—and managing both is the great challenge of making any video game. Empathy is a shock to GTA’s system and to the audience alike.

In a recent development, replicating humanity with computer software is a daunting and costly task, and one that only Rockstar seems to have the limitless resources to pursue. Those resources—the luxury to work as long as necessary to complete Grand Theft Auto VI, and the obscene amount of money it cost (our only solid figure is “less than $1.5 billion”)—are rapidly dwindling in the video game business, as all available tech funding is vacuumed up by AI companies and private equity loots what’s left. Grand Theft Auto VI may very well be one of one: the most expensive game ever and the last of its kind, the peak of Rockstar’s ambition and also of every massive-budget video game. Here, at the end of everything, the biggest of video games has finally decided that it’s time to try empathy. And for a woman, even, should gamers choose (and not all will).

It’s a bit of an indictment of the billion-dollar video game industry that the most expensive game in history is also the first major game to commit to a realistic heterosexual relationship. Grand Theft Auto VI deserves many of the accolades it instantly garnered upon release, but the nature of its accomplishment is not yet fully understood. It’s possible that what Rockstar has built is less a monument to video games’ potential than a magnificent funeral pyre, a testament to how things might’ve turned out if Rockstar’s priorities—and those of everyone chasing after its vision of what video games could be—were just a little bit different.
