Breaking: Glass Behaves Strangely When Stretched to One Atom Wide – the Discovery Could Transform Electronics

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

As engineers push to make electronics ever smaller, managing their heat grows harder, and a discovery about one-atom-wide glass could open new possibilities for miniaturization.

Glass is known for keeping heat where it belongs. It insulates windows, protects buildings from temperature changes, and helps keep electronic components separated. Yet when researchers reduced glass to a thread just one atom wide, they found it could transmit heat with remarkable efficiency, revealing an unexpected behavior that could reshape how engineers approach increasingly small electronics.

In a study published in Nature Nanotechnology, researchers at the University of Colorado Boulder observed heat traveling through an atomic-scale glass connection in discrete steps. Each step carried exactly 40% of the maximum heat a single channel can transmit under the laws of quantum physics. The observation marks the first time researchers have detected this kind of quantized heat flow carried by atomic vibrations.

According to recent reports, the finding could help address a growing challenge in electronics. As components become smaller and more powerful, managing the heat they generate becomes increasingly difficult. Glass, in the form of silicon dioxide, is widely used as an insulating layer in computer chips, making its behavior at extremely small scales particularly relevant.

It has been revealed that “We will eventually reach a bottleneck to design the next generation of electronic and photonic devices,” said Longji Cui, an assistant professor in the Paul M. Rady Department of Mechanical Engineering at CU Boulder. “Thermal management inside electronic devices is extremely important, but to do it more effectively, we need to understand how heat is flowing through these nanoscale devices.”.

Industry observers note that ordinary glass is a poor conductor of heat because its atoms are arranged in a disordered structure. That disorder scatters the vibrations that carry heat through the material, making it difficult for thermal energy to travel efficiently. When researchers stretched the glass into a connection just one atom wide, however, there was no room for the same disorder. Instead, the atomic vibrations could pass through the narrow connection with much less interference.

According to recent reports, “Glass is what we put in windows to keep heat in,” said Guilherme de Vilhena, a researcher at the Materials Science Institute of Madrid and a co-author of the study. “But stretch it into a thread one atom wide, and it does the opposite: it lets heat through with an efficiency we had never seen in an insulator.”.

The team made the observation using a scanning thermal probe microscope (SThM), an instrument equipped with a glass tip and a built-in thermometer. It measures temperature and heat flow through extremely small contacts by detecting the behavior of atoms and molecules. Cui compares the instrument to a fingertip capable of sensing heat and texture, only scaled down to the nanoscale.

“When you put your own finger to touch a surface, you can feel the texture and the heat,” he remarked. “With this microscope, we shrink this finger to nanoscale, and it can read the temperature and heat flow through a single atom.”.

According to recent reports, to observe the phenomenon, the researchers pressed two pieces of glass together and gradually pulled them apart. As the contact stretched and narrowed, it eventually formed a thread only one atom wide before breaking. This allowed the team to examine how heat moved as the connection approached the atomic limit.

The unexpected behavior could extend beyond glass. Cui believes other materials may exhibit similarly surprising thermal properties when examined at the atomic scale.

“We will now be able to study any material we want with this microscope, and there could be constant surprises that come from this instrument,” he mentioned.

It has been revealed that the microscope can investigate thermal transport in virtually any material, including metals and insulators. Cui hopes to use it to develop increasingly sensitive sensors capable of measuring phenomena that conventional instruments cannot easily detect.

It has been revealed that “I want to push the world record for thermal sensing, which means I need to build more and more sensitive platforms,” he remarked. “And people will use it to study fundamental thermal problems.”.

Recent findings suggest that semiconductors are among the team’s main interests because they form the foundation of nanoelectronics. By examining how heat moves through these materials, researchers could better investigate problems affecting the energy efficiency and reliability of increasingly compact electronic devices.

Sources indicate that “The materials we are particularly interested in are semiconductors that are the building blocks of nanoelectronics devices,” Cui remarked. “This new microscope we developed makes a great diagnostic tool to explore concerns associated with energy efficiency and reliability of electronic devices at the nanoscale.”.

Reference: “Thermal conductance of atomic-scale glass reaches 40% of quantum limit” by Sai C. Yelishala, Yunxuan Zhu, P. M. Martinez, O. Mateos-Lopez, Wan Xiong, Dishan Chooi, Sophie Kimura, Juan Carlos Cuevas, J. G. Vilhena and Longji Cui, 13 October 2026, Nature Nanotechnology. DOI: 10.1038/s41565-026-02308-9.
