In a recent development, from Magistrate Judge Virginia DeMarchi (N.D. Cal.) today in Doe 1 v. U.S.:.

Recent findings suggest that plaintiffs … filed this action against the United States, Google LLC ("Google"), and X.AI Corp. ("X.AI"). Plaintiffs sue for themselves and on behalf of a proposed class of "[a]ll Jeffrey Epstein victims whose private information, including their name, photographic image, telephone number, email address, physical address, or place of residence, was disclosed without their consent in unredacted documents released by the United States between December 2025 and the present." Plaintiffs also sue on behalf of an injunctive relief subclass of "[a]ll Class members whose disclosed private information is indexed, cached, hosted, or otherwise made available to the public through any service operated by the United States, Google, or [X.AI]." The subclass "seeks injunctive relief under Rules 23(b)(2) only.".

Sources indicate that plaintiffs allege that in "late 2025 and early 2026, the U.S. Department of Justice ('DOJ') outed approximately 100 victims of the convicted sexual predator, publishing their private information and identifying them to the world." Plaintiffs further allege that "[e]ven after the government acknowledged the disclosure violated the rights of the victims and withdrew most of the information, artificial intelligence products like Google's AI Mode and [X.]AI's Grok, continue to republish it, refusing victims' pleas to take it down." Plaintiffs' sole claim against the United States is for violation of the Privacy Act of 1974 ("Privacy Act"), 5 U.S.C. § 552a(b) and § 552(g)(1)(D). Plaintiffs assert several claims under California law and common law against both Google and X.AI for violation of California's Unfair Competition Law (Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200, et seq.), invasion of privacy, negligence, and negligent infliction of emotional distress. Plaintiffs seek injunctive relief, compensatory damages, statutory damages, punitive damages, reasonable attorneys' fees and costs, as well as pre- and post-judgment interest.

The Court rejected the federal government's motion to dismiss (Google and X.AI's motion to dismiss will be considered later):.

According to recent reports, "The Privacy Act prohibits a federal agency from disclosing a record contained in a system of records pertaining to an individual unless the individual requests the information or consents to the disclosure in writing." To state a claim for wrongful disclosure under the Privacy Act, a plaintiff must allege facts showing that (1) the information disclosed is contained in a system of records; (2) the agency improperly disclosed the information; (3) the disclosure had an adverse effect on the plaintiff; (3) the disclosure was willful or intentional; and (4) the plaintiff suffered actual damages. The Privacy Act "provides for damages only where 'the court determines that the agency acted in a manner which was intentional or willful.'".

Recent findings suggest that for the present purpose of resolving the United States' motion to dismiss, only the third element regarding "willful or intentional" disclosure is at issue. Under the Privacy Act, willful or intentional conduct is "only somewhat greater than gross negligence," and "the Privacy Act 'imposes liability only when the agency acts in violation of the Act in a willful or intentional manner, either by committing the act without grounds for believing it to be lawful, or flagrantly disregarding others' rights under the Act.'" ….

Sources indicate that the United States notes that the disclosures in question were made pursuant to the Epstein Files Transparency Act ("EFTA"), which required the DOJ to release, within a 30-day period, millions of pages of documents from the DOJ's investigation of Mr. Epstein. While acknowledging that DOJ "admittedly made some mistakes, including inadvertent disclosures of victim information," the United States notes that in enacting the EFTA, Congress imposed an "unprecedented" and "herculean task" upon the DOJ.

Additionally, the United States says that the DOJ made remedial efforts by, for example, establishing "a website through which victims could confidentially notify the [DOJ] of information that required additional redaction." In these circumstances, the United States argues that plaintiffs' allegations cannot, as a matter of law, meet the "'high hurdle'" for the requisite willful or intentional conduct….

Plaintiffs maintain that the FAC alleges sufficient facts plausibly supporting willful or intentional conduct, including allegations that the DOJ (1) prioritized "maximum transparency" and the rapid release of information over victims' privacy interests; (2) refused victims' requests to remove the subject information, or did not respond to such requests; and (3) acted out of retaliatory motives and released files as "a punitive response to victims' public advocacy for transparency, effectively 'doxxing' victims who spoke out by exposing their identities and contact information.".

The question of whether an agency has acted without grounds for believing the act to be lawful or has acted in flagrant disregard of others' rights under the Privacy Act depends on the context of the disclosure in each case and is a fact-intensive inquiry. Indeed, in its reply, the United States asserts that the conduct in question is measured on a continuum. If that is the case, then the United States' assertion only bolsters the conclusion that there is no "bright line" defining "willful" or "intentional" conduct, and that such matters often are not conducive to determination as a matter of law at the pleading stage….

Viewing the facts in the light most favorable to plaintiffs, and drawing all reasonable inferences in their favor, as is required at this stage of the proceedings, the Court finds that plaintiffs have sufficiently pled facts plausibly supporting their claim that DOJ willfully or intentionally violated their rights under the Privacy Act. While the United States maintains that the mere presence of an error or mistake is not indicative of an intentional Privacy Act violation, the Court need not adopt the government's characterization of plaintiffs' allegations.

In essence, plaintiffs allege that the DOJ's disclosure of the subject materials was a "deliberate policy choice" to prioritize DOJ's obligations under the EFTA over its obligations and victims' rights under the Privacy Act. For pleading purposes, those allegations appear plausible, and are not inconsistent with the United States' assertion that DOJ was faced with a "herculean task" to accomplish within "an impossible-to-execute time frame." … [P]laintiffs' allegations that the DOJ made a deliberate decision, as a matter of policy, to give priority to its obligations under the EFTA ahead of those under the Privacy Act, are sufficient to support a plausible claim for relief….
