If the outlets in a room aren’t where you need them and you’ve got extension cords running amok, adding an outlet can eliminate those trip hazards and make everyday living a bit easier. Or maybe you need a upcoming outlet in a bathroom, laundry room, or doggy wash station in your mudroom, which means you’ll need to install a ground fault circuit interrupter (GFCI) outlet, required for any outlet within 6 feet of a water source. The good news is, wiring an outlet, whether standard or GFCI, is a project that you can do yourself, but you must take proper safety precautions.

In a recent development, however, if an outlet’s cover has melted, if the outlet has been smoking, or if there’s a burning smell, there’s likely a bigger issue than just the outlet. In these instances, it’s best to consult with a licensed electrician before you attempt to rewire it.

Read through these steps for how to wire an outlet before you get started.

Sources indicate that while a capable homeowner can wire an outlet, it’s not a job for a reluctant or unsure DIYer. Start by assessing your comfort level, and call in a pro if you have doubts.

In many instances, you can wire an outlet from another outlet. In fact, if there’s an existing outlet in a room on the opposite side of the wall from where you’d like to add an outlet, the job can be pretty simple. In this situation, you can usually cut a new opening, install an electrical box, and add a new outlet without having to snake wires through studs. Use a stud finder to locate the studs on either side of the existing outlet and make sure to situate the new outlet within the same stud bay.

If you simply want more outlets in one location, it’s possible to replace an existing duplex outlet, which takes just two plugs, with a quad receptacle, which takes four. This can be helpful in a workshop or even in kitchens and bathrooms where two outlets just won’t cut it.

Industry observers note that in either case, it’s crucial that there’s enough capacity on the circuit to accommodate the additional draw. For 15-amp circuits, the maximum is usually eight outlets or lights. Any more receptacles than that might draw too much current for the breaker.

According to recent reports, old-fashioned two-prong outlet connections aren’t grounded, which makes them dangerous in the event of an electrical fault. Without an electrician, it’s safe to convert a two-prong to a three-prong outlet only if the electrical box housing the outlet is metal and the cable feeding the box is armored. If these conditions are met, the box provides ground-fault protection (even though the outlet does not).

Recent findings suggest that how can you tell, without opening the wall, if the electrical box meets the criteria? Simple: Use a voltage tester. Insert one prong into the outlet’s shorter slot (the “hot slot”), then touch the other prong to the screw securing the faceplate. If the tester lights up, the electrical box is grounded, and you can go ahead and convert the two-prong to a three-prong. If your electrical box isn’t grounded, you can still convert to a three-prong, but the replacement must be a GFCI (the type of outlet with a red button on its front).

Over time, electrical outlets can start to look dingy or dirty, or the plastic can crack and make the outlet dangerous to use. To keep power flowing where you need it, it’s important to learn how to replace an electrical outlet safely and efficiently.

Before you go any further, it’s imperative that you cut power to the outlet you are replacing. At your home’s electrical panel, toggle off the switch associated with the circuit that sends electricity to the outlet in question.

According to recent reports, after you shut off the power to the outlet, use a voltage tester to double-check that it’s really off. Insert the tester’s probes into the top two slots on the outlet. If the tester lights up, you toggled the wrong switch on the electrical panel and you’ll have to try again. Continue by trial and error until you are certain the outlet is no longer receiving electricity. (When you’re done, relabel the circuits in the panel so you know which is which.).

It has been revealed that don’t have a voltage tester? You can use a lamp instead, so long as you know the lamp works. Plug the lamp into the outlet, and if it doesn’t turn on, it’s safe to proceed. This method is inherently riskier, so use it only as a last resort.

After you confirm that the power is off to the outlet, stick a piece of black electrical tape across the breaker so no one can flip it back on while you are working.

Industry observers note that most faceplates have a single screw in the middle. Remove that screw, and the plate should come off easily. Next, unscrew the mounting screws that secure the outlet to the electrical box. Finally, gently pull the outlet away from the receptacle.

In a recent development, you can now see three wires extending from the wall to the outlet. If the wires are attached to screws on the outlet, loosen those screws to free the wires. If the wires are snaked into holes in the back of the outlet, press the release slot and pull the wires out, assuming they don’t come out on their own. Put aside the old outlet.

You’re now ready to wire in the replacement. First, connect the neutral wire (white) to the silver screw on the side of the outlet. Make sure to orient the hooked end of the wire so that its curve goes clockwise, the same direction in which you will tighten the screw.

Connect the ground wire to the green screw, using the technique described above.

Connect the hot wire (black) to the gold screw, the last one remaining on the outlet casing.

Sources indicate that carefully maneuver the wires back into the electrical box, then screw the outlet to the box using the mounting screws at the top and bottom. Finally, position the faceplate over the outlet and screw it back on.

It has been revealed that go back to the electrical panel and restore power to the outlet that you’ve finished replacing. Check the outlet installation by testing the device with a voltage tester or a lamp.

Recent findings suggest that sometimes a simple replacement isn’t enough—you absolutely need another outlet. Maybe you want to accommodate a latest appliance or entertainment device, or you’re setting up a workshop. You can frequently solve this problem by wiring a new outlet from an existing outlet on the other side of the wall from the desired location of the new outlet. This project is a bit more involved, but a safety-minded DIYer can take it on.

In a recent development, note: Be sure you’re using an “old work,” “rework,” or “remodeling” electrical box for the upcoming outlet. These boxes have tabs that squeeze the box against the back of the drywall to hold it in place.

Recent findings suggest that be sure to shut off the power to the existing outlet before doing anything else. Check the outlet for power with the voltage tester, and then go to the electrical panel to shut off the corresponding breaker.

Industry observers note that head back to the outlet and use a voltage tester to ensure that the power is off. Once you confirm that the power is off, it’s a good idea to place a piece of black electrical tape across the breaker to prevent anyone from carelessly flipping it back on.

Place the electrical box face-first against the drywall where you plan to install it. Trace along the box’s edges with a pencil, and then cut along the marks with a drywall saw. Sometimes it’s easier if you score the drywall with a utility knife first.

It has been revealed that head back to the existing outlet. If it’s been some time since you shut the breaker, test the outlet again to make sure it’s still off. Next, remove the faceplate and remove the screws holding the outlet in the box. Carefully pull the outlet out and let it hang to one side, providing access to the back of the box.

Industry observers note that push the new wire through an opening in the back of the box, feeding enough wire into the wall to reach the new outlet opening plus 1 foot. This can be challenging, so be patient and use pliers to pull the tab open if necessary. On this same side, cut the new wire approximately 10 inches from the box. Carefully strip back the plastic sheathing, leaving approximately 1 inch of sheathing in the box.

Use the screwdriver to loosen the screws holding the existing wires onto the outlet and straighten the hooks with needle-nose pliers. Use wire strippers to remove approximately ⅝ inch of sheathing from the hot (black) wire and neutral (white) wire.

Still working with the existing outlet, cut a 6- to 7-inch length from the spool of electrical cable. Remove the white, black, and ground wires from within the sheathing, and strip the ends back approximately ⅝ inch. This wire is called a pigtail.

Use the lineman pliers to twist the ends of the existing wire, the new wire, and one end of the pigtail into a clockwise twist. Do this in three sets: all black wires, all white wires, and all ground wires. Cap each twist with a wire connector.

Use the needle-nose pliers to bend the wires at the other end of the pigtail into a J-shape and hook them onto the outlet. White (neutral) goes to silver, black (hot) goes to gold, and ground goes to green. Be sure that the hooks are running clockwise before tightening the screws.

Head to the other side of the wall to the space you’ve cut out for the new outlet. If the new wire isn’t easy to find, use a fishhook or tape from a wire running kit to fish it out from the wall. Pull the electric outlet wiring through the new opening, and feed the end into the new remodeling box.

Fit the box into the opening and turn the screws attached to the tabs clockwise until the box is secure. You should have about 10 inches of latest wire coming through the box. Strip the plastic sheathing back to expose the hot, neutral, and ground wires, leaving approximately 1 inch of sheathed cable inside the box.

Use the wire stripper to strip the sheathing from the ends of the hot, neutral, and ground wires, exposing approximately ⅝ inch of bare wire. Use the needle-nose pliers to bend the ends of the wires into hooks, and slip them over the screws on the new outlet. Remember to follow typical receptacle wiring techniques: Connect the white wire to the silver screw, the black wire to the gold screw, and the bare copper wire to the ground screw. Finally, bend the wires so they will fold nicely as you attach the outlet to the box.

Tighten the screws at the top and bottom of the outlet until it is snug in the box. Put the faceplate on the outlet and tighten the screw. Flip the breaker back on and test the outlet.

Learning how to wire an electrical outlet isn’t overwhelmingly difficult, and it’s a skill that some DIYers will use time and time again. With a little bit of background and proper safety techniques, adding an outlet for a TV or a latest tool or appliance can be a breeze. Just remember to work safely, test and retest electrical outlets before you start, and follow the instructions above for a reliable and safe electrical outlet installation.

Sources indicate that you absolutely can wire a light switch to an outlet, and there are several ways to do so. The switch can either be installed between the source of the power (typically the panel) and the outlet, or on a loop after the outlet.

To install it in-line, simply run the wire through an electrical box, connect the hot wire to the switch so it can interrupt the flow of power, and then continue the hot wire out to the outlet and wire as normal.

Industry observers note that to install the light switch after the device, connect the neutral wire in the box as usual, then run a length of wire from the outlet to the switch. Connect one end of the wire to the existing hot wire in the box using wire connectors. Next, connect the black wire from this length to one side of the switch, and connect the white wire to the other side. Finally, head back to the outlet and connect the white wire to the gold screw and wrap black electrical tape around the wire to indicate that it is now the hot lead coming from a switch.

It has been revealed that the easiest way to fix an outlet that does not have a proper ground wire is to replace it with a GFCI outlet. These devices detect ground faults and interrupt the circuit right away. Install a GFCI in place of the first power outlet in the run to offer grounding protection.

Of course, contact a local electrical inspector to ensure this meets local codes.
