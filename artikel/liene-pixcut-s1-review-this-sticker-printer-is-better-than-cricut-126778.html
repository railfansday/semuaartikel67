Liene PixCut S1 review: This sticker printer is better than Cricut's StickerPix, and here's why

🌐 Source: Creativebloq • 📅 24 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 11 min read (2256 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways
  • The Liene PixCut S1 is expensive, but its excellent prints, clever templates and fuss-free batch production make it a better dedicated sticker printer than Cricut's StickerPix, and running costs are slightly cheaper.
  • Quality prints, with rich colours and sharp detail.
  • Clever 'Phone Back' feature for custom phone decals.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Laminates and cuts stickers in one simple process.

Quality prints, with rich colours and sharp detail.

10,000+ templates and 50,000 graphics to use.

Clever 'Phone Back' feature for custom phone decals.

Price: $279.99 (Starter Kit), $346.99 (Inspire Kit); £379.99 (Bundle).

Print tech: Four-layer dye-sublimation thermal transfer (D2T2), with a laminate pass.

Resolution: 300 × 300 dpi, 256 shades per colour.

Sticker size: 4 x 7in; printable area 3.9 x 6.7in.

Photo size: 4 x 6in; printable area 3.94 × 5.83in.

Cutting: Single axis, up to 2ips; fine-point carbide blade, 45-degree angle, 1.0mm tip.

Capacity: 36-sheet paper cassette; ribbon cartridge covers 36 sheets of 4 x 7in.

App support: iOS 9.0+ and Android 4.1+.

Dimensions: 281 x 196 x 104.5mm.

In the box: Printer, paper cassette, 18 sheets each of sticker and photo paper, ink cartridge, blade, power adaptor, manual.

It has been revealed that the Liene PixCut S1 prints, laminates and cuts stickers in one go, straight from your phone. It costs between $280 and $347 in the US depending on the bundle (around £349 to £380 in the UK), and it only does a relatively narrow range of things. But it does them very well, and I prefer it to Cricut's StickerPix Print + Cut, even though the PixCut S1 costs around £100 more.

That distinction becomes more vital when you start making several sheets. The PixCut S1 is designed to get a job ready, print it, laminate it and cut it, without having to think too much about the process. It's a specialist machine with an approachable app, and that's actually its biggest strength.

It measures 281 x 196 x 104.5mm and weighs 2.9kg, so it sits on my craft table without taking over and is light enough to shift onto a shelf when you're done. You'll need a bit of clear space at each end for the paper to feed through. Setup is quick. The booklet has clear diagrams for removing the packaging and fitting the cartridge, and the paper tray clicks easily onto the back. Then scan the QR code, install the app, verify your email and pair over Bluetooth. It can pair with up to three devices, which is handy if more than one person in the house wants to use it, or if, like me, you tend to spend the day moving from phone to laptop to tablet without ever settling.

My review unit shipped with a US plug adaptor, so if you're buying in the UK, check what's in the box, but assume, as this is a US-based manufacturer, that you will need to pick up a plug adapter (luckily I review the best drawing tablets, so I have a drawer of adapters).

Sources indicate that the PixCut S1 uses four-layer thermal dye sublimation: it lays down colour in passes, then seals it with a protective laminate layer. It prints at 300 x 300 dpi with 256 tones per colour, delivering a smooth, continuous finish rather than the dotty look of inkjet.

There's no liquid ink here. The cartridge holds dye film, so swapping it is clean and quick, and each one lasts about 36 sheets. It prints stickers on 4 x 7 inch sheets (roughly 3.9 x 6.7 in printable) and photos on 4 x 6 inches. That's the limit of what it can make, so think small stickers, labels, tags and prints rather than substantial projects.

Recent findings suggest that printing times vary from about one to two minutes per sticker sheet, depending on the design complexity. It's quite mesmerising to watch the little printer layer yellow, magenta, and cyan dye as it feeds the design in and out of the printing slot. Looking at the app, you can see each layer scrub down in real time, which is a nice touch.

It has been revealed that the paper tray and cartridge each hold about 36 sheets, so you can work through a decent batch without constantly stopping to replenish supplies. You don't have to print a design, export it, move it into another piece of software, and then set up a separate cutting job. Everything happens in one machine, which makes PixCut S1 feel like what it is: a mini-printer that makes stickers and prints, not a craft machine that wants to do more.

I found the workflow dependable and, importantly, repeatable. Once you've got your design sorted, you can send another sheet through without rethinking the process. That's useful if you're making a set of labels for a small business, preparing gift stickers for Christmas or producing several sheets of your own artwork. It isn't a machine for huge commercial runs, but for short batches it makes a lot of sense.

According to recent reports, speaking of the app, it's incredibly easy to use. If you've used a Cricut and Design Space, you'll know that designing and prepping a job can be a project in itself. Liene's app avoids that because it only has to drive one machine, not a whole ecosystem of craft cutters, printers and heat presses. The Canvas section lets you search templates – type 'boats', and you get a selection – or browse categories like Christmas, holidays and decorative stickers. Pick one, edit it, add your own images and text, and you're away.

Industry observers note that the modes are Stickers, Labels, Phone Back, Cards and Invites, and Photos. They all follow the same workflow, so learning one means you've learned the lot, which again plays into the streamlined nature of using the PixCut S1.

There are 10,000+ templates and 50,000 graphics to use, which is a lot, but they're easy to categorise and search.

Sources indicate that stickers are the mode I've been using most during this review because they're so fun and useful. Choose a style (Original, Watercolour or Comic Crew), upload a photo, and the app generates six sticker designs from it. Edit them on the canvas, delete some to make a bigger sticker, or add text and shapes. Hit Make It, load the paper the app asks for, and press Cut.

Labels come with many ready-made designs: pantry, living spaces, medicine, business, names, plus office, planner and bath collections. They're all editable, which makes them handy for home organising or a small business.

It has been revealed that phone Back stood out to me, and I created a number of fun decals, including a Christmas-illustrated pattern from my own photos. It's simple: pick your phone and model from the Apple, Samsung, Motorola and Google ranges, then use a template or build your own with photos, text, shapes and lines. The PixCut S1 prints it, coats it and cuts it to fit your exact model. I haven't seen anything quite like this on other machines, and it shows what a combined printer and cutter can do that separate devices can't. (You could do something similar on Cricut, but you'll need to measure and design the templates yourself.).

The Photos section covers standard prints, AI portraits, ID photos and collages of up to nine images. The ID photo tool is useful, with on-screen guides to help you frame yourself. The poster option is weaker. Some templates add text I couldn't work out how to edit, which is annoying in an app that is otherwise so flexible.

According to recent reports, photo prints are where the PixCut S1 excels. With 300dpi and 256 tones per colour, you get rich colour and sharp detail, far better than I'd expect from a machine this size. As we're getting overloaded with images, I'm reassured that I can create professional-looking prints from my camera roll at the touch of a button. I tried it with some of my digital art too, and the results were excellent, making professional-quality postcards.

Sources indicate that one area where it will excel over the holidays (or for birthdays and events) is cards and gift tags. Cards print on sticker paper and are cut into rectangles, so you can peel and stick them onto a card. Tags are better because you can edit each one separately and print a single tag for one person instead of wasting a full sheet.

The other big difference between the Liene and Cricut is the cost of keeping them fed. The PixCut S1 isn't cheap to buy, but the running costs are more reasonable than you might expect for a dedicated sticker printer. My review bundle includes one cartridge, 18 sheets of sticker paper and 18 sheets of photo paper – similar to the PixCut S1 deal on Amazon. A refill pack with two cartridges and 72 sheets is around £39.99 on Amazon, which works out at roughly 55p per sheet.

According to recent reports, remember that each cartridge is rated for around 36 sheets, so you're getting 72 prints from the two cartridges and 72 sheets of paper in a refill bundle. You also can't buy a mixed sticker-and-photo pack, so you'll need to decide which material you'll use most.

Industry observers note that only Liene's paper works, so it's not a completely open system, and you're limited to 4 x 7 in sticker and 4 x 6 in photo sheets, so you don't get the material variety you would with a Cricut. But the cost per sheet is reasonable for a laminated, cut-to-shape sticker, especially when you fill a sheet with several designs rather than making a single small sticker.

Cricut's StickerPix Print + Cut has a similar restriction around its own paper and cartridges, and its $299.99 / £249.99 price makes it cheaper to buy initially, but its refills cost more at $47.99 / £47.99. But the lower entry price doesn't necessarily make it better value if you'll use it regularly. You're still buying dedicated consumables, and you're also buying into Design Space.

There's another practical difference when you're making multiple copies. When I used the Cricut, I found that finished sheets need to be manually cleared from the cutting area before the next one can start. It's hardly a deal-breaker, but it adds another little intervention to what should be a simple print-and-cut process. The Liene's appeal is that it feels more like feeding a printer than operating a craft machine.

In a recent development, but oddly, the limitations of the PixCut S1 play into its favour. It's a precise, small and relatively fast device made for one thing – printing and cutting stickers. You're not overburdened with options and can focus on making great prints. Within this narrow category, the PixCut S1 excels and offers options Cricut doesn't, like iPhone case decals. You can create your own templates in Cricut Design Space and fiddle around, but the PixCut S1 does it out of the box for every iPhone (and most Androids).

Here's an at-a-glance look at Liene versus Cricut, because the difference is wafer-thin, but for me, the Pixcut S1 just edges it on running costs and its narrow but specialist nature – especially when it comes to everyday uses like quick phone stickers.

Sources indicate that prints, laminates and cuts stickers in one pass from your phone, using a dedicated app with 10,000+ templates and built-in Phone Back decals. Four-layer dye-sublimation at 300dpi gives rich, sharp prints. It's limited to two paper sizes and Liene consumables, with refills around 55p a sheet.

A cheaper way into print-and-cut stickers, but it runs through Cricut's Design Space app and ecosystem. Refills cost more (£47.99 for 72 prints), it's tied to Cricut's own paper and cartridges, and phone decals mean designing your own templates.

If you only make stickers occasionally, this is a lot of money for a specialist machine. And if you're mainly after ordinary 6 x 4 inch photo prints, there are cheaper ways to get them. But if you regularly make stickers, labels, phone backs, tags and photo prints – and will make good use of the thousands of templates – it's an easy recommendation.

It won't replace a full craft cutter, and it isn't intended to, but as a focused machine for making stickers and art prints, I prefer the Liene PixCut S1 to the Cricut StickerPix Print + Cut. The Cricut is a bit cheaper and has its own appeal, but the Liene is the more straightforward, better-rounded tool. It feels like something you can keep on your desk and actually use quickly, rather than another craft machine that needs a bit of planning every time you want to make something.
