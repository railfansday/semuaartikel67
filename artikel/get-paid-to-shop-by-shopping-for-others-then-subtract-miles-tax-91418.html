In a recent development, the shopping that pays is shopping for someone else, as a grocery shopper on a delivery app, a personal shopper, or a mystery shopper, since cash back on your own purchases only lowers what you spend. Before you count an app’s payouts as income, subtract what your miles cost and the self-employment tax you’ll owe.

A shopper app lets you pick your orders and hours around a day job, and it shows each order’s pay before you accept it.

According to recent reports, instacart pays per batch, its name for a job, based on the driving distance, item count, and expected shopping time, plus promotions when demand runs high and 100% of the tip. Shipt pays per order based on its own estimate of the time and effort, at least $16 an hour by that estimate, plus bonuses and tips. Taking longer than the estimate earns nothing extra. Both require a reliable car and treat you as an independent contractor, so no tax comes out of your pay.

Price each mile at the IRS’s standard mileage rate, which is also what you can deduct for each business mile. The IRS bases it on what it costs to own and run a car, not just gas. At an illustrative 75 cents a mile, an hour of work that pays $25 and takes 20 miles of driving leaves $10 after the car. Self-employment tax, due once your year’s profit reaches $400, takes about 14% of that $10, so you keep roughly $8.60 before income tax. Over a full week, counting the hours you wait between orders, the same math gives your real hourly pay.

Instacart never penalizes you for passing on a batch, so you can skip any whose miles would cost most of what it pays. Log every mile you drive for work, since the deduction lowers your income tax and self-employment tax.

Before you accept any order or assignment, subtract its miles, priced at the IRS rate, from what it pays, then set aside about 14% of the rest for self-employment tax.
