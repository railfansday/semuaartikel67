Blood proteins may flag prostate cancer risk more than a decade before diagnosis

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Oxford found men's blood differed 15 years before a prostate cancer blood test or diagnosis, with an immune protein among the clearest signs.

According to recent reports, the blood test men know for prostate cancer measures one protein, PSA, which is made by the prostate itself. In blood drawn from about 2,500 European men in the 1990s, researchers at Oxford measured more than 6,000 proteins instead.

The men diagnosed with prostate cancer more than 15 years later already differed in a dozen of them.

One of the clearest differences was an immune protein those men had less of, not more. None of the proteins is ready to become a test, the team said. Most of the differences were modest, and not all of them held up in other groups of men.

Zhe Huang, a postdoctoral epidemiologist at Oxford Population Health, part of the University of Oxford, led the work with senior authors Karl Smith-Byrne and Ruth Travis.

Industry observers note that “Prostate cancer is not one disease. Many prostate cancers grow slowly and may never cause harm, while others are aggressive and can be life-threatening,” Huang remarked.

The samples came from EPIC, a European health study that recruited more than half a million people in the 1990s and stored their blood.

It has been revealed that huang’s team picked 982 men from Italy, the Netherlands, Spain and the United Kingdom who later developed prostate cancer. They compared them with a random sample of 1,573 men from the same study, followed for a median of about 16 years.

Sources indicate that men who developed the disease were a median of about six years older when they gave their blood. So the analysis adjusted for age, along with body mass index, smoking, drinking and education.

The idea was to compare the blood itself, not the men’s ages.

A lab platform called SomaScan measured more than 6,400 proteins in each sample. The team asked which proteins differed years ahead of any diagnosis, and separately which differed ahead of an aggressive cancer.

Aggressive meant high grade, spread beyond the prostate, a very high PSA at diagnosis, or death from the disease, and 290 men fit that definition.

Sources indicate that the researchers linked twelve proteins to prostate cancer diagnosed more than 15 years after the blood draw. The clearest was GZMK, a protein made by the immune cells that recognize and destroy abnormal cells.

Men with more GZMK in their blood were less likely to be diagnosed, and the longer the gap to diagnosis, the stronger that link became.

Recent findings suggest that the authors offered two readings. More GZMK may mean the immune system is doing a better job of clearing early abnormal cells.

Or the men who later developed cancer may have had fewer of those cells in the blood because the cells had moved into the prostate.

In an interview with Earth.com, Huang and Smith-Byrne stated researchers generally consider prostate cancer an immunologically “cold” tumor, one that tends to draw a weak immune response.

“This raises interesting questions about the role of the immune system in the very early stages of cancer development,” they added.

Two proteins tied to inflammation, IL-17B and OSCAR, ran the other way. Higher levels went with higher long-term risk.

Recent findings suggest that seven proteins were linked to aggressive prostate cancer. Researchers linked one of them, ANKRD1, to every serious form of the disease examined. These included cancers that had spread and cancers that proved fatal, the authors wrote.

Another, AMIGO2, linked to aggressive disease, and most strongly to cancers that had grown beyond the prostate.

This is an adhesion molecule, part of how cells attach to their surroundings, and lab studies have tied it to the process by which tumor cells break away and travel.

FLT4, a receptor involved in growing upcoming blood and lymph vessels, was linked to nearly every outcome the team measured.

Recent findings suggest that the strongest single protein was KLK3, which is PSA under its gene name. Higher PSA levels years earlier increased a man’s likelihood of receiving a diagnosis later.

That isn’t surprising, the authors wrote, because a high PSA reading is what sends a man for a biopsy in the first place.

According to recent reports, the team also looked at 71 genes already known to drive prostate cancer when they mutate, and asked whether the proteins those genes produce showed up differently in blood. Five did.

Proteins made by three of those genes, ERBB3, KRAS and NRAS, were linked to aggressive cancers diagnosed about a decade later.

The driver-gene result was the one that surprised the two researchers most.

“These genes are well established in prostate cancer biology, so it was quite surprising to find that their circulating protein products were associated with aggressive prostate cancer,” Huang and Smith-Byrne said.

It has been revealed that that doesn’t mean the cancer was already there when the blood was drawn, the authors wrote. Nor is a protein level in blood proof that the gene itself has gone wrong.

Their reading is that the proteins mark early disturbances in the biological pathways to which those genes belong.

The team checked its findings against a U.S. study of 4,284 men, 590 of whom developed prostate cancer. Only about half of the proteins had been measured there.

Two of them, KLK3 and ACP3, another prostate protein, were clearly linked to the disease there too, and two more revealed the same direction of association.

Several others, including OSCAR, AMIGO2 and FLT4, were associated in opposite directions in Black and White men. The U.S. study had too few Black participants to settle which direction is real, the authors wrote.

In the U.K. Biobank, 18 of the proteins had been measured with a different technology, and the two platforms agreed poorly.

None of those proteins was linked to prostate cancer there. Genetic analyses supported a handful, including AMIGO2, KLK3 and OSCAR.

The aggressive-cancer results also come from fewer men. Tumor grade was unknown for 31 percent of the cases and stage for 46 percent, and only 102 men died of the disease.

Some of the associations may be chance, the authors wrote, and the study had no information on family history.

Whether any of these blood proteins can predict a man’s prostate cancer risk better than his age and PSA already do hasn’t been tested.

The findings still have to be confirmed in larger and more diverse groups of men, Huang and Smith-Byrne stated. Their study also mainly looked for proteins that may help a prostate cancer develop, not proteins that could spot a cancer already there.

“Developing a blood test for early detection would require further dedicated prediction studies,” they told Earth.com.

Huang said measuring thousands of proteins in blood collected years early lets researchers begin to ask how men who later develop aggressive prostate cancer differ from men who don’t.

The next step runs through a Cancer Research U.K. program on prostate cancer causes and prevention, which the team mentioned will test whether the findings can improve prediction or prevention.

Some of the proteins may even play a causal role in how the cancer develops, the two researchers remarked.

“If confirmed by future experimental and clinical studies, this could open up exciting opportunities for earlier prevention, potentially even through approaches such as cancer vaccines,” they said.

For now, the GZMK result is a clue rather than a tool. Men who were never diagnosed had more of an immune protein in their blood 15 years earlier. Whether measuring it would help any patient is the question still open.

The full study was published in the journal European Urology.
