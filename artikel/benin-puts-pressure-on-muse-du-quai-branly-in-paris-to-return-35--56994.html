It has been revealed that A lot has happened between November 2021, when France formally returned 26 Benin Bronzes from its national collections to Benin, and today, when news of the West African nation’s latest request was reported by Le Monde. Namely, in May 2026, French Parliament passed a historic law designed to simplify the process by which artworks looted during the colonial era are returned to their nations of origin.

In a recent development, the latest request was first broached last summer and reiterated in the fall, when Beninese President Romuald Wadagni visited Paris and met with French President Emmanuel Macron. Like the 2021 repatriation, the latest request is directed to the Musée du Quai Branly – Jacques Chirac, whose collection is made up of objects from Indigenous groups in Africa, Asia, and Oceania. In a recent development, “We felt somewhat unsatisfied after the first return,” Alain Godonou, project manager for heritage and museums for the presidency of Benin, told Le Monde. “We drew up a second list which, in our view, meets the criteria for return under the [new] law. We are confident in our rightful claim.”.

According to recent reports, the latest request includes a divination tray and several types of weapons. But the most notable object still in the French state’s collection is a hammered iron sculpture of Gou, a god of metalworking and war. This particular representation of Gou was made by Akati Ekplekendo in the mid-19th century and was taken from Benin by the French captain Eugène Fonssagrives in 1892. The 5½-foot-tall sculpture was one of 600 objects included in the 1935 exhibition “African Negro Art” at the Museum of Modern Art in Fresh York and was also featured in the portfolio of photographs Walker Evans made in response to the show.

The latest law stipulates that a joint committee—made up, in this case, of experts from France and Benin—has two years to review new claims, at which point France’s highest administrative court will make a ruling. “In France, [the Gou] sculpture has been underestimated,” said Marie-Cécile Zinsou, a French-Beninese art historian who is a member of the committee. “Whereas in Benin it will be highly valued. It is crucial for rebuilding our memory.”.
