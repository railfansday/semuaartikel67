Recent findings suggest that vietnam's Ho Chi Minh International Airport (officially Tan Son Nhat International Airport) is the airport serving Ho Chi Minh City (Saigon) and southern Vietnam. Its passenger volume has grown to the point where it is considerably over capacity, and the international terminal is struggling.

Sources indicate that I have lived in Vietnam (a few minutes' drive from the airport) for more than two years, and I regularly fly through it on both international and domestic flights. My gym offers a view of aircraft taking off and landing. For me, the convenient proximity of the airport is worth much more than the modernity of the soon-to-open Long Thanh International Airport located well outside the city.

However, the airport is over capacity and cannot accommodate future growth. The existing terminal was built to handle 8-10 million passengers a year and was expanded to 13 million a year in 2018. Currently, the terminal is handling 18+ million passengers annually. The new airport being constructed (expected to open at the end of 2026 or early 2027) is to complement, but not entirely replace, Tan Son Nhat International Airport.

Sources indicate that terminal 2 is the international terminal. Domestic services use Terminal 1 and Terminal 3 (more on them below). From my experience, Terminal 2 has enough boarding gates to serve its international flights, although the low-cost airline, VietJet, uses airport shuttle buses. Overall, the terminal is old, somewhat cramped, and run-down.

Industry observers note that meanwhile, many of its vendors feel more like ad hoc street vendors than modern international airport vendors. I have seen the check-in queues for VietJet wrap around much of the area with wait times of at least an hour. Sometimes VietJet's queues are modest, but they are highly variable. Those flying with VietJet should arrive at the airport sooner.

Industry observers note that the check-in queues for other airlines are generally fine, with VietJet being a major outlier. Besides VietJet, the airport's major bottleneck is immigration (emigration). As a rule of thumb, the queues for checking in for Vietnam Airlines, AirAsia, and international flag carriers are modest or otherwise acceptable.

After immigration, the queues for security are relatively better, but it can still take another 15 minutes. Altogether, I consider the terminal to be old, prone to significant queues at immigration, strained, but also perfectly functional. Even under strain, with a single runway, the terminal operates smoothly, and flights are not normally delayed.

Once past immigration, prices are listed in US dollars, and everything feels overpriced relative to the state of the terminal and the presentation of the vendors. It is common to see the same Vietnamese noodle soup (Pho) that costs the equivalent of $3 outside the airport cost around $25 inside it.

I'm anticipating that the vendors will be one of the biggest changes at the upcoming Long Thanh International Airport. Most of the vendors sell Vietnamese souvenirs such as traditional conical hats, figurines, Vietnamese coffee, and Vietnamese chocolate. The airport does have a duty-free section, but it is relatively limited.

Separately, when arriving, there is also a relatively modest duty-free section at the baggage reclaim area. The waiting area of the airport is functional, but dated and limited in options. There are dining options offering a selection of Western and Vietnamese food, some lounges, and cafes.

Industry observers note that one upshot is that the terminal's windows and main dining area have excellent views of the runway and airplane operations. Many of the stock photos used by Simple Flying's articles are from Shutterstock, and a meaningful percentage of these were taken by plane spotters at Tan Son Nhat International Airport​​​​.

Recent findings suggest that generally, all the immigration and emigration booths are manned all the time. I have never had any concerns with immigration, nor have I seen other passengers having issues. That remarked, I did talk to one Azeri father of several children who said he shouted at the Vietnamese immigration officers until his voice was hoarse.

It has been revealed that as I recall, his distress was due to immigration refusing entry to his daughter on account of a spelling error on her visa. They eventually let her through. I have passed through Vietnamese land and air checkpoints dozens of times with no concerns. The queue for emigration is commonly 45 minutes. This can be less, but it can also be more, with queues lasting an hour.

There are some e-Gates, but generally no one, apart from flight crew, seems to use them. I understand there is a fee to use them, but I'm not exactly sure. Unlike most countries, Vietnamese immigration and emigration at this airport do not have separate lines for citizens and foreigners. Having dual nationality, I can choose to visit Vietnam on a British or Latest Zealand passport.

Vietnam is visa-free for British citizens and most of Europe. US, NZ, Australia, and most others can get a straightforward 90-day eVisa. As a note of caution, there are many third-party websites online offering Vietnamese eVisas. Sometimes these can be faster than going through the official site, but it is important to remember these are not official sites. Bring a printed copy of the eVisa.

In a recent development, vietnam now requires visitors to fill out the online arrival card. The queues at immigration are around the same as those at emigration. It routinely takes around 45 minutes to get through immigration, with 20 minutes being impressive. An hour is common, depending on how many flights have arrived. All the booths are typically staffed. Arriving passengers can purchase a hassle-free local SIM card before immigration or after customs.

After immigration and collecting baggage is customs. As of the time of writing, Vietnam does not require a customs declaration form, and it's somewhat random as to whether arrivals need to go through the queue to scan baggage or just walk through the Nothing to Declare line. Plenty of taxis are waiting outside customs. Passengers can book a taxi, or go by e-hailing (Grab is popular).

Recent findings suggest that there are also currency exchange booths for those wanting Vietnamese Dong. In my experience, Vietnam's ACB bank has the lowest cash withdrawal fees: for me, it's the only bank I've found that is free for international withdrawals. Taxis typically need to be paid in cash (or QR code for those with a Vietnamese bank). The e-hailing app Grab accepts international credit cards, but others may not.

According to recent reports, the airport has two domestic terminals. Vietnam's low-cost carrier, VietJet, uses the old Terminal 1, while the flag carrier, Vietnam Airlines, has moved operations to the recently opened Terminal 2 (along with Bamboo Airways, Pacific Airlines, Vasco, Sun PhuQuoc Airways). This has freed up capacity in Terminal 1.

When I've flown through Terminal 1, VietJet was not making use of the extra capacity and check-in counters, meaning queues were as long and frustrating as ever, with terminal capacity idle. Some of the terminal's security capacity was also not in use, resulting in more queues.

Terminal 3 is the opposite. It is a modern, gleaming latest terminal with plenty of capacity, bright lighting, large windows, and modern vendors. Vietnam is considering looks to continue to expand and upgrade the terminal with more technology. The security check is rapid, and checking in is straightforward.

Recent findings suggest that getting between the three terminals is a short five-minute, mostly covered walk between Terminal 2 (the international terminal) and Terminal 1 (VietJet's domestic terminal). However, Terminal 3 is isolated and cannot be reached on foot or within the airport itself. There is a free airport shuttle connecting them, or passengers can take taxis.

Industry observers note that the routes taken are through dense parts of the city and take around 10–15 minutes. Vietnam is presently considering building a tunnel to connect the two domestic terminals. Passengers flying with VietJet should proposal for more time at check-in and security than those flying with Vietnam Airlines.

Ho Chi Minh City is soon to join other megacities in operating a two-airport system: Mumbai and Bangkok are other notable Asian examples of such cities. When the newly introduced airport, Long Thanh International Airport, opens, it will take over long-haul international operations. However, the old airport will continue operating as a domestic and regional international airport.

Vietnam Airlines has its new terminal for domestic flights, and there are renovation aims for VietJet's rundown domestic terminal. Proposals will see Terminal 1 demolished and rebuilt, as well as the international terminal upgraded and Terminal 3 progressively expanded. Several rail and road links are being built or expanded to connect the two Ho Chi Minh City airports.

Sources indicate that work is underway to build the Thu Thiem – Long Thanh Railway to the newly introduced airport. Construction started in July 2026. Thu Thiem is still a considerable distance across the densest parts of the city, so it will improve connectivity, but not directly connect the airports. Meanwhile, future metro lines are expected to reach the original airport in the future. Authorities are discussing a direct highway link, but that is in the early planning stages.

According to recent reports, ​​​​​​​Until then, the connection between the airports will likely be an concern. Tan Son Nhat is expected to remain a regional international airport, mostly for Southeast Asia destinations. Overall, its traffic is expected to increase to around 40 million annually by 2030, with domestic passengers accounting for around 75% of the total. By that time, Long Thanh should be handling around 45 million passengers annually.
