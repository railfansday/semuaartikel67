Sources indicate that A couple of years ago, I’d made the painful decision to leave my marriage, and my beloved dog of 13 years had died. Suddenly, I found myself deeply alone.

The life I had always imagined for myself—a long-term stable marriage and a family nested in community—had never materialized.

One day, though, I realized that there was something that I had dreamed of when I was younger, that suddenly might be possible.

Industry observers note that I went. And I went again. I discovered a lesser-known town in my favorite area, and an affordable apartment that I could use periodically in the off-season. I committed to going, using airline points, when I had lulls between stretches of speaking engagements.

Because of my youthful obsession with all things Italian, I had studied the language. I’d also had a long-distance Italian boyfriend for a few months in my early 30s.

I brushed up via a language app, and this was the key that opened all doors.

In Canada, I was a solitary woman who had to book appointments to see her busy friends, all of whom had families.

In Italy, I found myself swept up into a large, generous family and their circle of friends, and rarely had enough time to sit and read a book.

The way I learned to live in Italy transformed the way I live at home.

Recent findings suggest that I always thought my phone aversion was due to being an introvert. Even if the name on my screen was a friend, I’d send it to voicemail and text them a little bit later.

In Italy, I discovered the delight of being interrupted.

According to recent reports, at least once a day, my friend Vincenza knocked at my door unannounced, calling out my name. My old Canadian self might have pretended not to be home, but I began to associate these unexpected visits with delight. Vincenza usually had a plate of hot pasta or pastries in her arms, or her sister Angela would call to invite me for lunch.

The effect stuck. Today, when a friend or family member calls me, I find myself answering. Sometimes I even call them.

Sources indicate that part of why I wouldn’t answer the phone when it rang was because it wasn’t part of "the plan." I ran a tight ship. Or perhaps not actually that tight, because I was usually already behind on the day’s to-do list. There wasn’t room for an unplanned 15-minute chat with a friend.

In Italy, Angela randomly calls to let me know that they’ll gather in the piazza in 10 minutes “because the sun is so beautiful today.”.

An impromptu lunch invitation may stretch into a couple of hours around a table with friends.

When I run into Vincenza’s daughter, Nausica, while out running errands, she often invites me for a spontaneous coffee or aperitivo, or to go to a store around the corner and help her pick out a dress for an event.

In my past life, I believed that because I was doing something (anything) else, the natural answer was, "Sorry, not now.".

I’ve learned that unless I absolutely must be doing something else, the best answer is “Sure, why not?”.

If you’ve seen the meme of the hyper-independent woman carrying a clothes washer up the subway steps by herself, that’s me.

Recent findings suggest that I don’t like needing or asking for help. I am, however, a professional helper. Literally. I’m a medical doctor, an executive coach, and a professional speaker/educator. Perhaps most importantly, I’m also the first-born daughter of four.

I will help you with things you didn’t even know you needed help with.

Then there are my Italian friends. They don’t need my help. They don’t seem to even want my help.

Recent findings suggest that they stealthily pay for my aperitivo or pizza, sneaking the waiter money while pretending to use the washroom. I once tried to pay for parking, and my friend wrestled me out of the way so intensely that I hesitate to ever attempt it again.

Recent findings suggest that but what about medical help? Surely, there might be potential for heroics. Nope, they already have a doctor in the family.

The thing is, they seem to just enjoy having me around—without me doing anything other than showing up and eating the food they place in front of me, with great enthusiasm. This seems to be all one needs to do to earn permanently adopted status.

I think that was the ultimate blow that broke the chains of my very North American loneliness. In a “What can you do for me?” world, it’s remarkable to discover a collective of people, all of whom seem to live by the same rules: “You’ll always have a place at my table—just don’t argue when I serve you more pasta.”.
