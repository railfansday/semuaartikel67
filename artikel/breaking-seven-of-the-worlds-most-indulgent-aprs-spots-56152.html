Under the sun-drenched, south-facing slope of the Matterhorn, après-ski tastes different—like homegrown Alpine herbal Génépy liqueur, Italian grappa, cloves, and orange zest all mixed with coffee.

Sources indicate that this local mountain drink has been passed around the table for centuries in a multi-spouted little goblet fit for a hobbit, chiseled from a local walnut tree. But to sip from the Friendship Cup, or “Coppa dell’Amicizia”, you’ll need to drive two hours northwest of Milan or ski 14 miles on one of the world’s longest interconnected trails, leaving Zermatt’s vertical terrain, to cross into the Italian border recently redrawn by climate change.

You’ll know you’ve arrived in Italy’s Breuil-Cervinia when you pass directly beneath the Alps’ famous Toblerone-shaped peak (even after it was kicked off the candy bar wrapper).

Recent findings suggest that here in northwest Italy’s Aosta Valley, at the base inside VRetreats’ Zéro Lounge Bar & Bistrò, over affordable polenta, mountain cheese, and speck, the Friendship Cup is still rounding the table—always counterclockwise and never placed back down until every drop is gone. That would be bad luck.

Sources indicate that “Its purpose is to bring friends together on the slopes,” says Daniel Menabreaz, manager of VRetreats Cervino Hotel, home of Zero Lounge, where guests can sip from the Friendship Cup while gazing out a wall of windows at the Matterhorn. “For our mountain community, where solidarity has always been an important value, it has become a genuine symbol of local identity.”.

Skiers have been warming up with drinks and snacks after a day on the slopes ever since, toasting their survival in 19th-century Norway. Après-ski was popularized in Chamonix over “vin chaud” (mulled wine) paired with Chartreuse from Carthusian monks, and with the evolution of skiing came distinct regional après traditions. Today, after sizing up the vertical drop and sending it down the chute, the surest way to get to know a ski area is still celebrating like the locals.

Hot Take: Après Should Only Be For Those Who Ski.

“Après-ski traditions are part of what gives each ski destination its own personality. They offer a window into a resort’s history, from local food and drink to the people who built the mountain community,” notes Dana Zapfel, curator and director of collections for the Colorado Snowsports Museum in Vail.

Passing around the Friendship Cup in Italy, table dancing in ski boots in Austria, soaking in a Japanese onsen, or sharing a gourd of yerba mate around an Argentinian fire pit—this is what après-ski looks like around the world.

Imagine huddling around a backcountry fire pit in Patagonia. The kettle is steaming. The wind is whipping. Someone passes you a hollowed-out gourd filled with bright grassy green dried tea leaves, no booze involved. No one’s in a hurry. You’re still riding the natural high of taking turns overlooking sub-Antarctic ocean passages at the edge of the world.

Industry observers note that yerba mate’s earthy, bitter flavors have gotten skiers through the shortest, coldest days at the southern tip of Tierra del Fuego. They’re also a constant at Cerro Castor in Ushuaia, Argentina—and at the largest lift-served ski resort in the southern hemisphere, Cerro Catedral in San Carlos de Bariloche.

Recent findings suggest that this Argentinian ritual started over 1,000 years ago with the region’s indigenous Guaraní people and was later carried by the gauchos through the plains and mountaineers looking for connection on the trail. Today, with the gourds in the backcountry, it’s also consumed via thermos cups in the chairlift line or while admiring the granite spires and sprawling glacial lakes (with a nibble of Argentinian chocolate and churros to cut the bitter taste).

On the same side of the Continental Divide, but worlds away from warm tea leaves and metal straws, is the parking lot at Arapahoe Basin.

Like many Colorado ski resorts, A-Basin was developed in the mid-20th century by 10th Mountain Division vet (Larry Jump with Olympic skier Frederick “Sandy” Schauffler), and while many of the other resorts just got bougier, A-Basin stayed true to its purpose.

This no-frills skier’s mountain is always one of the first to open and among the last to close out North America’s season, making the high-altitude spring ski party a non-negotiable. The question, with no slopeside accommodations, was how. In the 1980s, skiers pulled out their folding chairs and tables, popped up their tents and fired up the grills, in bathing suits no less. It’s been shot-skis all around at the bottom of the slope in the Early Riser parking lot, affectionately known as the Beach, ever since.

According to recent reports, if A-Basin’s “Beach” out west is for tailgating, the East Coast Beach was made for reggae. Since 1988, when the spring snow begins to melt, die-hards have come out to play at the base of Sugarloaf for the annual Reggae Festival. This season’s festival will take place April 9-11, 2027.

Expect this massive mountain block party to fill up with “Loafers,” aka die-hard Sugarloaf skiers decked out in Pan-African red, gold, green, and black, neon tie-dyes and retro onesies—beach ball in one hand, can of official Orono Brewing Company Reggae Fest in the other.

From the mid-mountain stage at Bullwinkle’s Lodge (and a brand-new ice igloo inspired by Sunday River) to late-night bands at the Widowmaker and King Pine Room, this one’s for the rain-or-shine crowd.

In a recent development, in Japan, après-ski brings unmatched coziness. About three hours northwest of Tokyo in the Japanese Alps, on the main island of Honshu, skiers warm their sore muscles by baring it all (nudity required) in 13 natural hot springs inside bathhouses, known as onsen. This once-sacred bathing ritual, which starts from the chairlift, has been happening here for over twelve hundred years, since long before Nozawa Onsen Snow Resort opened in 1924.

After soaking it all up, walk the narrow cobblestone lantern-lit streets past steaming hot water channels to stop for sake in izakaya pubs—and if you’re feeling really rejuvenated, keep it going at traditional late-night karaoke and midnight ramen right there in the village.

With a 3,560-foot vertical drop and 400-plus inches of seasonal deep powder, skiing never felt better.

Whether you’re 22 or 72, table dancing in your ski boots in St. Anton’s MooserWirt (or neighboring Krazy Kanga) is unmatched, mostly because everyone’s dancing together. This is the world’s foremost legendary ski bar for good reason, beer consumption aside. With 23 miles of underground tubing pumping beer into the MooseWirt’s seven bars for 2,000 thirsty skiers a day—it’s no surprise, St. Anton claims Austria’s highest beer sales per square foot.

It has been revealed that there are more key things to celebrate here, though. St. Anton am Arlberg is, of course, famously home to one of the oldest ski clubs and the most famous ski school in the world, where the father of modern skiing, Hannes Schneider, first taught the stem Christie ski method, the same instructional ways still used today.

According to recent reports, ride the chairlift over the Stuben hamlet where Schneider grew up in 1890, fuel up on a mid-mountain lunch at Valluga View, get up the nerve to ride the tin can gondola originally built in 1955 up to the summit for views of five countries, ski down for eight miles (but only with a certified mountain guide). And by the time you reach the bottom of Piste 50, you’ll hear the thumping—not of the bass, but from the stomping ski boots on the picnic tables (good thing they’re bolted down).

Somewhere between a simple mug of mulled mountain wine at a post-war ski gathering and the invasion of Hollywood and global billionaires, things got fancy in Courchevel, France.

Built on empty cow pastures to boost the economy, the resort made room to grow by design and kept getting more extravagant. Today, you have your pick of all the Michelin-starred restaurants and lavish slope-side parties in private chalets and five-star hotels—or maybe you’re after an ultra-luxury cigar from The Winston Club. Sink into the leather armchair next to a crackling wood fireplace by the window with sweeping summit views; then let the cigar sommelier select a rare Cuban to pair with your vintage cognac and finish off the day.

And, if you dream substantial enough, you might make it back for the 2030 Winter Olympics (Alpes 2030), when Courchevel becomes an official host once again.
