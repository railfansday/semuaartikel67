Sources indicate that early in your career, you’ve probably been told you have to choose: Make money, or make a difference. Do well first. Then, once you’ve earned it, do good through philanthropy. I believed that for most of my career. I spent decades on Wall Street, eventually becoming a partner in one of the country’s leading alternative investment firms, where I oversaw its real estate business. Simultaneously, I spent years as a philanthropist. Both paths left me unsatisfied. Growing net worth felt hollow, and many of the charities I funded treated symptoms without “curing the disease.” A FALSE CHOICE What I’ve learned since is that the choice is false. Some of the best investment opportunities for the next generation sit inside society’s hardest problems. They start with the foundations every community depends on: housing, education, and healthcare. That’s where my firm invests, because when those foundations are strong, everything built on them gets stronger too. The investors who see this opportunity first will do well, and they’ll help reshape our world. They will build economic success and solve societal problems at the same time. When Magic Johnson and I launched our first urban real estate fund in 1998, I thought raising the capital would take six months. It took two-and-a-half years. Investors believed then, as many still do, that attaching a social goal to an investment means sacrificing returns. We proved them wrong. Over 15 years, those funds raised $2 billion, facilitated $6 billion in urban revitalization, and created tens of thousands of jobs while generating strong returns. Potential investors also didn’t believe that an athlete could be a financial success of this scale. Yet today, Magic is a billionaire and proved those doubters wrong. WHAT IMPACT INVESTING LOOKS LIKE Done right, impact investing can deliver better risk-adjusted returns than traditional strategies. This type of investing targets large, growing, unmet needs in markets less tied to the broader economy, where government and philanthropy have historically been the only players. Take workforce housing. In 2023, over 21 million renter households (49.7 percent of renter households) spent at least 30 percent of their income on housing. Households spending more than 50 percent are considered severely cost-burdened. I’m talking about teachers, police officers, firefighters, and healthcare workers who earn too much for subsidized housing but not enough to buy a home. They don’t need a handout. They need housing that’s within reach. Building new affordable housing at market-rate returns without public incentives is nearly impossible. Meanwhile, existing affordable units keep disappearing as investors buy them, renovate, and raise rents on the people who can least afford it. We flipped the model by buying existing properties and keeping rents affordable. Then we offer reduced rent to teachers, law enforcement officers, and healthcare workers who live on-site and provide tutoring, community safety, and health programs in return. The result is pride in rentership. When residents feel invested in where they live, they stay longer and treat the property better. In our first housing fund, lease terms increased 32%, crime fell 48%, and economic loss dropped 37%. Net operating income grew 13%, and the fund delivered a 25% internal rate of return, all without raising rents. The social outcome drove the financial outcome. IMPACT MARKETING As impact investing grows, so does impact marketing. Plenty of funds now put “impact” on the label without changing anything underneath. Here are four things to look for. 1. Ask where the impact indicates up in the financials. If the social goal could be cut without touching returns, it’s an add-on, and it will be the first thing cut when times get tight. Real impact drives the business. In our housing, the community programs are why tenants stay longer, which is why the numbers work. 2. Look for measured outcomes, not good intentions. Lease duration, crime rates, graduation rates, patient outcomes. If a fund can’t show you the data, the impact is a story, not a strategy. 3. Follow the demand. Durable impact investments meet an existing and growing need. Be wary of anything that depends on creating demand or on a single subsidy that could disappear. 4. Look at who’s on the team. Ask whether the people making decisions understand the problem firsthand. Our team includes former teachers, physicians, law enforcement officers, and frontline workers alongside recovering bankers. That expertise is how we spot financial and social risk before it costs anyone. FINAL THOUGHTS Too many Americans have their health, education, and economic outcomes determined by where they were born. That isn’t sustainable, and government and philanthropy can’t solve it alone. If you’re early in your career, you have something my generation didn’t: proof that you don’t have to choose. You can build a career and a portfolio around solving the problems that matter most, and get paid for doing it. That’s the conversation I want to have with the next generation of investors. Impact investing has never been more critical, and it has never been more rewarding. Bobby Turner is founder of Turner Impact Capital.
