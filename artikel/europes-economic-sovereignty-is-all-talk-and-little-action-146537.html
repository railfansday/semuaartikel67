European debates about economic security often sound like a succession of buzzwords: “strategic autonomy,” “de-risking,” “diversification.” For all the lofty aspirations, none of these policies have done much to change Europe’s trajectory. As the European Council on Foreign Relations’ new report on global interdependence (which I co-authored) documents, the European Union keeps growing more reliant on the United States in finance, technology, and trade.

Let’s start with finance. Nearly 25 years after the euro began circulating, one might assume that it is the go-to currency for European Union firms doing business outside the bloc. The data tells a different story: In 2024, EU firms used the euro for only 46 percent of their transactions outside the EU, down just 2 percentage points from 2016, compared to a steady 41 percent invoiced in dollars. Despite long-standing calls from the European Commission for companies to switch to euros, the dollar’s prominence partly reflects its dominance of energy markets; in 2024, EU firms used dollars for 87 percent of their petroleum imports.

According to recent reports, next, look at European financial investments. Since U.S. President Donald Trump embraced tariffs in 2025 and then threatened to seize Greenland in early 2026, there has been talk in some EU capitals that divesting Europe’s significant holdings of U.S. Treasurys could be a way to pressure Washington. EU-based investment funds and central banks must have missed the memo: Together, they made net purchases of U.S. Treasurys totaling $56 billion between January 2025 and this August. (The data excludes Belgium, Ireland, and Luxembourg, as these countries’ hefty holdings mostly reflect their role as financial hubs.).

Recent findings suggest that despite Paris’s role as Europe’s vocal champion of strategic autonomy, French investors accounted for 61 percent of these purchases—cementing France’s status as the top EU holder of U.S. Treasurys, with $363 billion in holdings as of August.

EU appetite for U.S. public debt stems from one simple fact: At just $7 trillion, the combined stock of German, French, and EU-issued sovereign bonds is five times smaller than the stock of U.S. Treasurys. Without a greater supply of euro-denominated safe assets, EU funds have little choice but to accumulate U.S. Treasurys. This increases Europe’s exposure to threats from the United States, such as the advice of former Federal Reserve Board of Governors member Stephen Miran to withhold part of the interest that Washington pays on foreign-owned Treasurys.

Sources indicate that these days, all eyes are on artificial intelligence, and this sector highlights the extent to which calls for European de-risking are going unanswered in the tech field. In late October, Epoch AI ranked 64 models at or above the level of OpenAI’s GPT-5. Of these, 49 were American, 14 were Chinese, and just one was European—France’s Mistral Large 4. It’s no surprise, then, that American models are becoming the default choice for European firms. According to the German tech industry association Bitkom, 76 percent of German companies using AI rely on OpenAI’s ChatGPT, up from 70 percent last year. Not a single one of the surveyed firms uses Mistral.

Industry observers note that U.S. dominance of Western AI is unlikely to end any time soon. Epoch researchers also found that between January and June 2026, U.S.-based firms bought 78 percent of all AI semiconductors powering data centers and supporting the training of new, more powerful models. EU-based companies purchased just 5 percent of these chips during the same period, paving the way for the trans-Atlantic AI gap to widen further.

Current EU investment metrics make it hard to imagine a more positive outcome for European ambitions in other frontier sectors, such as quantum computing and biotechnology. In the field of quantum technology, data from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology demonstrates that only four EU countries (Finland, France, Germany, and the Netherlands) have funding levels worth noting. Yet together, they accounted for just around 10 percent of global financing in 2025.

In a recent development, meanwhile, the United States captured nearly two-thirds of worldwide funding for quantum technology, while Britain and Canada—both much smaller economies than the EU—are also notable players. In medical biotech, the pattern is the same: U.S. firms secured 63 percent of global venture capital between January 2015 and June 2025, nine times the 7 percent raised by EU firms, according to the European Commission.

Recent findings suggest that the abysmally low levels of funding for quantum computing and biotech suggest that current European concerns about dependence on the United States for AI are only the start of a long line of similar debates. Time and again, EU firms and consumers end up buying critical technology from elsewhere that they did not finance and develop at home. As the United States’ decision in June to cut off global access to Anthropic’s Fable 5 indicated, this gives Washington a simple way to deprive Europe of critical technology.

Trade is the final area in which the EU is reliant on the United States. Consider EU exports. Despite pledges to diversify, European firms have become more dependent on the U.S. market over the past decade. U.S. firms bought 21 percent of the EU’s goods exports in 2025, up from 17 percent in 2015. While a rush to stockpile European goods before the start of U.S. tariffs can partly explain the spike in EU exports to the United States last year, it does not change the long-term trend. Nor is this Europe rerouting exports that China no longer buys; at 8 percent in 2025, China’s share of EU goods exports was unchanged from 2015.

Industry observers note that instead, two factors help explain why European exporters have become increasingly reliant on the U.S. market. The first is pharmaceuticals. Half of the 244 billion euro rise in EU exports to the United States between 2015 and 2025 can be attributed to medicines, such as the Irish-manufactured ingredients of Eli Lilly’s blockbuster obesity drug Mounjaro. In 2025, the United States absorbed 43 percent of EU exports of drugs and medical ingredients, up from 28 percent in 2015. Booming pharmaceutical exports are good news for Europe, but shipping nearly half of them to the United States gives Washington a clear target.

The second factor is that the EU’s pledge to diversify trade ties toward middle powers is materializing only slowly. The share of EU goods exports going to 10 major middle powers, including India, Indonesia, and Turkey, remained unchanged between 2015 and 2025, at only 15 percent.

Industry observers note that in terms of imports, the EU’s reliance on the United States is growing, too—particularly in energy and intellectual property (IP). Increased dependence on U.S. energy following Europe’s shift away from Russian supplies is familiar by now: U.S. firms now supply almost two-thirds of EU imports of liquefied natural gas, up from zero in 2015. The lesser-known story has to do with IP. Contrary to Britain, Japan, and the United States, the EU is a net importer of IP, such as software, patents, or trademarks. U.S. firms supply 80 percent of these imports, and the trade is one-sided. Eurostat documents that at 213 billion euros in 2025, the bloc’s imports of U.S. IP have grown sevenfold over the past decade. Last year, the EU bought six times as much IP from the United States as vice versa.

Failed attempts to achieve strategic autonomy, de-risking, and diversification have a precedent. In 2019, Britain, France, and Germany created INSTEX, a barter mechanism intended to maintain European trade with Iran despite U.S. secondary sanctions. INSTEX handled just one transaction before closing in 2023.

Industry observers note that europe’s ambition to achieve economic security stands a better chance of succeeding if Brussels gets serious about creating a euro-denominated safe asset, investing heavily in frontier technology sectors, and doubling down on signing trade deals with middle powers. Delivering on these reforms would be a winning strategy—and not just a succession of buzzwords.

The European Council on Foreign Relations’ report, Interdependence, Revised: A Fresh Dictionary for the World Economy by Agathe Demarais and Chiara Malaponti, was released earlier this month.
