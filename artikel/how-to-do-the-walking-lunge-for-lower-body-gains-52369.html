How to Do the Walking Lunge for Lower Body Gains

YOUR LEGS ARE built for movement, whether you’re running, walking, or jumping. So why do most go-to leg exercises have you planting your feet in one spot?

It has been revealed that enter the walking lunge, a truly dynamic lower-body exercise that’s a perfect complement to exercises like heavy squats and Romanian deadlifts. (A bonus: It’s a unilateral exercise that isn’t the dreaded Bulgarian split squat.) The walking lunge is one of the few strength moves that truly gets you moving. And in doing so, it exposes muscle imbalances, trains balance and coordination, and challenges your core and upper body more than you might expect—all while attacking every muscle in your legs.

In a recent development, walking lunges might appear deceptively simple at first, especially if you have done standard reverse lunges before. And in truth, they can be simple. Here, Men’s Health fitness director Ebenezer Samuel, C.S.C.S. walks you through the steps to take so that you’re not dragging your feet but making gains toward powerful legs.

Recent findings suggest that FORM IS EVERYTHING with this move. You’ll want to stand with your feet shoulder-width apart, squeeze your glutes and shoulder blades while keeping your gaze at a neutral spot straight ahead of you.

Industry observers note that once you’re comfortable with this slow and controlled movement, you can add weights to build extra muscle and strength. You can hold them at your sides, hold them by your shoulders in what’s called a front rack position, or grab one heavy dumbbell and hold it to your chest in a goblet position. “Regardless of how you hold the load, make sure to keep your upper-body tight,” notes Samuel. “Grip the dumbbells like you mean it, squeeze your shoulder blades, and tighten your abs, too.”.

Sources indicate that don’t obsess over keeping your torso too upright, though, claims Samuel. “Especially as you work with heavier loads, don’t be afraid to lean forward slightly,” he says. “That can help you capitalize on your glutes, one of your strongest muscle groups, with each stride.”.

According to recent reports, when you’re starting out, states Samuel, aim to take a step that keeps your front shin perpendicular to the ground when you lower your torso into the lunge. But as you grow more experienced, you can play with the length of your stride to chase different goals. “If you take very small steps forward,” says Samuel, “you’ll attack your quads more. Larger steps can help you build hip mobility and knee health. If you take larger steps, go lighter with the load.”.

“The greatest benefits of the walking lunge come from how it challenges us while in motion,” claims Samuel. “In addition to building strength and muscle, every step forward challenges your front leg (primarily tibailis and quad muscles) to decelerate your body in space, and then quickly work to accelerate into the next step. That’s building coordination and balance, in addition to training almost every lower-body muscle and building serious strength.”.

You’ll work a number of major leg muscles when you do walking lunges, including your calves, hamstrings, quads, and glutes.

SMALL MISTAKES IN your walking lunge technique can turn the lower body exercise into a potential knee-wrecking disaster.

It has been revealed that you want to land on the heel of your foot first to provide a strong base for the rest of your lunge position. When you get started with walking lunges, aim for a vertical shin as you lower down to the ground to stay at a 90-degree angle in your front and back legs.

Industry observers note that you also don’t want to think of taking a step forward, but stepping out a couple inches by slightly turning your glute. Another adjustment to protect your knee is to shift your knee slightly outward instead of toward the midline. (If you still feel any knee pain during lunges, try a reverse lunge instead).

Keep your torso straight and avoid dipping forward, which you may be more inclined to do if you’re carrying heavy weights for this exercise. Your chest should be aligned with the back of your thigh the entire time. As you get up from the lunge, you want to avoid using momentum. That means no shoulder or torso rocking.

THE WALKING LUNGE is a great way to sneak in some movement on leg day if most of your lower-body exercises have both feet anchored to the ground. Load up some weights, and try to aim for 3 sets of 8 to 10 reps per leg, 1 to 2 times a week. You can alternate each leg or finish up all your reps on one leg before switching to the other. Samuel states that will keep more tension and gives you an opportunity to really focus on the muscle.

An advantage of this move is its versatility, allowing you to do it anytime, anywhere. If you’re crunched for time or in a hotel room, try bodyweight walking lunges. Do it for 45 seconds with a 15 second rest for 5 minutes to break a sweat and build muscle.
