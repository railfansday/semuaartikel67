Breaking: Yosemite At Last! — John Muir Trail Day 21

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Total Elevation Gain:  46,975 feet.

Pace (minutes per mile):  28:21.

It was a fitful, fretful night. It continued to rain for a while, off and on, and the wind came up again, continuing throughout the night. The whole time I was worrying about staying warm, staying dry, and making the fewest possible excursions outside to pee.

In a recent development, on one of those, in the middle of the night, I did something clever. I picked up my poncho, which was just lying on the ground soaking wet, and I draped it over a pine tree, so it looked like a scarecrow. Or maybe a scare-bear?

Above all, I worried about what I would be facing in the morning.

Industry observers note that indeed, the morning came. I got up reasonably early, at 6:30, and had breakfast in my tent. In my warm, now semi-dry tent.

Recent findings suggest that the good thing was that the rain had stopped. The poncho I stood up as a decoy in the night, was now wind dried. So, I used it for staging. I put it on the wet ground and pulled all of my stuff out of the tent and placed it on the dry poncho. If things were dry, they stayed dry; if wet, at least they didn’t get any wetter.

Things looked a LOT better at my camp site in the morning!

This mattered, because I had a lot of wet stuff. I had a wet hoodie. I had wet shorts, wet socks, and wet shoes.

Sources indicate that after evacuating the tent, I took it down. Thanks to the wind, the fly – that had been soaking wet — was basically dry. But the ground cloth and the bottom of the tent were very wet.

According to recent reports, what to do? I could either dry them and be done with it, or pack them wet, and dry them later. While thinking about this, I put them on logs, and laid them out to dry, so that my indecision became a decision. When the sun peeked out a little, I put them on rocks. Yes, it ate up a lot of time, but when they were finally dry, I could stop worrying about that issue and just hike.

At 9:00 a.m. I set off. I was wearing wet-cold shoes, wet-cold shorts, wet-cold gloves, a wet-cold hoodie, and a wet-cold hat. Only one thing was not wet and cold:  I decided to go ahead and use my dry socks. I hoped that my shoes were just moist and not soaking wet, and my dry socks would be a barrier to that moisture. That plan worked out pretty well.

Because it was cold in the aftermath of the storm, I started with a lot of energy, to get my blood flowing. I needed it too, for the trail sang one refrain:  up, up, up, and up.

Looking back at the hill I climbed — but at least I was warm, and getting dry!

Sources indicate that by the time I reached the ridge, and looked down on Garnet Lake, I was mostly dry and all warmed up. Then I got lost.

I could see Garnet Lake, I just couldn’t see how to GET there!

According to recent reports, I knew I had to go east, and I spotted a little trail to the east, so I started down that trail until it kind of disappeared. Then I spent about ten minutes wandering around up there, trying to find my way, unable to believe the east-going trail was a false trail. Finally, I retraced my steps far enough to notice a separate west-going trail. That was counterintuitive, but it was the correct trail, because it eventually looped around, lower down, and went east, as I knew it should.

I name this “One Pine Tree Island” in Garnet Lake.

According to recent reports, walking along the shore of Garnet Lake I envisioned what might have happened if I had made it to this destination in the storm the day before. I could imagine myself, struggling with my tent in the rain, and having a huge gust of wind lift the tent like a beach ball and deposit it in the middle of the lake! It would have been bad! Yes, hilarious, but bad.

To reinforce the power of the storm the day before, this was Ninja Queen’s  description of her experience, as she sent it to me via text, with almost no editing:.

“…you can imagine how horribly wrong the day of the storm went for me. I should have hunkered down early like most other hikers had, but I foolishly trusted my (Garmin) InReach weather forecast a little too much, went over Island Pass (a tiny pass but a pass nonetheless) when the storm hit full force two hours early. It was bad. Really, really bad. The trail flooded very rapidly, got washed out and started diverging in multiple directions from the torrential downpour. They were ankle-deep and I kept losing the actual trail; it had become mini rivers at that point. Needless to say, I got wetted out through and through, shivering intensely, my hands no longer functioning, unable to take another step. I ended up setting up camp on some random flat-ish spot over some vegetation that also started pooling water. I was so afraid my tent was going to get flooded. Almost everything inside the tent was wet and muddy as well, though I was able to change into dry sleep clothes and sleeping bag, thanks to my nylofume pack liners. It took me a couple of hours, but I eventually warmed up in the bag, cooked and ate food inside (bears were the last of my worries at this point) and drank as little water as possible so I wouldn’t have to go out until the next morning. Putting back on cold, dripping wet clothes the next morning felt torture-adjacent; something I hope I’ll never have to go through again.”.

“It gave me a small chuckle seeing other hikers also emerge from non-established tent sites the next morning. I quickly learned what I went through was not a typical rainstorm experience. Almost everyone I talked to remarked it had been one of the worst storms they’ve seen on trail. One guy, a firefighter, told me this was far worse than the hurricane weather he had caught on the AT (Appalachian Trail) a few years back. Now I felt like a survivor, instead of feeling like a fair weather backpacker who crumbled at the slightest hint of bad weather.”.

I filled up my water at the bridge where a stream exits the lake. There were a lot of folks around there, laying out their tents to dry from the storm. It was surprising, and a little disturbing, to see how much foam, and even spume-like foam, was on Garnet Lake, which looks otherwise pristine. I didn’t ask anybody about it at the time, who would I ask?

But later I Googled AI and discovered:  “…foam usually forms naturally when decaying plants, algae, and organic debris release compounds that lower water surface tension, and wind or wave action whips air into bubbles.”  In other words, the wind from the storm had produced the foam, not some giant industrial complex just out of view!

I continued up, from Garnet to beautiful Ruby Lake, then to Emerald Lake, and finally the Thousand Islands Lake. This was an exceptionally scenic stretch.

I think this was Ruby Lake… if the water were sparkling red, it would be easier to tell!

Of course this was Thousand Islands Lake — with a pretty terrific backdrop!

According to recent reports, there was very little elevation gain from there to Island Pass. Once the trail reached the ridge on the opposite side of the lake it seemed to stay at a mostly constant elevation for a very long time. The longer it went on, the more I wondered, “Pass? Is there really a pass up here somewhere? Where?”.

No pass, but a pretty cool-looking mushroom!

In a recent development, how can you tell when you have reached a pass? When you can see the other side of the mountain. It felt like that was never going to happen.

Finally, it was getting to be lunchtime. I decided to sit next to a charming little pond up there, and to eat my lunch.

Basically, I ate half of the food that I had brought as lunch, saving the other half for “second lunch.”  But afterwards, I just didn’t feel like I had much energy. Even finally reaching the edge did not inspire me. I saw the edge of the pass and started heading down, but my legs felt heavy. My legs felt like limp spaghetti.

When your legs don’t want to go, you need your brain to execute an override. So, I forced myself to keep going. Finally, I stopped again and made myself a second lunch in that sort of halfway space between Island Pass and Donohue Pass. About five miles separated them and Donohue represented the entrance to Yosemite National Park.

A dead tree by a stream; dead is how I felt before second lunch!

After lunch I had more energy, and I continued.

Throughout the JMT, whenever I approached a location where I knew there was a pass in the ridgeline, I always looked up to see if I could figure out where it might be. “Is that it? No, is that it? Wait, is that it?”  I suspect most hikers do this.

According to recent reports, with Donohue Pass it was hidden around the corner of an extending hump of rock, so of course I guessed wrong. Just short of the summit, I saw a full backpack about 25 yards from the trail. That seemed strange and I looked around. Was someone off going to the bathroom? There was nothing but silence. I continued on, but with a nagging worry for whoever was not connected with their pack.

According to recent reports, when I arrived on top, at 11,066 feet elevation, I was all alone. In honor of my son Gabriel, who coined the pun, I bellowed, “YO! SEMITE!!!”  I got no answer, not even an echo. Even so, I was in Yosemite. It felt strange to be just a few days from the end — barring injury, of course.

As I headed down from Donohue, I thought, “On the one hand, I don’t want to set up in the dark. But there are not too many campsites up here and, of course, I am a couple of miles behind schedule because of the storm.”  I resolved to take a chance and push it a little bit.

As a result, the riverside campsites just below Donohue Pass did not tempt me; they were filling up in any case.

The first camp north of Donohue Pass was by this ugly little lake….

According to recent reports, instead, I descended rapidly and with determination. As I did, I encountered a group heading southbound, up to the pass behind me. One young man was not carrying a pack. Apparently, he had come down to assist his family in making the ascent. He told me, “I left my pack up near the Pass so that I would not be encumbered in helping my family.”  I breathed deeply; mystery solved.

Recent findings suggest that the first stretch went through rocky scree. I asked the mountain gods, “Why is there so much rocky scree?”  They rewarded me with forest.

It has been revealed that see the meadow, far below there? My campsite was somewhere down there….

The descent continued sharply through the woods, and I passed several gorgeous camping sites near the steeply dropping steam. Each time I told myself, “No! Not yet!” and then each time I regretted it, and wondered if I would ever find another spot, after that perfect one I had just rejected.

At last, the steep downhill tapered off, and I reached the valley at the bottom. The stream, now wider and larger, and the trail continued to parallel each other. The going became easier for both the water and my legs.

Following the river there were a few more possible places. As the sun slipped behind the mountains, but still with plenty of light, I saw a knoll overlooking a wide meadow. On that knoll was a lovely camping spot. I was home for the night.

The sun remains on the hillside, even as it gets dark in the valley.

My dinner was rice and beans and chicken, Santa Fe style. I let it soak as I pitched my tent and arranged my site. I let it soak some more as I explored higher up, in the giant granite boulders above the site, to find a cat hole location. It soaked still longer as I dug the hole in preparation for the next morning. But then, with no chores left to do, I got impatient. I ate.

According to recent reports, the dish was very well-seasoned, very tasty. This was certainly a meal I would recommend having again, on another hike, in another place. But rice takes a long time to absorb water when cold-soaking, and I didn’t wait long enough. The flavors were great, but the rice was… crunchy. I didn’t care much. It had been a strenuous day, coming the day after the intense storm. I was tired and hungry and starved for calories. I ate it all with gusto, crunch and all!

Sources indicate that I was feeling good. It was a long day, and I had crossed two passes. I was finally in Yosemite. I haven’t mentioned the aftermath of my fall too much in recent posts, but my ribs were finally hurting less. My wrist was still a problem, to some degree, but it was functional.

Sources indicate that the next day, I would be passing through Tuolumne Meadow. There I would either get a permit for Half Dome, or I would not. If I did, I probably had three days of hiking left. If I did not, then it would likely be two days. I had well under forty miles to go.

As the twilight deepened, I was loving my campsite facing the open meadow. I was proud of the mileage I had achieved, after the shortened day in the storm. My eyes closed and I welcomed blissful sleep.
