Switzerland Picks 57 Countries for Crypto Tax Data Sharing Starting in 2028

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Switzerland’s Federal Council has approved 57 countries and territories with which the country aims to automatically share tax information on crypto owners starting in 2028, extending its long-standing bank data exchange to bitcoin and other digital assets.

The list includes all European Union member states, the United Kingdom and various G20 countries, the State Secretariat for International Finance (SIF) noted in a release on Wednesday. The exchange will run under the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development’s Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework, known as CARF.

Industry observers note that with the decision, Switzerland intends to implement the crypto exchange at the same time as other global asset management centers, according to the release. The government did not name the 57 jurisdictions beyond those broad groups, and it did not publish the full list alongside the announcement.

Recent findings suggest that data will not flow to every name on the list. An exchange will take place only if a partner jurisdiction wants to swap information with Switzerland and meets the conditions of the OECD standard, in particular its requirements on confidentiality and data security.

Before any financial account or crypto data changes hands, the Federal Council will run a targeted check of whether each partner complies with OECD standards. It will rely on a review mechanism Parliament approved on Sept. 28, 2026, which covers both the older exchange of bank account data and the newly introduced crypto exchange.

Industry observers note that the domestic legal basis for the crypto exchange took effect on Jan. 1, 2026. Earlier this month, though, the SIF said the framework could not be implemented before Jan. 1, 2027, because the partner list still needed approval and notifications had to go to the OECD. The rules do not apply this year.

The Council also approved Armenia and Rwanda as fresh partners for the exchange of financial account information, with first exchanges due in 2028, and signed off on two notifications needed to carry out the exchange. Both partner lists were broadly supported during public consultations in 2024 and 2025, the government noted.

It has been revealed that the final list is shorter than first planned. In June 2025, the Federal Council proposed 74 countries relevant to the crypto market, including all EU states, the UK and most G20 members. That plan left out the United States and Saudi Arabia, and it targeted a 2026 start with first data exchanges in 2027.

Wednesday’s release did not say which countries were dropped or whether the U.S. is now on the list. The U.S. has committed to begin CARF exchanges by 2029, according to the OECD’s Global Forum, which lists Switzerland among 27 jurisdictions aiming for first exchanges by 2028.

That U.S. timeline is already shaping policy elsewhere. South Korea pushed its 22% crypto tax back to 2029 partly because it will not receive American crypto trading data until CARF exchanges with the U.S. begin that year. Officials remarked taxing earlier could put Korea’s domestic market at a disadvantage.

In a recent development, switzerland’s decision lands as European governments debate how much they should know about crypto holders. On Tuesday, France’s Senate rejected a rule that would have forced bitcoin holders to declare self-custody wallets, while EU states began collecting crypto transaction data under the bloc’s DAC8 directive this year.

For Switzerland, CARF extends a system it already runs for bank accounts. The country has exchanged financial account information automatically under the OECD’s Common Reporting Standard since 2018, ending much of the secrecy once tied to Swiss banking. Its regulated crypto market keeps growing, with the country’s financial regulator clearing its first bitcoin ETF in October.

Under CARF, crypto service providers in Switzerland will have to collect information on users who are tax residents of activated partner jurisdictions and report it to Swiss tax authorities, which then pass it on to those partners once the exchange begins.
