The Harm From Plastic Is Happening In Plain Sight

It is known. Plastic is a major cause of the climate crisis, yet governments continue to ignore it.

Recent findings suggest that the Environmental Justice Foundation (EJF) has issued a fresh briefing published to coincide with the UN Climate Change Conference, COP31, in Antalya, Türkiye. The briefing, “Reducing plastic production in a post-1.5°C world: A test of resolve for the international community,” calls on governments to reduce global plastic production.

Plastic already emits more than two billion tons of CO₂e a year, about twice as much as aviation, and under current policies this would more than double as the fossil fuel industry turns to plastic as its hedge against electrification.

“There’s only so much carbon the world can still emit if we want to avoid blasting past the Paris Agreement targets. Plastic’s harm to our climate is happening in plain sight, and governments are choosing to look away. Every newly introduced plastics plant locks in decades of emissions the planet simply cannot afford. As leaders gather at COP31, they must recognise plastic as a major cause of carbon pollution that is rapidly heating our world.” — Steve Trent, CEO, Environmental Justice Foundation.

In a recent development, over 90% of plastic emissions come before a plastic product is even used, during extraction, conversion and production. If current policies remain unchanged, global plastic production is projected to almost triple by 2060, and annual emissions from fossil-based plastics to more than double, to 4.3 billion tons of CO2e.

On this trajectory, plastics would emit 67 billion tons of CO2e between 2026 and 2050. That is just over half of the remaining carbon budget for a 50% chance of meeting the 1.5°C target, and 27% of the budget for a very good chance of limiting the overshoot to 1.7°C. None of this is hidden; emissions are quantified, the projections are published and the industry’s expansion aims are public, yet plastic remains on the margins of climate negotiations.

EJF is calling on governments at COP31 to recognize plastic for what it is: a major driver of climate change that must be tackled at its source, by cutting production.

The petrochemical industry’s bet on plastics.

In a recent development, as the world electrifies transport and power, the fossil fuel industry is using plastic as its hedge against electrification. With oil demand for transport and energy expected to decline, petrochemical companies are pivoting to plastics to keep oil and gas flowing. The plastics sector could account for 20% of global oil consumption by 2050, up from around 12% today. The top fifteen petrochemical companies received an estimated US$590.8 billion in financing between 2019 and 2025, and the sector was propped up by state subsidies estimated at over $80 billion in 2024 alone, almost half of which came from Saudi Arabia.

A test of resolve for the plastics treaty.

Sources indicate that A total of 145 countries from every continent have expressed support for provisions to reduce plastic production during the global plastics treaty negotiations. Rwanda and Peru have proposed a global target to cut plastic production by 40% by 2040 against a 2025 baseline. Modelling cited in the briefing reveals that a 40% cut in high-ambition countries, joined by China and emerging producers, could reduce global emissions from plastics by as much as 21% compared with business as usual.

But negotiations have reached a deadlock, driven by a small group of oil- and plastic-producing states, including Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iran and China, with the United States recently aligning with their position. At least 234 fossil fuel and chemical industry lobbyists attended the latest round of talks in Geneva — more than the combined delegations of the EU and its 27 Member States.

Final Thoughts about Plastic and our Leaders.

In a recent development, the truth about plastics is perverse. Plastics are widely used around the world due to ease of manufacturing, low cost, stable chemical properties, and good water resistance. They have infiltrated every part of the environment and human life cycle. More than 16,000 chemicals are used in manufacturing plastics, and at least 4,200 of these have been identified as hazardous to human health and the environment. Thousands more chemicals used in plastic production have never been tested. Many of these chemicals migrate out of plastic packaging into our foods, drinks, household products, and bodies.

Sources indicate that researchers have come up with many possible solutions to plastic pollution, as well as ways to mitigate microplastics in the environment. But will governments have the wherewithal to stand up to the powerful fossil fuel lobby that is behind plastic propaganda?
