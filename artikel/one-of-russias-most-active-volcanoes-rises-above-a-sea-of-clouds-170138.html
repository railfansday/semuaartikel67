A striking Landsat 9 image reveals the snow-covered summit and active lava dome of Shivelyuch volcano on Russia’s Kamchatka Peninsula.

Today’s Image of the Day features one of Russia’s most active volcanoes rising above a blanket of clouds on the Kamchatka Peninsula.

Snow covers the upper slopes of Shivelyuch, while a dark formation near the summit offers a glimpse of the volcanic activity taking place within the mountain.

The image was captured on November 8, 2026, by the Operational Land Imager aboard Landsat 9. Although clouds concealed much of the surrounding landscape, the volcano’s summit remained visible from space.

The contrast between the snowy mountainside and the exposed volcanic rock offers a striking portrait of a landscape that is continually being rebuilt from within.

Shivelyuch, also known as Shiveluch, is among the largest and most active volcanoes on the Kamchatka Peninsula in Russia’s Far East.

According to recent reports, the mountain has a complex geological history. Its older structure, known as Stary Shiveluch, dates back roughly 65,000 years.

A younger volcanic complex, Molodoy Shiveluch, subsequently developed within the remains of the older mountain.

The Smithsonian Institution’s Global Volcanism Program has documented at least 60 major eruptions at Shivelyuch during the Holocene, the geological epoch that began approximately 11,700 years ago.

It has been revealed that successive eruptions and collapses have left extensive deposits of volcanic material across the surrounding terrain. These deposits preserve a record of the mountain’s turbulent past.

One of the most intriguing features of Shivelyuch is its enormous caldera, a depression measuring approximately 5.6 miles (nine kilometers) across.

Within this depression, a lava dome has developed as magma reaches the surface. The dome stands out against the snow in the satellite image.

Unlike the fluid lava commonly associated with Hawaiian volcanoes, the thick lava that builds volcanic domes does not travel easily across the landscape.

Instead, it accumulates around the opening from which it emerges.

As more material reaches the surface, the dome can expand, develop fractures, and become increasingly unstable.

It has been revealed that sections of these domes sometimes collapse. Such events can unleash pyroclastic flows – rapidly moving mixtures of scorching gas, ash, and fragmented rock that sweep down a volcano’s slopes.

The process can dramatically alter the appearance of a mountain, sometimes within minutes.

Kamchatka’s extraordinary volcanic landscape owes its existence to the movement of enormous sections of Earth’s outer shell.

The peninsula sits along the Pacific Ring of Fire, a vast region of geological activity surrounding much of the Pacific Ocean.

Here, the Pacific tectonic plate descends beneath another plate in a process called subduction.

Water released from the descending plate promotes melting in the overlying mantle, generating magma that can eventually rise toward the surface.

Over millions of years, this process has created a remarkable concentration of volcanoes, along with hot springs, geysers, and other geothermal features.

Kamchatka provides scientists with an opportunity to observe some of the fundamental processes responsible for reshaping Earth’s surface.

The effects of volcanic activity at Shivelyuch can extend well beyond the peninsula.

Although volcanic ash resembles ordinary dust, it consists of tiny fragments of rock, minerals, and volcanic glass.

These abrasive particles can damage aircraft engines and other critical equipment.

High-altitude winds can carry ash clouds across international borders and oceans. In July 2026, ash from Shivelyuch reached Alaskan airspace after traveling across the Bering Sea, disrupting air travel.

Even between major explosions, previously deposited ash can become airborne again when strong winds sweep across the landscape.

This means that an ash plume does not necessarily indicate that a new explosive eruption has occurred.

Volcanic activity at Shivelyuch had persisted for several months when Landsat 9 acquired this image.

Monitoring a remote volcano surrounded by unpredictable weather presents considerable challenges.

Satellites offer scientists an invaluable perspective on places where direct observations are difficult.

In addition to capturing changes in the appearance of volcanic landscapes, instruments aboard some satellites can detect infrared radiation associated with unusually hot surfaces.

These measurements help researchers identify areas of elevated temperature that may be associated with freshly exposed lava or other volcanic activity.

By comparing observations collected over extended periods, scientists can also track the growth and destruction of lava domes, map volcanic deposits, and document changes to the surrounding environment.

Weather clouds can interfere with many of these observations, making clear views of active volcanic features particularly valuable.

Sources indicate that seen from hundreds of miles above Earth, Shivelyuch appears almost serene. Its snow-covered slopes emerge from the clouds, revealing a rugged summit seemingly isolated from the world below.

Yet the mountain’s appearance belies the geological activity taking place beneath its surface.

The dark lava dome visible in today’s image represents the latest chapter in a history of destruction and renewal spanning thousands of years.

Long after the surrounding clouds have dispersed, the forces responsible for building Shivelyuch will continue to transform the mountain.
