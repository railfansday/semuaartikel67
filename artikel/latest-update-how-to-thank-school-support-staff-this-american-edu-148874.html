My son’s favorite person in elementary school was the nurse. She was warm and welcoming, always quick with a comforting hug. More importantly, she had the best Band-Aids in the building, and she wasn’t stingy about giving them out. A scraped knee got one. So did a paper cut, and even some invisible injuries, too. She knew when a kid headed to see her, it wasn’t necessarily about needing that Band-Aid.

Industry observers note that that nurse lovingly cared for kids who needed far more support than my son did. There were kids who came to her office with a belly ache that wasn’t a stomach bug, but because they hadn’t had breakfast, and possibly dinner either. There were kids who could barely stay awake in class because they’d spent the night in a car instead of a bed. She met those kids with the same warmth she gave my Band-Aid collector, and I’m certain she kept a stash of snacks in her desk for those visits in particular.

People like her have a name in education circles. They’re education support professionals, or ESPs, and they include school nurses, cafeteria workers, bus drivers, security staff, paraeducators, technical personnel, custodians, skilled trade workers, and front office staff. Ask kids about their favorite grown-up at school, and there’s a decent chance the answer is one of them.

The people our kids adore are often the people schools pay the least. Nearly 32% of full-time ESPs earn less than $25,000 a year, according to the National Education Association, and on average, they’re paid below a living wage in all 50 states.

That’s not a livable wage in most areas. “Our wages are so low, we are forced to have two or three jobs,” paraeducator Yahaira Rodriguez told NEA Today. The nurse who stays late with a hungry kid may be heading to a second job after school, eating dinner in the car on the way. That’s a long day for someone whose job is to take care of everyone else’s kids. It also makes it hard to stay in the job. Before the 2024–25 school year, 69% of public schools reported trouble filling at least one non-teaching position, according to the National Center for Education Statistics. That means the kids who rely on them the most are the ones who lose out when they leave or the positions can’t be filled.

According to recent reports, the NEA ESP Bill of Rights lays out what these professionals need to stay in the jobs our kids depend on. Developed by ESPs themselves, it asks for pay that covers the bills without a second job, health care they can afford and a workplace ‘free of violence, including physical, verbal, and emotional abuse.’ It’s a practical document about what it takes to keep these jobs filled.
