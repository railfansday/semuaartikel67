Pakistan minister to visit India, but no breakthrough expected

🌐 Source: Dw • 📅 19 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 4 min read (883 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Pakistan's Climate Change Minister Musadik Masood Malik will travel to India from November 20 to 22 to participate in the Shanghai Cooperation Organization (SCO) Civilizational Dialogue in Upcoming Delhi.

It marks the first India visit by a Pakistani federal minister since the two nuclear-armed archrivals fought a four-day war last year.

The fighting erupted after armed gunmen killed over two dozen tourists, mostly Hindus, in India-administered Kashmir in April 2025. New Delhi accused Islamabad of backing the attack, a charge Pakistan denied.

Since the military confrontation, diplomatic tensions have remained high, leaving little room for official engagement between the two sides.

The SCO Civilizational Dialogue was proposed by Prime Minister Narendra Modi at the SCO summit in Tianjin in September 2025.

As the forum's host, India has invited all member states to participate in the event.

Recent findings suggest that the SCO is a political, economic, and security alliance established in 2001. Its current members are Belarus, China, India, Iran, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Pakistan, Russia, Tajikistan, and Uzbekistan.

In a recent development, ajay Bisaria, a former Indian ambassador to Pakistan, sees significance in the fact that neither side sought to avoid the engagement. "The real takeaway is that the relationship isn't so broken that India won't invite Pakistan, or that Pakistan won't come. That alone is a good sign," he told DW.

But Bisaria cautions against assuming that the invitation followed a diplomatic breakthrough.

Industry observers note that "The trigger was procedural. This forum grew out of Modi's proposal at the 2025 SCO summit in Tianjin, and as host, Newly introduced Delhi invited every SCO member. I see no evidence of a back channel or outside mediation, and I wouldn't read much into that either way," he stated.

The multilateral setting serves both countries, though for different reasons.

Pakistan wants to retain its influence within the organization. It presently holds the chairmanship of the SCO Council of Heads of State for 2026–27 and is due to host the next leaders' summit in Islamabad in August 2027.

Ayesha Siddiqa, a Pakistani expert on military and security affairs at King's College London, argues that Islamabad's decision to send a senior minister should be understood primarily in terms of its position in the grouping.

In a recent development, "Pakistan is keen to maintain its position and influence within the SCO and does not want to relinquish its role in the organization. The minister's visit should therefore be seen primarily in the context of Pakistan fulfilling its commitments under the SCO Charter, rather than as a gesture towards India," Siddiqa told DW.

T C A Raghavan, another former Indian ambassador to Pakistan, shares a similar sentiment.

"Both India and Pakistan attach importance to the SCO, and Pakistan currently holds its chairmanship. This visit should be seen primarily in the context of the SCO, rather than as a development in India-Pakistan bilateral relations," Raghavan told DW.

"Islamabad is also being pragmatic in keeping channels open during its SCO chairmanship by participating in an initiative championed by Prime Minister Narendra Modi. There will be no real impact on the India-Pakistan bilateral.".

Since the April 2025 attack, India has maintained that terrorism and talks cannot go hand in hand.

Sources indicate that "Post the Pahalgam attack, we have a Pakistan policy. That Pakistan policy remains in place. There has been no change," Randhir Jaiswal, spokesperson for India's Foreign Ministry, said at the end of October.

Pakistan, meanwhile, continues to raise the issues of Kashmir and the Indus Waters Treaty, which New Delhi placed "in abeyance" following the attack.

Malik, the climate change minister traveling to India, has been a prominent voice in Pakistan's campaign over water rights. In June, he warned: "We will protect ourselves... if anyone lays a hand over our share of water, we'll cut off that hand.".

Sanjay Kumar Bhardwaj, professor at the Center for South Asian Studies at Jawaharlal Nehru University, said the SCO is part of India's "multi-aligned foreign policy.".

He noted that the forum "doesn't provide any space to normalize relations with Pakistan.".

The absence of official talks, however, does not mean all bilateral communication has stopped.

Since the May 2025 confrontation, Indian and Pakistani former diplomats, strategic experts, parliamentarians and retired military officers have reportedly taken part in a series of informal or semi-official back-channel meetings.

Such exchanges allow participants to assess the other side's thinking, keep informal lines of communication open and reduce the risk of miscalculation.

Industry observers note that but they do not amount to formal negotiations. Upcoming Delhi has noted retired officials attending such meetings do so in their personal capacities.

An assessment by the International Institute for Strategic Studies in November remarked both governments appeared reluctant to resume secret talks, despite the role earlier back-channel contacts had played in stabilizing relations.

Bisaria described the visit by a Pakistani minister to India as "a modest step forward, not a breakthrough.".

What happens next, he remarked, "depends on Pakistan.".

According to recent reports, "If it avoids grandstanding in Delhi and offers credible assurances on terrorism, publicly or quietly, it becomes more likely that Indian leaders will attend next year's SCO summit in Islamabad. That will be the real test.".
