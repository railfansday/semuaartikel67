Paul Deraval, Cofounder & CEO of NinjaCat, is a software veteran with 20+ years driving innovation in martech, AI and agency growth.

According to recent reports, right now, companies of all sizes and sectors are racing to adopt artificial intelligence. Many consider AI the next major thing, and no one wants to miss out.

For many enterprises, this means increasing access to AI tools. They are paying for Claude subscriptions, licensing ChatGPT Enterprise and rolling out Microsoft Copilot across departments. According to Gallup’s latest workplace survey, 47% of employees say their organization integrated AI tools in Q2 2026, up from 41% the previous quarter.

Unfortunately, more tools don’t necessarily equate to better outcomes or higher productivity, and many executives aren’t seeing ROI even as they spend millions on software seats and infrastructure. A National Bureau of Economic Research study found that 89% of executives reported AI had “no impact on labor productivity.” ​.

Companies have real reasons to be excited about AI, but those that use it to gain a competitive advantage and add real value will invest in the governance and infrastructure needed to operationalize these models effectively at scale. As Forbes contributor Güney Yıldız reports: “AI spend does not become ROI simply because usage goes up. Value capture requires workflow redesign, not just license distribution.”​.

Here’s how forward-thinking leaders can redesign workflows to achieve real ROI from their AI investments.

Sources indicate that AI should deliver tangible outcomes like making work easier, better or cheaper. Its purpose is not, as many leaders mistakenly assume, to simply increase AI adoption or add another tool to the technology stack.​.

According to recent reports, executives can be forgiven for misunderstanding that point. Adoption can feel like action, and they face immense pressure to act. In reality, enterprises have different teams, roles and workflows, each with its own needs. Some can benefit from an AI tool or autonomous agent while others really don’t.

Rather than forcing an AI tool or agentic layer into every process, match AI capabilities to a specific problem. Right now, many businesses conflate success with usage. However, as the tokenmaxxing debacle indicates, measuring usage isn’t the same as promoting real impact.

Instead of just measuring AI use, audit workflow bottlenecks before selecting tools so their capabilities match the problem they’re trying to solve. At the same time, prioritize tools that embed directly into existing systems, so workflows stay integrated and frictionless. If a tool can’t integrate directly with CRM, ERP or other core systems of record, its organizational impact will be limited.

Recent findings suggest that the enterprise technology stack is already crowded with companies deploying hundreds of apps and SaaS solutions. Many work independently, disconnected from the disparate app ecosystem surrounding companies major and small. Adding newly introduced AI tools to this mix has limited impact and can even create more work for employees. Effective AI adoption that justifies AI spend will go beyond tools that answer questions or generate generic content. It will involve AI solutions that can proactively identify opportunities, recommend actions and even execute work. ​.

For example, in marketing, where AI ad spending alone will top $32 billion in 2026, effective AI adoption could mean onboarding a solution that detects campaign performance complications, identifies underappreciated opportunities across a campaign or helps implement improvements to existing campaign collateral.

To maximize AI’s impact, invest in tools that make this possible and clarify the human element. Clearly define when AI can act independently, when approval is required and when humans must take the lead. ​.

Combining the right tools with the right purpose in structured workflows, with clear roles and responsibilities, is the best way to invest in AI that gets work done.

Industry observers note that AI is only as useful as the information and context it can access. Without clean, unified and reliable data, outputs can be inaccurate, inconsistent or disconnected from business priorities. Before making significant AI investments, smart enterprises will focus on organizing the technology they already have, connecting systems and simplifying workflows so AI can operate within business realities.​.

Focus on infrastructure that connects AI to relevant business data, organizational knowledge and workflows while respecting role-based permissions and account-level boundaries.​.

It has been revealed that understandably, security is foundational to these efforts. It can’t be bolted on later or retroactively applied. A governed environment, or “sandbox,” is a good place to start. Here, businesses can define what AI can access, what actions it can take and when it needs human approval.​.

Industry observers note that boosting productivity is great, but it shouldn’t come at the expense of data security or privacy. Clear accountability, auditability and oversight help businesses scale AI without losing control over sensitive data or consequential decisions.​.

It has been revealed that customers aren’t inherently impressed by a company’s AI strategy, and neither are boards or shareholders. Everyone is looking for results.

That’s why the competitive advantage comes from how well organizations connect AI to their data, people, processes and business goals. It’s the product of organizations that build the right foundation, enabling AI to do the right work with the right context and under the right controls.

Investing in these fundamentals may not make headlines, but it’s the AI infrastructure bet that smart enterprises are making to deliver lasting business value.​.
