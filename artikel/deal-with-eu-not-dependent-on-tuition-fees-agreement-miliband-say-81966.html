Deal with EU not dependent on tuition fees agreement, Miliband says

A deal agreed between the UK and the European Union on Friday will come into force regardless of whether the two sides can negotiate a separate tuition fees agreement, Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband has told the BBC.

The measures agreed in Brussels include scrapping most checks on British food exports in return for the UK following some EU regulations from 2028, and a fresh youth mobility scheme.

However, a proposal from the bloc to lower the level of tuition fees paid by EU students in the UK has not been agreed.

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen remarked she was "confident" a solution would be found, with Prime Minister Andy Burnham adding "me too" during a press conference announcing the deal.

Meanwhile, asked in his interview with the BBC's Sunday with Laura Kuenssberg programme whether rejoining the EU would be looked at, Miliband mentioned "all the options will be on the table".

It came after the prime minister mentioned that he would discuss possible ideas for even closer ties with the bloc in the coming months.

UK and EU leaders have hailed the closer cooperation agreement, with Burnham calling the deal a "win-win" that could lower the cost of living and reduce red tape.

The prime minister noted the Treasury believes the deal could boost the UK economy by up to Â£17bn a year.

EU sources had earlier suggested that the bloc might hold up applying the wider deal until it was agreed that EU students who wanted to study in the UK would pay the same fees as British students.

Miliband noted it was "absolutely clear" the agreements on food exports, energy and youth mobility would apply, even though there has not been a deal on tuition fees.

According to recent reports, "President von der Leyen... was very clear about this at the press conference we just had. She said there are those three agreements and then there's a separate piece of paper and a separate matter, which is looking at the issue of tuition," he said.

Asked again whether the agreements would definitely become law, the foreign secretary stated: "Yes, absolutely.".

"The dates are set out in 2028 when these things are coming in, we're introducing legislation in Parliament, so definitely.".

Miliband remarked there would be "no cost" to the UK taxpayer for anything agreed at Friday's summit, adding that there would be no fresh contributions to the EU budget.

He said previously unveiled agreements on EU schemes like Erasmus+ were a "separate decision" and the summit "was not about cost to the taxpayer".

Speaking on Friday, Burnham argued that polling suggests the UK public "want closer ties" with the EU and the deal "delivers that".

Miliband told the BBC that "of course all the options will be on the table" when pressed on whether rejoining the bloc would be considered, adding that "we will look at the benefits and the costs of all the different options".

He noted that while he accepted the result of the 2016 Brexit referendum, "the world has changed massively".

"Europe is changing too, so we've got to look at what that means. It's not about this parliament, we're respecting the red lines in this parliament," he added.

Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch stated the deal was "not fair", arguing that UK farmers would have to follow rules set in Brussels and young people from the EU would "compete" with young Britons struggling to get jobs at home.

She also claimed Burnham had "plucked" the Â£17bn figure out of thin air, and noted the only modelling she had seen of how the agreement could boost the economy put the figure at "0.0%".

Reform UK leader Nigel Farage mentioned Friday marked a "betrayal of the British people" and a good day for the EU to "try and humiliate" the UK.

Farage, who was a leading figure in the 2016 campaign to get Britain out of the EU, wrote on social media: "Like every prime minister before him, Burnham is a total fraud.".

Liberal Democrat leader Sir Ed Davey remarked there needed to be more than "tweaks" to the UK's relationship to the EU.

Sources indicate that "A youth mobility scheme is a massive step forward, but it's nowhere near enough. We need a whole new deal that actually works for Britain, and fast.".

Speaking to BBC Radio 4's Today programme, the University of Manchester's vice chancellor Professor Duncan Ivison said closer UK-EU relations were a "good thing" but "the concern of tuition fees is something we do have to figure out because there is a cost".

He warned that EU students being allowed to pay domestic university fees would "take Â£500m, we estimate, out of the higher education sector at a time where there's a lot of pressure on the sector".

He added: "If EU students come and are paying domestic fees, it's not clear we'd be able to increase the number of places for EU students anyway.".

Environment Secretary Dame Angela Eagle noted the Treasury and Department for Education would work with universities to find a solution during negotiations.

According to recent reports, she told BBC Breakfast: "I don't think anybody wants to do deals that negatively impact anything. We want win-win deals.".
