In a recent development, 40 years ago, the crime drama “Heat” opened in France and received positive reviews from Paris critics. No, not the “Heat” directed by Michael Mann that starred Robert De Niro and Al Pacino and would go on to become a revered classic — that came nine years later. The “Heat” that played in Europe in 1986 starred Burt Reynolds, was directed by multiple journeyman helmers, and was almost immediately forgotten only months after it limped into U.S. theaters in March of 1987.

American reviewers dismissed “Heat” with casual cruelty, but as so often happens in film history — think Jerry Lewis, Michael Cimino’s “Heaven’s Gate,” or Brian De Palma’s “Mission to Mars” — the French got our cinema right where our own critics got it embarrassingly wrong. “Heat,” in which Reynolds plays an aging mercenary held prisoner by a crippling gambling addiction, is not only one of Reynolds’ most interesting films but one of his most personal, and a top-tier entry in the canon of the subgenre that is “Burt Noir.” Related Stories Before He Played Sylvester Stallone in ‘I Play Rocky,’ Star Anthony Ippolito Fought Hard for His Own Big Break ‘The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth’ Review: David Fincher’s Breezy Tarantino Spinoff Is a Disappointing Compromise Between Great Directors.

From early on, one of the most interesting things about Burt Reynolds was the tension between his affable onscreen persona — the smiling, charming fellow who had major box office hits with crowd-pleasers like “Smokey and the Bandit” and “The Cannonball Run” — and the visible agitation simmering underneath. Reynolds was one of the premier talk show guests (and occasional guest hosts) of the 1970s and early 1980s, a guy who could always be counted on to tell a funny, self-deprecating anecdote that made the audience feel like a part of his inner circle.

But Reynolds always gave the sense that there was a chip on his shoulder — that he was mad about not being taken seriously enough, or being taken too seriously, or losing out on the best parts to his friend and competitor Clint Eastwood and other stars. (Never mind the fact that Reynolds lost out on most of his potentially successful roles — “Terms of Endearment,” “Die Hard,” “Pretty Woman” — because he turned them down, not because anyone took them from him.).

According to recent reports, early on, Eastwood let his controlled rage be a defining trait of his star persona; Reynolds generally tried, and failed, to cloak his under chuckling humility. Many of Reynolds’ best performances exploit this tension, from John Boorman’s “Deliverance,” in which Reynolds’ male bonding fishing expedition descends into a horror, to Paul Thomas Anderson’s “Boogie Nights,” in which the actor’s initially nurturing father figure turns dark and violent when tested.

In a recent development, reynolds’ memoir, “But Enough About Me,” offers a fascinating glimpse into the contradiction between his healthy self-regard (hilariously exhibited in passages in which he disparages two of the best directors he ever worked with, Anderson and Peter Bogdanovich) and his persistent self-loathing (demonstrated in his candid appraisals of his many terrible personal and professional decisions). The latter informs an ongoing, if intermittent, cycle of Reynolds movies of which “Heat” is a key example: movies I define as “Burt Noir,” in which the actor plays supremely confident men of action tormented by regret and the knowledge that they’ve failed to live up to their potential.

It has been revealed that the first, and possibly best, example of Burt Noir is the 1975 cop drama “Hustle,” in which Reynolds plays a Los Angeles detective surrounded by moral decay and corruption. Reynolds and director Robert Aldrich got to make “Hustle” after their commercial success with “The Longest Yard,” and they immediately squandered whatever mainstream goodwill that comedy had bought them with a film so grim and pessimistic it made James Ellroy look like Fred Rogers.

It has been revealed that in “Hustle,” Reynolds is surrounded by evil both banal and terrifying, and he meets almost all of it with a weary resignation — aside from one desperate, possibly pointless act, he doesn’t even try to fight the men to whom he knows he is morally, intellectually, and physically superior. The movie reeks with sadness in spite of being marketed as a muscular action movie — though it is a muscular action movie, as is a later and more commercially successful Burt Noir, “Sharky’s Machine.”.

That movie, in which Reynolds plays a cop who falls for the prostitute he’s got under surveillance, further explores the sense of moral outrage that marked “Hustle” and Reynolds’ directorial debut, “Gator,” and it gives Reynolds another of his tortured heroes who alternates between spurts of violent action and long passages of almost complete impotence — a combination that reaches its apotheosis in “Heat.”.

According to recent reports, A clue to what Reynolds is up to in terms of deconstructing his persona comes in the opening of “Heat,” a 12-minute prologue that doesn’t have much to do with the rest of the film other than breaking down Reynolds’ heroic image for audience members who still see him as a conventional leading man. Reynolds’ Nick Escalante tries to pick up a woman in a bar in the ugliest, aggressive way possible, and it’s confusing for the audience — why has the guy who was such a sweet charmer to Sally Field, Goldie Hawn, Jill Clayburgh, and Julie Andrews in earlier movies turned into such a misogynistic pig?

It has been revealed that even more confusing is what follows, when the meek twerp the woman is dating indicates up and beats the hell out of Reynolds in the parking lot. We learn, later on, that the whole thing was a set-up — the guy hired Nick to fake everything so he could look like a hero in front of his girlfriend. But in the audience’s mind, the seed has already been planted; subconsciously we can never quite see this guy as a straightforward hero, and that’s the way Reynolds and screenwriter William Goldman want it.

The film progresses as a kind of hangout movie familiar to anyone who loves Quentin Tarantino’s “Jackie Brown” or “The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth”; there’s a relatively solid genre movie structure running underneath everything, but Goldman takes plenty of time for character-developing digressions that give the whole movie a rich sense of melancholy texture.

Escalante’s tale of attempted moral redemption in the form of his efforts to help the victim of a sadistic young gangster avenge her beating moves from sharp wit (in the form of the best dialogue Goldman ever wrote — which is saying something when you’re talking about the guy who wrote “Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid”) and contemplative scenes of self-examination to shocking bursts of expertly choreographed violence and wistful romance.

It’s all given added layers of irony and resonance by the Las Vegas setting, which seems to mock Escalante’s dreams of hitting it big and escaping to a peaceful retirement in Italy, and Goldman’s choice to set the movie at Christmas, which only mocks Escalante more cruelly. Although “Heat” is one of Goldman’s least well known films (a movie that is easily forgotten amidst the more honored “All the President’s Men,” “Marathon Man,” and “Misery”), it’s one of his most beautifully written, with badinage as snappy as it is revealing and a weaving plot whose emotional effects really sneak up on you.

Industry observers note that so why were American critics so dismissive? As is so often the case, they mostly seemed to be reviewing the chaotic circumstances of the production more than the movie that actually resulted from them. “Heat” was a famously troubled movie, whose misfortunes made their way into the press long before the film actually hit American screens. Its beginnings were auspicious: Goldman, one of the most respected writers in the business, was adapting his own novel (with a clause in his contract that said he couldn’t be rewritten), and Robert Altman was set to direct.

Then, only days before shooting, Altman quit the project after his favored cinematographer, Canadian cameraman Pierre Mignot, was denied an H-1 Visa to work in America. Altman claimed that over the course of eight pictures he had developed a style and way of working with Mignot that he didn’t want to abandon, and certainly didn’t want to have to reinvent in a matter of days with another director of photography. Whether or not this is the whole story is anybody’s guess — it was reported that the real reason Altman left had to do with creative differences between him and producer Elliott Kastner, and that Mignot merely gave him the excuse he was looking for to walk.

Recent findings suggest that goldman suggested Reynolds, who had helmed “Sharky’s Machine” and other fine pictures, take over directing himself, but Burt stated he wanted to focus solely on his performance. Thus began a game of directorial musical chairs that would be widely reported and which gave “Heat” the patina of a disaster in the making. Reliable craftsman Dick Richards came on first, only to leave the production after getting into a fistfight with Reynolds on the set. In spite of lawsuits flying back and forth, Richards returned to the set, only to leave again when he fell off a camera crane and ended up in the hospital.

Sources indicate that on the days when Richards was out second-unit director and stunt coordinator Stan Barrett took on directing duties, and some of the most electrifying moments in “Heat” can be attributed to him. Barrett had worked as Reynolds’ stunt double for more than 20 years and had a great sense of how to capitalize on the actor’s physicality and image, and the set pieces he directed for “Heat” — like a hotel room fight between Reynolds and some thugs — are beautifully staged and filled with brutally effective and original instances of sudden and shocking violence.

In a recent development, DGA rules at the time prohibited Barrett from taking over the reins for more than five days, since the guild forbade any preexisting member of a troupe from replacing the director — a rule, ironically, that was created when Reynolds’ friend Clint Eastwood fired director Philip Kaufman from “The Outlaw Josey Wales” and replaced him with himself. With only eight days of filming left on “Heat,” television stalwart Jerry Jameson (who had directed Reynolds years earlier on “Dan August”) stepped in and finished the picture.

According to recent reports, the experience doesn’t seem to have been a happy one for many of the principals involved, especially Richards, who gave interviews begging people not to blame him for what he saw as the film’s failures. A DGA arbitration determined that Richards was responsible for 40% of the film — more than any of the other directors on the project — and thus should have the credit. Not wanting his name on the movie, Richards eschewed his usual credit for “Directed by R.M. Richards” and tried to wash his hands of the entire debacle as soon as he could.

Sources indicate that with the movie’s own director badmouthing the film in the press, “Heat” never really stood a chance. Yet the movie that exists doesn’t show signs of any of the production’s dysfunction, aside from a slightly abrupt and abbreviated finale (one of several that was shot). In the end, Goldman and Reynolds, more than any director, are the true auteurs of “Heat,” and the consistency of their conception of Nick Escalante as an honorable, self-destructive, romantic, tortured, possibly doomed hero gives “Heat” a lingering emotional resonance and power that still works 40 years later.

Burt would continue his forays into Burt Noir both in film (“Malone,” “Rent-A-Cop,” “Physical Evidence”) and television (“B.L. Stryker,” “Hard Time”), but he never played this kind of character better than he did in “Heat.” The movie was remade, incidentally, in 2015 by “Con Air” director Simon West as “Wild Card.” That version didn’t connect with critics or audiences either, in spite of Goldman once again writing the screenplay. At this point it’s fair to assume that “Heat” is just a doomed project, which is a shame — since it also happens to represent one of Goldman’s finest artistic moments.
