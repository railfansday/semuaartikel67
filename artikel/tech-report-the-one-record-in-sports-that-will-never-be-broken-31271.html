No one told me there would be math.

Recent findings suggest that back in my full-time sportswriting days—and, in fact, throughout my journalism career—I managed to disguise my fundamental innumeracy. Then suddenly, quite recently, numbers became paramount in sports. Baseball led the way, as it does in so many areas where boredom is involved. Now, thanks to gambling’s explosion in popularity, statistics have become one of the primary ways to be a fan.

In this era of analytics and the tyranny of numbers, there is one statistic that remains not only my favorite but that is likely never to fall, and it comes from, of all sports, hockey. It is this, which boggles the mind: Wayne Gretzky ended his career in 1999 with a total of 2,857 points, which includes goals and assists. Factor out the goals, and he’s still at 1,963, which is 42 more points than the player with the next-highest total, Jaromir Jagr, who is retired.

Sources indicate that the first time I ever saw Gretzky play was during the 1988 Stanley Cup playoffs, when my Boston Bruins faced his Edmonton Oilers. These were the dynastic Oilers, loaded with talent: Mark Messier. Jari Kurri. Glenn Anderson. Grant Fuhr between the pipes. All of them Hall of Famers. Amid all this clamorous talent, Gretzky was a phantom. He had this strange and wonderful ability to vanish in the middle of the action and then, suddenly, appear in exactly the right spot at exactly the right time to make exactly the right play.

In a recent development, gretzky will likely be the leading point scorer in NHL history for the next several decades. In fact, you have to go all the way down to seventh place on the list to find an active player, Sidney Crosby, who is 202 short of Gretzky’s 1,963 assist-only number. Another active player, Alex Ovechkin, is in tenth place, but he’s got one skate out the door already. The next active players on the list are Evgeni Malkin and Patrick Kane, at 23rd and 24th.

Like Gretzky himself, this statistic disappears among all the more notable numbers until it suddenly pops out to amaze and astound. I like Sidney Crosby a great deal as a player, but I hope he never gets there.

Charles P Pierce is the author of four books, most recently Idiot America, and has been a working journalist since 1976.
