Five sightings of Samoa's critically endangered manumea, a rare relative of the dodo, offer hope for a bird threatened by hunting and habitat loss.

It has been revealed that the manumea, or tooth-billed pigeon, is Samoa’s national bird and one of only three surviving relatives of the extinct dodo. Its scientific name is Didunculus strigirostris, and the genus name means “little dodo” in Latin.

During a survey from October 17 to November 13, 2025, the Samoa Conservation Society recorded five manumea sightings in Upolu’s Uafato rainforest.

No photograph was taken, the society mentioned. The bird’s call is also easily confused with that of the more common Pacific imperial pigeon.

Recent findings suggest that like the dodo, the manumea is a pigeon. It is stout and short-toed, about 12 inches (31 centimeters) long.

Its head and neck are a glossy blackish green, and its back and wing coverts are chestnut brown.

The bird is named for several notches along the cutting edge of its lower bill. Its bill is adapted to saw through the tough seeds of Dysoxylum trees, relatives of mahogany, according to the EDGE of Existence program.

Recent findings suggest that it flies clumsily and slowly along a low path, which helps set it apart from other Samoan pigeons. The only nest ever recorded sat about 82 feet (25 meters) up in a banyan tree.

Recent findings suggest that the bird prefers the large, hard-coated fruits of Dysoxylum. Hunters interviewed for the conservation effort said it also feeds on several other fruiting trees, and it may forage on the ground.

The species has been listed as Critically Endangered by the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) since 2014. It had been listed as Endangered from 2000 to 2014.

Samoa’s Ministry of Natural Resources and Environment and the Samoa Conservation Society published a recovery plan for the species in March 2020. At that time, the last definite, confirmed sighting dated from August 4, 2017, in the Uafato forest.

A juvenile photographed on Savai’i in December 2013 was the first certain record of the species this century. It also showed that the manumea was still breeding.

The species is known from ten localities on Savai’i, and remote areas of forest on that island have never been surveyed.

Earlier fieldwork suggested that one definite sighting was possible every three to five years, even with medium to intensive search effort.

The 2025 observers saw only adult birds, and the society has noted it is unclear whether the sightings involved one bird or several.

The manumea’s advertising call is nearly identical to one of the calls of the Pacific imperial pigeon, or lupe. Even local hunters and experts struggle to tell the two apart.

A 2020 study in Pacific Conservation Biology tested whether hunters’ traditional knowledge could help. Recordings at nine forest sites on Upolu and Savai’i yielded 104 clear call sequences.

It has been revealed that two top hunters each identified the species behind 80 sequences. They agreed on 54 of them, or 67 percent.

Sound analysis found that the manumea’s coo calls are lower in pitch and more regularly spaced than those of the lupe. The regular rhythm may help observers recognize the bird in the field.

The study also described the manumea as actively hiding in the dense canopy when it hears people moving through the forest.

Surveys in the late 1980s put the population at 4,800 to 7,200 birds across Samoa.

Recent findings suggest that A later estimate of about 500 birds, from 2005 and 2006, is now considered far too high. It relied on calls that are easily confused with the lupe’s.

Surveys in 2012 and 2013 estimated fewer than 50 birds on each of Samoa’s two main islands, Upolu and Savai’i.

Sources indicate that the recovery strategy names three main threats: habitat loss and degradation, hunting and invasive predators. In recent decades, it appears that manumea have been killed mainly by mistake or as bycatch during lupe hunts.

Black rats and feral cats may also harm the species, though there is no direct evidence of it yet. Cyclones Ofa in 1990 and Val in 1991 reduced forest canopy cover by an estimated 27 percent, and the population appears never to have recovered.

Samoa has banned hunting all endemic pigeons, doves and fruit bats since at least 2004, but the rule has never been enforced nationally.

Recent findings suggest that the ten-year plan sets out eight objectives and 26 activities, with an estimated budget of about $1.8 million. Its goal is to improve the species’ status from Critically Endangered to Endangered by 2029.

Early priorities included a conservation working group, a two-year fundraising campaign and a dedicated manumea coordinator.

A 2019 to 2020 national campaign strategy called the manumea “highly threatened and at risk of extinction in our lifetimes.”.

Surveys of remote forest areas are still needed to find any undiscovered groups of the bird.

The information was obtained from the Samoa Ministry of Natural Resources and Environment and the Samoa Conservation Society.
