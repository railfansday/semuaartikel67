Your home may be full of wildlife you’ve never noticed

Homes across the United States and Canada are full of wildlife.

A continent-wide survey has found nearly 2,100 species of wild organisms living indoors, mostly insects and spiders, plus centipedes, snails, fungi, and many animals most people have never heard of.

One record is an alligator that turned up in somebody’s garage.

Industry observers note that this is the largest study of indoor wildlife so far. Nearly all of it is built from photos taken by ordinary people.

The study was led by researchers at North Carolina State University and Colorado State University.

Over 60 co-authors contributed, including museum curators, university entomologists, and independent experts from across North America and Europe.

Ecologists have spent decades cataloging life in rainforest canopies and on the deep sea floor. The places where most of us spend most of our time have received far less attention.

“While researchers have focused significant attention on a variety of natural ecosystems – from rainforest canopies to the deep sea – we know remarkably little about the ecosystems of manmade, indoor environments,” said lead author Bradley Allf from Colorado State.

“Our goal with this work was to treat the indoors, at the continental scale, as an ecosystem that we wanted to explore as ecologists,” he explained.

Earlier surveys of indoor life either covered much smaller areas or relied on DNA traces, which can’t always pin down exactly which species was present.

This is the first survey of indoor life spanning the United States and Canada.

The team released a citizen science project called Never Home Alone on iNaturalist, a website where people share photos of the living things they come across.

Participants were asked to photograph wild organisms in their homes, offices, and other indoor spaces. The rule for “wild” was simple: anything that was neither a pet nor a potted plant.

Sources indicate that submissions came from at least five continents. This study focuses on North America.

About 3,000 people contributed 23,782 observations, from every US state and every Canadian province. Most were taken in homes, but some came from offices, schools, and airports. The records cover almost three decades, from 1996 to 2024.

Industry observers note that every observation was checked by specialists. Dozens of experts identified each organism as precisely as they could.

“This work underscores the value of natural history expertise and the ability to identify species, which is a skillset that is less common among biologists than it once was,” Allf said.

It has been revealed that about 95% of the observations were arthropods, the group of animals with jointed legs and hard outer skeletons. Insects made up 60% of all records, mostly beetles, flies, moths, wasps and ants, and true bugs.

It has been revealed that spiders accounted for 30%. Animals with backbones made up only 4%, most of them reptiles and mammals.

The rest included fungi, plants, earthworms, snails, small crustaceans, and even single-celled organisms.

Eight species were recorded more than 200 times each.

In order, they were the Asian lady beetle, the brown marmorated stink bug, the house centipede, the varied carpet beetle, the long-bodied cellar spider, the triangulate combfoot spider, the bathroom moth fly, and the eastern parson spider.

“The organisms we found ranged from Asian lady beetles – which were the most commonly found species – to an alligator that unexpectedly turned up in one person’s garage,” Allf said.

“But one of the things that was striking was how common predator species were – including hundreds of different spider species.”.

Half of the top eight are hunters, and some of them do useful work.

“One study participant noted that they view jumping spiders as working animals, because the spiders play an active role in reducing the population of flies and other pests that they don’t want in their home.”.

Senior author Rob Dunn from NC State pointed out that most people share their homes with these animals every day and have no idea what they are.

“Another thing that is striking about this work is that most people could look over a list of the most commonly found species and wouldn’t know what most of those animals even are – and yet they are around us, all the time,” he said.

“For example, I suspect most people are unfamiliar with comb-footed spiders, but we have more than a thousand observations of this family of spiders.”.

He sees this as a fairly recent loss.

“This is a stark departure from what we would have expected historically, when our ancestors would have been more aware of the living things around them.”.

In a recent development, “That’s something we lost. And this is a first step toward regaining that understanding.”.

Recent findings suggest that the list has a very long tail. About 52% of the species were recorded indoors just once.

The true total is almost certainly higher than 2,100, because 44% of the observations couldn’t be identified down to species.

According to recent reports, the authors also point out the dataset’s limits. Nobody controlled how hard each person looked, so large, eye-catching animals are probably overrepresented.

And not every animal that ends up indoors actually lives there. Some had simply wandered in and become trapped.

The survey also reveals how little is known about even the most common indoor animals.

“It could inform future research in many ways,” Dunn remarked.

“For example, house centipedes were the third most commonly found animals, and yet I’m not aware of anyone who is studying them. That’s worth exploring.”.

Some of these animals might even turn out to be useful.

“For example, we now know that camel crickets carry a microbe that can break down paper products. That could be useful. What else might be out there, in our own homes?”.

“Once we know what’s there, we can begin asking additional questions,” Dunn noted.

According to recent reports, “Why are these organisms there? What are they doing? Are they beneficial?”.

In a recent development, “But knowing what’s there is the first step. And it’s exciting because these are the animals we’re around all the time, but we just overlook.”.

It has been revealed that the scientists want to investigate how the climate outside shapes what turns up inside. They also plan to figure out which species have actually adapted to life in buildings.

Every record has a date and a location, so the dataset can also serve as a baseline for spotting newly introduced arrivals or disappearances in the future. The data are free for anyone to use.

The project is still running, and anyone with a phone can contribute.

“People can still submit observations to Never Home Alone. The more data the project collects, the more insight we’ll have on the unique and growing ecosystem of the great indoors,” Allf concluded.

The study is published in the journal Ecology.
