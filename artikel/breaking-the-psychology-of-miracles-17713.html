Breaking: The Psychology of Miracles

Posted November 16, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk.

Recent findings suggest that the human psyche has always upheld a tireless faith in the extraordinary. When tragic events decimate our sense of safety and security, such as an unexpected medical diagnosis, a devastating personal crisis, or a profound loss, we seldom default to reason. Instead, we survey horizons looking for signs and wishing for the seemingly impossible to step in and rescue us from the inevitable.

It has been revealed that this reaction is not rare. Surveys from Gallup and Pew Research show that up to 79 percent of U.S. adults believe in miracles, while more than 67 percent strongly believe in phenomena beyond scientific explanation. When reason falls arguably short, divine intervention becomes the next best thing.

In a recent development, for millennia, our existence has relied heavily on our ancestors’ ability to anticipate threats and secure their surroundings. This important skill helped to avoid near-certain death from starvation or the elements, in the event we were ostracized from our tribe. The robust urge to create some kind of order in a confusing and hostile world was paramount. Consequently, strict cause-and-effect accounts often fall emotionally flat. Whenever we are confronted with catastrophic hardship or existential dread, statistics might explain how an event occurred, but oftentimes it can't answer more profound human questions.

Recent findings suggest that whenever human beings reframe a striking coincidence as divine intervention or maybe even the hand of God, you have to wonder: Is it simply impulsive denial, or is it a hardwired inclination to believe in the supernatural? Accordingly, it's hard to negate the obvious that perhaps miracles act as psychological defense mechanisms—safeguards explicitly designed to protect us from crippling despair. That's why we so often hear overused proverbs like “Everything happens for a reason” and “God works in mysterious ways.”.

Whenever we utilize this defense mechanism, the human mind reclaims a sense of agency when circumstances beyond our control leave us feeling powerless. Anticipating miracles helps to regulate the body's built-in stress response by lowering cortisol levels (the stress hormone) and sustaining emotional stamina during prolonged crisis. It's part of our evolutionary adaptation—a sophisticated cognitive mechanism intended to stimulate hope, develop resilience, and drive us forward even when the odds are stacked against us. Novelist and philosopher Fyodor Dostoevsky, who profoundly examined human psychology, wrote, “since man cannot live without miracles, he will provide himself with miracles of his own making.".

But like most things in life, there are downsides to having a miracle mindset. Shielding us from hopelessness and relying too heavily on the mystical can pose psychological, medical, and existential risks. For instance, individuals might reject evidence-based medical treatments, therapeutic interventions, and, perhaps, lifesaving procedures by instead waiting for an unlikely intervention from above.

Additionally, it can lead to passivity and hesitation when needing to assert free will in making key decisions. It could also cause spiritual injury because when anticipated miracles fail to happen, the ensuing fallout can be debilitating. In place of organically processing grief, people might also assume cosmic guilt, blaming themselves or believing their faith was not strong enough. This could potentially worsen their trauma.

It has been revealed that the miracle mindset could also foster detachment from reality, ignoring hard facts, leaving individuals vulnerable to exploitation by predatory people, control groups, and buying into false promises. In the end, it could train the mind to overlook sound rationality and established scientific data that is invaluable to our health and well-being.

In America, presenting cold, hard facts to people who are deeply entrenched in their beliefs can trigger a reaction. For many, perhaps it feels too much like an existential threat, frequently promoting defensiveness and condemnation. Whenever we challenge any kind of sacred doctrine, we invite accusations of blasphemy or a lack of a moral compass.

Industry observers note that our personal beliefs are much more than just opinions. They are vital pillars of safety, identity, and communal belonging. Science demonstrates that when an overpoweringly held worldview is questioned, the brain processes it like a threat of physical danger. It does not compute it as a rational debate, but as a menace to psychological existence.

According to recent reports, finally, believing in miracles fulfills a human need that offers structure and comfort. It helps us manage the fear of what lies beyond more easily. The idea of a meaningless, dark oblivion that lacks determination is hard for many people to swallow. Hence, the concept of divine intervention acts as a soothing balm against uncertainty, providing reassurance in a shaky and unpredictable world.

In addition, every human being feels the omnipresent specter of death. The anxiety around mortality will always be something we have in common. But what separates us is the degree to which we allow fear to govern our choices and our worldviews and how it might compromise our ability to exercise our own free will.

Therefore, how we respond to it is wholly a choice. Perhaps the true measure of our freedom is not how we escape our fear of mortality, but how we live it every day, in spite of it. And sometimes, enduring that reality requires a trace of the supernatural. There’s no harm in a spark of magic in the midst of inescapable tragedy.

Industry observers note that as the saying goes (often credited to Albert Einstein), "There are only two ways to live your life: One is as though nothing is a miracle. The other is as though everything is a miracle.”.
