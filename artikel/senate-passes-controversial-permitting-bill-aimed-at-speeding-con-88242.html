Senate Passes Controversial Permitting Bill Aimed at Speeding Construction and Lowering Costs

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A bipartisan bill to streamline permitting for major infrastructure projects could have implications for both housing and energy costs.

The Senate voted 62-33 to pass the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, or BAAJA, which would streamline permits and limit the scope of government reviews under the National Environmental Policy Act and Clean Water Act. Much of that was aimed at big energy infrastructure, but it also has implications for housing and even data centers.

Senate committee leaders Sheldon Whitehouse (D-RI), Martin Heinrich (D-NM), Shelley Moore Capito (R-WV), and Mike Lee (R-UT) introduced the bill. It's since been attached to a larger and more popular bill, but lawmakers have been debating and tweaking it for several weeks.

The latest version released this month updated language to assuage concerns that it would weaken environmental protections and rights of Native American groups. The bill has been popular but divisive: While the speed would boost green energy like wind and solar power, plus electric infrastructure that lowers costs, it could also speed along fossil fuel investments, like natural gas pipelines, and nuclear power.

"Our permitting system takes too long, costs too much, and leaves too much uncertainty for the people trying to build what our country needs," Lee mentioned on the floor before the vote. The bill could make "everything you buy" less expensive in the long run.

In a recent development, but several housing advocates have cheered the bill, saying it would make it easier to build affordable housing and invest in local communities. They include the National Association of Home Builders, YIMBY Action, the Niskanen Center, Associated Builders and Contractors, and the National Community Development Association.

"America’s housing shortage demands solutions that make it easier and more affordable to build," National Association of Realtors® Chief Advocacy Officer Shannon McGahn said. NAR "supports streamlining permitting processes and reducing unnecessary regulatory burdens that drive up development costs, delay construction, and limit housing supply.".

A White House policy paper estimates federal permitting requirements for major infrastructure take 4.2 years to complete—and produce 1,700 pages of paperwork. It expects reforms to increase the GDP by $82 billion to $164 billion and produce $14 billion to $28 billion in tax revenue.

It also noted permitting reform could cut electric bills by $178 to $251 a year for the average household. President Donald Trump urged the bill's passage in a post on Truth Social, calling it "a MAJOR WIN.".

"This Landmark Permitting Reform Legislation will codify our America First Energy Agenda, and ensure that we can build Pipelines, Mines and Power Plants faster than ever before, lowering Energy Costs for American Families, and creating High Paying American Jobs," the president posted.

"We can lower costs, juice the clean energy economy, build massive things again in America, reduce pollution, and create tons of fresh well-paying union jobs," Whitehouse mentioned.

A number of groups opposed to the bill argued that some of the protections it removes would endanger protections for Native American groups and hamstring environmental preservation.

In a recent development, the Association on American Indian Affairs noted the bill would "threaten Native sovereignty and the protections that safeguard our country's cultural and historic heritage." The Endangered Species Coalition says it would weaken protections. Both continued to oppose the bill even as it was amended.

The House would need to vote on the bill next, and members have already began debating its merits.

Industry observers note that "Even as amended, it undermines Tribal sovereignty, environmental laws, and locks in dangerous projects," Rep. Melanie Stansbury (D-NM) noted in an X post. "This is NOT the deal, NOT the time, and NOT what the American people asked for.".

Senate Majority Leader John Thune (R-SD) cast the bill as a way for Republicans to use the remaining weeks of their majority to improve affordability for Americans.

"One of the biggest things we can do to address affordability for the long term is to ensure a reliable and affordable energy supply," Thune stated. “And that starts with ensuring that energy projects don’t face unnecessary permitting barriers.".

The federal permitting process, he later said, "has unfortunately gone far beyond these reasonable expectations.

Sources indicate that “And that’s due in large part to senseless delays caused by cascading litigation from environmental groups and by agencies determined to study a project to death," Thune said. “We’ve seen those delays happen to everything from water projects to broadband deployment to affordable energy and improving roads and bridges.".

In a recent development, A poll by moderate think tank Third Way found bipartisan support for the tenets of the bill. About 62% of respondents named lowering utility bills as a top concern, and 57% keeping the power grid reliable.

“It’s more vital than ever to pass permitting reform this year. The overwhelming support for the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act reflects the Senate’s commitment to addressing the nation's energy affordability challenges.".

Recent findings suggest that the bill was also host to Congress' latest effort to rein in data center developments proliferating throughout the country. One amendment by Sen. Ron Wyden (D-OR) would have banned data centers on public lands. Republicans blocked it.

"There is enormous concern that this bill puts the pedal to the floor on data centers across the country," Wyden noted on the floor.

According to recent reports, following Democrats' gains in the House after the 2026 midterms, a number of left-leaning groups have called for a hesitant Congress to formulate more laws involving data centers. Among them, the Chamber of Progress called for federal AI safety standards and requirements for data centers to cover their own costs.

It has been revealed that A recent study by the Wharton School indicates that data centers have indeed increased industrial electricity prices in the nation. But it also said that residential customers absorbed relatively little of that price gain.

But that doesn't mean they don't affect communities. A group of Senate Democrats, including Elizabeth Warren of Massachusetts, Chris Van Hollen of Maryland, and Richard Blumenthal of Connecticut, released a report pinning other costs on them. It also criticized the tax incentives.

"This investigation finds the data center industry is bulldozing local communities and using their economic resources to play states and local communities against one another, creating a race to the bottom in which state and local policymakers feel they have no choice but to accede to the data centers’ demands—and leaving ordinary citizens stuck with the bills," they said in a joint statement.

In a recent development, voter anger has put data center incentives in the spotlight. Virginia spent over $4 billion in data center incentives over 10 years, according to a state report. Ohio lost $2 billion in foregone revenue from its incentive in two years alone.
