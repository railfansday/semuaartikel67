Recent findings suggest that the kelp forests that support swathes of marine life can reproduce asexually — but prefer to do it with a plant of the opposite sex. When isolated in the lab, both males and females of the Atlantic-coast species Laminaria pallida were able to produce offspring without fertilization. The gene expression in males changed, making them more similar to females. Meanwhile, kelps raised in mixed-sex groups seemed to be able to detect nearby partners through chemical signals. Understanding how kelp reproduce in different ways could help us support their success in the wild.

Epidemiologists have used mobile-phone data and test records to map the early spread of Ebola in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) in the weeks before the outbreak was declared. The study reports that although the first case was confirmed on 15 May, that person displayed symptoms of infection as early as 25 April. The findings reveal how disease-surveillance systems failed to catch the virus early, and how people’s movements were a key driver of spread in the early phases of the outbreak, the team says.

Sources indicate that A gene therapy called Lonvo-z (lonvoguran ziclumeran) could become the first that deploys the CRISPR–Cas9 gene-editing system directly inside the body to be approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA). The therapy was designed to treat a rare genetic disorder that causes painful and potentially dangerous swelling in the throat and other parts of the body. Lonvo-z continues to be effective four years after treatment, according to data released by its developer Intellia Therapeutics. Intellia has now applied for the FDA approval of the therapy and expects a decision by 10 March.

Researchers have published a huge atlas of a brain region called the basal ganglia — a group of cells deep in the brain that process motor, cognitive, mood and decision-making information. The atlas comprises 25.5 million brain cells from humans, macaques and marmosets. The research — published across a whopping 26 papers — includes understudied neurons, provides insights about how some brain cells change with age and suggests which might be linked to diseases. “It’s a massive dictionary that we needed in the field,” indicates neuroscientist Ana Muñoz-Manchado.

Reference: 26 papers in Cell, Cell Reports, Cell Reports Methods, Cell Systems, Cell Genomics and Neuron.

Since Denmark introduced a nationwide policy of vaccinating pregnant people against the respiratory pathogen RSV in 2025, hospital admissions of newborns with the virus have plummeted. RSV usually causes mild symptoms but can lead to severe illness and even death in vulnerable people, including infants. “It’s just remarkable,” states paediatrician Buddy Creech, to have gone “from no mechanism to prevent RSV to an embarrassment of riches”. (Nature | 5 min read).

Reference: The Lancet Regional Health — Europe paper.

Over the past four years, a group of researchers dubbed the ‘Pathogen Hunters’ have published dozens of papers detailing their analysis of the genomes of pathogens — such as H5N1 avian influenza, the mpox virus and SARS-CoV-2 — from tens of thousands of samples. The group’s findings have raised eyebrows among some infectious-disease researchers who say that the Pathogen Hunters’ results are implausible, reports Science. Some of these concerns have led to retractions, but in other cases the group has stood by their conclusions after making substantive corrections to their papers.

In a recent development, severe drought and warming brought on by climate change is putting pressure on hundreds of species of cacti. “Even though we think of cacti as these very drought-adapted organisms, they are really not that different” from other plants, ecologist Michiel Pillet tells the Nature Podcast. Pillet and colleagues created a climate model to assess how severe drought could affect the habitat of nearly one thousand cacti species across the Americas. They found that if current climate trends continue, up to 35% of these species could lose four-fifths of their habitats by 2100.
