According to recent reports, years ago, I was talking to a very menswear-y buddy at a very menswear-y event, and he proposed a theory of outerwear that’s stuck with me: Your coat is your car. Sounds pretty … menswear-y, right? But bear with me here, because I think it actually makes perfect sense, so long as you’re willing to make a couple (major) concessions.

No, your coat cannot help you travel at 75 miles per hour down an interstate. You cannot fill it up with gasoline or top it up at a charging station. It contains, ideally, zero percent steel.

Sources indicate that but if you live in a place where you spend more time walking or taking public transit than you do behind the wheel, your coat does indeed become quite car-like. You ensconce yourself in it when you move about the world. It’s the first thing people see when you arrive and the last thing they see when you depart. It shields you from the elements while simultaneously signaling to friends and strangers alike your combined senses of utility and aesthetics. So, yeah, if you squint and look at the situation sideways, your coat is your car. Even if you live somewhere that demands personal automotive transportation, your coat is your second car—unless you’re one of those kooky types who just dart from the interior of a sedan to the interior of a restaurant in nothing but a sweater—for all the reasons stated above.

The point is: Your coat is the most visible and functional part of your winter wardrobe, and you should approach the buying and wearing of remarked coat with all due respect for its position. Which is exactly why I created this guide. We’ll run through the key styles that every guy should know, where to get a great example (or three!) of each, and how to wear them with style all season long. You won’t find truckers or bombers here—those are jackets—but you will find parkas, topcoats, trenches, and more. All of them will serve you well until the spring thaw, so let’s get into it.

In a recent development, if you’ve been keeping up with Esquire style guides, you’ll know that we’ve covered a lot of ground over the past few years, breaking down everything from the shoe styles you should have in your rotation to the winter essentials you shouldn’t go without. One story that really resonated with readers was about sweaters. In it, we talked about the fact that you can score very good knitwear from a huge range of lifestyle brands these days. We also mentioned the prudence of keeping a few category specialists on your radar. The same advice holds true for coats.

According to recent reports, on the lifestyle side, the brands will look familiar to longtime readers. You’ve got Todd Synder and Buck Mason. J. Press and J. Crew. Ralph Lauren and Drake’s and Alpha Industries and Madewell and Billy Reid and Filson ... the list goes on. You’ve also got fashion behemoths like Burberry, which famously started out with the trench coat. And then you’ve got brands that may not just do outerwear but are truly steeped in it. That could be a heritage player like Mackintosh or Schott or Gloverall, a parka expert like Canada Goose, or an upstart label like Wythe, which makes a wonderful range of products but has a cult following for its exceptional overcoats.

As we’ve warned before: This list is not exhaustive. But it’s a good place to start. Now, on to the coats themselves.

This is the dressiest of the bunch, though it wasn’t always. If you’re a real traditionalist, you might have a velvet-collared Chesterfield in your closet to wear with your fanciest togs. Most of us, though, don’t really need something that formal. The topcoat is similar to and derives from the Chesterfield, but it’s less stuffy.

Still, it looks plenty dressed up to modern eyes. It’ll have lapels, which can be notch or peak (for a little extra personality), and can be single- or double-breasted. If you suit up with any regularity, that means it’s likely the most essential for your wardrobe. Look for one made of wool or a wool blend; this is not the time to overspend on synthetics.

In a recent development, colors are usually pretty conservative—think camel, navy, gray, and black. That might seem limiting, but remember that this thing should be able to play nicely with your whole wardrobe, so opting for a more subdued hue is usually the right move. As for length: Some folks will swear something that hits above the knee looks more modern. We just think it looks off. Go for at or below the knee.

Eschewed by many as a bit too fusty and old-fashioned as recently as a decade ago, the raglan overcoat has come roaring back into the spotlight in recent years. We enthusiastically endorse its return.

The style is defined by its raglan shoulder construction, in which a diagonal seam runs from around the collarbone to the underarm, allowing for an uninterrupted panel over the top of the shoulder. Don’t worry too much about the technical details, though. The main thing to remember is that this shape is more relaxed and tends to layer over other garments (a chunky sweater, a sport coat) more easily and comfortably than a traditional “set in” shoulder seam.

Sources indicate that the raglan overcoat also tends to be cut wide and long, so you can bundle up underneath it and never worry about chilly knees. It leans more casual than a topcoat, for sure, but you can still wear it with a suit. And it lends polish to something as simple as jeans and a sweatshirt. One of our favorite subgenres of raglan overcoat is the balmacaan, which features a Prussian collar (which looks like a shirt collar instead of a lapel), a “fly front” with a placket that covers the buttons, and buttoned fasteners at the wrists so you can cinch up to keep out the cold.

According to recent reports, if you’re into the warm and wooly vibes of the two styles above but simply can’t stand a long coat, the peacoat is for you. Double-breasted and hip length, it’s a shorter—but no less capable—take on the woolen overcoat that dates back about three centuries.

Industry observers note that originally created for sailors of European (and, eventually, American) navies, the peacoat is tough enough to defend from open-ocean winds and sea spray, which means it’s definitely up to the task of keeping you toasty on your way to work. There are a lot of versions out there these days that are done up in frankly wimpy wool blends that might feel nice at first but don’t do the job.

Recent findings suggest that go for a heavyweight wool like melton, but make sure there’s enough room to layer up underneath it—and keep an eye on the lapels. They should be big. Big enough that you can pop that collar and shield yourself from an incoming gust without feeling even a tickle of wind touch your neck. Plus, it looks cool.

In a recent development, thanks to a quirky little phenomenon we like to call “global climate change,” there’s a very good chance that some of the winter days ahead will feel a lot more like fall or spring. On those days—especially if it’s raining—you should reach for a trench coat or a mac. The two styles aren’t synonymous, but they’re related in both vibe and history, so we’re coupling them here.

Maximalists will prefer the trench, with its storm flap and epaulettes and double-breasted closure. Minimalists will reach for the mac, with its clean lines and streamlined shape. It’s really a matter of preference, because both fulfill the same roles in your outerwear lineup.

In a recent development, they’ll keep you warmish, especially when combined with additional layers. They’ll help block the wind when it kicks up. And although we can’t guarantee you’ll walk away dry if you stand around for an hour during a deluge, they’ll fend off a shower—or a sprint through a thunderstorm—quite capably.

It has been revealed that if it’s really fucking frigid, you need a parka. Make sure it’s properly insulated, whether with a synthetic fill or down. And unless you like freezing your literal ass off, get one that’s hip length or longer. You could get something technical and bright that looks like it just stepped off the ski slopes, but we’re more inclined toward rugged-looking styles that take their cues from the military and outdoor exploration.

The N-3B, or “snorkel parka,” is a burly beast that looks absolutely fantastic with a casual outfit and, if you ask us, can be fun with a suit too. (Though if you’re worried about raising eyebrows or breaking a dress code, maybe opt for a topcoat instead.) Something like Canada Goose’s Langdon parka is a little more pared back in looks but overdelivers on warmth, meaning you’ll be cozy in even the worst weather. The trick is finding one that balances form and function so that it looks as though you intended to wear it on the way to the bar—not like you wandered off some snowcapped mountain and forgot to change.

Sources indicate that like the peacoat, the duffle coat is a part of maritime history. It was adopted by the British Royal Navy in the late 19th century and widely issued to British troops during World War II. Thanks to the sheer number produced and postwar surplus, it rapidly became a civilian staple and eventually hopped across the pond to become a fixture of the American preppy uniform. Made of thick wool and equipped with a massive hood, it’s very warm. And thanks to the large toggle closures, you don’t even need to take your gloves off to fasten it. Is it a little odd? Perhaps. But that’s what makes it so charming.
