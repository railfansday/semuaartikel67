El Greco Masterpiece Gets Its Colors Back After More Than 400 Years

King Philip II of Spain wasn’t particularly impressed with El Greco‘s The Martyrdom of Saint Maurice and the Theban Legion when he first saw it. The following four centuries of dirt, varnish, and enthusiastic repainting hadn’t done the picture any favors, either.

A painstaking nine-month restoration, recently finished, has found the monumental painting looking considerably more like the one El Greco envisioned. The muddy browns and sickly greens that had dulled the picture for generations are gone. In their place are brilliant blues, reds, yellows, and purples, along with a newfound depth that had been buried beneath grime. The restoration was conducted at Madrid’s Royal Collections Gallery, under the supervision of Rafael Alonso, an 81-year-old former Prado Museum restorer, who has spent decades working on El Greco’s paintings. The project was supported by the Fundacion Callia in partnership with Spain’s Patrimonio Nacional, which oversees the country’s royal collections.

The results were reported in detail by El País, whose writer followed the restoration from the painting’s removal from El Escorial in February through its finished restoration in October. And what a job it was.

In a recent development, alonso had initially expected the restoration to take about four months. But once he and fellow restorer Adela Murgui began cleaning the enormous canvas, which measures nearly 15 feet high and 10 feet wide, they discovered that its condition was far worse than even feared.

According to recent reports, “When I put the cotton to the painting, my heart sank,” Alonso told El País. Centuries of varnish and repainting had darkened the colors and flattened the composition. In the 19th century, he explained, painters had simply painted over damaged areas, often adding their own interpretations of what El Greco intended.

The restorers spent months carefully stripping away those additions, revealing the original paint beneath. Afterward, the repaired the damaged areas using soluble materials, taking pains not to add anything that couldn’t be removed later.

The restoration is particularly striking given the painting’s rather unhappy beginnings.

El Greco painted The Martyrdom between 1580 and 1582 for the basilica at El Escorial, Philip II’s vast monastery and palace outside Madrid. The king wanted a powerful statement of Catholic faith, while the artist hoped the commission might secure him a permanent position as a court painter.

Neither’s wish was totally granted.

Rather than concentrate on the grisly execution of Saint Maurice and his fellow Christian soldiers, El Greco pushed the violence into the background. Up front, he placed a group of handsome soldiers in brightly colored clothing, their elongated bodies and theatrical poses exemplary of the skills he had learned in Venice and Rome.

According to recent reports, philip II apparently admired the painting’s quality, paying the artist a substantial sum of 800 ducats, but felt it wasn’t right for its intended religious setting. The work was relegated to another room, and El Greco never clinched the royal appointment he’d hoped for.

The painting stayed there for most of the next 444 years, apart from a trip to Madrid for an exhibition in 1999, all the while gradually losing most of its remarkable color and atmosphere.

Recent findings suggest that the restoration also marks another achievement for the Fundacion Callia, founded by Carmen Reviriego, which has helped restore works by Zurbarán, Rubens, Brueghel the Elder, Velázquez, and others through its partnership with Madrid’s Royal Academy of Fine Arts of San Fernando. According to El País, the foundation has helped mobilize €5 million for the preservation of artworks.

Sources indicate that when ARTnews spoke with Reviriego in March, she described Callia as an “incubator of patrons,” bringing together collectors and philanthropists from Spain, Latin America, and beyond. Her goal is to encourage a stronger culture of private support for the arts in a country that has traditionally relied heavily on public funding.

Reviriego had specifically mentioned The Martyrdom during that interview as an example of the work the foundation hoped to support. “El Greco was the Jeff Koons or Damien Hirst of his day,” she told ARTnews this week, “and Phillip II was the most power man and greatest patron of the 16th century.”.

There’s a nice historical symmetry here. Reviriego argued that great works of art are rarely the achievement of an artist alone. Without patrons, many wouldn’t exist at all. She pointed to the Royal Collections themselves, built in part through the patronage of Philip II, the very king who commissioned The Martyrdom more than four centuries ago. Now, private patronage has helped give that painting a fresh life.

For Alonso, meanwhile, the completed restoration marks something of a farewell. After decades spent working on El Greco’s paintings, he told El País that The Martyrdom was the last of the artist’s great masterpieces still awaiting restoration.

Industry observers note that the newly restored painting is expected to go on temporary display at Madrid’s Royal Collections Gallery before returning to El Escorial. This time, visitors will get a much better idea of what Philip II saw more than four centuries ago.
