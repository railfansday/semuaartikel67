📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a recent development, for about 80 miles near its halfway point, the Ice Age Trail is split in two. When planning my hike, I decided to go east. It’s five miles longer than the west branch, but has 12 more miles of trail and better overnight options.

At Karner Meadow, not far south of the Pleasant Lake bifurcation, I pitched my tent just in time to shelter from a thunderstorm. It was my first of five nights on the east branch.

The Baraboo Hills contain the four biggest climbs on the IAT. I started my day with number one: almost 700 feet of elevation gain. (Numbers two and four were next. I’d conquered number three the previous afternoon without realizing.) At some point during the uphill, I crossed the boundary into Devil’s Lake State Park, but it was still early, and the trail was empty.

At the first campground, there were few signs of life. I stopped at the small store for a drink and a snack, and in that time, Wisconsin woke up. At the next campground, there were cars and pedestrians everywhere. On the north shore of the lake, there were plenty of people swimming and soaking up the mid-morning sun. (The east and west branches rejoin nearby, fyi.).

Things quietened down during the hike up the West Bluff, and I didn’t meet anyone on the summit ridgeline. For almost a mile, the views were among the best of the entire IAT.

Devil’s Lake, from the West Bluff.

Back down at the lake, I found the south shore even busier than the north. I ate an early lunch at the concession stand, then took an extended break before I tackled the East Bluff. The views at the top were also spectacular, and lots of people were enjoying them.

The throngs thinned for a mile or so, and disappeared completely after another mile. I completed my final descent of the day, headed for the Merrimac Ferry, and arrived just in time to see it leave. Still, that gave me time to buy an ice cream. The ferry returned less than 10 minutes later, pulling itself across Lake Wisconsin using three underwater cables.

The half-mile ferry ride only took a few minutes, but it was enough time for my first ice cream to settle. I bought another ice cream on the south shore, replenished my water supply, and walked one more mile to my campsite. A 2700 foot day might not be huge, but it was my biggest on the IAT. It was also my favorite.

According to recent reports, the morning marked the beginning of another three day town-to-town relay. I started by admiring the view from Gibraltar Rock, then made a beeline for Lodi. Piggly Wiggly was my first stop, followed by Dollar General. As I continued south along Main Street, I grabbed the opportunity for a spontaneous game of charades with a nearby motorist.

Recent findings suggest that A Mazda CX5 was pulling out of a parking lot, and I made what I thought was the universal sign for “Roll your window down.” In fairness to the driver, she was on her own, and car windows haven’t been cranked by hand since before the turn of the century. She shook her head. Undeterred, I strung together a sequence of three more signs. She shook her head again and drove away. In fairness to me, “Your passenger-side rear tire is underinflated” is a difficult message to get across without words. I hope she had second thoughts before starting the drive back to Texas.

I repacked my food on a picnic table in the park, visited a Kwik Trip on my way out of town, and reached my campsite by late afternoon.

Leaving camp, early the next morning.

My next destination was Cross Plains. It was a low mileage day, and I arrived in time for a late lunch: sandwich and Cheetos for my main course, apple fritter and Snickers ice cream for dessert. Afterwards, I visited the IAT Alliance’s office to say hello, then the pavilion beside the library to charge my phone. The afternoon was warm, so I waited in the shade before eventually hiking up a small hill to a dispersed camping area (DCA).

I stopped at the local Kwik Trip again for breakfast. Afterwards, the trail led me through a park, along the edge of some cornfields, and on to quiet country roads. There were lots of speedy Saturday cyclists enjoying the cool, overcast weather.

Recent findings suggest that the rain, when it inevitably began to fall, wasn’t worthy of a rain jacket. I continued following the road along the perimeter of a sprawling residential area until the yellow blazes directed me to cut through the neighborhood. For the next few miles, I squeezed and sidled and slunk between houses and back yards and construction sites. I peered furtively out from between hedges and trees before darting across roads. Similar behavior, I imagined, to foxes and raccoons. I crossed a golf course, passed a handful of soccer fields, and entered Badger Prairie County Park without a moment to spare. Drizzle became downpour, and a park pavilion once again saved me from a soaking. I’d made it to Verona.

Recent findings suggest that the rain stopped an hour later. I spent another hour in a nearby Culver’s, bought snacks and dinner at Festival Foods, then returned to the park. I waited for the last visitors to leave before putting up my tent at the DCA, in the dark.

I got up at first light, broke camp promptly, and walked 200 feet to the pavilion. The sun wasn’t fully over the horizon when the first visitor arrived. I charged my phone while eating breakfast, then made use of the drinking fountain and toilet facilities. It was, without a doubt, the most luxurious DCA of the entire trail. Writing a couple of emails delayed me by 40 minutes, and by the time I left, more people had arrived by car and on foot.

According to recent reports, there were plenty of hikers enjoying Verona’s extensive trail system as I headed south, but I soon had to return to the road. I also had to make a quick trip to Kwik Trip before leaving town.

During the next few miles, I left noisy county highway for quiet country road. Eventually, I picked up the trail again. Where the IAT joins the Badger State Trail, I met a friendly cyclist who gave me a recommendation: Dot’s Tavern. An hour later, passing the tiny community of Basco, I made the necessary detour. I was eight miles from where I intended to camp, and it was still morning, so I figured I could spare an hour for a beer and some socializing.

According to recent reports, dot’s is a tiny place, 100 yards from the trail, and was busy that particular Sunday. About 20 people were sitting around a small bar and a handful of tables in what looked like someone’s back yard.

It has been revealed that well, long story short: one hour turned into five, one beer turned into six, and yet again, I ended up racing to a campsite while trying to outrun a thunderstorm. Thank you, Dave, Paul, and Laura, for your conversation. The best days sometimes contain the fewest miles.

Cornfield south of Cross Plains, Wisconsin.
