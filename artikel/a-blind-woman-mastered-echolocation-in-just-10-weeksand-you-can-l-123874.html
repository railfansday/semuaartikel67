A Blind Woman Mastered Echolocation in Just 10 Weeks—And You Can Learn This Superpower Too, Scientists Say

Earlier this year, Jayne Hyde-Dryden was walking down a busy sidewalk along a noisy, two-lane road with her friend when she realized just how much her perception had changed. Mid-conversation, her friend interrupted and noted, “Oh, that was fascinating. You just avoided a van without realizing you’d done it.”.

This amazed her friend because Hyde-Dryden is blind. She had used echolocation to detect the van, which was parked on the sidewalk, and navigated around it without even noticing. “I wasn’t consciously thinking about what I was doing at the time. Instead, I was naturally using echolocation, picking up changes in the way sound reflected from the vehicle and using that information to navigate around the obstacle,” says Hyde-Dryden, a rehabilitation teacher in the UK. Like many blind echolocators, she creates a sharp, quick sound by clicking her tongue and then listens for the echo.

While echolocation comes easily to her now, Hyde-Dryden had to work hard to learn it. Almost 20 years ago, while she was losing her sight to glaucoma, she attended a short workshop and spent hours practicing the clicking technique she learned there. But she didn’t feel confident using it until 2019 when she signed up for an intensive echolocation study at Durham University.

That study was spearheaded by Lore Thaler, PhD, a neuroscientist at Durham University whose work over the last 15 years has uncovered how echolocation changes the brain. Her research goes beyond studying the variability of perception between people, and highlights just how much one person’s perceptual reality can change, even in a short timeframe.

Thaler started her research journey studying visual perception, but her work took an unexpected turn when a collaborator introduced her to a blind echolocator. At the time, she hadn’t heard of human echolocation, but “the way he described the world was very easy to relate to from a sighted person’s perspective,” she claims.

After diving into the literature, Thaler found scattered and disjointed research on echolocation, and “very little was known about the brain basis of it,” she says. “That’s where we came in.”.

She and her collaborators used functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) to track two blind participants’ brain activity while they listened to recordings of their own echolocation clicks and echoes.

The auditory cortex responded more to echoes than non-echoes in both participants but so did regions of their visual cortex, including areas associated with visual shape perception. Even though this was what she had hoped to find, actually seeing it “was a wonderful surprise,” indicates Thaler, leading to “a great feeling of discovery.” When she and her team submitted the paper in 2011, the findings were contentious—so contentious that the journal they submitted to asked double the typical number of reviewers to take a look at it. Some thought it was an incredible finding; others thought it was predictable. But in the end, the paper got published, and when it did, it made a huge splash in the neuroscience community.

The project shifted the way Thaler’s collaborators, and later many in the field, thought about the visual cortex. Perhaps the “visual cortex” was a misleading term, some experts thought. Instead, “maybe it’s a thing for processing spatial information,” says Melvyn Goodale, PhD, a neuroscientist at Western University and Thaler’s collaborator on the project.

In a recent development, after that first study on echolocation, Thaler was hooked. She launched an entire lab of her own at Durham University dedicated to human echolocation research. She went on to study how echolocators perceive motion, navigate new spaces, and distinguish between textures like velvet or glass.

By the time Hyde-Dryden made her way to Durham in 2019, Thaler was embarking on her most ambitious project yet: a 10-week echolocation training program for both blind and sighted people.

According to recent reports, each two-hour training session involved a slew of tasks. One involved standing in front of two foam discs and deciding which was larger. Another required participants to navigate through a virtual maze with recorded clicks and echoes. During every session, participants also explored an indoor or outdoor space with the help of Thaler or one of her collaborators to practice echolocating in the real world. Thaler even brought Hyde-Dryden to the Newcastle train station to help her learn the intricacies of echolocating in a busy, loud environment. Over time, Hyde-Dryden built up a mental “library of echo patterns” for familiar objects.

In a recent development, both blind and sighted participants improved substantially at each of these tasks over the course of the 10 weeks. Just as in the initial 2011 paper and many of her follow-up studies, Thaler also wanted to understand brain activity during echolocation. This time, she had participants hop in the MRI machine both before and after learning to echolocate.

It has been revealed that before the study, Hyde-Dryden had heard from an expert blind echolocator that being inside of the MRI would feel tight and uncomfortable, but when she went into the MRI before the echolocation training, it didn’t bother her at all. After the training, however, the MRI felt quite different. “When I went in at the end, I felt horrendous. I felt ill afterwards. I felt really sick. And I think it was because I was more aware.”.

Those fMRI scans revealed that participants’ brains became attuned to echoes, whether or not they were blind. Echoes elicited a larger response in both the auditory cortex and in the primary visual cortex.

According to recent reports, “It paired I think nicely with what we, up to that point, had only been able to show in these expert echolocators,” Thaler claims. But it pushed the findings even further, demonstrating that participants don’t need to be blind to have malleable visual cortices. After just 10 weeks of training, the visual cortex becomes attuned to sounds that carry information about space. And other labs have shown that sighted people don’t even need to learn echolocation to retrain their visual cortex; a few days with a blindfold can tune your visual cortex to sound and touch signals it wouldn’t normally process.

Since that training, Hyde-Dryden has started to use echolocation every day. “It really was an absolute game changer for me,” she indicates. Thanks to echolocation, when her daughter was young, Hyde-Dryden was able to use both hands to scoop her up from the bath, bringing her into the bedroom without having to hold a cane at the same time. In conjunction with her cane and guide dog, echolocation has made Hyde-Dryden feel much more confident navigating new spaces. Even in busy train stations with high ceilings where the echo can get lost, she can find her way without assistance.

Despite success stories like hers, “it’s not a skill that is widely taught anywhere in the world,” Hyde-Dryden says. Echolocation is taught by a small number of people and is sometimes loosely included in rehabilitation curricula, “but it is certainly not given the emphasis that it should.”.

Industry observers note that thaler hopes to change that. She has offered free workshops for years and, this year, she looks to give training sessions to more than 100 rehabilitation professionals at Guide Dogs UK who will then train their hundreds of clients.

It has been revealed that “I think the neuroscience stuff has been a good vehicle to drive home the message ‘this is real, this has to be taken seriously,’” indicates Thaler. “But then to actually go out and do the training and make it useful for people, of course, that’s still another step.”.
