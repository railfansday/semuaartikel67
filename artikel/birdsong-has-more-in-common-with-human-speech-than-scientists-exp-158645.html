Birdsong has more in common with human speech than scientists expected

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A comparison of 367 bird species and 52 human languages reveals unexpected similarities in the rhythms and patterns of vocal communication.

Birds and humans share some surprisingly similar patterns when they make sounds. Even birds that never learn their songs naturally produce rhythms much like those found in human speech and music.

The finding comes from a comparison of the songs of 367 bird species with people speaking and singing in 52 languages.

Three patterns of human speech and music appeared across the birds, in those that learn their songs and in those that don’t.

The authors interpret that as a sign that the patterns come from the shared mechanics of making sound rather than from learning alone.

Industry observers note that logan James of McGill University and the University of Texas at Austin led the work with Jon Sakata of McGill. Several co-authors work at McGill’s Centre for Research on Brain, Language and Music.

In an interview with Earth.com, James noted that the shared patterns were what excited the team most.

“These results highlight that, despite the tremendous diversity across human languages and hundreds of species of birds, there are still fundamental shared sequencing patterns,” he remarked.

The recordings came from xeno-canto, where birders around the world upload recordings. The team kept only recordings the recordist had rated top quality and labeled as song rather than calls.

No second species could be in the background, no playback could have been used to provoke the bird, and the singer had to have been seen.

Software then split each song into its separate sounds, and experienced annotators checked every sound. That work produced more than 74,500 individual sounds from about 5,900 songs.

The researchers paired each group of vocal learners with closely related birds that do not learn their songs: songbirds with suboscines, and hummingbirds with nightjars.

On the human side, the team used recordings of people speaking and singing in 52 languages, made for an earlier project.

In human speech and song, the pitch rarely leaps far from one syllable to the next. The team measured the pitch at the end of each bird sound and the start of the next.

Across the birds, the jumps were smaller than chance would produce.

Human voices also keep a beat, with sounds spaced evenly in time.

Birdsong showed the same pattern more often than would be expected by chance.

And people generally stretch the last sound of a phrase, the way a singer holds a final note.

Birds did that too. Their songs also tended to grow louder through the middle of a phrase and fade at the end, as human music does.

According to recent reports, songbirds have been the usual comparison for human speech because they, like us, learn their songs by listening. This study could set them against close relatives that don’t.

Sakata told Earth.com that the team expected birds that learn their songs to show greater similarities to human speech, since people also learn to speak.

“What surprised us was that the songs of birds that do not learn their songs (e.g., suboscines, nightjars) actually had comparable patterns to us relative to the vocal-learning birds,” he noted.

Where vocal learning did make a difference, it did not act the same way in songbirds as in hummingbirds. The authors counted that as no consistent effect.

In a recent development, the matches were not perfect. Unlike human voices, which tended to become quieter throughout a phrase, birdsong tended to grow louder in the middle before fading at the end.

The authors suspect breathing explains part of it.

A person usually speaks or sings a whole phrase on one breath, and they suggest that is why the volume drops toward the end. Birds take tiny breaths between the sounds in a song.

In a recent development, lung size may matter too. Across the birds, bigger species stretched their final sounds more, and a human has much bigger lungs than any of them.

But nightjars, among the largest birds in the study, stretched their final sounds even more than people do, so size can’t explain all of it.

The team also ran a broader measurement of how pitch and rhythm change together across a whole phrase, the same one that separates speech from song in human recordings.

It has been revealed that by that measure, bird songs as a group were closer to human speech than to human singing. Hummingbirds and suboscines came out nearest to both, closer than the songbirds.

James said no large group of birds was closest to human patterns overall.

A few species were, on that combined measure, among them the scaled antbird, the blackhead antbird and the rufous-tailed hummingbird.

Songbirds and suboscines did keep a steadier beat than hummingbirds and nightjars, so they are the better birds to study for the roots of human rhythm.

In a commentary published alongside the study, Kazuo Okanoya of Teikyo University wrote that the work gives researchers a basis for testing the mechanisms behind the shared patterns.

“Linguistic concepts can guide such tests but should not be used in place of experimental data,” he added.

The study was based entirely on recordings, so the researchers could not confirm whether similarities in how birds and humans produce sound explain the shared patterns.

It also remains unclear whether listeners’ preferences help shape these patterns.

Sakata said very little is known about the shape of the voice box in many of these species, and that mapping that variety is a next step.

James mentioned earlier work by the group found that people tend to like the same male courtship sounds that females of that species like.

“So we are also curious to examine how much birds (and humans) like the sound of patterns that conform to these universal patterns,” James remarked.

According to recent reports, other features of human music, such as distinct notes and nested layers of structure, have not been checked in birds yet. The authors wrote that those are the next measurements to take.

The full study was published in the journal Science.
