Sources indicate that for decades, the word “Palestinian” conjured a very specific image in the minds of many intellectuals and members of the public: the commanding presence of Edward Noted, credited with no less than revolutionizing our collective understanding of the dynamics of oppression. Now, as Israel’s genocide in Gaza continues, Said’s legacy is not just urgent, but also, in some spaces, the target of censorship. Today, author Moustafa Bayoumi, who studied under the late critic, activist, and professor at Columbia University, pens a thoughtful review of Edward Said: Between Worlds (2026), a fresh documentary by Maiken Baird whose audio consists almost entirely of Said’s own words. “To recover Said’s voice is to rediscover some of the most compelling arguments that have been made for Palestinian liberation,” Bayoumi writes.

In a recent development, can contemporary art make us laugh (and not just when we’re making fun of it)? Greta Rainbow visits a Newly introduced York exhibition that’s all about embracing humor in art, and somewhere between Brian Belott's absurdist fan-embedded assemblages and Ebecho Muslimova’s delightfully transgressive depictions, she learns a thing or two about her own funny bone.

—Valentina Di Liscia, senior editor.

In their (still brilliant) 1990 experimental film Introduction to the End of an Argument, filmmakers Jayce Salloum and Elia Suleiman splice together troves of found footage about Palestinians and Israelis. The result is an exquisite mash-up of Hollywood cartoons and political clichés, a comic pastiche illustrating the common bond between Orientalism and zombies. Both just won’t die. | Moustafa Bayoumi.
