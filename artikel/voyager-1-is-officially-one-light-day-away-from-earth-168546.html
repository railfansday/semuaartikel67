Voyager 1 is officially one light-day away from Earth

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

After traveling through space for almost 50 years, NASA’s Voyager 1 spacecraft should be one light-day away from Earth. The probe reached this record-breaking milestone at approximately 5:16:07 a.m. EST on Wednesday, November 18. Since takes about 24 hours to receive any signal from Voyager 1, NASA officially confirmed that it reached the distance on Thursday, November 19.

William Kurth, a research scientist at the University of Iowa who has worked on the Voyager mission since 1974 tells Popular Science that this mission is about more than just science.

“The Voyager missions represent humanity’s curiosity and hunger to understand our species’ place in the universe,” he states. “They show that we are explorers and can work together to do wonderful things.”.

It has been revealed that astronomers often describe vast cosmic distances in light-years. It’s arguably the most famous unit of measurement when talking about the universe, in part because of its sheer incomprehensibility. A single particle of light travels at a speed of about 186,282 miles per second. Hypothetically speaking, if you fired a beam of light into the night sky at the outset of reading this story, it would travel around 44.7 million miles into space by the time you finished.

But even a unit like a light-minute isn’t particularly useful to astronomers. For them, cosmic measurements only start making sense on a scale of light-years. Voyager 1 is nowhere close to a light-year away from Earth, but a light-day (approximately 16,094,799,105 miles) is still basically impossible to envision as a distance. And since radio communications travel at nearly the speed of light, any messages to and from the spacecraft will require about 24 (Earth) hours to receive.

“Voyager 1 crossing the light-day threshold is yet another historic feat on an extraordinary voyage of discovery,” Dave Gallagher, the director of NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory, remarked in an emailed statement confirming the milestone. “Above all, this milestone stands as a testament to the exceptional teams who built, programmed, and fly these spacecraft today, and who have overcome countless challenges to keep them connected to Earth across the vastness of space. As NASA pushes toward new frontiers in human and robotic exploration, Voyager and the people behind its success continue to inspire us to reach farther and dream bigger.”.

Even at one light-day from Earth, Voyager 1 isn’t done just yet. While several of its instruments are no longer functioning, it still routinely sends back invaluable information from its vantage point beyond the solar system.

“Voyager 1 is telling us about our sun’s interaction with its galactic neighborhood. It has told us about the amount of galactic cosmic rays, unfiltered by the sun’s magnetic field,” notes Kurth. “We are learning about the variation of plasma density in the interstellar medium just outside the heliosphere (sun’s extended atmosphere). We’re anxious to see where the direction of the magnetic field rotates from one similar to the heliospheric direction to that of the pristine interstellar medium.”.

“Though it might be a long shot, I hope to see Voyager 1 crossing into the predicted hot hydrogen wall,” Adam Szabo, a Project Investigator on Voyager’s Magnetometer Experiment, tells Popular Science. “The wall is composed of neutral hydrogen atoms, so it will not register easily, but it must have some magnetic and plasma wave signature.”.

Voyager 1 introduced on September 5, 1977, heading for Jupiter and Saturn. Voyager 1 has 10 scientific instruments onboard, including two Golden Records engraved with sights, sounds, and depictions of life on Earth onboard—should the probe encounter any extraterrestrial life.

In March 1979, the Voyager 1 discovered a thin ring around Jupiter and two upcoming Jovian moons: Thebe and Metis. When it reached Saturn in 1980, the spacecraft spotted five new moons and a new ring called the G-ring.

In a recent development, as part of a concept that NASA called the Grand Tour, the Voyager mission as a whole took advantage of a once-every-175-year alignment of the outer planets for a grand tour of the solar system. Its twin spacecraft Voyager 2 later explored Neptune and Uranus.

Industry observers note that on February 17, 1998, Voyager 1 became the furthest human-made object in space, after overtaking NASA’s Pioneer 10 probe. It crossed into interstellar space in August 2012, and became the first spacecraft to cross the heliosphere. This boundary is where the influences from outside of our solar system are stronger than those from our sun.

All good things must come to an end, of course. Voyager 1 is powered by three radioisotope thermoelectric generators (RTGs) fueled by decaying plutonium-23. The isotope possesses a 87.74-year half-life, so the batteries are currently operating at around two-thirds their original strength. NASA has slowly shut down onboard equipment in recent years to conserve energy, but there will come a time when there simply isn’t enough left in the RTGs for anything to operate. Barring any technical issues, mission engineers expect to bid Voyager 1 a final farewell sometime during the 2030s.

The spacecraft may cease its work as a cosmic laboratory, but its very existence ensures it will remain a monument to humanity’s potential as it barrels forward for eons.

Industry observers note that “Voyager 1 and its companion Voyager 2 have taught us time and again that nature is not as simple and predictable as we thought,” Kurth explains. “Voyager has always provided surprising differences from our best guesses as to what it will see in any new region.”.

Additionally, Szabo adds that it has an important lesson for anyone on Earth who has ever had a dream.

“Do not be afraid to dream big. In 1977, even the Grand Tour seemed like a long shot,” he notes. “We did not even conceive of making it into interstellar space. Dream big.”.
