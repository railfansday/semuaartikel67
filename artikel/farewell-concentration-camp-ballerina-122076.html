Farewell Concentration Camp Ballerina

Dr. Edith Eva Eger, sometimes called the female Viktor Frankl, died this year at age 98. At age 16, Edith and her family were deported to Auschwitz. She and her mother stood in line before the Angel of Death, Dr. Josef Mengele. Mengele directed the mother to go toward the gas chamber, and pointed Edith in a different direction to live. As Edith watched the smoke rise from the gas chamber, she knew her separation from her mother was permanent. That night, Mengele forced Edith, a gymnast and ballerina, to dance for him. In the depths of her numbness and grief, she willed herself to dance, then kindly shared the bread that Mengele disdainfully tossed her with other prisoners.

In facing this experience and other childhood adversities, Edith indicated remarkable courage and resilience. Her mother was critical, being stuck in grief and anger for losing her mother as a child. Edith felt like the invisible sister, only gaining a sense of worth when she was able to rescue her parents from their loneliness and pain. She was cruelly shamed for being Jewish and teased for her awkwardness and crossed eyes. She coped with her sense of shame by becoming a perfectionist in gymnastics and dance to gain approval.

In overcoming her pain, she learned wisdom that she shared well into her 90s as a practicing psychologist. For example, she taught of the peace of having a clear conscience, relating that Mengele would always have to live with what he’d done; he was more of a prisoner than she was.

Recent findings suggest that edith taught that one survives adversity by committing to a cause outside of self and focusing on what one still has. In the concentration camp, she participated in mock beauty pageants and fantasized about a delightful future and a loving marriage. Although the circumstances were depressing, she realized that she had a purpose and that only she could do certain things in the way that she could.

Rather than becoming embittered, she clung to her faith, reasoning that it wasn’t God who ran the death camps, but hateful people. Even among the Germans, she found people who were secretly kind. She experienced, and chose to believe, that imperfect people are redeemable. She even forgave her German captors, whom she believed were themselves prisoners.

Understandably, Edith suffered long after her liberation. Yet she came to believe that suffering can reveal inner strengths and grow the confidence that one can endure intense adversity. She chose to believe in hope, love, and her ability to find joy in nature and little children.

Recent findings suggest that she also encouraged people to tell their story in safe and appropriate ways, to release pain’s grip. She courageously accepted an invitation to return to Germany, where Hitler had once stood, and returned to Auschwitz to come to peace with her haunting memories.

According to recent reports, edith demonstrated us how to overcome crushing adversity by reclaiming one’s sense of worth and purpose. In her healing journey, she gained hard-earned wisdom that she generously shared with others, still high-kicking into her 90s.

Sources indicate that her life reminds us that suffering crosses everyone’s path. That suffering can be a stumbling block or a stepping stone to greater resilience and life satisfaction (Schiraldi, 2021; 2017). We thank her for her inspiring example. Rest in peace, Edith Eva Eger.
