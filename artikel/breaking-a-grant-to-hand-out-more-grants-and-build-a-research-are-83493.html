According to recent reports, amid political instability and decreased funding for science worldwide, some non-profit organizations are testing whether and how microgrants — awards of US$10,000 or less — can spur innovation. The latest development comes from the Experiment Foundation, a non-profit science funding organization in Berkeley, California, which has introduced the Field Building Accelerator to cultivate ingenuity hubs around specific fields of research using small, rapidly deployed grants.

In a recent development, the initiative will, in early 2027, award at least 50 individuals — called field builders — $70,000 each. The recipients, in turn, will design their own microgrant programme to distribute up to $10,000 to researchers exploring distinct facets of the same niche scientific problem. The grants will be hosted on Experiment.com, an online platform that allows organizations to advertise and fund research projects.

“We want to create a different persona in science — the people who can chart newly introduced research territory,” says David Lang, executive director of the Experiment Foundation.

Industry observers note that the accelerator programme is open to proposals that broadly target oceans, climate, toxicity, future science and conservation technology, says Lang — reflecting the interests of its funders, including non-profit organizations such as the Gordon and Betty Moore Foundation in Palo Alto, California. Prospective field builders must propose a name for the field, define its core questions and challenges, and identify at least eight researchers, laboratories or communities they would consider funding.

Judges will select field builders who they think are best placed to identify overlooked talent, back unproven ideas and bring like-minded researchers together. Ideally, states Lang, they will reflect diverse scientific perspectives, including early-career researchers, science journalists, science-fiction authors and venture capitalists. “There are people that build momentum in scientific fields, but they are different from the ones who are getting substantial publications or spinning out companies,” he says.

And, in a bid to take chances on unconventional ideas, the judges will not average scores; instead, if any of the two or three judges champions an application, it moves forward.

In a recent development, although some might baulk at the idea that $10,000 grants are large enough to spur latest research disciplines, others say it is time to experiment with new ways to distribute research funding. “If we really want to help to seed challenging, high risk, ‘who knows what will happen’ ideas, then small-scale grants are a good strategy,” states Brian Nosek, executive director of the Center for Open Science, a non-profit organization in Charlottesville, Virginia, that promotes transparent and reproducible science.

Sources indicate that in 2025, The Science Foundation in Seattle, Washington, organized public donations to award up to $10,000 to researchers who have a compelling ‘what if’ basic-science proposal in the areas of mathematics, physical sciences and biological sciences. The foundation revealed the second round of winners on 12 November.
