Children at risk for depression get “stuck” on sad faces

Industry observers note that A smile or a frown may reveal more than emotion alone. The faces that capture a child's attention could provide vital clues about their mental health and how depression develops.

Latest research from Binghamton University, State University of New York, suggests that depression may influence how strongly children focus on emotional expressions such as happy or sad faces. The pattern appears to differ depending on whether a child has a family history of depression.

Researchers at the Mood Disorders Institute at Binghamton University study how depression develops in children and adolescents, with particular attention to how family history and emotional experiences may influence vulnerability over time. By identifying these patterns while children are still developing, scientists hope to better understand who may be at greater risk and eventually improve prevention strategies.

"Most of the vulnerabilities that we focus on are still developing during this time period," remarked Brandon Gibb, director of the Mood Disorders Institute and SUNY distinguished professor of psychology. "You can catch things as they're developing, rather than only studying them once they're already there and pretty stable.".

Following Children's Attention Over Time.

It has been revealed that earlier research has linked depression with greater attention to sad faces. However, those associations have generally been modest, and scientists have not been sure whether biased attention contributes to depression or develops because of it.

The Binghamton study was designed to investigate that question by tracking both depressive symptoms and attention patterns over time. According to the researchers, it is the first study to examine how changes in each may predict later changes in the other among children.

"The real novel piece is that we looked at these transactional relations," noted Kelly Gair, a PhD student at Binghamton and lead author of the paper. "Between attentional biases and depressive symptoms, we looked at the way that they were mutually predicting one another across the time points, which is especially novel and hasn't been done before.".

The research team, which also included Leslie A. Brick from the University of New Mexico, followed 242 children and their mothers for two years. Participants were assessed every six months.

Industry observers note that during each visit, children viewed pairs of faces on a screen. One face showed a neutral expression, while the other displayed an emotion (happy, sad, or angry). Eye tracking technology measured which face held the child's attention and for how long.

Family History Changes the Pattern.

The results suggest that increasing depressive symptoms do not affect every child's attention in the same way. Family history appeared to play an vital role.

Recent findings suggest that among children whose mothers had a history of major depressive disorder, rising depressive symptoms were associated with greater attention to sad faces. In other words, as these children experienced more symptoms themselves, their attention became increasingly drawn toward expressions of sadness.

"For those who are already at risk, the more these children experience depression themselves, the more they lose their ability to pull their attention away from the sad things around them," Gibb said.

In a recent development, "We know that when you're depressed, it changes what you pay attention to," Gair said. "Our results suggest that these changes may be more long-lasting and may differ depending on family history. One thought is that for children of mothers with depression, who are exposed to more facial displays of sadness from interactions with their mom, these types of facial expressions become even more salient when they experience depression themselves, so their attention becomes increasingly stuck on sad expressions.".

Happy Faces May Lose Their Pull.

A different pattern emerged among children whose mothers had no history of depression.

It has been revealed that when depressive symptoms increased in these children, they did not become more focused on sad expressions. Instead, they paid less attention to happy faces.

"In our lower-risk children, what seems to be happening is that experiences of depression are eroding a protective factor, which is how much they pay attention to happy faces," Gibb noted.

According to recent reports, the distinction suggests that depression may alter emotional attention through different pathways depending on a child's underlying level of familial risk. For some children, sadness may become increasingly difficult to ignore. For others, positive emotional signals may simply become less likely to capture their attention.

It has been revealed that the researchers are continuing to follow the children as they enter adolescence, a period when the risk of developing depression increases for many young people. They hope to determine whether these early attention patterns are associated with a greater likelihood of receiving a depression diagnosis later on.

The paper, "Transactional Relations Between Attentional Biases for Affective Stimuli and Depressive Symptoms in Offspring of Mothers With and Without Major Depressive Disorder," was published in the Journal of Psychopathology and Clinical Science.
