Recent findings suggest that few modern artists have proven as reliably sought after as Wassily Kandinsky, the Russian modernist rightfully deemed the father of abstract art, and few have rewritten their own auction record as often. The ascent charted here is steady rather than meteoric: records have tended to fall by razor-thin margins, sometimes by less than a million dollars, and in one case, at Sotheby’s London in 2017, the mark was broken twice in under an hour. Each new high nudged the ceiling higher, rather than shattering it outright.

That changed on Tuesday. At Christie’s Fresh York’s 20th century evening sale, the sale of Betonte Ecken (1922-23) sent Kandinsky record soaring to $85.8 million, good for nearly double his previous auction high.

Here is how the market got there.

Sources indicate that in Kandinsky’s view, painters were akin to prophets, and logically, the apocalypse would follow: a cataclysmic collapse of tangible reality, spiritual rapture, and figuration giving way to abstraction. Zum Thema Jüngstes Gericht (On the Theme of the Last Judgment), with its deluge of yellow unleashed upon the canvas, captured the religious fascinations that fueled his creative inquiries. The work is “bristling with the exciting energy of avant-garde abstraction,” our reporter wrote in November 2018, when it appeared at Sotheby’s Impressionist and Modern art evening sale (the same sale at which Improvisation on Mahogany set the record benchmark). It sold to an anonymous telephone bidder for $22.8 million, against an estimate of $22 million to $35 million.

Kandinsky’s Study for Improvisation 8 (1909), a cardboard and canvas work, was the highlight of Christie’s November 2012 Impressionist and Modern art auction in New York. The auction house expected it to fetch $20–$30 million, matching the $20.9 million record Sotheby’s obtained for Fugue (1914) in 1990. It was a safe bet: the work sold for $22.8 million.

It has been revealed that fully realized Improvisation studies rarely appear at auction; most of the finished pieces landed in museum collections. The final numbered work in the series, Improvisation 8 is a semi-abstraction set in a hilltop fortress. In it, a man wields a sword in Old Kiev, the medieval district of modern-day Ukraine, amid kneeling pilgrims—mystical spirituality-lite, relative to his later works. The piece was exhibited at Tate Modern, Kunstmuseum Basel, and Fondation Beyeler, but before it came to auction, it had mostly been on loan to the Kunstmuseum Winterthur in Switzerland, where it had hung since 1960.

It’s forgivable to forget the auction record set by Tiefes Braun, one masterwork among many to appear in “Visionary: the Paul G. Allen Collection”, the largest single-owner art auction in history. The sale generated more than $1.6 billion over its two-day run at Christie’s in November 2022 (The opening night alone brought in $1.5 billion.) For a refresher: Tiefes Braun went to an unnamed buyer for $23.2 million. That, along with its history, makes it a significant work, at least by Kandinsky standards.

In a recent development, kandinsky joined the faculty of the Bauhaus in 1922, finding it fertile ground for his experiments in expressive abstraction. Out of them grew Tiefes Braun, a psychological portrait of shape, color and space. According to Christie’s, Tiefes Braun was exhibited widely by Kandinsky through the mid-1920s, and formerly in the collection of the Guggenheim, which acquired it from the estate of the art dealer Karl Nierendorf. Allen, the late Microsoft co-founder, purchased it from the museum in 2000.

According to recent reports, it was no shock that Rigide et courbé broke the artist’s record when it appeared at Christie’s New York Impressionist and Modern sale in November 2016. The 1935 work was estimated to fetch between $18 million and $25 million, placing its $23.3 million finish squarely within expectation. The painting was acquired in 1936 by Solomon Guggenheim directly from Kandinsky and was last exhibited by a museum in 1949. It had been held by the same family since acquiring it at Sotheby’s in London in 1964.

It has been revealed that measuring at 45 by 63.5 inches, the painting “ranks alongside the biggest pictures of his last years,” Conor Jordan, the deputy chairman of Impressionist and Modern Art at Christie’s, said in 2016. Christie’s also said the picture marked Kandinsky’s first experiments with sand. “He may have been inspired by Georges Braque, who often used sand, particularly in paintings from the 1920s and 1930s,” Jordan added.

According to recent reports, improvisation auf Mahagoni (Improvisation on Mahogany) was completed while Kandinsky was putting the finishing touches on his treatise, On the Spiritual in Art, and the two make a perfect pairing. The treatise envisioned non-objective art as the soul’s portrait, compelled onto canvas by spiritual imperative. Improvisation auf Mahagoni is theory in practice: a tapestry of jewel tones applied in staccato brushstrokes, caught mid-pulse like a frozen metronome.

Recent findings suggest that it arrived at Sotheby’s Impressionist and Modern art evening sale in November 2018 alongside two other Kandinskys, Le Rond Rouge (1939) and Zum Thema Jüngstes Gericht (1913). Selling for $24.2 million against an estimate of $15 million to $20 million, it proved the most coveted of the three, falling a few million dollars short of the night’s top lots, a Magritte and a Schiele. The painting did, at least, spark “a round of appreciative applause in the York Avenue salesroom,” according to our reporter.

Given the significance of Kandinsky’s “Murnau” works within his artistic evolution, their appearance at auction is a notable event. When Murnau – Landschaft mit grünem Haus (1909) came up for auction at Sotheby’s London in June 2017, the auction house praised it as an elusive “masterpiece,” the work having remained in the same Swiss family collection since the 1920s. It arrived with an estimate of £15 million to £25 million and sold for £20.9 million ($26.5 million), surpassing the artist’s previous auction record of £13.5 million ($21 million) set in 2013.

But as noted below, top lot is a tenuous honor when the competition is the artist himself, and the same sale saw another record broken when Kandinsky’s 1913 Painting with White Lines came to the block six lots later. Both Kandinsky paintings helped push Sotheby’s evening total to £149 million ($187.7 million), a 45% jump from the previous year’s comparable sale, giving the London blue-chip art market a much-needed boost of optimism.

The two top lots on this list speak to the value of Kandinsky’s early 20th-century steps toward abstraction. Tensions calmées, painted decades later, presents a world of biomorphic forms that is far more fantastic. As Sotheby’s noted when offering the painting at its London sale in June, 2021, the work’s formal language is full of contradictions, as its title suggests. “The structural logic of the Bauhaus meets Surrealist fancies that, rather than fight, flow along his soundscape,“ the auction house wrote.

According to recent reports, painted in Paris in 1937, Tensions calmées was one of 50 Kandinsky works famously sold off by the Solomon R. Guggenheim Foundation in London in 1964—the first in the world devoted entirely to a single modern painter, according to Sotheby’s. (The New York art world, meanwhile, was outraged; a foundational patron of the Guggenheim described it as “a tragedy for the United States,” to the New York Times.).

Willard Gidwitz, of the Helene Curtis family, scooped up the piece, and it spent 57 years in the family’s private collection before his heirs consigned it to Sotheby’s. After brisk bidding, it left the block at £21.2 million (roughly $29.3 million), within its presale estimate range of $24.8 million to $34.4 million, but just enough to break a record.

In a recent development, on October 14, 2026, Kandinsky’s Das bunte Leben arrived on the block, heralded by Christie’s London auctioneer Adrien Meyer as “the most important early work by the artist to ever come to auction.” The panoramic procession of peasants, pilgrims, and folkloric beings teeters between figuration and abstraction. The characters are recognizably human but not of this world, rendered in an acidic palette that reads like a psychedelic trip.

According to recent reports, according to our reporting, bidding opened in a flurry, with around 20 competing claims in the opening salvo alone. The most determined bidders were Christie’s specialists Amelia Walker, Katharine Arnold, and Maria Los, with Los ultimately securing the painting on a £27 million ($35.7 million) hammer bid, clearing its high estimate of £25 million before fees. With fees, the final price came to £31.56 million ($41.5 million).

Industry observers note that originally acquired by the Dutch collector Emanuel Lewenstein in 1907, the painting disappeared during the Nazi occupation of the Netherlands in 1940. It reappeared on long-term loan at Munich’s Lenbachhaus from the Bayerische Landesbank. In 2023, it was restituted to the heirs of Hedwig Lewenstein-Weyermann, amid a push from the German government to reunite Nazi-looted art with its rightful owners and following decades of legal action.

Sotheby’s June 2017 Impressionist and Modern art sale in London was one to remember thanks entirely to Kandinsky: the artist’s record was broken twice in less than an hour. First, Kandinsky’s Fauvist-era landscape of Murnau from 1909 went to a phone bidder for £21 million ($26 million). Its reign was short-lived. Six lots later, Painting with White Lines came to the block with a guarantee in excess of $35 million (£27 million) from, if rumors are to be believed, mega-collector Ronald Lauder. A rarely available example of Kandinsky’s foray into abstraction, the picture had left Russia for the first time in 1974, when the Cologne collector William Hack acquired it from the State Tretyakov Gallery in Moscow. His heirs brought it to Sotheby’s.

Four bidders battled for the work, which was ultimately won by an Asian phone bidder for £33 million ($41.6 million) “to thunderous applause—especially from Sotheby’s staff,” according to Artnet News.

Kandinsky’s Murnau mit Kirche II (Murnau with Church II) (1910) depicts the Bavarian village of Murnau, nestled in the foothills of the Alps, in vibrant brushstrokes that experts say heralded Kandinsky’s turn toward abstract art. At Sotheby’s London in March 2023, it set the artist’s auction current auction record of $44.7 million.

Ahead of the painting’s sale, Helena Newman, chair of Sotheby’s Europe, remarked that “Kandinsky’s Murnau period came to define abstract art for future generations, and the appearance of such an important painting—one of the last of this period and scale remaining in private hands—is a major moment for the market and for collectors.” Lucian Simmons, Sotheby’s vice chairman and head of restitution, added that the timing of the sale carried particular weight: 2023 marked the 25th anniversary of the conference that established the architecture for the restitution of artworks looted by the Nazis.

According to recent reports, according to Sotheby’s, the painting hung in the dining room of Johanna Margarete Stern-Lippmann and Siegbert Stern, founders of a textile company and collectors whose vast collection included works by Renoir and Munch, among other masters. After the Nazis rose to power, Stern died, Stern-Lippmann died in Auschwitz, and the collection was looted. In the early 2010s, the painting was discovered at the Van Abbemuseum in Eindhoven, Netherlands, where it had been since 1951. A long legal battle ensued, before it was restituted to the 13 descendants of the Stern family in 2022.

“Though nothing can undo the wrongs of the past, nor the impact on our family and those who were in hiding—one of whom is still alive—the restitution of this painting that meant so much to our great-grandparents is immensely significant to us, because it is an acknowledgement and partially closes a wound that has remained open over the generations,” the family said.

Betonte Ecken (1922–23) measures just over four feet square and is marked by dynamic, abstract geometric shapes; the four corners are each pointed to with strong shapes and lines, in keeping with its title, which translates as Accentuated Corners.

Recent findings suggest that the painting has changed hands a number of times. Christie’s provenance shows it first coming to auction at the Jacob Hecht Auction House in Berlin in 1926, from the Schwarz collection, then going to that city’s Galerie Nierendorf, which then sold it, probably in 1929, to Hilla Rebay. She was then the principal adviser to museum founder Solomon R. Guggenheim, an early supporter of abstract art in the US. Guggenheim later acquired it from Rebay, and it remained in the museum’s collection until 1964, when the museum sold it at Sotheby’s London to Upcoming York art dealer Stephen Hahn. At some point, Hahn sold it to Boston collectors Sandra and David Bakalar, who put it up for auction at Sotheby’s in 1986, when it went to the Nahmads.

On November 17, the painting changed hands again, when it sold at Christie’s 20th-century evening art sale. It hammered for $74 million; with the house’s fees, the final sale amount was $85.8 million. The work sold after a short bidding war between Christie’s global president Alex Rotter, Sara Friedlander, chairman of post-war & contemporary art, Americas at Christie’s, and art advisor Ralph De Luca. Rotter came away the victor, with the winning $74 million bid. —Brian Boucher.
