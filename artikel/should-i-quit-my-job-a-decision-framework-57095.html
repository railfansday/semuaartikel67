The worst time to make a career decision is when you are emotionally overwhelmed. A terrible meeting, a passed-over promotion, or a toxic interaction with a coworker can make quitting feel urgent when the situation may actually be temporary or fixable.

Industry observers note that use the 30-day test: if you have wanted to quit for fewer than 30 consecutive days, it is likely a temporary frustration. If the feeling has persisted — or intensified — for 30+ days, it is a pattern worth taking seriously.

In a recent development, how you leave a job matters as much as why you leave. Your professional reputation follows you for your entire career, and the people you work with today may be the hiring managers, clients, or references of tomorrow.

Two weeks notice: This is the minimum professional standard in the United States. For senior roles, three to four weeks is more appropriate. Check your employment contract — some roles require 30, 60, or 90 days notice.

The resignation conversation: Schedule a private meeting with your direct manager. Keep it brief and positive. Say: "I have decided to move on to a new opportunity. I want to thank you for [specific positive thing], and I am committed to making the transition as smooth as possible. My last day would be [date].".

The transition blueprint: Document your ongoing projects, key contacts, passwords, and processes. Offer to train your replacement. Complete as much outstanding work as possible. A strong exit creates goodwill that pays dividends for years.

What not to do: Do not badmouth the company, your boss, or your coworkers — not in the resignation meeting, not in your exit interview, not on social media. Even if your grievances are legitimate, airing them publicly hurts your reputation more than theirs.

According to recent reports, conventional advice says never quit without another job lined up. The FI community takes a more nuanced view: it depends on your financial position and the severity of the situation.

Recent findings suggest that if you are leaving due to company changes — layoffs, restructuring, or role elimination — negotiate your severance package. Many employees accept the first offer without realizing severance is negotiable. Key leverage points include your tenure, difficulty of replacement, willingness to sign a non-compete or non-disparagement clause, and any outstanding commissions or bonuses.

Common severance components to negotiate: extended pay (push for 2-4 weeks per year of tenure), continued health insurance coverage, outplacement services, vesting acceleration for equity, and a positive reference commitment.
