Instagram posts may reveal subtle clues about loneliness

Instagram habits may reveal loneliness in college students but cannot reliably show their support networks, according to a new study.

Instagram users carefully choose what they share, but their posts may still reveal signs of loneliness.

A new study of U.S. college students found that posting habits, captions, and photos offered clues about how lonely students felt.

Those same accounts revealed far less about how many friends or relatives students could turn to for support.

The work was led by Sherry (Yueyi) Jiang with psychology professor Piotr Winkielman at the University of California San Diego. Jiang completed a doctorate in experimental psychology there in 2025.

All of the students attended that one university, and most of them were women. The results describe patterns across a group. They are not presented by the authors as a way to screen any single person.

Loneliness is difficult for other people to see. The authors also described it as a serious risk factor for early death. The effects are comparable in size to those of smoking and obesity, they wrote.

Each student completed questionnaires, then downloaded a full archive of their Instagram account and uploaded it to a secure server.

Industry observers note that in total, 541 students took part, and 415 of them were kept in the analysis. A total of 93 students were excluded by the team because posts, likes, or comments were missing from their archives.

A further 33 students were removed since their questionnaires had been left unfinished.

Only part of an account can be seen on a public profile. About 62% of the students had private accounts.

Posts, likes, and comments that remain invisible to strangers on the app could therefore be studied by the researchers.

The archives contained 9,353 captions and 353,407 comments, together with the photos and the time of every post.

Faces in the photos were detected by one program, and the expression and head tilt of each face were estimated.

According to recent reports, the tone of captions and comments was scored by other programs. Words were also sorted by these programs into themes, such as family or the past.

The share of each student’s posts made late at night, defined as 11 p.m. to 5 a.m., was also calculated.

Computer models were subsequently trained by the team to identify the lonelier students from those Instagram measures alone.

Data from 80% of the students were used to build the models, which were tested on the remaining 20%.

From the questionnaires, five scores were obtained for every student, and four of these reflected how lonely the student felt.

Industry observers note that the fifth score addressed a separate matter. It reflected how many relatives and friends a student contacted regularly and could ask for help.

The main loneliness questionnaire included 20 statements, among them “I feel isolated from others.”.

According to recent reports, model performance was judged on a scale where 0.5 equals a coin flip and 1 is perfect. A value of 0.77 was reached by the best model for this main questionnaire.

According to recent reports, for each of two more specific loneliness scores, the models achieved 0.74, while a single-question score was predicted at 0.68. The first three results were described by the authors as moderate, and the last one as modest.

Weaker performance was recorded for the fifth score, which concerned support.

The best model reached 0.61, with an uncertainty range of 0.42 to 0.79. Because that range included 0.5, the result did not clearly establish performance better than chance.

Two kinds of loneliness have been separated by researchers since the 1970s.

Industry observers note that emotional loneliness is the distress of having no one close. Social loneliness is dissatisfaction with a person’s wider social circle.

On Instagram, students with higher social loneliness scores tended to be less active with other people.

These students liked fewer posts. They also commented less often and posted less on weekends.

In a recent development, A different pattern was seen in students with higher emotional loneliness scores. More of their posts went up late at night.

Their captions were more negative in tone, and sad or angry facial expressions were more common in their photos.

Industry observers note that the emotionally lonely students also posted more often, not less. Frequent posting, the authors suggested, might be an effort to find emotional connection when close ties are missing.

Word choice was examined as well. Students who wrote “we” more often tended to score lower on emotional loneliness. Those who wrote “I” more often tended to score higher on social loneliness.

“Loneliness isn’t a one-size-fits-all experience, and neither are the solutions,” Winkielman said.

Two ideas were proposed by the authors for future testing. Social loneliness might be eased by more chances to engage with others.

For emotional loneliness, help with low mood and disrupted daily routines was suggested instead.

Lonely people are often depressed or anxious as well, and the same was true of these students.

It has been revealed that the team therefore checked each Instagram sign again. This time, each student’s depression and anxiety scores were taken into account.

Only one sign was still linked to loneliness: fewer faces per post. Students whose photos contained fewer faces were more likely to score high on two of the loneliness measures.

In a recent development, according to the authors, fewer moments shared with other people may be reflected in photos that contain fewer faces. The same link between loneliness and the number of faces had been reported in an earlier study of Instagram users.

It has been revealed that past-focused words in captions were tied to two of the loneliness measures. Profile photos with the head tilted upward were tied to three.

That tilt is probably no accident, Jiang’s team wrote, given how carefully people pick their photo angles. But once depression and anxiety scores entered the analysis, neither sign was still tied to loneliness.

Nobody in the study was followed over time. Each of the 415 students filled out the questionnaires once and handed over an archive.

So the order is unknown: the Instagram habits may have come first, or the loneliness, or both.

According to recent reports, the group itself was narrow. About 85% of the students were 21 or younger and about 79% were women.

Asian students (43.9%) and White students (38.8%) made up the two largest groups.

Age and culture change how loneliness is felt, the authors wrote, and they change posting habits on social media too.

Patterns found among San Diego undergraduates need to be tested in other populations.

There is also the question of misuse, which the authors raised themselves.

Flag the wrong person, and the result may be stigma or outreach that was never wanted. Miss the right person, and support does not arrive.

For that reason, the team limited its suggestion to consent-based research and to support services that people opt into.

Covert screening built only on these signs was advised against.

Two kinds of follow-up were proposed. One would measure the same people’s loneliness repeatedly while their Instagram activity is collected.

The other would briefly change how lonely people feel in an experiment, then record what they do online afterward. Cause is easier to isolate that way, according to the authors.

The full study was published in the journal npj Mental Health Research.
