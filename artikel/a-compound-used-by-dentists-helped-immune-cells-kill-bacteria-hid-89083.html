A compound used by dentists helped immune cells kill bacteria hiding inside them

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In lab tests, K21, a compound used in a dental cleanser, helped some human immune cells kill bacteria that can survive inside them.

White blood cells protect the body by swallowing and breaking down bacteria. But some bacteria survive inside these cells, making them harder to get rid of.

A new study found that K21, a chemical used in dental cleaning products, helped certain human immune cells kill more bacteria in laboratory tests.

Interestingly, the cells continued to kill more bacteria even after researchers removed K21, suggesting that the chemical had changed how the cells worked.

The researchers also found that K21 increased the breakdown and recycling of mitochondria, the tiny structures that provide energy to cells.

When they tested the chemical on small worms, the treated worms lived longer than those that received no treatment.

Christopher Rongo of the Waksman Institute of Microbiology at Rutgers University led the research.

Bhupesh Prusty of Riga Stradiņš University was a co-corresponding author, with other researchers working in Germany, Latvia, the UK and the United States.

It has been revealed that the findings are interesting, but they come from cells grown in the laboratory and experiments with worms. Nobody has yet shown that K21 can treat an infection or help people live longer.

The cells in question are macrophages. Swallowing germs, digesting them and keeping infections in check is their job.

They don’t all work the same way, though. Some are built for fighting infection, others for repairing damaged tissue.

Could K21 change how either type behaves? That was the question.

Sources indicate that blood came from healthy donors. Over nine days the team grew those cells into the two types of macrophages, and a small dose of K21, 0.28 micromoles per liter, sat in some of the dishes the whole time.

Enterococcus faecalis went in next. It lives harmlessly in the intestines, and it turns up in hospital infections.

It has been revealed that that species was picked for one trait. It can survive inside a macrophage rather than being destroyed.

The bacteria stayed with the cells for two hours. Gentamicin, an antibiotic, then killed off whatever remained outside them.

Bacterial DNA inside the cells was measured first. Treated and untreated macrophages held much the same amount.

So K21 wasn’t simply making the cells swallow more bacteria.

The macrophages were broken open next, to count the bacteria still alive inside.

Sources indicate that whatever spilled out went onto a gel. Living germs grow into visible colonies there, which makes counting possible.

The infection-fighting macrophages needed no help. Almost nothing survived in them, treated or not.

Industry observers note that but the macrophages involved in tissue repair told a different story. Untreated repair cells let more bacteria live. Where K21 had been present earlier, far fewer came through.

Rongo mentioned the treated cells were “much more robust at killing and clearing that bacteria.”.

That was the first real clue. Whatever K21 did to those cells outlasted the chemical itself.

There are standard ways to check whether an immune cell has changed.

It has been revealed that one is the chemical messages cells send each other. Another is the set of proteins on their surface, which marks what kind of cell it is.

The team checked both, expecting to see some obvious differences.

Most of it came back unchanged. Of the two messages measured, one moved: repair-focused macrophages put out less of a substance that normally calms inflammation.

The surface proteins barely changed either.

It has been revealed that so the repair cells had not simply been turned into the bacteria-killing type. Whatever K21 did was subtler than that.

To find what they were missing, the team read gene activity inside thousands of individual cells, one at a time.

This time, differences appeared.

Two groups with stronger inflammation-related activity grew more common under K21. One group of repair-focused macrophages thinned out.

Three genes caught the team’s attention: IDO1, IDO2 and KYNU.

All three help break down tryptophan, a substance we get from food. Serotonin is built from the same amino acid.

The connection was worth exploring, although the study did not show that K21 changed serotonin levels.

Industry observers note that measuring serotonin in animals given K21 is what the researchers want next. That would show whether the gene activity means anything inside a living body.

The team also noticed something interesting about mitochondria.

They produce a cell’s energy. Like anything else in there, they wear out and need replacing.

Cells handle that themselves. They break mitochondria into smaller pieces and recycle the damaged ones, through a process called mitophagy.

The researchers wondered whether K21 was affecting this cleanup system.

Macrophage mitochondria are hard to examine closely, so two other types of human cell took the imaging.

In both, K21 left the mitochondria more broken up. Signs of recycling grew stronger too.

Other chemicals push cells into recycling their mitochondria. K21 was not working the same way.

Industry observers note that it increased a protein called Drp1, which helps mitochondria split into smaller pieces. Similar activity turned up in the macrophages, though only in the cells associated with tissue repair.

That was especially interesting because these were the same cells that had become better at killing bacteria.

Could changing how mitochondria are maintained help explain the stronger response to bacteria? The results point in that direction, but they don’t yet prove how the two changes are connected.

In a recent development, rongo’s laboratory already works on mitochondria and on how animals cope when oxygen runs low. That gave the researchers another way to investigate K21.

They turned to Caenorhabditis elegans, tiny, transparent worms that scientists often use to study basic processes inside living cells.

The team added K21 to the worms’ food and watched what happened.

Sources indicate that the worms did what the human cells had done. More breaking down and recycling of mitochondria, and the mitochondria themselves came out shorter and rounder.

Worms missing certain genes for that process were tested too. In those animals nothing changed.

That suggested K21 depended on the worms’ normal machinery for dividing and recycling mitochondria.

The researchers then asked a different question. Would any of this affect how long the worms lived?

They followed 151 worms given K21 and compared them with 128 untreated worms across three experiments.

In a recent development, the difference was clear. Worms that received K21 lived significantly longer.

But there was something the team needed to rule out first.

It has been revealed that A worm on short rations lives longer, which has been known for years. Reduced feeding would have been the dull explanation.

Sources indicate that the researchers checked, and the opposite happened. Worms given K21 actually fed a little faster.

The chemical also did not appear to affect their growth, body size, reproduction or ability to survive through development.

Recent findings suggest that there were many changes happening inside their cells, though. K21 increased the activity of 1,848 genes and reduced the activity of another 1,312.

Sources indicate that even so, Rongo warned against reading too much into the lifespan result. Helping a tiny worm live longer is very different from helping a person live longer.

There are still some important gaps in the research.

The immune cell changes demonstrated up only in cultures holding a mixture of cell types. Take the mixture away and the effect went with it.

Much of the detailed work on mitochondria also took place in cells that weren’t macrophages.

The researchers used HUVEC cells, which come from the lining of blood vessels, and U2-OS cells, which come from a bone cancer cell line.

In a recent development, the bacterial experiments were limited too. The team tested one bacterial species, using one K21 pretreatment schedule, with three replicates.

They did not give K21 to an infected animal or test whether it helped a human wound heal.

There was a reason wound healing had attracted their attention in the first place. Earlier research by Gulve and colleagues, beginning in 2016, had reported faster wound healing in animals.

Those findings helped lead the team toward the immune cells examined in this study.

The worms bring another problem when thinking about human health. They don’t have macrophages at all, and roughly a billion years of evolution separate them from people.

So the worm experiments can tell scientists something about basic cell processes. They cannot tell us whether K21 would have the same benefits in humans.

The researchers also disclosed where their funding came from.

Part of the work was supported by the National Institutes of Health. The remaining support came from FiteBac, the company that markets K21.

FiteBac also supplied the material used in the worm experiments.

Rongo has no ownership stake in the company and no patent on K21, although he received a pilot grant from FiteBac. Prusty received a research grant.

Industry observers note that these details matter because the manufacturer has a commercial interest in the compound. Independent studies would help establish whether the findings can be repeated.

Even after all these experiments, the team still doesn’t know exactly what K21 attaches to inside a cell.

Its chemical structure offers one possible explanation.

In a recent development, K21 has four fatty acid chains attached to a central structure known as quaternary ammonium silane. Together, these give the compound properties that might allow it to collect in the fatty membranes surrounding mitochondria.

The researchers suspect that could help explain what they observed.

But they have not identified a specific molecule that K21 binds to, so the idea remains unproven.

Recent findings suggest that the researchers now want to test K21 in living animals. They plan to measure serotonin levels to see whether the gene changes found in cells also matter in the body.

They could also give K21 to infected animals and check how many bacteria survive. This would help show whether the compound can fight infections outside the lab.

So far, K21 has helped human immune cells kill more bacteria and made tiny worms live longer. Whether it can benefit people remains unknown.

The study is published in the journal Life Science Alliance.
