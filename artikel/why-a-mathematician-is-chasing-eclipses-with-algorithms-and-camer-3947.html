Sources indicate that advanced image-processing software developed by Miloslav Druckmüller combines scores of individual images, captured during a total solar eclipse and painstakingly calibrated. The techniques reveal fine details in the sun’s corona, which is white in this image. The tiny pink shapes seen along portions of the edge of the blacked-out solar disk are prominences, which are extensions of relatively cool plasma that can jut out hundreds of thousands of kilometers into space.

In a recent development, the sun hangs low over the plains of Castile and León, in the north-central region of Spain. But from a slope of the mountain Pico Trigaza, it appears that a few puffy white clouds have started to block the sun. That’s vexing.

A group of researchers, some wearing cardboard eclipse glasses, is clustered around sets of portable telescopes and cameras connected to laptops. All this gear is scattered on tabletops perched on the mountainside. On the computer screens, the sun’s disk looks like a deep orange crescent that is slowly waning. That’s because the moon began to crawl across it about half an hour ago. In 25 minutes, it will obscure the sun completely, producing an eerie and wondrous phenomenon—a total solar eclipse.

In a recent development, in the late-summer, late-afternoon heat, the researchers watch anxiously as those fluffy clouds linger around the sun. From an abandoned hut on the slope a few meters above, where the researchers have set up camp, one of the grad students emerges and nods toward the cloud formation. “Do you think it will dissolve?” he blurts out.

Industry observers note that “It will.” Miloslav Druckmüller glances calmly at his group of doctoral candidates, now making final checks of the equipment in preparation for totality. Here in Spain, on this 12th day of August, Druckmüller is getting ready for his 19th solar eclipse. He knows from long experience that weather can turn rapidly when the moon’s shadow suddenly pushes the temperature down. Plus, there’s no sense in riling up the grad students any more than they already are.

Recent findings suggest that miloslav Druckmüller led the design and construction of a unique optical instrument that incorporates eight astrocameras. Each is equipped with a spectral filter for sensing and recording specific emission spectra from the solar corona. Each of those spectra, in turn, is associated with a particular ionic species of iron atoms. Because the temperature needed to ionize iron varies depending on the ionic species, analyzing the locations and relative abundance of these ionic species offers valuable insights into the distribution of heat in the corona.Václav Široký.

During totality, the moon blocks the overwhelming glare of the solar disk, revealing delicate structures in the sun’s outer atmosphere, the corona. Using custom-built telescope systems and image-processing techniques that Druckmüller has spent decades developing, the researchers aim to gain insights into the processes in this enigmatic region that is otherwise hard to study. In addition to imaging the visible coronal light, the team will try to track the dispersal of superhot ions of iron into interplanetary space, which could give rare insights into the pattern of heat emanating from the corona.

And they’ll have 107 seconds to do all that.

Druckmüller is a professor of mathematics and image processing at the Brno University of Technology in the Czech Republic and a leading figure in the Solar Wind Sherpas, an international team that studies the sun’s corona during total eclipses. In the solar eclipse world, he is nothing short of a legend, albeit a reluctant one.

An image from Miloslav Druckmüller’s first solar-eclipse expedition, to Hungary, vividly shows Baily’s Beads. These elongated white lights along a piece of the left edge of the blacked-out solar disk are caused by beams of sunlight shining through gaps in the irregular lunar surface during the eclipse. The pink shapes are solar prominences, which are jets of plasma extending out into space and held in place by the sun’s intense magnetic field.

Industry observers note that he photographed his first solar eclipse in 1999, in what he thought would be a one-off lark. During that eclipse, the moon’s deep shadow missed Druckmüller’s native Czechia by just a few tens of kilometers, so he chased it to Hungary with a 30-year-old Soviet MTO 1,000-millimeter lens. All he remembers from that day is the rush of adrenaline he felt while trying to find the sun in the sky with the lens’s minuscule field of view and keep it in focus for the two minutes of the eclipse.

He insists that he has never enjoyed any of the eclipses he has photographed. “In fact, I have never properly seen any of those eclipses. You are just watching the screens, making sure that it’s all doing what it’s supposed to do. There are so many things that can go wrong and you can’t redo it.”.

A composite sequence of photographs demonstrates the 12 August 2026 solar eclipse as seen from a slope of the mountain Pico Trigaza in Spain.

Václav Široký and Miloslav Druckmüller.

Despite Druckmüller’s outward tranquility on this Spanish hillside, the stakes are high. Just 45 minutes ago, his group heard from their colleagues in Iceland, where viewing conditions had promised to be better than in Spain. But a vast low-pressure system had clouded the entire sky, leaving all three Iceland teams without a view.

On the Pico Trigaza mountain in Spain, two postgraduate researchers used a laser to get a rough, initial alignment for the tracking mount of an optical instrument. The alignment was necessary to precisely compensate for the Earth’s motion.

According to recent reports, the corona has been a mystery for centuries. Though dimmer—by a factor of a million—than the sun’s visible surface, the corona is nevertheless extremely hot: about a million degrees Celsius compared to 5,500 degrees on the surface. Because the sun’s nuclear fusion engine churns at the star’s heart, it might seem that temperatures should fall farther from the core.

To observe the faint corona in visible light, researchers must block the sun’s ferocious glare. Specialized telescopes called coronagraphs do this with an opaque disk called an occulter. But because light bends around the occulter’s edges in an effect called diffraction, it must block an area much larger than that of the solar disk to prevent light from spilling over and ruining the images. But doing so means obstructing the inner part of the corona, which harbors the most key processes that underpin the puzzling phenomena of the sun’s upper atmosphere.

Industry observers note that that’s why total eclipses matter so much to solar physicists. By sheer chance, the moon is exactly the right size and at just the right distance to completely cover the sun during a total solar eclipse. Because the moon is so far away from Earth, the view is not marred by diffraction, and the corona reveals itself nearly all the way down to the sun’s surface.

As astrocameras recorded spectral data about the sun’s corona, a laptop computer [on table, at left] displayed the phase of the eclipse.

Space probes can image the solar surface in other wavelengths, but those views have limitations, too. Spacecraft such as NASA’s Solar Dynamics Observatory do see the innermost parts of the corona in extreme ultraviolet light, explains Shadia Habbal, a solar physicist at the University of Hawaii and leader of the Solar Wind Sherpas project. “But it cuts off very quickly, so you don’t see its extension into space as well as we can see it in the visible emission lines” during an eclipse. “That’s the biggest advantage,” she says, of solar eclipse photography.

The European Proba-3 mission, introduced in 2024, simulates total solar eclipses by flying two spacecraft in a formation 150 meters apart with one acting as an occulter and the other as the telescope. The instruments aboard that satellite, however, have limitations compared to the equipment used by the Solar Wind Sherpas team.

When Druckmüller developed the film from his 1999 eclipse expedition, the photographs didn’t do justice to the event’s eerie beauty. He spent countless evenings applying his expertise in numerical image processing in an attempt to re-create in his photographs the sights he witnessed in that Hungarian corn field.

It took two years, but eventually he succeeded. Where before there were dull views of the blackened lunar disk surrounded by the blunt wisps of the corona, Druckmüller revealed the subtlest details of the magnetic field lines stretching from the sun far into space. As his photographs circulated among solar physicists, invitations to eclipse expeditions began to arrive.

Solar researchers Miloslav Druckmüller and Shadia Habbal have been collaborating for two decades and 15 eclipses.

Industry observers note that habbal learned about the images from a Ph.D. student who had seen them at a conference presentation that didn’t credit Druckmüller. When Habbal’s team later published the images in a paper, they misattributed them to another researcher. Druckmüller found out through an astrophysicist friend. “I wrote her a rather disappointed email and she responded within 10 minutes,” Druckmüller recalls. “She really didn’t have a clue.”.

According to recent reports, the error was corrected, and within weeks, Habbal and Druckmüller began a collaboration that grew into a friendship. “Milos’s processing brings out details that nobody has seen before, and they tell you a lot about very tiny features in the corona that you don’t see otherwise,” says Habbal.

Capturing the corona’s delicate structures means finding a way to show its brightest and faintest features in the same image. “During the solar eclipse, the contrast between the darkest and brightest parts of the corona is enormous, possibly one to a million,” Druckmüller explains. “But a computer monitor only allows you to display a contrast ratio of one to about 800.” (Some modern monitors, particularly OLED ones, can deliver higher contrast ratios.).

Industry observers note that the human eye can adjust to this enormous range of brightness, allowing an observer to see both the bright corona and the faint stars and planets that briefly emerge in the darkened sky. To bring those details together in a photograph, Druckmüller uses custom image-processing algorithms to combine exposures that capture different parts of that range.

Researchers Martin Šebek and Peter Urgoš make fine adjustments to the focus and alignment of the astrocameras on a key optical instrument before the 12 August solar eclipse.

It has been revealed that to create a single image, Druckmüller’s teams need to take 200 individual frames during the eclipse at exposure times ranging from 0.001 second to two seconds. The data must then be calibrated against a vast number of reference images to filter out noise and imperfections. Flat fields, which depict a uniform light background, help correct for defects caused by telescope optics, dust spots, and variations in pixel sensitivity. Dark frames, taken with light blocked from the sensor, help correct for artifacts created by the camera’s image sensor itself. “It takes the computer more than two days to process one image,” Druckmüller states. Including the calibration data, a single finished image requires about 3,000 input frames.

It has been revealed that since 2008, teams directed by Habbal and Druckmüller have traveled to every total solar eclipse, spreading out across multiple locations to hedge against bad weather. About half of the teams return with usable data. For the others, it’s just a short and stressful voyage. For the 12 August eclipse, three groups deployed to Iceland and two to Spain.

The teams seek sites close to the center of the band of totality, where the moon blocks the sun for longest. In Spain, the 12 August band of totality cut across the north of the country; the center line of the shadow passed within 11 kilometers of Pico Trigaza, promising Druckmüller and his colleagues a precious 107 seconds of totality.

Industry observers note that but duration is only part of the calculation. Totality on Pico Trigaza will commence 51 minutes before sunset, with the sun just 8 degrees above the horizon. That low, the rays must pass through a much thicker layer of the atmosphere than when the sun is higher in the sky, increasing the light distortion that complicates photography. At the researchers’ camp on Pico Trigaza, 1,600 meters above sea level, the thinner air should help a bit.

An abandoned hut on the mountain Pico Trigaza in Spain was spruced up so that it could serve as a home base for the research team recording the 12 August solar eclipse.

“Iceland would have been a much better place for us than Spain because the sun was higher up, some 25 degrees above the horizon,” Druckmüller states wistfully. But Iceland’s notoriously unpredictable weather made Spain an essential backup.

The sleeping arrangements for the grad students at the Pico Trigaza hut [bottom] were spartan, but the views were first-rate.Top: Martin Šebek; Bottom: Václav Široký.

In a recent development, on the mountain, locals have spruced up a rustic, long-unused hut for the researchers, running power cables from a nearby telecommunication outpost and bringing in a water tank. Five Ph.D. students drove more than 2,000 kilometers from Brno and have spent nearly a week preparing the equipment. Druckmüller and his wife joined them two days before the eclipse. They cook on a small propane grill and sleep in sleeping bags on yoga mats. “We have plenty of space here to set up our telescopes, beautiful views, and a roof over our heads,” Druckmüller indicates with a grin. “It’s perfect.”.

Industry observers note that but even the best equipment and a carefully chosen site count for nothing if the clouds roll in. “So much work goes into the preparations and then, if you end up with nothing, you just feel terrible sadness,” indicates Jana Hoderová, an assistant professor of mathematics at Brno University of Technology and one of Druckmüller’s key collaborators.

According to recent reports, as the moon continues to slide in front of the solar disk, the pesky cumulus cloud that kept everybody on edge begins to dissolve. It’s 10 minutes to totality. The relief is palpable.

Sources indicate that the countdown on the computers that control the cameras has begun. The screens are now showing only a tiny orange sliver, and the evening is becoming perceptibly less bright.

Recent findings suggest that twenty astrophotography cameras, most of them drawing power from a car battery that was also buffering the cameras from any grid instabilities, are pointed at the sun. Alongside equipment capturing the corona’s visible light, the team has built an assembly of eight cameras to detect light emitted by various iron ions that arise in the corona.

It has been revealed that among the optical gear assembled at Pico Trigaza to capture the 12 August solar eclipse was a unique instrument designed to record the distributions of iron ions in the corona [at left]. Václav Široký.

Industry observers note that those instruments looking for iron ions have been put together with resourcefulness and thrift. A colleague of Druckmüller’s laser-cut the aluminum plates holding the carefully aligned lenses in exchange “for two bottles of whisky.” The brackets and attachments were 3D-printed at Brno University of Technology. The lenses, produced for the defunct camera maker Contax, were bought at steep discounts on eBay. By far the most expensive elements are interference filters that isolate the wavelengths emitted by the iron ions.

Sources indicate that “That’s something nobody else in the world can do,” Druckmüller says with visible satisfaction. The unique technology, codeveloped with Habbal’s Hawaii team, has already enabled researchers to map the distribution of temperatures across the entire corona and to discover previously unseen structures, dubbed “smoke rings,” in the coronal plasma. The photographs also revealed the boundary region where the solar plasma becomes so thin that its ions stop colliding.

It has been revealed that the optical assembly designed to record the distributions of iron ions in the corona is outfitted with astrocameras, each of which captures spectra from a different ionic species. To do that, each astrocamera is equipped with a spectral filter whose bandwidth is so narrow—one nanometer—that the filter appears as a mirror.Martin Fornůsek.

“Iron is the most abundant of the heavy elements that we see in the solar corona,” indicates Habbal. “And because the corona is so hot, the iron atoms lose electrons and get ionized. The larger the loss, the higher the temperature.”.

Sources indicate that for example, a temperature of around 1.6 million °C is necessary to produce an iron ion that has lost 12 electrons. But only 1.4 million °C is needed to strip an iron atom of 11 electrons. Once it’s in a particular electrical state, the ion retains its specific ionization, even as it travels outward with the solar wind. Each of those ions, whether +11 or +12, emits a different wavelength of light, allowing the researchers to map not just temperatures within the corona, but also how those iron ions spread into interplanetary space and distribute heat. Habbal and her colleagues had long been trying to image those iron ions during solar eclipses. But it wasn’t until Druckmüller entered the picture that they were able to develop the gear and the mathematical techniques to do it.

Recent findings suggest that with just a minute to go to totality, the researchers begin removing protective filters from the lenses. It’s 50 minutes to sunset, and the waning sun has just begun to light up the clouds above the horizon with the first tinge of orange. Then, suddenly, the evening begins a rapid and premature descent into darkness. Along the edge of the fantastically thin solar crescent, the brilliant orange points known as Baily’s Beads flash and disappear—the last fragments of photospheric light visible through deep lunar valleys. As totality begins, observers remove their glasses. The hot air cools. The graduate students remain quietly and intently focused on their gear.

Sources indicate that druckmüller looks up from his camera toward the darkened plains. Around the blackened solar disk rises the magnificent golden halo of the corona. In the hush on the mountainside, it seems that even the birds have gone still. The sky is ablaze with fiery colors all along the horizon where the deep lunar shadow drops off. Venus burns brilliantly in the southwest, a sharp white pinpoint in the darkened sky. The sound of camera shutters suddenly fills the air. Then, as rapidly as it fell, the darkness begins to recede, bid farewell with another handful of golden Baily’s Beads.

The crowd erupts in cheers. The Ph.D. students throw themselves at each other and hug like they mean it.

It has been revealed that “The students did most of the work this time, so I actually allowed myself to really look at it,” Druckmüller claims. “It was beautiful. Pico Trigaza may not have been the best for science photography, but from the aesthetic point of view, it was extraordinary.”.

According to recent reports, but for Druckmüller, this is just the beginning. While other eclipse chasers will race to release their images, he will take his time. He expects to publish the first visible-light images of the corona in about two weeks. Processing the iron-ion images, and uncovering whatever insights they may hold, will take months—maybe even a whole year.

“Probably until the next eclipse in 2027” in North Africa, Druckmüller indicates.

In a recent development, who knows? Maybe he’ll even enjoy that one, too.
