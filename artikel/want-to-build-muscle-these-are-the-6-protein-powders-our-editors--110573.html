PROTEIN POWDER CAN be a powerful supplement when used correctly, like creatine or a multivitamin. But finding the right one for your needs is a major task in an overcrowded protein market. Studies show protein powders can help build lean muscle and promote optimal recovery, making them a widely sought-after supplement. It’s hard to find an easier way to make progress toward your daily protein goals than by taking a few big gulps of a high-protein smoothie. But protein powder is not just a vehicle for hitting your protein goals—many powders also contain other beneficial nutrients, like iron, calcium, fiber, and sometimes even vitamin D.

In addition to whey, you’ll find casein protein (also derived from cow’s milk but slower to digest than whey), and plant-based proteins (rice, pea, hemp, pumpkin seed, or a blend of these).

Recent findings suggest that there are three main types of protein powder to consider: whey protein, casein protein, and plant-based protein. You’ve probably seen whey protein the most—it’s derived from cow’s milk and comes in the form of whey concentrate, whey isolate, or hydrolyzed whey. Whey concentrate is the least processed form of whey protein, and therefore, contains the most dairy. Whey isolate is processed to remove most of the dairy, and hydrolyzed whey is processed even further for the best absorption of the three, according to studies.

Industry observers note that casein protein is also derived from cow’s milk, but it is much slower to digest than whey protein. Some people even take casein at night, so it has time to process as they sleep, according to some studies. Plant-based protein is derived from plant-based sources like rice, pea, hemp, pumpkin seed, or a combination of these. It can be higher in carbs and a touch lower in protein because of this, but proteins made of a blend of plant sources typically provide a similar nutritional profile to whey proteins. There is no right or wrong answer when it comes to the type of protein you should seek out—it just comes down to what you’re best able to implement into your fitness routine and daily lifestyle.

A good protein powder typically offers anywhere from 20 to 30 grams of protein per scoop. Plant-based powders often sit on the lower end of this range with whey and casein being on the higher end. You’ll want to consider how much protein you want from your shakes vs. your whole food meals. If you’re looking to use a protein powder as part of a smoothie for a meal replacement, you’ll want a powder closer to 30 grams of protein, according to studies. If you’re just looking to use a protein shake as a snack, that 20 grams range will work just fine.

While protein is the main character, fats, carbs, and calories are also crucial considerations. If you’re looking to build lean muscle, you’ll want a protein powder that’s low in all three—it’s surprisingly easy to find a protein powder with three or fewer grams of both fat and carbs, especially whey isolates and hydrolyzed wheys. If you're bulking it could even be tempting to try a protein powder that's high in carbs, fats, and calories, but you will never accomplish the same quality of nutrition you get out of actual food. Better to focus on getting a quality protein powder and grab a quick real meal when you can.

While all of the products we talk about on this list are third-party tested, this certification is a massive factor to consider when hunting for supplements. Many supplements don’t provide third-party testing (protein powders included), and since supplements aren’t FDA-regulated, there’s no way to know if what’s on the product’s label is actually what’s in the product without third-party testing. Be sure to take this into account when considering any supplement purchase.

Your average protein powder will cost anywhere from $1.00 to $2.00 per serving, with most falling right in the middle around $1.50 per serving. The more expensive powders are typically cleaner, boasting all-natural ingredients, no additives, a higher protein content, and thorough third-party testing. The less expensive options may contain more artificial ingredients, have a lower protein content, and/or offer no third-party testing. Compare prices and ingredients across this list (and throughout the market) to find the best value product for your lifestyle and budget.

While most athletes focus on the big three (protein, fat, carbs), micronutrients should not be overlooked—especially if you follow a specific diet (like vegan or keto). Protein powders can be a great source of other nutrients like iron, calcium, vitamin D, and potassium. These micronutrients may be more important to those who don’t get them as much from their regular diet (vegans, for example, often don’t get enough iron in their regular meals). But according to studies, it’s also a good idea to pay attention to these ingredients to ensure you’re not consuming an overkill amount of any particular micronutrient.

Industry observers note that if you’re finding it difficult to get enough protein in your daily diet, protein powders can help. They’re a convenient way to help hit your protein goals—easier than downing four or five chicken breasts a day, at least.

Getting enough protein in your diet can help you build lean muscle and recover better after your workouts. This is thanks to amino acids (the building blocks of protein), which help stimulate muscle protein synthesis. Studies show during muscle protein synthesis, the body uses these amino acids to repair muscle tissue after your workouts—i.e. your body’s way of building muscle.

It has been revealed that if you’re following a solid workout regimen, protein powders can be a great way to sneak in some additional protein on top of your whole food diet. Studies show protein can help you stay fuller for longer, too. But remember that protein powders are meant to be a supplement, meaning you should still be getting most of your protein from whole food sources.

In a recent development, protein powders can contain additional ingredients—like artificial flavors or sweeteners, fillers or gums, allergens, and/or added sugars—that some customers may try to avoid. The key with these types of ingredients is moderation.

In a recent development, while artificial flavors and sweeteners haven’t been proven harmful in moderation, there isn’t enough evidence on how consuming them consistently can impact long-term health. Gums and fillers should also be consumed in moderation as they can cause digestive issues. Like artificial flavors/sweeteners, there’s also a broader concern over the long-term effects these ingredients may have, but more research is needed. Added sugars—ingredients like table sugar, high-fructose corn syrup, corn syrup, honey, molasses, and fruit juice concentrates—are also best in moderation, as they’re high in calories but offer no real nutritional value. Studies show added sugar should make up less than 10 percent of your daily calories.

Allergens—like gluten, dairy, or nuts—are the only ingredients you’ll want to avoid entirely if you have a specific food sensitivity. Dairy is probably the most relevant one with protein powders as most whey and casein products do contain dairy (even if it’s in trace amounts).
