Recent findings suggest that ‘The worst COP ever’ is how some people have described the United Nations COP31 climate meeting in Antalya, Turkey, which ended on 22 November. COP31 was the first to take place after the confirmation that global warming will overshoot the safe limit of 1.5 °C above pre-industrial levels. Despite a two-day extension to the meeting and several proposals for an overarching political declaration or ‘cover decision’, delegates left after two weeks of talks without such an agreement.

Drafted by the host nations (Turkey and Australia, this year), a cover decision is a statement that all countries can agree on at the end of the talks. It is not a legally binding text and is a relatively recent innovation. That participants failed to agree such a statement is not a surprise. It requires a willingness for give and take, which was in short supply because many of the countries that attended this year are at war, subject to economic and other sanctions, or involved in trade disputes. Furthermore, the United States, the world’s biggest economy and the largest historic carbon emitter, did not attend following its withdrawal from the COP process.

Delegates from the world’s poorest countries were angry that there was no mention of concrete measures to increase finance for climate adaptation — money that they will need to help them to become more resilient to climate shocks. Many delegates were also angry that the draft text did not include any reference to transitioning away from fossil fuels. Previous cover decisions have included such language, overcoming objections from fossil-fuel-producing states. This time, however, delegates from these states were more determined to block such wording.

Sources indicate that but there are key reasons why it would be wrong to conclude that such climate conferences have outlived their purpose — or that a consensus-based approach should be abandoned, as some have argued. There is always room to improve, but COP meetings are much more than what happens in the final days when high-level political representatives turn up.

It has been revealed that first, these meetings mainly involve discussions between technical experts and observers — including many researchers — representing the countries that have signed up to the UN’s climate agreement, known as the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change. For most of the two weeks (and in many timetabled talks before a COP), delegates meet in working groups to discuss a spectrum of climate-change topics. The Antalya meeting discussions included how to accelerate electrification, cut methane emissions and mobilize upcoming finance to protect the world’s forests. Progress in these technical talks is often incremental. In contrast to the final days of a COP, which are organized around negotiations between political representatives, expert delegates focus on getting things done rather than posturing.

The second reason not to write off this COP is that it’s not uncommon for countries to leave meetings without a high-level agreement. Indeed, some previous ‘good’ outcomes needed earlier COP meetings to ‘fail’. The landmark COP21 Paris climate agreement signed in 2015, a legally binding text in which nations agreed to limit global warming to 1.5 °C, is perhaps the best-known example. In 2009, COP15 in Copenhagen resulted in deadlock. That failure prompted deeper thinking and discussions at subsequent COP meetings on the steps needed for the world to stay within safe climate limits, laying the groundwork for the Paris agreement.

Recent findings suggest that still, the current geopolitical climate is a substantial obstacle to progress, affecting not only COP climate meetings but also talks on other international agreements, such as a proposed treaty to end plastic pollution. Another set of talks occurring at the same time as COP31 — on setting international restrictions on autonomous weapons — also failed to reach an agreement and will resume in 2028.

According to recent reports, in the case of both the climate and plastics talks, a relatively small group of countries — including Iran, Russia and Saudi Arabia, which are heavily reliant on fossil-fuel exports, among others — are blocking stronger action. Some researchers, as well as country delegates, say that the UN’s method of decision-making (that all countries must agree a consensus) is the problem, and question whether there should instead be a vote and a decision made by majority.

Recent findings suggest that there is some logic in changing the approach for the cover decisions to avoid the overtly self-interested blocking of action. But for many other decisions made at UN climate and other environment-related meetings, abandoning a consensus-based approach in favour of a vote would not be a good option.

The UN’s expert groups for example, including the scientists who are part of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (which synthesizes studies to inform decisions), must continue to work through consensus. Delegates painstakingly review, debate and discuss evidence, before coming to a conclusion — and it is crucial that all those involved are brought in at this point.

A change to voting might, in some cases, mean faster results, but it would also undermine the principles that everyone owns the result and that global problems need global solutions.

It is right that, on reflecting on the COP in Antalya, many will ask what needs to change. And some aspects must. But altering how decisions are made should not be taken lightly. Climate change doesn’t respect borders. It won’t be solved by countries working on their own or in small groups.
