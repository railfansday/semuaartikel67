Mitchell took three journeys while making Hejira: a portion of Bob Dylan’s all-star Rolling Thunder Revue Tour in late 1975, her short-lived Hissing trek in early 1976, and a cross-country drive she made in a white Mercedes without a license (she’d often wear a red wig in disguise, and go by names like Charlene Latimer and Joan Black). Released in November 1976, the album is a timeless travelogue of jazz-folk magic, when Mitchell left it all behind in search for more. “I’m porous with travel fever,” she sings on the aching title track. “But you know I’m so glad to be on my own.”.

It’s impossible to pinpoint the genius of Hejira on just one track. There’s the opener “Coyote,” about the affair she had with the playwright Sam Shephard on the Rolling Thunder tour. That song is now immortalized in a viral video from Martin Scorsese’s documentary, where she casually performs it for a stunned Dylan, Roger McGuinn, and Gordon Lightfoot. There’s “Furry Sings the Blues,” about her encounter with the Memphis blues legend who didn’t exactly take a liking to her (yes, that’s her longtime pal Neil Young on the harmonica). The stunning “Amelia,” named after Amelia Earhart, was her ode “from one solo pilot to another …sort of reflecting on the cost of being a woman and having something you must do.” Hejira is arguably her most cohesive body of work, featuring the fretless bass of Jaco Pastorius — whom she called the bass player of her dreams — plus guitarist Larry Carlton, percussionist Bobbye Hall, and others.

Looking back, Mitchell knew she was at the height of her powers. “I feel a lot of people could have written ’Chelsea Morning,’” she stated. “But I don’t think anyone else could have written the songs on Hejira.” In honor of the 50th anniversary of Hejira, Rolling Stone spoke with musicians and actors about its legacy, and why it continues to resonate with listeners today.
