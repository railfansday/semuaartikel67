Former Rep. Mary Peltola has defeated Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan in Alaska, giving Senate Democrats their ninth pickup of the midterm elections and bringing their caucus’s total seats in the next Congress to 56.

The Associated Press called the race for Peltola around 10:19 p.m. Eastern time on Tuesday, a full two weeks after Election Day. The latest tranche of ballots counted put the former congresswoman past the 50-percent threshold to win the election outright without Alaska’s ranked choice voting system kicking in.

With her victory, Peltola, who is Yup’ik, becomes one of the first Native American women elected to the Senate, along with fellow Democrat Peggy Flanagan, who won the open seat in Minnesota this month.

“As a lifelong Alaskan who has dedicated my life to serving our state, there is no greater honor than having Alaskans’ support in our fight to take on corporate greed, lower costs, and put Alaska first.,” Peltola mentioned in a statement after the race was called.

The Alaska race was the final Senate contest to be called in a midterm election that saw Democrats win commanding majorities in both chambers of Congress. Besides Alaska, Democrats also flipped Senate seats in North Carolina, Maine, Ohio, Texas, Iowa, Kansas, South Carolina and Mississippi.

Peltola, a former state legislator, made history in 2022 when she became the first Alaska Native elected to Congress, winning a special election for the state’s at-large district. She comfortably won the general election that fall but was narrowly unseated in 2024 by Republican Nick Begich.

In a recent development, she entered the Senate contest in January, positioning herself as an independent-minded Democrat and invoking the legacies of two well-liked Alaska Republicans, the late Sen. Ted Stevens and the late Rep. Don Young, whom she succeeded in the House.

She campaigned on the slogan “fish, family, freedom” and played up her moderate views and deep roots in the state.

According to recent reports, but the race was marked by allegations of political gamesmanship. In addition to the incumbent Sullivan, a retired colonel in the U.S. Marine Corps Reserves, the Senate ballot also included a retired teacher named Dan J. Sullivan. The New York Times reported late last month that operatives aligned with Peltola’s campaign were involved in the effort to recruit the second Dan Sullivan in an attempt to confuse voters.

Peltola has stated she had no connection to the scheme.

Prior to Election Day, the Trump administration filed a lawsuit seeking to delay certification of the result if Sen. Sullivan were to lose. The lawsuit was tossed earlier this month by a federal judge.

According to recent reports, trump also suggested that all votes cast for Dan J. Sullivan should be added to the incumbent’s total. “If you add them up, Dan Sullivan wins,” the president told reporters Monday. But as of Tuesday evening when the race was called, Peltola had more votes than both Sullivans combined.

Recent findings suggest that the Alaska race was initially viewed as a long shot for Democrats. The Last Frontier has elected a Democratic senator just once in the past 50 years – in 2008, when Mark Begich ousted Stevens. Begich, the Democratic uncle of the current Republican congressman, served a single term before he was ousted by Sullivan in 2014.

But anger in Alaska over the rising cost of food and fuel — already among the highest in the nation — provided Democrats with an opening.

"56 seats was an unimaginable result even just a few months ago," Senate Democratic leader Charles E. Schumer mentioned in a statement Tuesday night. "But I knew if we concentrated on getting the best candidates, and if we focused like a laser on the costs, corruption, and chaos that Donald Trump has wrought throughout America, we would have shot.".

In other Alaska races, Democrats flipped the governor’s mansion, with former state Rep. Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins winning an open-seat race to become the first Democrat elected governor of the state since Tony Knowles won a second term in 1998.

The race for the state’s at-large House district will be decided by ranked choice voting, with tabulations expected to take place Wednesday. Begich, the GOP incumbent, was leading Tuesday with 47.5 percent of the vote and is expected to prevail over independent Bill Hill, who had the backing of national Democrats and trailed with 42 percent. Another Democrat and a Libertarian combined for the remainder of the vote.
