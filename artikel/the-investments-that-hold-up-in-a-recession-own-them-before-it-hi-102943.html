Treasury bonds, cash, and the steadiest stocks have held up best in past recessions, but they protected only the investors who already owned them. Choose your stock and bond mix and set aside emergency cash while the economy is growing, then rebalance through the slump instead of selling stocks for safety once the news turns bad.

Sources indicate that that switch gets the timing backward. By the time a recession is in the news, you’d be selling stocks after they’ve fallen and buying safe havens after other investors have already pushed their prices up.

The 2008 crash shows the cost. That year the S&P 500 lost 37% while 10-year Treasury bonds gained 20%, according to yearly returns compiled by NYU Stern professor Aswath Damodaran. The National Bureau of Economic Research, which determines when U.S. recessions start and end, didn’t announce that one until Dec. 1, 2008, about a year after it began. An investor who moved from stocks to Treasury bonds at the end of that year then lost 11% on the bonds in 2009, while the S&P 500 gained 26%.

Which haven protects you depends on what drives the downturn. In a crash that sends investors looking for safety and pulls interest rates down, as in 2008 and 2020, high-quality bonds gain while stocks fall. In a drop driven by inflation, as in 2022, rising rates cut the value of bonds already issued, so bonds fall along with stocks. Consumer prices rose 6.5% that year, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reports.

According to recent reports, the bonds have to be high quality. Corporate bonds with a middling credit rating, Baa, lost 3% in 2008, the year 10-year Treasury bonds gained 20%.

You can’t tell in advance which kind of downturn is coming, so a mix built for a recession holds both high-quality bonds and cash.

Some ideas pitched as recession-proof rarely belong in an ordinary investor’s mix.

Recent findings suggest that pick a split between stocks and high-quality bonds that you’d stick with if stocks fell 37%, as they did in 2008. Then write your investing rules down, including that split and the date you’ll check it. Keep an emergency fund that covers your bare-bones bills in cash outside the mix, so a layoff doesn’t force you to sell stocks at a low. Money you’ll need within a few years belongs in safe places that earn interest instead.

Recent findings suggest that rebalancing your portfolio through the slump means selling some of what held up to buy more of what fell.
