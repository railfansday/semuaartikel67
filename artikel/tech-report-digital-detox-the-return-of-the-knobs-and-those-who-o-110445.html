📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a recent development, my most enduring professional memories of the Aughts are as follows: (1) Driving a Porsche Carrera GT with Walter Röhrl. (2) Being constantly harangued by German auto engineers about the absolute superiority of diesel engines. (3) Hyperextending my neck while driving the Grave Digger monster truck. And (4) listening to Audi rhapsodize about its obsession with the quality and feel of its switchgear.

This story originally appeared in Volume 38 of Road & Track.

Recent findings suggest that with two decades’ distance from that time, here is my assessment of the above: (1) Sick! Peak experience. Could have probably just stopped after this and been satisfied. (2) The German auto engineers were wrong, although it took a gargantuan scandal, the expenditure of many fortunes, and the threat of a diesel-powered Audi R8 for the evangelists to finally accept the truth. (3) That hurt a lot, but I also ripped the door off the truck on a landing, so call it even. (4) I might have scoffed at Audi’s fixation at the time, but in the touchscreen world we now live in, I miss those switches and knobs. So much so, in fact, that I would happily hear again about the company’s knob-research department.

Industry observers note that and so, as I stood out in the California sunshine on a recent day, listening to the Audi CEO, Gernot Döllner, positively geek out over the switchgear and beautifully designed accoutrements of the manufacturer’s fresh Nuvolari supercar, it was a most comforting flashback. Seriously, the man clearly relished dragging a fingernail over the aluminum air vents inside the car and listening to them ring. The lever to adjust the vent is a fully realized and glorious device. Yes, I do understand how ­ridiculous that statement sounds. But this is an exquisitely rendered piece of aluminum operating so smoothly and consistently that your brain assumes you are in the presence of quality. You can’t help it. It’s biology! Inside and out, the car is peppered with these small, beautiful delights.

In a recent development, and yes, the Nuvolari is almost entirely a Lamborghini Temerario under its spare, Bauhausian skin. And yes, the limited-edition Nuvolari costs over $300,000 more than the Lambo, which, to a sensible human, makes no sense. But it is a marvelous thing to behold and touch. It’s a statement of intent from Audi to return the company’s focus to design and quality in general (as well as in switchgear and trimmings specifically). Let the rhapsodizing begin again. I’m here for it. Again.
