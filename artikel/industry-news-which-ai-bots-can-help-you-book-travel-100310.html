📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that A multitude of new start ups promise AI assistants will “handle your travel.” I’m skeptical of anything that promises to handle my travel, mostly because I’ve spent years learning exactly where airline websites like to hide the landmines and sharing it with our audience. But following Kirby’s (United’s) statement that AI bots would be blocked from its sites, I got curious about where we are with airlines and bots. I wanted to see what happens when an AI agent, one that drives a real web browser the way you or I would (in this case, Grok as ChatGPT, and Claude work differently), sits down at the airline’s own website and tries to book a real itinerary.

In a recent development, to be clear, this is not a test of chatbots that spit out a list of flights and then send you off to do the work yourself. The question here is narrower and more practical. Can an agentic AI search, pick a fare, fill in the forms and get a family of four to the payment page without a human bailing it out? And just as interesting, which airlines let it in the door at all? For this hypothesis, I presumed United would be a certain “no” but what about the others?

I gave the a Grok Bot (a single AI agent) the same basic trip on every airline: Pittsburgh (PIT) to Fort Myers (RSW), one way, on Tuesday, December 15, 2026, for four adults. On American, I used a real award rebooking for my own family while signed in to my AAdvantage account. On Delta, United and Southwest, the agent shopped cash fares while signed out. Grok is unique in two ways, first that it has its “own computer” where it runs your searches instead of yours, and second, that it can securely store your login credentials and operate as you would but it never sees those credentials (don’t ask me how.) I understand that Muse may have some of the same features, but my setup experience there was not as straight forward and I don’t need a fifth AI tool even if it’s integrated into my existing Instagram and Facebook accounts.

It has been revealed that I drew one firm line. The agent could go as far as the passenger or payment page, but nothing got purchased in any of these tests. Card details, CVVs and the final “Purchase” click stayed with me and I never processed any of them.

According to recent reports, american was the most realistic test and also the most frustrating. The agent had no trouble with the award search on aa.com using my account logged in. It pulled up 40 results for PIT to RSW, with Main Cabin at 10,500 miles one way per person and First at 25,500 miles.

In a recent development, it found AA 3030 acceptable flights from Pittsburgh to Charlotte, connecting to Fort Myers, in Main Cabin for 42,000 miles plus $22.40 for all four of us. If I wanted to, I could train the AI to understand whether I would want a morning or evening flight typically, an acceptable layover range, it could look at my schedule and ensure that we have sufficient time to get to a meeting and work around my schedule and preferences, not just my price. This test didn’t need all of that.

It got that itinerary all the way to “Review and pay.” Then the friction started. My saved card on the account required the CVV to be entered again, which the agent could not supply (and, frankly, should not have). While that was being sorted out, the checkout session expired.

When the agent rebuilt the cart, aa.com threw up its “Please confirm you’re a human” challenge with an “I’m not a robot” box. The AI dutifully lied but identified where and how that was running. That page comes from a bot manager, which assigns every visitor a score from human to bot and can raise that score as the same visitor keeps making requests. Rebuilding a cart several times looks automated (because it was.) At that point a human had to step in.

I’d rate this as a 90% solution with a final human check and ultimate purchase as the last 10% to complete. This is likely a best case scenario to keep control of your miles or money and ensure what you book is correct.

Delta was the cleanest run of the bunch. The agent set up the one-way search, added four passengers, picked the date and landed on a results page with flights connecting in Atlanta and arriving after 9pm, at $192 per person in Main because this one was not a logged in redemption.

From there it handled the Basic vs Main Classic choice, clicked past the upsell screens (an airline’s worst nightmare) and reached “Review and Pay” with a trip total of just under $965 for four in Main Classic. No captcha, no “are you human”, nothing. If Delta’s site was suspicious of my agent, it did not show it. There was no resistance. I flag this as an advantage for Delta, because tech forward clients will love that they can get an entire booking done start to finish without their intervention.

Recent findings suggest that united, like Delta, also let the agent through to the end of my test, but it took more attempts. The date field was finicky, with the calendar jumping ahead to January and February 2027 before the agent got back to December as I watched it work. The travelers dialog took several retries before it stopped saying “1 Adult” and accepted four. Not as smooth as Delta, but not a total block which I would have thought they’d install.

Recent findings suggest that once the search ran, the agent took the first result (which it did not do for the others) a 6 AM departure via Washington Dulles, and correctly skipped Basic Economy (United makes you tick a “Basic Economy works for me” box first) in favor of the Standard fare. That is a reasonable choice for a family, but my kids would have something to say about a 6 AM departure, which is a good reminder that an AI agent only knows the preferences you give it.

Industry observers note that it then reached the “Traveler Info” page with a total of $964.80, which is, amusingly (but from a competitive standpoint, unsurprising), the exact same total Delta showed for four people. No captcha appeared on United at any point.

In a recent development, southwest ended up with the best itinerary and the lowest price. The first result was a nonstop leaving Pittsburgh at 9:50 AM and arriving in Fort Myers at 12:30 PM. The agent chose the Choice fare at $222 per person, for a total of $885.60 for four. If it only it knew my kids, this nonstop would be the clear winner even if the price wasn’t better than United and Delta. That stated, I would also need to input information about what’s preferred when comparing miles and points, and my personal valuation so that it doesn’t choose even an $885 one-way for four people if American happened to be running 5,000-mile one-ways.

Still, it got to the “Passengers” page, step 2 of 5, without any bot restrictions.

In a recent development, this isn’t an entirely comprehensively list as not all of them were useful for both the Live And Let’s Fly audience as well as my purposes. But what I did do is ask the AI to check whether each airline has its own AI assistants that can actually search, book, change or cancel a flight, or whether those tools just answer FAQs and hand you to a person.

Recent findings suggest that alaska is the one that has built something close to a trip-planning AI. Its “Trip inspiration with AI” tool, Alaska Inspires, sits in the Book menu on alaskaair.com. Alaska and Microsoft (I suspect an even further neutered version of Co-Pilot) say you can type or speak a request in plain language, get destination ideas, and discover, compare and book in the same flow. In a March 2025 interview, Alaska’s director of innovation, Bernadette Berger, gave examples like “Where can I go in Europe for under 80k miles?” Microsoft’s write-up claims a 7% conversion rate versus 5% for the standard booking widget. My AI was not able to run my booking through Inspires so this would be a failure in my experience but I am willing to try again using different parameters.

JetBlue does have a customer-service AI but I was unsuccessful in attempting to advance it further.

Breeze Airways has gone further on digital customer care than most low-cost carriers. VP of Guest Experience Danny Cox, says Breeze uses Gladly’s generative “Answer Threads” tool to present change and cancellation options in chat or SMS, and that AI fully resolves 37% of conversations. That is post-booking help, not a bot that will shop a new itinerary for you. Breeze’s own contact pages blocked my lookup, so I could not independently confirm how the current chat or text flow works for a brand-new booking. That would be a failure but again, I suspect a logged in account might perform better.

Recent findings suggest that allegiant does put a bot on its site but this is for customer service alone. Sun Country and JSX are even thinner on the consumer-facing side and my bot failed for all of these carriers too.

According to recent reports, on the cash side, AI agents can already do the boring part of booking. All three cash bookings reached a passenger or payment page, and none of them hit a captcha. The agent picked sensible fares, navigated upsell screens and dealt with clunky widgets on its own, even if United’s date picker and Southwest’s sluggish buttons cost it some time. Adding that personal context could make this a very practical option.

The real wall is at the end. Payment details, CVVs and timed checkout sessions are designed to need a human, and I think that is fine. The bigger issue is bot detection because it wasn’t really built to detect or negate AI bots but general hacks that could operate massive attacks on the sites. American is the airline that blocked my agent outright, and it happened on the most complex booking of the test, involving award miles and a signed-in account. Whether that was because of the account, the repeated cart rebuilds or just AA’s settings that day, the result was the same.

But United is the only one that has said it won’t allow AI bots and absolutely did.

Out of all of the tests, Delta was the easiest and had me most encouraged, while United and Southwest made it harder but not impossible and not specifically against AI, just the design of the site. American was the only one that really made it difficult and that shocked me. This test, and travelers using these tools for booking purposes are narrow at the moment but as AI agents/bots continue to advance and become normalized, the carriers will likely do something more robust. That mentioned, there could be a strong advantage for those that are “AI-friendly.” Competitive markets like Los Angeles and New York might see more flights sold on Delta from indifferent travelers solely because it’s easier. The real question becomes how comfortable the general public becomes and with that, hopefully not too comfortable or websites will crash from users searching flights hundreds of times a day.
