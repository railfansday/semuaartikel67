Sources indicate that the topic of U.S. trade controls precluding the export of Nvidia and AMD high-end AI accelerator chips to China has taken a few twists lately. Jensen Huang's affairs aside, it's an open secret in the industry that Nvidia's Blackwell silicon finds its way into China, whether physically or virtually. UnemploymentLabs, an independent research outfit, has come up with strong evidence that those chips were used for training open-weight Chinese AI models, including DeepSeek V4, Kimi K3.1, MiniMax-M3, and Ling-3.1-Flash.

The scientists are careful to hedge their findings, noting that while their math checks out, it does not reveal where the chips were, how they were accessed, or prove that export controls were broken. As far as we can tell, it's presently not illegal for Chinese companies to rent Blackwell hardware abroad, and even though Nvidia's grip is tightening, U.S. companies aren't strictly prohibited from doing so, as evidenced by the recent Tencent-Oracle deal.

This identification method is limited to models with open weights, and depends on both hardware and software stacks used for model training — and yet it's both clever and simple, like the Egg of Columbus.

According to recent reports, let's establish some base facts. First, different AI accelerator families have unique counts of shader modules (SMs), marginally analogous to CPU cores — Nvidia's Hopper chips have 132, Blackwell B series have 148, and Blackwell GBs come with 152. Second, while there's more than one software stack for training AI models, PyTorch and its CUDA-aware friends are by far the most widely used on the green team's hardware.

It has been revealed that the first step for training a model is initializing its weights, or how strongly the model considers each individual input. In modern LLM architectures, their initialization goes through a randomization step in which their values are adjusted plus/minus pseudo-randomly, using cuRAND — as in, randomly, but a seed starting value results in the same adjustment figures every time.

According to recent reports, as you'd expect, doing this for an entire model is a lot of computational work, so this process is spread out across all the SMs, further subdivided into threads. For large weight sets, the thread count per SM is 2,048 for Blackwell and Hopper silicon, and it's surely carefully chosen and optimized for.

Sources indicate that the researchers repeated a facsimile of the initialization process using the same chips, software, and thread counts. Using relatively simple statistical analysis, they spotted that their final statistical distribution of weight values precisely matched that of DeepSeek, Kimi, et al. As a test, they changed the SM and thread counts, and predictably, the correlation completely broke down.

Industry observers note that this let them pick out each family of Blackwell/Hopper chip used for each model, and eventually they also figured out an approximate count of cards used. They cannot, however, pinpoint which exact variation produced the weights, as this analysis is linked to initialization layout counts alone, thus SM count and usage pattern — not to VRAM capacity, memory bandwidth, or other characteristics that distinguish individual cards.

Industry observers note that what about AMD cards like the MI300X, or other silicon with a high enough SM count, using the same software stack? The scientists say that it's not impossible that an AMD card could be set up to show up as two groups of 152 or 148 shaders, although we imagine there'd be little reason to do so. Nevertheless, for DeepSeek V4, they also ruled out that possibility by analyzing the actual weight values themselves, and found that AMD cards still didn't produce the same weight value pattern.
