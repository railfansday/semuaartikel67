Top-ranking parakeets face a hostile welcome after time away

🌐 Source: Earth • 📅 17 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 6 min read (1201 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways

  • When a high-ranking monk parakeet disappears from its flock, the other birds adjust to its absence.
  • Researchers kept some high-ranking birds away from their flocks for eight days.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Monk parakeets change their fighting habits when dominant birds leave, often bullying them when they return to the flock.

When a high-ranking monk parakeet disappears from its flock, the other birds adjust to its absence. But a fresh study shows that its return can trigger a shift toward bullying.

Researchers kept some high-ranking birds away from their flocks for eight days.

When they returned, most were bullied by the other birds. They were attacked much more often than they attacked others.

The work was led by Annemarie van der Marel while she was a postdoctoral researcher in Elizabeth Hobson’s lab at the University of Cincinnati.

Van der Marel now works at the Institute of Ecology and Biodiversity in Chile.

Monk parakeets are highly social parrots. They ease into friendships and they spend much of their time squabbling.

According to recent reports, every bird in the study was held in captivity. The researchers counted fights over space. In each one, a parakeet moved in on another bird, which then gave up its spot.

Between 2020 and 2022, 51 monk parakeets were observed in four flocks. The birds were kept in flight pens in Gainesville, Florida. The pens belong to the USDA National Wildlife Research Center.

In a recent development, most of the birds had been caught in southern Florida. The species comes from South America but now lives wild in that region.

Observers logged more than 118,000 contests over about 1,460 hours.

“Each bird received a color mark so that we could identify them individually. We all had our favorites. They have a lot of personality,” van der Marel stated.

According to recent reports, every three days, the wins and losses were used to rank the birds. In this way, the researchers worked out each flock’s pecking order.

In three of the flocks, one bird at a time was then removed. Each bird was chosen for its rank and housed apart for eight days. It was then returned to the same pen.

Fifteen birds went through that cycle. Nine of them ranked at or near the top of their flock. The other six held middle or lower ranks.

Three fighting patterns were described in a 2021 study by Hobson and two colleagues. Each pattern reflects how the members of an animal group pick their opponents.

In the first pattern, an animal attacks any lower-ranked group member.

Recent findings suggest that in the second, it mostly attacks rivals ranked just below it. The third pattern, called bullying, involves mostly attacking group members ranked much lower.

According to recent reports, each undisturbed flock maintained its existing fighting pattern. One flock showed bullying for as long as it was watched.

Another flock attacked any bird of lower rank during the month before its removals. That pattern was not changed by a thunderstorm.

It was also not changed when two injured birds were taken out for care. A change of partner by the top-ranked bird had no effect either.

According to recent reports, the patterns began to change once the removals started. Sixteen switches were recorded immediately after a bird was removed or returned.

Recent findings suggest that other switches were recorded between those events. No switch was recorded after some of the removals and returns. A group changing its pattern over time had not been documented before, the authors wrote.

A returning bird loses its rank according to the team’s earlier study.

The researchers therefore expected the other birds to have little reason to change how they fought.

The flock’s fighting pattern changed after six of the nine top-ranked birds were removed and after six of their returns.

Recent findings suggest that however, the patterns adopted afterward differed. Any lower-ranked bird was most often attacked after a top bird was removed. That result was recorded in five of the nine cases.

Bullying was recorded in eight of the nine cases after a top bird was returned.

On average, each of the 15 returning birds was the target of about 530 contests.

According to recent reports, those contests were counted over the three days after its return. Birds of similar rank had been targeted about 220 times over the three days before.

The flocks were not fighting more overall. About the same number of contests was recorded in each three-day period of the experiment. The birds had simply changed their targets.

“When removing a top-ranked bird, all the remaining birds have updated their rank in the other bird’s absence,” van der Marel said.

“And when the absent bird returns, the group shifts toward a bullying dominance pattern, maybe because they don’t want to lose their new rank position.”.

Returning top-ranked birds received much more aggression than returning lower-ranked birds did.

Sources indicate that the researchers compared responses by rank in the two flocks studied in 2022. Bullying was recorded in eight of 12 cases after a top bird was removed or returned.

The flock attacked any lower-ranked member in eight of 12 cases after a lower-ranked bird was removed or returned.

Removing a top-ranked bird also changes the flock’s mix of ranks. The experimental design therefore could not fully distinguish between two possible responses.

The flock may have reacted to the loss of that bird or to the new mix of ranks.

Memory may play a part, the authors suggested.

It has been revealed that earlier work by Hobson found strong evidence on this point. Monk parakeets remember whom they fought, who won, and how fights between other birds turned out.

Flock members that remember a former top bird may gain from bullying it, the authors wrote. Such bullying could stop the bird from retaking the top rank.

The authors also suggested that bullying could work as a signal to other birds that are watching.

According to recent reports, the birds were handled differently during removals and returns. The whole flock was netted and handled on removal days. Only the returning bird was handled on return days.

Recent findings suggest that the effect of handling could not be fully separated from the effect of the return, the authors wrote. They gave two reasons for thinking that handling was not the main cause.

According to recent reports, bullying was rarely recorded after the return of a lower-ranked bird. The storm and other unplanned events did not change the flock’s fighting pattern.

It has been revealed that the birds were also captive. Wild flocks of monk parakeets split apart and merge again often.

A wild flock can contain between one and 95 birds, and the average flock size is about four.

It has been revealed that A wild bird may be separated from its flockmates more often than the captive birds were. Those separations may also be shorter or longer than eight days.

What a flock or its members gain by switching patterns is still not known.

Industry observers note that the team is now splitting and merging flocks in ways closer to wild conditions. The fighting patterns of those flocks will then be checked for changes.

The full study was published in the journal PLOS Computational Biology.
