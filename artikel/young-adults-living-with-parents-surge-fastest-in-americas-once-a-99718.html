Young Adults Living With Parents Surge Fastest in America’s Once-Affordable Regions

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Young adults living with their parents have become a familiar symbol of America's housing affordability crisis. But the geography of that living arrangement is changing, according to new research.

A record 25.2 million Americans aged 18 to 34 lived with their parents in 2025, or roughly 1 in 3 young adults. Nationally, that share barely budged between the pooled 2020–22 and 2023–25 periods, according to The Geography of the Crowded Nest, a upcoming report from Realtor.com®.

Beneath that apparent stability, however, the South and Midwest—long considered the nation's affordability havens—recorded the largest year-over-year increases in 2025, with the share living at home rising 1.4 percentage points to 32.3% and 31.7%, respectively.

Meanwhile, in the notoriously expensive Northeast—where living with parents has historically been most common—the rate fell 2.3 percentage points to 36.9%, its lowest level since 2010.

It pushes back against conventional logic that cheaper housing markets offer young adults an easier path to independence.

"This is not a coastal problem that has stayed coastal; it has spread to even the more-accessible parts of the country," writes Hannah Jones, senior economist at Realtor.com and author of the report.

Perhaps the clearest illustration comes from two metros that, at first glance, seem worlds apart: San Antonio, TX, and Boston, MA.

Sources indicate that one is steeped in cowboy culture; the other, Ivy League academia. In October, the median asking rent in Boston was $2,954, more than double the $1,117 in San Antonio. Yet the share of young adults living with their parents was nearly identical: 33.6% in the Alamo City and 34.3% in Beantown.

“The short answer is that different affordability constraints can produce the same outcome,” says Jones.

Take the Midwest, where the median home listing price is nearly $183,000 below the Northeast's. It's a sizable gap that obscures the fact that prices in both regions have climbed at nearly the same pace since before the pandemic: 48.4% and 50.8%, respectively, according to data from Realtor.com.

Even excluding 18- to 24-year-olds, who may still be establishing their careers and are therefore more vulnerable to economic pressures, the Midwest reached a record: 14.8% of those ages 25 to 34 lived with their parents in 2025, the highest rate for that age group in 25 years.

At the same time, many affordable markets didn’t see the same run-up in local wages as they did in home prices, which only deepened the divide.

"In San Antonio, the problem is the wage-price gap," says Jones.

Geoffrey Sanzenbacher, an economist at Boston College who has studied the rise in young adults living with parents, points to another side of that relationship.

"The same things that might make an area expensive—lots of good jobs with good pay—also serve to keep people out of their parents' home," he tells Realtor.com.

"Areas with hot labor markets may see kids living with their parents because they can't afford an apartment. Areas with poor labor markets may see kids living with their parents because they can't find a job," he adds.

His September research found that higher rents were associated with greater co-residence among adults aged 25 to 34. Stronger employment and wages were associated with lower rates among men, although the relationship was not statistically significant among women.

Of the 20 major metros examined, 12 recorded increases between the pooled periods, while eight saw declines.

Las Vegas experienced the largest increase, followed closely by San Francisco and San Jose, CA—markets with vastly different housing costs. Even in Texas, San Antonio's rate climbed while Houston's fell.

One possible explanation lies in who benefits when housing becomes more available.

In San Antonio, active home listings are now more than 40% above pre-pandemic levels, according to Jones. Yet the metro recorded one of the largest increases in young adults living with their parents.

"By and large, the market has homes available, but local young adults can't qualify for them at current prices and rates," Jones states, noting that higher-earning newcomers and existing homeowners looking to trade up are better positioned to purchase those properties.

But San Antonio's rental market has also improved. Approximately 6,900 apartments were completed in 2025, according to Northmarq. By year's end, vacancies had reached 11.8% and asking rents had fallen 4.5%.

Boston offers a counterpoint. Jones suggests that new rental construction, combined with the metro's higher-paying industries, may have helped some young adults establish independent households despite a much tighter housing market.

"Markets where supply growth is concentrated in the mid-to-upper tiers still see entry-level households struggle to keep up," Jones indicates. "The lesson for other markets is about the type of supply that moves the co-residence needle.".

There's one last side to the story of young adults moving back home: A growing number are opening their own homes to their parents.

In his research, Sanzenbacher found that the share of adults aged 25 to 34 hosting their parents increased from 1.6% in 1996 to 4% in 2025—a living arrangement outside the scope of the newly introduced co-residence figures from Realtor.com.

In Boston, George Sarkis, co-founder and CEO of The Sarkis Team at Douglas Elliman, says some parents are helping adult children with down payments, while other families are considering shared living arrangements.

"It can also work in the other direction, with adult children helping aging parents while allowing everyone to remain close," he claims.

Sarkis adds he's seeing buyers considering how a property can serve different generations over time. That can mean finding homes with private suites or separate living quarters that allow family members to maintain their independence.

"Living under the same roof doesn't necessarily mean sacrificing independence. With the right property and layout, it can be a strategic financial decision that benefits multiple generations," he adds.

But those arrangements can introduce another financial hurdle.

Industry observers note that A separate analysis from Realtor.com found that homes marketed for multigenerational living in San Antonio received 65.2% more page views than standard listings in 2025. Yet their median asking price was $573,105—nearly 69% higher than standard homes.
