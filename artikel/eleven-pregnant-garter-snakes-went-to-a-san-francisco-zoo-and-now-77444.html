Eleven pregnant garter snakes went to a San Francisco zoo, and now 62 of their young are in the wild

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

62 endangered San Francisco garter snakes born at the San Francisco Zoo were released in San Mateo County this fall, the first head-start release.

In spring 2025, U.S. Geological Survey biologists caught 11 pregnant San Francisco garter snakes on the San Mateo County coast, in a preserve run by the Midpeninsula Regional Open Space District (Midpen).

They took them to the San Francisco Zoo, where the snakes gave birth later that year.

Sources indicate that after giving birth, the mothers were returned to the wild, the zoo says. The zoo’s team raised their young until they were significant enough to be released.

On November 10, 2026, the San Francisco Zoo & Gardens remarked 62 of those zoo-born garter snakes had been released this fall in San Mateo County.

It was the first release under a head-starting program for the endangered subspecies. The announcement does not say how many are still alive.

Industry observers note that the San Francisco garter snake (Thamnophis sirtalis tetrataenia) is a subspecies of the common garter snake. The zoo states it lives only in San Mateo County and a small corner of Santa Cruz County.

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service describes red on the top of the head, a greenish-blue or blue belly, a wide pale stripe down the back and red and black stripes on the sides.

According to recent reports, A major loss of habitat came after World War II. In the 1940s, the sag ponds along Skyline Boulevard, small ponds in low spots along a fault line, held hundreds of the snakes.

By the 1960s, nearly all of those ponds had been drained and built over, according to the Golden Gate National Parks Conservancy.

It has been revealed that the snake went onto the first federal endangered species list on March 11, 1967, six years before the Endangered Species Act of 1973. The zoo estimates that a couple thousand remain in the wild today.

Recent findings suggest that federal biologists blame illegal collection, construction, drought and saltwater in coastal ponds. Bullfrogs and largemouth bass are on the list too, because they eat the snakes and their prey.

That prey includes threatened California red-legged frogs, which the snakes rely on for food, the zoo indicates.

Industry observers note that since 2004, the conservancy and the National Park Service have restored and enhanced 33 acres (13 hectares) of wetland, grassland and coastal scrub for the snakes, according to Midpen’s 2025 release. The work added new ponds for breeding habitat.

It has been revealed that garter snakes give birth to live young instead of laying eggs. In this subspecies, birth comes in July or August. A litter can hold 6 to 35 babies, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service species profile notes.

It has been revealed that after the 11 females arrived, the team scanned them with ultrasound. The early scans revealed that a goal of 80 to 120 live babies was feasible, Midpen reported in September 2025.

Later that month, the district estimated that the zoo’s Conservation Team was raising about 120 newborns.

The zoo had practice with the species. Its staff bred San Francisco garter snakes in the 1990s to work out how to keep them.

From 2005, they developed care protocols with the San Diego Zoo on housing, diet, growth tracking and parasite checks, Midpen stated in 2025.

Rochelle Stiles, the zoo’s director of field conservation, described the goal of the zoo’s snake rearing work.

“By providing these young snakes with a safe environment during their most vulnerable stage of life, we’re giving them a stronger start in the wild,” she stated.

In a recent development, head-starting works that way. The young are fed and sheltered until they are big enough to handle predators and competition for food, a method that also saved an endangered wallaby population in Australia.

In a recent development, each snake born at the zoo had a genetic test before it left, and the results set its destination. About one-third went back to the area where its mother had been caught.

According to recent reports, the rest went to several other populations that were short on both snakes and genetic variety. Isolated groups slowly lose diversity, the same problem behind a proposed genetic rescue for Lassen red foxes in northern California.

Disease data mattered too. Laura Patterson coordinates amphibian and reptile conservation statewide for the California Department of Fish and Wildlife (CDFW).

She said a CDFW survey for snake fungal disease, an emerging disease found in many wild snakes, “aided in the selection process.”.

The partners will not name the release sites. The zoo noted secrecy protects the snakes from collectors, cars and disease.

Recent findings suggest that midpen biologist Karine Tokatlian also commented on the release. “Seeing these young snakes returning to the wild represents years of research, collaboration, habitat restoration and careful conservation work,” she stated.

Removal from the endangered list requires at least 10 viable, self-sustaining breeding populations. Each one needs at least 200 snakes.

U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) ecologists had already tested the idea on a computer. In a 2023 paper in PLOS ONE, a team led by Jonathan Rose of the USGS Western Ecological Research Center simulated newly introduced populations started with newborns, juveniles, adults or a mix.

Head-started juveniles gave those populations the best chance of lasting. Releases would have to continue for at least 15 years to keep the risk of near-extinction low, the model said.

Raising juveniles also takes fewer adults away from the donor site. Justin Sloan, acting deputy field supervisor at the USFWS Sacramento office, credited research, genetic testing, habitat work and specialized care.

Ariel Starr, a Midpen wildlife ecologist, looked beyond this one subspecies.

“The success of this project and the associated research will not only benefit the San Francisco garter snake but will also hopefully inform the recovery of many imperiled reptile species,” she stated.

According to recent reports, the November announcement gives no survival rate for the 62. It also does not say how long they had been out when it was posted.

Survival is low even for snakes born in the wild. One study found only 2 percent of females born live to age 5, according to the USFWS.

There is also a gap between the 2025 estimate of about 120 newborns and the 62 released. The zoo did not explain it.

It has been revealed that the 2023 model focused on snake demography. Its authors wrote that the genetics of the donor, captive and released snakes would also be a major concern for any reintroduction plan.

It has been revealed that more than 130 garter snakes were born at the zoo in 2026. They are being raised for a second round of releases in 2027. Monitoring of both rounds will show whether the populations at the sites grow.

In a recent development, rachel Townsend, a National Park Service wildlife biologist, put the first round in proportion. “Releasing these snakes is a significant moment, but it is one step of many in a much larger recovery effort,” she said.

In a recent development, the mothers are back in the wild. This fall, 62 of their young were let go at sites the partners will not name.

The information was obtained from the San Francisco Zoo & Gardens.
