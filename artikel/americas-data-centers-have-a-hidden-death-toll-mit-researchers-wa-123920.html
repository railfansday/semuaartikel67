America’s Data Centers Have a Hidden Death Toll, MIT Researchers Warn

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

An MIT analysis estimates that powering America’s data centers contributed to hundreds of premature deaths in a single year, with rural populations facing greater risks than city dwellers.

In a recent development, as artificial intelligence drives the rapid construction of data centers across the United States, concerns about their environmental impact are taking on a upcoming dimension. The electricity needed to keep these facilities running around the clock can increase air pollution in communities far from where the data centers themselves are built, potentially putting the health of people who live nowhere near them at risk.

Researchers at MIT estimate that air pollution associated with data center electricity consumption contributed to approximately 650 premature deaths in the United States in 2023 alone. Iowa, Nevada, South Dakota, and Kansas had the highest estimated rates of premature deaths per capita, even though much of the electricity demand was concentrated in northern Virginia and Texas. Rates in Virginia and Texas were only about half those in most Midwestern states.

Sources indicate that “When people think about the negative impacts of data centers, they usually focus on the neighbors. But our study indicates that the air quality impacts of data centers are very widespread across a large region. Even if you don’t think you are affected because there is not a data center near you, think again,” indicates Noelle Selin, senior author of the study, a professor in the MIT Institute for Data, Systems, and Society (IDSS) and the Department of Earth, Atmospheric and Planetary Sciences (EAPS), and director of the Center for Sustainability Science and Strategy.

Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory estimated that data centers’ electricity needs accounted for about 4 percent of total U.S. energy demand in 2023 and approximately 4.4 percent of the nation’s overall emissions. Those proportions have likely grown as more facilities have been built.

Determining who bears the health consequences of that growing demand is complicated by the interconnected nature of the nation’s power grid. A data center in Virginia, for example, may increase electricity production at power plants in the Ohio Valley, where a greater proportion of older coal and natural gas plants produce higher levels of emissions.

Sources indicate that “Different regions have different mixes of power. If you turn on a major newly introduced demand source, by plugging a data center into the grid, you might have to fire up your really dirty power plant to meet that new demand,” Selin says.

Sources indicate that the pollution can then travel into neighboring states, where local atmospheric conditions can make matters worse. In agricultural regions such as Iowa and Nebraska, ammonia released from fertilizer promotes the formation of fine particulate matter (PM2.5), microscopic airborne particles that can cause heart attacks, strokes, and lung disease.

The estimated health consequences also extend to Kansas, South Dakota, Missouri, and Arkansas. Overall, the researchers found that rural populations face a significantly higher estimated mortality risk from data center electricity demand than urban populations.

To understand how these risks are distributed, the team developed a model that tracks electricity production and transmission between regions on an hourly basis. While many previous studies relied on average power grid emissions, the researchers assumed that data centers operated around the clock and identified which individual generators would respond to their additional electricity needs.

“Generators that respond to additional electricity demand are often natural gas plants, although the response varies by place and hour,” notes Yuang (Albert) Chen, an MIT graduate student and co-author of the study.

“It was a challenge to gather all the data,” Chen says. “There are more than 10,000 generators in the U.S. grid, and we needed to make sure the data provided for each generator is valid and validated across other data sources.”.

Industry observers note that the researchers calculated emissions of nitrogen oxides and sulfur dioxide from individual generators. Both pollutants contribute to the formation of fine particulate matter, while nitrogen oxides also help produce ground-level ozone, commonly associated with smog.

Industry observers note that using a separate atmospheric model, the team simulated how these pollutants formed and traveled across regions. They then drew on epidemiological research linking air pollution to premature death to estimate the resulting health impacts.

It has been revealed that of the approximately 650 premature deaths estimated for 2023, 513 were attributed to fine particulate matter and 137 to smog. Together, they represented about 5.3 percent of all estimated electricity-related premature deaths nationwide.

“While it is not surprising that any additional source of air pollution will result in mortalities, with data centers, when and where these deaths are occurring are different than you might expect,” Selin indicates.

As companies continue to expand their computing infrastructure, the findings suggest that decisions about where to build data centers and how to power them need to account for public health well beyond the immediate surroundings.

According to recent reports, “When people are thinking about data center siting and operations, it is key to consider more than just things like land and energy prices. They should also take air quality considerations into the equation,” Chen states. “This is a good baseline to think about where we should build data centers and how we should supply those data centers with electricity.”.

Recent findings suggest that the researchers hope to investigate the air quality consequences of a growing industry trend in which data centers incorporate their own on-site power plants. They could also examine whether adjusting when these facilities consume electricity could reduce their impact on air quality.

In a recent development, reference: “Spatially distributed air quality and health impacts of data center electricity demand in the United States” by Yuang Chen, Shen Wang, Juan Ramon L Senga, Andrew Scott White, Anthony YH Wong, Lyssa M Freese, Michelle L Bell and Noelle E Selin, 19 November 2026, Environmental Research Letters. DOI: 10.1088/1748-9326/aebbd4.
