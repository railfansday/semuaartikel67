I've spent the past 20 years pushing back against the periodically popular notion that markets are replaying the late-1990s and early-2000s tech bubble and bust.

We've simply never matched the levels of gross overvaluation, pervasive public greed, fevered IPO issuance, corporate malinvestment and Wall Street malfeasance that set up a generational market peak, a halving of the S&P 500 over two-and-a-half years and a lost decade for stocks.

In a recent development, we still haven't. Even if June was the peak in AI speculative fervor, as seems possible, and if Oct. 6 proves to have been a consequential broad-market top, the major indexes simply never stretched far enough into the thin air to make a potential retrenchment nearly as damaging as the early 2000s were.

Still, in a more muted fashion, markets have been behaving in ways that generate uncomfortable comparisons to past buying climaxes accompanying a technology revolution.

Sources indicate that there is still room and time for the market to prove that the messy sell-down in AI-reliant growth stocks is simply a painful adjustment phase and the S&P 500's six-month purgatorial range a mid-cycle refresh. But it will need to start getting on with it.

Everyone wants to celebrate the way the market has absorbed the damage to semiconductors and other AI-buildout plays, rendering it a controlled demolition rather than indiscriminate destruction.

The Financial Times Unhedged newsletter on Monday called it "The perfect sell-off," the same day the Wall Street Journal cheered, "When Nvidia Stumbled, Equal-Weight Funds Delivered.".

Recent findings suggest that this is true, as far as it goes. A rush into defensive and "quality" stocks has largely offset the drag from Nvidia's $2 trillion market-cap loss from its October peak.

But recent defensive leadership, especially from low-growth consumer staples and Massive Pharma, sends a less-reassuring macro message, and those groups lack the earnings momentum, narrative appeal or market-cap heft to carry a bull market to new heights.

It has been revealed that BTIG technical strategist Jonathan Krinsky has noted echoes of the 2000 period when "boring" defensive stocks ripped as tech rolled over, supporting the tape for a while. Barclays equity desk noted Monday that the "quality factor" basket of stocks just had one of the best 100-day runs ever of the past 20 years.

According to recent reports, and JP Morgan here plots the stampede into "low-volatility" stocks, mirroring the hasty retreat from "high-momentum" areas. There's room here for defensives to get even more popular before tripping multi-year extremes, but is that what an index investor should root for?

As for the ascendance of everyone's favorite, the equal-weighted S&P 500, Ned Davis Research analyst London Stockton this month noted that just over 50% of S&P 500 stocks were outperforming the index itself in 2026. Sounds healthy, right? Only to a point.

He explains: "Levels [of stocks outperforming the index] above 58% have most often occurred in and around years where there has been a large bear market." The current phase follows a period when leadership was extremely narrow, producing a "resemblance of the prior three years (2023-2025) to the two years prior to the last Tech bubble (1998-1999) with a very small percentage of companies outperforming the overall S&P 500.

"It is not likely that we are in a situation like in 2000," Stockton concludes, "but this highlights the importance that these large tech stocks at the very least do not decline substantially for the S&P 500 to continue rallying higher.".

Industry observers note that there are other straws in the wind that harken to prior tech busts:  The Federal Reserve is trying to restrain the corporate sector from overheating in the latter part of a cycle, same as in 1999. Retail traders have maniacally bought the cascading decline in Nvidia shares the past week or so, invoking the voracious dip-buying made famous in the late-'90s which worked until it failed spectacularly. The market's refusal to pay up for sizzling earnings growth raises alarms about whether they are pulled forward, unsustainable or overstated; profits from 2000 were later revised down radically.

And Nvidia's alleged breach of chip-export rules and the forced departure of CEO Jensen Huang are a reminder that major corporate scandals were a key feature of the Tech Bubble's prolonged reckoning, though nothing resembling the multiple accounting frauds of that era are implied here.

Again, this is the kind of litany of worry and woe that gets recited each time a tech-propelled bull market breaks stride for a bit; more often than not a recovery follows to clear the gloom.

According to recent reports, perhaps Nvidia's results on Tuesday clearing the way for the organization to restart stock buybacks and the final allocation of Anthropic's IPO shares will refresh the tech bid. With luck, the huge "hyperscaler" tech-platform stocks will move back to the fore as well-funded stewards of a more sober AI expansion.

Maybe, after weeks of Wall Street insisting that hedge-fund tech-stock positioning is scraping multi-year lows, this will finally presage an upside pain trade.

The vaunted seasonal, post-election tailwinds that the consensus was expecting to fill market's sails and lift the indexes from the floor of an enervating six-month trading range are running late. They need to arrive quite soon. Otherwise, the soggy tape - along with signs of ebbing liquidity reflected in Bitcoin weakness a strong dollar and jumpier equity volatility - will make those tech-bust echoes louder.

In a recent development, john Kolovos of Macro Risk Advisors on the latest reading: "Despite pervasive bearish sentiment and the average stock trading near latest highs, our historical studies suggest the market remains vulnerable to disappointment. With defensive leadership still dominating, risk appetite weak, and financial conditions still tight, maintaining a short-term cautious/bearish stance still seems warranted.".

-Twenty years ago this week, I wrote a cover story for Barron's that antagonized plenty of friends in the stock-trading profession by foretelling a future of fast, cheap electronic markets that has largely come to pass.

Called "Death of the Floor," the piece detailed the regulatory, technological and behavioral shifts that were ushering in high-frequency, low-touch trading and pressuring the old exchange model.

Reading it now, the conclusions seem utterly obvious and inevitable, though at the time there were still 2,500 NYSE floor brokers and specialists trying to remain in the middle of most trades.

It has been revealed that "For the specialists who manage trading in individual stocks and for the floor brokers who depend on the in-person auction model, a tough challenge is becoming even tougher. To stay in the game, they must master cybertrading. For the small investor, this is almost entirely good news. Trading costs should continue to fall, while the likelihood of getting the best price should improve.".

Happily, exchanges have adapted and evolved – to zero commissions, thousands of ETFs and microsecond executions - and we have a market that can handle multiples of the capital flow that was routine back then.

If I have one regret about the piece, it's that my good friend and former colleague Bob Pisani thought the CNBC reporter skeleton illustrating the article was meant to depict him.

I assured him, many times, that it was not.

-Almost every business relies heavily on its most loyal repeat customers. But this skew seems extreme for online-betting companies.

Recent findings suggest that A Bloomberg piece picked up here by Yahoo Finance reports that one-third of all sports bettors produces some 97% of revenue at digital sportsbooks. It says the 1% most active gamblers on average lost 78 cents of each dollar bet, twice the loss rate of the norm among all customers.

One can view this as simply paying for entertainment (I "lose" 100 cents of every dollar I place on a movie ticket), but the social disapproval toward the industry seems to have grown.

Add it to the swirl of complications surrounding the legal and de-facto decriminalized wagering industry.

Perceived hostility of Congressional Democrats to prediction-markets venues taking sports bets in states that haven't explicitly approved sports gambling is seen as a plus for DraftKings and Flutter.

Meantime, Robinhood has blurred the lines between investing and gambling by aggressively expanding into prediction markets. And on Monday it was reported that Kalshi has looked to combine investing and betting from the opposite direction, acquiring a brokerage firm in order to offer stock trading to its prediction-markets clientele.

It has been revealed that adults should be free to behave unwisely with their money in search of a windfall. But brokers promoting the negative-sum game of wagering while professing to help the little guy accumulate long-term wealth sends a dissonant message.

The ducks that were quacking loudly ahead of SpaceX's June IPO are much quieter in advance of Anthropic's planned debut this week.

Here is the year-to-date performance of two ETFs that hold shares of recent fresh listings, Renaissance IPO and First Trust US Equity Opportunities, relative to the S&P 500.

As of June 12, when SpaceX priced, these funds had gone vertical as the public worked itself into an anticipatory lather, the Renaissance fund running ahead of the S&P by some 20 percentage points in the prior six months.

Since then, the IPO funds have deflated (both now have large positions in SpaceX, of course) and there's a bit more focus on whether Anthropic's target valuation is justified given the vast rethink of the AI-proliferation thesis.

All else being equal, a more favorable setup now versus then for a market being asked to swallow a latest $1 trillion-plus ticker.
