Breaking: Take 15 Credits, Not 12 – A Fifth Year of College Costs a Year’s Pay

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, take 15 credits a semester instead of the 12 that count as full time for financial aid, and map every required course with your adviser in your first year. Finishing in five years instead of four means another year of housing, food, and fees, plus a year without a full-time paycheck.

Only 46% of first-time, full-time students who started at a public university in 2017 graduated from it within four years, according to the National Center for Education Statistics. Another 14% finished in year five.

Federal Student Aid counts 12 credits a semester as full time, so 12 feels like a normal load. But a typical bachelor’s degree takes 120 credits, and 12 a semester adds up to 96 after four years, leaving you 24 credits short. Credits from a major you drop late may not count toward the fresh one, and a required course that fills up before you register can push graduation back a semester.

Take 15 credits a semester, or 30 a year with summer courses included. At a school that charges one tuition rate for anything from 12 to 18 credits, as Indiana University Indianapolis does, the three added credits cost no extra tuition. At a school that charges by the credit, you pay for the same 120 credits whether you take four years or five.

Say you attend an in-state public university with flat-rate tuition and pay the average sticker price. You land a $55,000 job at graduation, below the $60,000 median the New York Fed reports for early-career graduates working full time. Moving from 12 credits a semester to 15 skips a fifth year that costs $26,720 at 2026-27 prices for tuition, fees, housing, and food, according to the College Board. It also starts your paycheck a year sooner. Counting the $55,000 before taxes, that’s about $81,700.

Divide the credits you still need by the semesters left in your four years, and register for at least that many each term.
