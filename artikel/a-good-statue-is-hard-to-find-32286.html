It was the organization the bronze kept: The squares belonged to poets and playwrights, astronomers and philosophers, figures in stone cloaks gazing over bustling pigeons as if weighing one more line. Here and there, a statesman sulked on his plinth, and the courthouses guarded their jurists, but officeholders were scarce.

The exception was the kings, those equestrian gentlemen forever reining in horses that never move.

Sources indicate that the pattern has a plausible explanation. Europe was hardly a stranger to electoral politics. Athenian citizens voted in assembly, the Roman Republic chose its consuls, Iceland’s Althing has convened since 930, and Venice elected its doges for roughly 11 centuries.

So, the scarcity of political monuments cannot be explained by the absence of voting. A better account is that a reputation lasts about as long as the work behind it remains usable.

Once that circumstance passes, the achievement survives chiefly as historical record. The principal lessons learned from political figures are the consequences of their decisions rather than the reasoning that produced them, which is seldom preserved and less often consulted.

Political monuments are also unusually exposed to revision. The removal of Lenin statues across the former Eastern Bloc after 1989, and the toppling of Edward Colston in Bristol in 2020, suggest that a statesman’s standing stays open to dispute in a way a poet’s rarely does. Southerners know this quite well, having watched Confederate monuments come down in town after town.

It has been revealed that their replacements fared little better. One was set ablaze during a celebration after a win over Louisiana State University, and the corner has been replanted more than once since.

In a recent development, passion for a game can kill the very things it gathers around. Yet something survived.
