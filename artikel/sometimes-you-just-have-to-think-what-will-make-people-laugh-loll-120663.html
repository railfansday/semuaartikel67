If there’s one piece of advice Lolly Adefope wants to pass on to the next Mary Todd Lincoln, it’s from Broadway’s Maya Rudolph: Wear the kneepads.

The British star has taken her undeniably brilliant comedy chops to the stage, making her West End debut in Oh, Mary!‘s London transfer and joining a steadily growing — and deliciously mischievous — lineup of actors to take on the neglected wife of Abraham Lincoln at the Trafalgar Theatre (Catherine Tate, Mason Alexander Park, Jinkx Monsoon, as well as the show’s playwright Cole Escola among them). In a recent development, the 80-minute musical, chronicling the days before the U.S. president’s assassination from the point of view of his alcoholic, cabaret-fanatic spouse, is a breathless romp with dance numbers, growling and some belt-out show tunes — all in a wig and corset.

Recent findings suggest that it took Adefope a couple of performances to realize it wasn’t do-or-die each night. “At first I was like, ‘This has to be all of my energy in every single second of the show,'” she begins to The Hollywood Reporter. “If you’re going to do one major show at Wembley Stadium, then yeah, you can let it all out. But it’s just not sustainable for the body to be under extreme tension for an hour-and-a-half. In the beginning, I was just kind of doing it like a panic attack. And then after a while I was like, ‘You can actually chill.'”.

Recent findings suggest that it tracks that Adefope performs Oh, Mary!‘s absurdity with the precision of a surgeon. She’s been described as the best part of everything she’s in — and it’s true. The South Londoner might be globally recognizable for roles in Hulu comedy Shrill or even the canceled-too-soon Franchise, but here in the U.K., she’s a titan of the British comedy circuit and possesses the kind of make-it-look-easy talent that some performers spend their entire careers searching for. Never is that more on display than in the BBC‘s Ghosts, where Adefope ascertained household-name status with her scene-stealing portrayal of Kitty, an adorable Georgian noblewoman who also happens to be dead.

Industry observers note that “I think it came naturally to me,” states Adefope about character comedy and her early stand-up aspirations. “It was probably a good thing that I didn’t really have people in my life who were doing stand-up. It was all just a very class clown vibe, listening to lots of Radio 4 comedy, watching [comedy duo] Mitchell and Webb and absorbing comedy on Nickelodeon and Disney Channel.” It wasn’t until late into her teens that Adefope thought she could make a living out of it. “I had an idea that I wanted to be a comic actor,” she says, “but that felt like a made-up thing.”.

Industry observers note that what does any young person with dreams of making a career out of cracking everyone else up do? Hit the Edinburgh Fringe Festival, of course. The must-stop for aspiring stand-ups and comedy performers has taken over the Scottish capital every August for 79 years, and served as a springboard for some of the most famous creatives in the business, such as Phoebe Waller-Bridge, Richard Gadd, Emma Thompson and Graham Norton. “It’s this one month where you get to live with all your friends and see your friends’ demonstrates and when I was doing it, I was like, ‘I wish this was what real life was like!'” remembers Adefope. “Sadly, it’s not.”.

But it did land her an acting agent, and with that came one of her first paid gigs: Josh Widdicombe’s 2014 sitcom Josh. “The character’s name was Catherine or something. But in the beginning,” says Adefope, “it was called, like, ‘Annoying Girl at Party.’ And I was like, ‘Sign me up!'”.

The more she acted, the more Adefope believed she thrived in that space — particularly character work. It was on the game show Taskmaster and a slew of other now-non-existent British panel indicates that her profile rose even higher. But it was a 2017 sitcom with Jim Howick, Loaded, that crucially got her acquainted with a member of theater collective Them There. “Jim called me and he was like, ‘We’re making this taster of this show. It’s called Ghosts,'” she recalls. “‘Would you want to be in it?'”.

Them There, comprised of Howick, Mathew Baynton, Simon Farnaby, Martha Howe-Douglas, Laurence Rickard and Ben Willbond, raised a generation with the still meme-spawning series Horrible Histories, a sketch show depicting caricatured versions of England’s most prolific historical figures.

They had a new pitch for the British broadcaster — a quasi-flat-share comedy in which a couple inherits an enormous stately home filled with the ghosts of those who spent time and died there throughout the centuries. Among the apparitions are a lightning-zapped caveman, a Regency-era poet shot in a duel, a boy scout counselor from the ’80s with an archery arrow through his neck, a victim of the Stuart era’s witch hunts, and a disgraced Tory minister who died mid-sex and is now destined to spend eternity without trousers on.

Recent findings suggest that five seasons later, and Ghosts stands in pride of place in the U.K.’s comedy hall of fame. Its unprecedented success has made superstars out of its cast, hatched versions in the likes of the U.S., Germany, and France, and accrued a cool $11.2 million at the British box office with last month’s release of a feature film Ghosts: The Possession of Button House, reuniting the beloved characters after the show’s end in 2023.

Since Katy Wix’s Mary ascended to a higher plane, or, as the ghosts say, got sucked off (“What can you do?” asks her former co-star with a faux pout), Adefope is the only performer who isn’t also a member of the writing team. She remains one of the show’s blindingly bright spots (of which there are many). “Kitty was pretty similar to how they first described her, but I think at first she was more stupid,” laughs Adefope about the initial conversations around her giggling, pop music-loving poltergeist. “But to this day, I’m like, ‘She’s the smartest ghost!’ I didn’t want it to be too two-dimensional — not that they would have ever written anything two-dimensional — but I am obsessed with Tituss Burgess [in Unbreakable Kimmy Schmidt] and Jack McBrayer in 30 Rock; [the idea] that there’s something going on underneath the surface of this sweet character,” she says.

According to recent reports, indeed, it’s in Adefope’s immediate understanding of the smartest possible decision — a sly eye roll, sudden scream, or childlike outburst — that makes her work as Kitty so impressive. “I think a massive chunk of it is probably wanting to make the rest of the guys laugh,” says the star about keeping the rest of us guessing with Kitty’s mannerisms. “Part of it is probably having a background in doing live comedy — it’s quite good training for that kind of show because there are loads of us, and you don’t get hours to do a take. Sometimes you do just have to think: ‘What is going to make people laugh? What’s the unexpected thing?'”.

Industry observers note that she couldn’t have predicted the way the show would resonate with Brits. “It’s a very rare experience to make something that you love making and that people also love watching, because it’s sometimes one or the other,” indicates Adefope. But there certainly wasn’t any talk of a feature film after that fifth and final season wrapped up, or Adefope would have “really held onto that.”.

In the movie, the ghosts race to rescue a wandering spirit who’s possessed the body of a young boy before the child loses his life forever. “The writers always wanted to make sure that it wasn’t just a bonus, extra-long episode,” she indicates about the direction they took. “[They wanted it to feel] like it warranted being on a massive screen, and it’s so nice having people watch and validate that. […] It does deserve to be longer and bigger.”.

She would admittedly do “100 sequels,” but the group is facing some major continuity hurdles. “The ghosts aren’t meant to age, and every year we all think we look older,” she laughs. “We don’t know how long we could do it for, but I don’t think there are any intends to do a sequel. But there were no plans to make any more after the TV show, so who can trust what they say?”.

Industry observers note that adefope is certainly well-acquainted with the false hope of a fickle entertainment business. The Franchise, HBO’s satirical superhero comedy from Jon Brown, Sam Mendes and Armando Iannucci, got the axe after one season (though it did also earn Adefope a BAFTA TV Award nomination). She repeats THR‘s question with a knowing smirk: “Was it chopped down in its prime? It’s a great question. I was disappointed about The Franchise,” she begins. “I think it was so funny when the trailer came out for The Studio and it was so similar to the show we were making. And it’s won the most Emmys of any comedy ever [now],” she says about the Seth Rogen-led Apple TV hit. “So did we stand a chance?” The question lingers.

Sources indicate that another U.S.-based project, Aidy Bryant’s three-season Shrill, would take Adefope to Portland every year for 12 weeks. “That definitely felt like, ‘Whoa, this is actor lifestyle vibes,'” she says about playing Bryant’s British best friend and flatmate, Fran. “I loved Shrill so much, it was a dream,” she continues. “[But] because I’d done a show before that called Miracle Workers in Atlanta, there was a period of time [and] even now people say, ‘Where are you living at the moment?’ I’m like, ‘I do actually live in London,’ but after that I [thought], ‘Maybe I should try and do some British work just so that people don’t think I’ve left.'”.

It might not get much more British than the West End, where Adefope has set up camp for the first time in Escola’s Tony Award-winning show. Its transfer has denied the naysayers here in London, extending its run and consistently pulling enormous audiences, including to Adefope’s slot, which wraps up Dec. 5.

It has been revealed that she saw the Broadway version — ”I was so giddy” — and immediately told Escola afterward that they should consider bringing it to London. It never occurred to Adefope that she could take on Mary Todd Lincoln herself. “I have never really known where to start in the theater world,” she muses. “But Oh, Mary! is just so my humor and it felt like my comic sensibilities matched it quite well. I let the phone ring,” she adds. “I didn’t beg for the audition myself,” a glimpse of Kitty emerges in those fluttering eyelashes, “because I was too shy.”.

It might not be a shock to hear that Adefope is an electrifying First Lady, imbuing her portrayal with the no-holds-barred cheekiness the character necessitates. As is the joy of Oh, Mary!, seeing her version is an entirely different experience compared to her predecessors’. Adefope’s Mary is uniquely her own. “I feel so lucky to be able to put my spin on something and for it to feel like it fits really well,” she states. “Sometimes you get a script for something and you’re like, ‘It doesn’t quite match with how I see the character.’ But this [was] an amazingly lucky fit that I felt I could inhabit it.”.

In a recent development, after agreeing to take on the role, the nerves followed soon after. “I had these weird thoughts,” confesses Adefope. “I was like, ‘I’m going to forget to go to the theater.’ And they’re going to be like, ‘Where is she?'” But those jitters were swiftly reduced when she remembered it’s already a beloved, award-winning show. (The only tough part now is a hard-to-please audience, “[or] if they’re quiet when I’m doing my manic, physical comedy.” It’s a rarity, she assures us.).

Until December, Lolly Adefope has her kneepads on. “For the characters to get slightly further from myself is always fun,” she tells THR about what comes after. “I mean, Mary Todd Lincoln is quite far from myself, even though now I feel like she is me. But I’m doing lots of writing, and seeing where that goes, and [I’m] open to whatever comes next.” What that might entail is anyone’s guess, but one thing is for certain: this Ghosts alum will stay a pro at lifting spirits.
