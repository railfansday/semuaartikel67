Loneliness and social isolation often show up in the exam room through an older patient’s high blood pressure, worsening depression or recent emergency department visit.

Lack of social connection is an increasingly common risk factor for both physical and mental health conditions that clinicians often recognize—but may not feel well-equipped to treat.

In a recent development, approximately one in four older Americans is socially isolated, and more than four in 10 report feeling lonely. Both are associated with high rates of morbidity and mortality, including elevated risks of heart disease, stroke, cognitive decline and dementia. Social isolation is also associated with an additional $6.7 billion in Medicare spending, mainly from avoidable hospital and nursing home utilization.

As leaders of foundations committed to improving the health and well-being of older adults, we invest heavily in programs that foster social connection, especially for underserved populations. But to truly address this public health epidemic, healthcare providers must be part of the solution.

Clinicians can play an essential role in identifying lonely and isolated patients and referring them to resources that promote social engagement. Integrating social prescribing into clinical workflows presents challenges in the current U.S. payment system, but promising approaches are showing success and can be replicated.

Sources indicate that food as Medicine (FAM) programs have already expanded our understanding of what a prescription can encompass. While physicians regularly recommend that patients adopt a healthy diet, that advice is rarely heeded for both behavioral and socioeconomic reasons. What has made a difference is a clinician’s prescribing of free or affordable individualized interventions in partnership with community-based resources and with systemwide support.

It has been revealed that similarly, social prescribing is a structured, team-based intervention that involves assessing and addressing loneliness and social isolation as part of a patient’s care blueprint. Taking the patient’s individualized needs and circumstances into account, a referral is made to a community organization or program that fosters social connections. These activities can range from in-person group programs to virtual activities or telephone-based companionship for people with mobility or transportation barriers.

In the United Kingdom (U.K.), Canada and Australia, established social prescribing programs have been linked to reductions in emergency department visits, hospitalizations and healthcare spending.

Brief, validated assessment tools, such as the UCLA-3-Item Loneliness Scale and the condensed Berkman-Syme Social Network Index, can be incorporated into the routine patient health questionnaires to help identify those at risk. Hospitals have a fresh incentive to use these screens with the recently implemented Age-Friendly Hospital Measure from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, which includes addressing social vulnerability as one of five required domains.

Industry observers note that but what then? Identifying suitable programs, partnering with local organizations, and making those connections for patients is a job in itself, one that clinicians cannot reasonably be expected to add to their already full workload. And this is especially complicated in rural areas where both clinicians and programs are more likely to be limited.

In a recent development, in the U.K., they have designated “link workers” to fill this role. Here in the U.S., value-based care team practices, which are becoming more common, can support a similar position or share those responsibilities among team members. A Social Prescribing Learning Hub from Social Prescribing USA offers practical guidance for how providers can get started, including choosing community partners and models, measuring impact and navigating the insurance landscape.

It has been revealed that the state of Connecticut has taken a different approach by building a social prescribing infrastructure in collaboration with SocialRx, a tech giant that matches patients with appropriate programs, enabling healthcare providers to prescribe participation in arts and cultural activities that are free to state residents. Other states, including Georgia, Massachusetts and California, have developed similar social prescribing ecosystems.

Industry observers note that some insurers and care providers are creating their own social connection programs. BCBS of Massachusetts has partnered with Elder Services of Worcester Area to reduce emergency department utilization among their Medicare Advantage members by providing Transition Coaches after hospitalizations or ED visits. The coaches screen patients for isolation and loneliness, connect them with social groups and companion visits, and help them use technology to stay connected with family, friends and healthcare providers.

Industry observers note that building a sustainable social prescribing infrastructure also requires government and philanthropic support for evidence-based programs that foster social connections in spaces where older adults commonly gather. One example is the American Library Association’s Aging Together initiative, supported by The John A. Hartford Foundation, which strengthens public library programs that reduce social isolation and promote older adults' health and well-being.

Industry observers note that other countries’ experience with social prescribing is building evidence of its effectiveness in improving social connectedness and health outcomes, as shown in a recently released evidence roundup from the Center for Health Care Strategies. However, more research is needed on how to implement effective social prescribing practices in the U.S. One such effort is a partnership between the Humana Foundation and the Aspen Institute to evaluate the prescribing, dosage and follow-up of relationship-building volunteer activities for diverse, underserved older adults who screen for loneliness or social isolation.

In a recent development, there is growing recognition of loneliness and social isolation as clinical risk factors impacting older adults’ health, independence and quality of life. Health professionals can play an essential role in identifying these risk factors and making social engagement a part of patients’ care aims. Support from health systems, philanthropy and the government is needed to staff and train teams, identify effective engagement strategies, build community partnerships, and establish pathways for referrals and follow-up. Working together, healthcare and community organizations can make social prescribing a practical component of age-friendly, whole-person care.
