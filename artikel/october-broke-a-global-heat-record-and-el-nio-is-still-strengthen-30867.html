October broke a global heat record, and El Niño is still strengthening

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

October 2026 was the warmest October on record, as an intensifying El Niño added to global warmth and ocean temperatures reached newly introduced highs.

The global average surface air temperature in October 2026 was 15.35°C.

That’s 0.90°C above the 1991–2020 average and enough to make it the warmest October in the ERA5 record, beating October 2023 by 0.05°C.

It was also 1.75°C above the estimated pre-industrial baseline.

Recent findings suggest that the data come from the Copernicus Climate Change Service, the European Union’s climate monitoring service. And the numbers keep stacking.

In a recent development, before August 2026, the last individual month above 1.5°C was November 2025. Then August, September and October crossed it.

The last streak of three or more consecutive months above 1.5°C ended in April 2025.

The rolling twelve-month average – November 2025 through October 2026 – now sits at 1.51°C above pre-industrial levels.

January through October 2026 ranks as the second-warmest such period on record, 0.07°C below the same stretch in 2024 and 0.05°C above 2023.

A statistical model trained on 1979–2025 data gives 2026 a greater than 99% chance of exceeding 1.5°C for the full calendar year. That would make it only the second calendar year above that level after 2024.

There’s roughly an 80% probability it finishes as the second-warmest year on record and about a 20% chance it ties or exceeds 2024 for the top spot.

If 2026 does exceed 1.5°C, the four-year average for 2023–2026 would also cross that line.

According to recent reports, an intensifying El Niño is adding to the global warmth. Sea surface temperatures across 60°S–60°N hit 20.95°C in October.

This is the highest October value ever recorded. The previous October record was set in 2023 during a developing El Niño. This one is considerably stronger.

It has been revealed that the Niño 3.4 region – the patch of equatorial Pacific used to define El Niño intensity – averaged 29.94°C in October. That’s an all-time high for the region in any month, surpassing the record set just one month earlier in September.

Industry observers note that the anomaly was +3.21°C, also a record, above September’s +2.99°C. For context, this exceeds the peaks of the monster El Niños of 1982–83, 1997–98, and 2015–16.

Across much of the central equatorial Pacific, temperatures ran more than 3°C above average. Along the Peruvian coast and west of the Galápagos, the anomalies reached 6 to 7°C.

The World Meteorological Organization expects this El Niño to peak in December 2026 and to persist through at least February 2027.

The ocean heat isn’t confined to the Pacific. Record October sea surface temperatures were also logged in parts of the northern Indian Ocean, the South Atlantic, and the subtropical North Atlantic.

Around Europe, records fell along the Atlantic coast and in the central and western Mediterranean.

The largest land-based anomalies on the planet were over West Antarctica.

There, temperatures ran more than 7°C above the October average and reached up to 11°C above normal in some areas.

The Copernicus report links this to high pressure over the Drake Passage and a wave pattern typically associated with El Niño. Siberia, the central United States, northern Canada, eastern Africa, and the Middle East also ran warm.

Cold anomalies demonstrated up across much of southern South America, Greenland, northern Africa, parts of central Asia, and western Australia.

Recent findings suggest that the warmth was not everywhere. But it was dominant.

European land temperatures averaged 11.08°C – 1.49°C above normal and the second-warmest October in the ERA5 record, behind only October 2022.

But unlike the record-breaking summer and September, the warmth was unevenly distributed.

Northern Fennoscandia and far-eastern Europe saw the most extreme departures, with Ukraine reaching up to 6°C above average.

Western Europe, by contrast, hovered closer to normal. Southern Italy, the southern Balkans, Greece, the eastern Mediterranean, and western Türkiye were actually about 2°C below average.

According to recent reports, individual months above 1.5°C are not the same as the sustained, multi-decadal average the Paris Agreement refers to. But the frequency with which individual months are crossing the threshold keeps increasing.

It has been revealed that the first calendar year above 1.5°C happened in 2024. The second is almost certainly happening now.

A four-year average above 1.5°C would be another sign that the world is nearing the Paris Agreement’s long-term warming limit.

El Niño is expected to strengthen further before peaking in December, keeping attention on how this exceptional year will end.
