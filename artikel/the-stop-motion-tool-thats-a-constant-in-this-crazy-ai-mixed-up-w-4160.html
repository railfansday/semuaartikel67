A lot is happening in front of the camera in “Wildwood” — the general Millennial chaos of Portland, Oregon, swarms of baby-napping crows, an army of wolves with cannons and trebuchets — and just as much behind the camera in Laika Studios’ latest stop-motion epic. But for all the innovation and blending of CG and stop-motion required to tell the story of Prue McKeel’s (Peyton Elizabeth Lee) impossible quest into the impassable woods, some key tools have stayed the same for Laika since “Coraline.”.

In a recent development, one such tool is Dragonframe, the frame-grabbing software developed by Jamie and Dyami Caliri in 2008, and essential to helping Laika animators craft the naturalistic performances that “Wildwood” director Travis Knight wanted to create. “Back in the old days, like the Ray Harryhausen days — it was still the same as how we do it: You have a puppet; you have an object; you move it a tiny bit; you take a picture; you move it a little more; you take another picture. But you had no way of knowing exactly what the frame that you just took looked like and how it related to the frame that you’re about to take,” Knight explained on an episode of the Filmmaker Toolkit Podcast. “You had these gauges where you could try to approximate the distance something moved, but it was this act of faith. You didn’t really know; you had to wait until you developed the film to see how it moved. And that’s why a lot of early animation has that kind of herky-jerky quality.”.

It has been revealed that dragonframe and other frame-grabbing software systems take a lot of the guesswork, if not quite all of the faith, out of that process. Laika animators can check what they’ve just done on a computer, moving between the most recent frames taken and the one that an animator is right now on. This allows animators to ensure that whatever motion they’re puppeting, be it walking, running, flying, or the unfathomable wrath of nature flowing out through a giant tower of living vines exploding forth from the Earth, flows together.

“Whether it’s a 20-frame shot or a 1000-frame shot, you can look at all the frames that precede it to make sure that the object or the puppet is moving the way it should,” Knight told IndieWire.

Laika has used Dragonframe on every one of its films, but just because a tool is that useful and reliable doesn’t take the artistry and effort of animating out of the equation, or lower the particular stakes of stop-motion.

“2D animation, CG animation, they’re iterative. You can set key frames. You do your breakdowns. You do your in-betweens. You can go back and refine and keep going and reworking things until you get the perfect performance. With stop-motion, it’s progressive. You start on frame one, and the animator, coaxing life with their hands into this puppet, is working their way through a frame at a time. You don’t get to go back and change it.”.

According to recent reports, that lack of take-backs permanence, though, also helps stop-motion animators be intentional and certain in their choices. “You’re contorting your body into all these positions to get these puppets to move properly. It’s mentally very draining because you have to do all this three-dimensional chess as you’re trying to figure things out, because it is technical, but it always has to feel organic,” Knight mentioned. “ It’s human ingenuity, it’s human creativity, it’s devotion, it’s care, it’s time.”.

It has been revealed that all that care and time goes into pulling off the magic trick of making steel, silicon, and fabric objects feel fully alive. That’s been the game for stop-motion since Ray Harryhausen. Whenever Laika develops newly introduced processes or sticks with tried-and-true tools, it’s to give animators the ability to do that. “[We don’t want] to get in the way of seeing this puppet as a person. The finest stop-motion animators can do all that with their hands. But we’ve developed different techniques and tools where some of the less experienced animators can actually get to that same level,” Knight remarked.
