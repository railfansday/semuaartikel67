When We Forgive, What New Meaning Do We Find in Life?

Posted November 20, 2026 | Reviewed by Davia Sills.

Suffering could lead to despair, depression, hopelessness, and even not liking oneself (Yu et al. 2021; Reed & Enright, 2006). Yet one aspect of the forgiveness process, especially as one nears the end of the forgiveness journey, is the sometimes surprising development of finding meaning in that suffering and a new purpose in life. Finding meaning centers on an vital question: What have I learned from going through this suffering, and what have I learned as I walked the journey of forgiveness? This is a cognitive exercise that can lead to new short-term and long-term goals (Enright, 2015). In other words, the cognitive exercise of finding meaning can lead to behaviors that support new purposes in one’s life.

The psychotherapist and Holocaust survivor, Viktor Frankl (1969/2014), pioneered this idea of finding meaning in suffering as he endured the persecution of his imprisonment. His motivation was to resist being destroyed by the abuse. By finding meaning, one can transcend suffering and make it more bearable.

Sources indicate that when I discuss the theme of finding meaning in suffering through forgiveness, I am not focusing on the injustice but on its aftermath. The basic question is this: What are you learning about life as you extend mercy to those who were deeply unfair to you? This post explores the different meanings people tend to generate as they forgive.

Let us first start by examining what finding meaning in suffering is not, because it can be misunderstood.

Consider three themes that sometimes are confused with finding meaning in what an unjustly acting person has done to the forgiver.

Recent findings suggest that first, the forgiver is not denying the disappointment, sadness, anger, or grief in what was suffered. The injustice did happen, and the negative response is real. In other words, finding meaning is not some kind of non-feeling, interpreted, at least temporarily, as relief from the effects of the injustice.

Second, the person does not play games with themselves, stating, for example, “Oh well, things happen for a reason, and I’ll just make the best of it by accepting it.” Yes, people can “make the best of it,” but if this is an exclusive meaning of suffering, the one who forgives is not going after the woundedness inside, which may linger for a long time. The “oh well” approach is passive. To heal from the effects of the injustice, a person needs a more active approach to the pain.

Third, the forgiver does not distort reality by thinking, “Well, all things happen for good reasons, and so I will try to see the good in what happened to me.” The injustice was not good. Some aspects of what happens after the injustice may be good, but the injustice itself is not.

Consider six points on this question.

1. Being more sensitive to the suffering of others, even those who never hurt you: James, upon forgiving, realized he had what he called a “short temper,” in which he imposed his anger on unsuspecting others. He has now committed, for example, to being more patient and kind to cashiers in grocery stores and pharmacies as he waits in line. He is more sensitive to the fatigue and stress of those working behind the cash register. This gives him a new sense of purpose, as he recognizes his intemperance in these contexts and now works to overcome his anger.

It has been revealed that 2. Being motivated to help others in their suffering: When Dr. Freedman (Freedman & Enright, 1996) worked with female incest survivors on a long-term forgiveness intervention, she saw that many of them, upon forgiving, became motivated to help other incest survivors overcome their deep resentment. They were not choosing as a goal to become mental health professionals, but instead to be of assistance to others who have suffered as they have.

It has been revealed that as another example, I talked with a man who is imprisoned for life. Upon forgiving those who abused him, he stated to me that he knows he will never get out of the correctional institution, but now he is motivated to help his cellmates forgive and to be unburdened of their anger. This led to a new purpose, or the behavioral manifestation of this desire to assist others.

3. Standing in the pain so you do not throw that pain onto others: The paradox of standing in the pain is this: As people bear the negative effects of unjust treatment by not throwing that pain back to the offending person or to unsuspecting others, the pain-bearers realize they are stronger than they thought. They see the positive outcomes of not seeking revenge. As Gandhi noted, if we keep taking an eye for an eye, the whole world will be blind. The pain-bearers realize they can now translate this into a new purpose of contributing to a more peaceful world.

According to recent reports, 4. Forgiving helps us think more deeply about justice: If people are continually treated unjustly, they can find meaning in living a life of mercy and fairness integrated together. Thus, their understanding of justice can become wiser, and therefore their appropriation of justice can become more helpful to others.

5. Eventually, you can develop an overall philosophy of life, such as committing to be a conduit of good in this world: This is what Dr. Frankl called supra-meaning, or a upcoming philosophy of life that encompasses an overall approach to how one interacts with others and why one does so in this way. For example, a person can commit to living a life of greater respect, kindness, and love toward both offenders and others. For example, Sally decided to resist impatience and negative emotions toward her own children, as a way of life in her family, so that these life-giving moral virtues can become norms there.

6. As people find meaning in their suffering, they can put their future purpose in life into sharper perspective: Finding meaning, in other words, becomes the cognitive rationale and emotional motivation to seek what matters to them.

In summary, our suffering is not for nothing. People who suffer and forgive can develop as they think carefully about what they are learning about suffering, their own humanity, and life itself. As Dr. Frankl learned and passed his wisdom on to us, finding meaning in suffering opens unexpected doors for survival and growing in our humanity.
