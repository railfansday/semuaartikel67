According to recent reports, american Airlines just lost its appeal to undo Chicago O’Hare’s gate reshuffle. But it’s already bought two gates from Spirit, earned back gate space through its own growth, and survived United’s effort to push it out of Chicago. The airline’s position is stronger than when it sued, and judges haven’t finally decided whether the city broke its contract. The latest ruling upholds the refusal to matter a preliminary injunction. The question of whether Chicago took American’s gates too early, after promising to give the airline a chance to grow into additional space first still hasn’t been resolved. I still think American has the better argument about the contract. I’ve also been skeptical from the beginning that they’d get an injunction, since money damages seemed possible. The November 19 appellate decision fits that distinction, although the trial judge’s preliminary reading of the contract was unfavorable to American. American objected to widening United’s advantage, and Chicago agreed to three additional gates on the north side of the L concourse extension. The lease called for gate assignments following an attached map, with those assignments remaining in place for at least twelve months. American’s argument was that it needed to actually receive the promised capacity before that protected period could run. Otherwise United could grow using its additional gates while American waited for its own, and then the city would use the unequal flying to take gates away from American. The way gates work at Chicago O’Hare is that prior flying determines future gate space (or, technically, ‘linear frontage’). That’s the problem I identified when American first filed its lawsuit. The timing was part of the bargain that got American to support the redevelopment agreement in the first place. Requiring the city to deliver the capacity shown there before starting a year of protected assignments doesn’t require inventing a promise outside the contract. And designating a gate common use doesn’t explain how a gate that isn’t operational can fulfill a promise intended to let an airline ramp up flying. Why American lost the injunction appeal The appellate court assumed American could satisfy the initial requirements for an injunction and still concluded that the harm from blocking the gate changes justified denying one. It was reviewing whether the trial judge abused his discretion, NOT deciding the contract dispute from scratch. American’s problem was that it didn’t establish losing the gates would destroy its Chicago hub. It planned to operate the same number of flights using fewer gates, with more flying at less attractive times and less room to recover from delays. That could cost money, make operations worse and harm passengers but it made the injury look measurable. That was my original point – that this would be a case for future damages rather than an injunction. Meanwhile, the other airlines had planned schedules around the redistribution. United projected more than 2,000 cancellations if it were blocked. That number was overblown but the court accepted that changing the arrangements would disrupt passengers, other airlines, and airport construction. And the expectation that American would win gates back in the next allocation (which it did) undercut the argument that it faced an irreversible decline. Timing hurt American, too. Chicago announced the redistribution in February 2025. American sued in May but didn’t request preliminary relief until July. By the September hearing, the October gate changes were imminent and preparations were well underway. American needs to be relevant in the largest markets where people spend heavily on cards. Attributing credit card revenue to the customers and markets that generate it produces a different picture than spreading that revenue across flights throughout the network. American needs to be relevant in the largest markets where people spend heavily on cards. Retreating from Chicago would make AAdvantage less valuable to Chicago customers, and make its Citibank cards harder to sell and less attractive to spend on. Attributing credit card revenue to the customers and markets that generate it produces a different picture than spreading that revenue across flights throughout the network.

It has been revealed that that’s why Chicago belongs alongside American’s efforts to rebuild Los Angeles as gates return after construction, and its plans to bring Alaska Airlines into its Atlantic and Pacific joint ventures. They’re working to regain relevance in the largest spend markets in the country.

Industry observers note that the Chicago fight has also given employees something to rally around. In the post-earnings employee meeting I covered in January, Chief Commercial Officer Nat Pieper called United the “common enemy” and made it out to be a battle for loyalty and credit card performance. He reported roughly 20% growth in loyalty enrollments, card acquisitions, and local market share over the preceding nine months.
