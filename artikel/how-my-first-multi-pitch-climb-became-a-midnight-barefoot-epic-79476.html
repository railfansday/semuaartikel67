How My First Multi-Pitch Climb Became a Midnight Barefoot Epic

Industry observers note that on July 4, 2026, Audrey Callaway climbed her first real multi-pitch: Diedre (5.8, 6 pitches) in Squamish, B.C. The catch? She didn’t start up pitch one until nearly 9 p.m., thus becoming voluntarily benighted in only the most literal sense of the term. “My story isn’t a disaster!” Callaway explains. “It’s about learning, the tenacity of a little psych, and extremely good tech giant.”.

After first getting into the sport at a gym in Chicago, Callaway later moved to upstate Fresh York near the Gunks. Currently, she’s studying forestry at the University of Vermont. “I like trees, I like climbing, I like birds,” she claims of her significant three hobbies. She’s mostly a boulderer and sport climber who wants to get more into trad climbing. She also works at a climbing gym.

Industry observers note that callaway wanted to share her experience to highlight the growing pains of “doing things poorly the first time”—and how the right partners ease those pains. Below, she shares her story and what others embarking on their first multi-pitch can learn from her experience.

It’s really cool to feel very bad at something again.

It has been revealed that I was planning to visit my girlfriend Lucy, who had been in Squamish for the whole summer. Then my best friend Will, who I grew up climbing with at the gym, told me he also happened to be going out to Squamish. I got there a few days before him and did a little bouldering.

When Will got there, he was even more psyched than I was. After bouldering all afternoon, we’re sitting around, playing chess and looking through Lucy’s guidebook when we saw a trad climb that looked so cool: Diedre (5.8, 6 pitches).

In a recent development, it was 7 p.m., but the sun didn’t set until around 9 p.m. It was very spur of the moment. We’re just like, “We gotta do this thing.” So we quickly got all our stuff together.

We knew it was going to get dark. It’s not like it snuck up on us. But Lucy had a lot of experience. Will and I didn’t know how to trad climb at this point, although he learned later on that trip. So she’s carrying the team. Afterward, she told me she brought only a single rack so that she could share in our sense of adventure and “empathize with the uncertainty.”.

In a recent development, first, we got super lost on the way there. We didn’t get to the base of the climb until like 8:45 p.m., but it was still super bright out.

Recent findings suggest that very quickly, I realized how bad I was at rope management. I was in the middle, belaying Lucy, and we had two ropes. Routefinding is something that you take for granted when you’ve been climbing outside for a while. I was taking the path of most resistance. There was a pretty solid crack on really good terrain, but I’m newer to crack climbing, so I was not about to put my hands in there and trust that. Instead, I was doing this very sketchy 5.10 slab. Eventually, I started using the crack.

Recent findings suggest that we got up to the first belay and we’re all on top of each other because we don’t really know where to be. Nothing was unsafe, but it was very slow. Lucy started going up this 5.6 very runout slab. But I had looped a knot of rope around where I was belaying. I didn’t realize that until I’d fed out enough of the slack. So I kept shorting her like every five feet. I knew that there was totally a way to fix this, but I don’t know how.

In a recent development, then the sun set. That’s okay, because we had headlamps—but Lucy’s headlamp was dead. It was funny because before we started climbing, Lucy was like, “Guys, make sure you have your headlamps.” But then when we put them on, she’s like, “Can I actually borrow yours?”.

So she took mine, and now I was climbing in the dark on slab. It was very precarious, but beautiful. It felt so scary in the moment. But the town was right there, we’re on the most classic 5.8 ever, and we’re a hundred feet off the ground—it wasn’t actually that scary.

Recent findings suggest that will and I both had super competitive shoes on. It had been two hours at this point and we’re standing on this slab and our feet were killing us. Every time I left the belay, Will told me he “would lowkey start crying a bit” because he knew he’d have to climb soon, enduring more toe pain. So we both took off our shoes because it’s easier to climb barefoot. Now I was barefoot in the dark on this slab.

Every time we got to a new belay, we tried to make the system better. Lucy was just trying to climb, but we’re shorting her every time. Now Will was in the way. We were right on top of each other.

It has been revealed that none of these things were particularly hard challenges. If we knew what we were doing, we could have fixed any one of these problems in 30 seconds. I would get back up to Lucy and she would be like, “Why didn’t you just do this?” Then something else would come up.

At one point, probably around 11:30 p.m., Will got a cam stuck. Lucy’s like, “Try wiggling it.” She told him to pull upward, but he mentioned it was literally impossible. Lucy lowered down and pulled it out, first try. There must have been times like this when Lucy was frustrated, but she never highlighted it. Will describes Lucy as the most patient person he’s ever met.

We got to the top at midnight. It was hypnotizing on the apron. The lights of town beneath us glowed surreally. I smoked my first cigarette at the top.

It has been revealed that I remember walking back and it was really quiet. We got back on the road and our gear’s rattling and the moon’s out. It was so quiet and peaceful.

It was one in the morning when we got back to the campground by the Chief. We didn’t want to be disrespectful with a substantial light, so we put a headlamp under this pickle jar we had. By the light of this pickle lamp, we ate pickles and cold canned minestrone and asked, “What are we gonna do tomorrow?”.

To really be benighted, I would say it has to sneak up on you. It kinda felt like it snuck up on me. Clearly, as a group we were aware it was about to happen. But I was surprised when all of a sudden it was dark and I had no headlamp and was climbing barefoot. It made me feel like a salamander, really in touch with the rock.

Lucy and I did another multi-pitch the next day, and since then I’ve done a couple two-pitch climbs in the Gunks when I got back to Newly introduced York. I will probably do lots of really awesome multi-pitches in my lifetime that are beautiful and scenic with really hard climbing, but I’m never gonna have another one that bad. I’m never gonna have another first one.

As told to Maya Silver. This story has been edited for length, structure, and clarity.
