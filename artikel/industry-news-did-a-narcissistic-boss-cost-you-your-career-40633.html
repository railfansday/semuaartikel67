Prada was a 29-year-old registered nurse when she came to my office in tears, saying she hated her job and was having panic attacks. Sharon, the hospital’s human resources director, perceived that the trauma exposure in her work in the emergency room was the source of the panic attacks and sent her to specialized resilience training.

In a recent development, devin, a 42-year-old software architect, came to my office with concerns about depression. He feared losing his job because he was too old and believed that new artificial intelligence programs were replacing him.

Industry observers note that at first glance, their struggles seemed understandable, given the unique demands of their professions. The trauma exposure, aging concerns, and technological pressures were real. But more conversations revealed something deeper and more destabilizing: a high-achieving, abusive, and narcissistic boss.

The challenge is that organizations often view a high-achiever as the goose that lays the golden egg, making them revered and supported. But when that high-achiever also commits narcissistic abuse, they can be untouchable. And the fallout of their abusive tactics resides in the long-term wounds of countless victims.

In It's Not You: Identifying and Healing from Narcissistic People, Ramani Durvasula describes how many therapists are trained to address the person’s isolated symptoms and often miss the larger impacts of narcissistic relationships. She also echoes what Lundy Bancroft explained almost 25 years ago in Why Does He Do That? Inside the Minds of Angry and Controlling Men—the abusive person is a master of disguise and actually gains footing with the therapist, while the abused person who demonstrates up as anxious and closed can get further gaslit and retraumatized by the therapist. Moreover, interventions focused on appeasement or better communication can reinforce and escalate manipulation and abusive tactics.

Now imagine that third party is HR, an executive coach, or an organizational consultant.

And imagine that the abusive person is exceptionally accomplished, charismatic, influential, and valuable to the organization.

In a recent development, who gets believed? Who gets coached? Who gets labeled difficult? And who quietly disappears from the organization?

According to recent reports, given these complex dynamics, I was surprised to read an article in the Journal of Business Ethics suggesting that clear codes of conduct and consequences could help address narcissistically abusive supervision. The authors’ research revealed that leaders high in narcissistic rivalry employ abusive supervision when they perceive employees as deviating from their authority or organizational rules; yet they suggest: “It is essential that organizations recognize the importance of fostering a supportive environment in which both leaders and followers are equipped with the skills and knowledge to interact respectfully with each other” (Gauglitz & Schyns, 2024).

While rules for conduct can establish best practices and delineate what is prohibited, they may fail to address the hidden dynamics of manipulation, coercion, and stealth sabotage.

It has been revealed that in Ten Keys to Staying Empowered in a Power Struggle, I explain how manipulative tactics often operate through confusing double-binds, intermittent praise, shifting expectations, selective memory, and gaslighting. These patterns can leave people questioning their judgment while the person causing harm appears credible and composed.

I have witnessed people who left jobs—and entire careers—without understanding how an abusive workplace dynamic eroded their confidence, health, and sense of agency.

In contrast, healthy workplace dynamics and leadership allow disagreement without humiliation, accountability without retaliation, and growth without diminishing someone's dignity.

Sometimes, recognizing what happened is the first step toward healing, which involves reclaiming what you lost, honoring what you endured, and breaking the cycle of abuse that continues long after the abuser is gone through self-blame, shame, and the transformation of someone else’s cruelty into your own inner voice.
