According to recent reports, while a diet of straight vinegar might not sound appealing to a human, to a microbe it's nourishing enough. In four separate 1,000L fermentation tanks, the engineered microbes eat this vinegar, combined with oxygen, packing themselves with a bioplastic called polyhydroxybutyrate, with the polymer eventually making up 70% of their body weight. At that point, a high-speed centrifuge harvests the bacteria cells from the liquid broth, and the resulting paste is split into two separate assembly lines.

Half of the raw paste is mixed with Martian dirt and fed into a giant gantry 3D printer, which then extrudes a corrugated, arched outer protective shell. Rovers then work to pile around 2m of dirt onto the outer shell, creating a barrier to the cosmic rays and micrometeorites that plague the Martian surface.

Recent findings suggest that the other half of the bacteria is subjected to a pressure bath of liquid carbon dioxide, again pulled straight from the atmosphere. This extracts the pure bioplastic, which is then melted and spun into an airtight, reinforced inner tent that is capable of holding a breathable atmosphere.

According to recent reports, so, in essence, this 20 ton system can be dropped on Mars, including a pair of helping rovers, and extract all of the necessary components to continually and consistently build atmosphere-tight, safe shelter for astronauts, at a rate of about 75 sq meters every 130 days. The paper notes that, comparing the system to directly launching 20 tons of pre-made plastic building materials from Earth, the break-even point of the stand-alone biofabrication facility is around 1,400 Martian days - which lines up nicely with the two orbital alignment cycles NASA is planning to utilize before sending human crews.

In a recent development, the main difference is that the biofabrication facility never stops producing those safe habitats, which clearly is a major advantage. Despite its promise, though, there are still plenty of engineering challenges to overcome before this system will be anywhere near launch ready. But, when that time comes, it appears that we might be able to literally grow safe habitats out of Martian water, air, and soil. And that is something any future mission planner will be thrilled to have as an option.
