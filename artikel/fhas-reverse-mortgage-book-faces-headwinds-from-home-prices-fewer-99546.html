FHA’s reverse mortgage book faces headwinds from home prices, fewer payoffs

The Federal Housing Administration’s fiscal year 2026 actuarial report to Congress claims the Home Equity Conversion Mortgage (HECM) reverse mortgage portfolio remains a small but volatile slice of the FHA’s Mutual Mortgage Insurance Fund, showing that its stand-alone capital ratio dropped significantly in the wake of lower endorsement numbers over the past year.

The report, released Nov. 16, found that the MMI Fund’s overall capital ratio dropped slightly during the year ending Sept. 30, moving from 11.47% last year to 10.29% this year. The current figure, however, is roughly five times higher than fund’s statutory minimum requirement of 2%.

In a recent development, looking exclusively at the HECM portfolio, the capital ratio was down significantly from 24.06% in FY 2025 to 5.43% in FY 2026. The FHA attributed this decline mainly to macroeconomic dynamics and program-specific cash flows.

The reverse mortgage portfolio, which accounts for less than 4% of the fund’s insurance in force, is “much more sensitive to home prices,” the report explained. Slower home price appreciation and updated macroeconomic forecasts have increased projected losses for the HECM program. In addition, loan payoffs — which represent more favorable recovery outcomes than other disposition methods like foreclosures — have become less common and have pushed losses higher.

The agency also noted that the HECM program “spent substantial capital resources on assignment claims in FY 2026, reducing available cash.” These claims, which are generated when FHA acquires loans that hit the 98% maximum claim amount threshold, are a known driver of volatility in the HECM book. They remain a key cost to monitor for reverse mortgage issuers and servicers.

According to recent reports, while the federally insured reverse mortgage portfolio is still in the black, the takeaway from the report is that its capital support can change rapidly due to shifts in home price appreciation, payoff behaviors and the frequency of assignment claims. These factors drive FHA policy decisions, as well as Ginnie Mae execution in the HECM Mortgage-Backed Securities (HMBS) market.

Industry observers note that the report illustrates that FHA-insured options remain central to the wider reverse mortgage market, even as volumes edge lower. In FY 2026, FHA insured 24,162 HECMs representing a maximum claim amount (MCA) of $12.81 billion.

Endorsements were down about 14% from the 28,149 HECMs endorsed in FY 2025. MCA volume also fell from about $15.5 billion in 2025 to roughly $13.5 billion in 2026.

But even with that pullback, the HECM program “remains a large part of the market,” according to the report. Historic data reinforces the sector’s structural shift. HECM endorsements peaked above 110,000 in 2009 with roughly $30.5 billion in MCA. Volumes have trended lower for more than a decade, with visible rebounds in 2020–2022 during a period of low interest rates and high home price growth. But higher rates and slower price growth since 2023 have softened demand.

It has been revealed that to fill the gap, lenders are focusing on proprietary reverse mortgages through their retail, wholesale and correspondent channels. The wider availability of these products has propelled a shift in market share as private-label loans accounted for more than 60% of reverse mortgage volume in September, according to Upcoming View Advisors.

“I think that product diversity is great because we’re able to serve more clients that the FHA product wouldn’t handle,” Jesse Allen, president of 55+ lending at Rate, told HousingWire‘s Reverse Mortgage Daily (RMD) at this month’s National Reverse Mortgage Lenders Association (NRMLA) Annual Meeting & Expo.

He noted these may be “mass affluent clients who have home values of $2 million or $3 million, and they really want to tap their equity without the debt service to achieve their goals,” but they can also be clients who don’t fit the HECM qualification requirements and are using reverse mortgage proceeds to consolidate debt.

“This is another regulatory guideline NRMLA has advocated for. ‘Hey, we’re paying off consumer debts. Let’s not count that toward the the qualification process.’ Proprietary allows us to do that and HECM does not,” Allen said.

FHA’s FY 2026 report includes a dedicated HECM chapter that details borrower profiles and loan features. These include HECM loan purposes (such as home purchases versus refinancing of existing debt), payment initiative options, interest rate types, borrower ages and maximum claim amounts and initial draws by recent borrower cohorts.

The agency emphasizes that its actuaries model HECM cash flows separately from forward mortgages and explicitly stress-test the reverse portfolio under varying home price appreciation scenarios.

In a recent development, home price appreciation assumptions affect multiple levers across the program — including the available equity at the end of the loan, the size of recoveries through property sales and the loss severity tied to claims. Indirectly, home price growth can also impact payoff incentives.

The report notes that waning price growth in 2026 had an “outsized negative impact” on HECM capital compared with forward mortgages. This underscores why even modest regional or national home price corrections are more consequential for HMBS investors and issuers than for traditional mortgage-backed securities.

The stand-alone capital ratio of 5.43% for the HECM portfolio suggests that FHA still has room to absorb stress, but far less cushion than in the prior three years when the ratio ranged between roughly 16% and 25%. It also may create more difficulty for industry advocates in their push to reduce upfront mortgage insurance premiums.

Sources indicate that in September, Ginnie Mae President Joe Gormley told RMD that the Trump administration is “very comfortable” with the current mortgage insurance structure for FHA single-family loans and reverse mortgages. Gormley’s comments prompted pushback from NRMLA, which said it would continue to “dialogue with them around more risk-based pricing.”.

An industry professional who was granted anonymity was more blunt in calling for lower premiums.

“The reality is this, if a client has to pay 2% to obtain the right to draw up to 30% of their home’s value, they will seek out other options,” the source mentioned. “It should come as no surprise that HEIs and HEAs have been popping up everywhere. Even the gains on the proprietary side are the result of FHA’s inaction. Clients would rather accrue 10% interest annually to a lender than pay 2% upfront to a government insurer.”.

The FHA report’s policy section does not identify any major upcoming structural changes to the HECM program, but it does highlight several items of operational relevance.

This includes contract funding reallocation for assigned HECM property preservation expenses. FHA mentions a reallocation of contract funding tied to property preservation on assigned HECM collateral. While it’s a technical issue, it could affect vendors and servicers who manage preservation and conveyance on properties with HECM balances that are acquired by the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development.

It has been revealed that fraud detection, data analytics and remediation were also mentioned. These areas are not limited to the HECM portfolio and apply across FHA single-family programs. Given the growing population of senior borrowers and the history of consumer protection scrutiny for reverse mortgages, these efforts may lead to tighter oversight of origination practices and borrower counseling outcomes in the HECM channel.

FHA also discussed ongoing system modernization efforts, including those tied to FHA Catalyst modules and uniform appraisal data initiatives. Over time, the report explained, better data capturing should improve the accuracy of HECM-backed property valuations — a key driver of assignment timing and loss estimates — and could result in more precise disclosures for HMBS investors.

The report also placed attention on the new permanent loss-mitigation waterfall for FHA forward mortgages that went into effect at the end of 2025. While that waterfall does not directly apply to HECMs, it signals a broader effort by the agency to standardize workout options and reduce cycles of repeated defaults. This approach could inform any future redesign of default resolution frameworks for reverse mortgages.
