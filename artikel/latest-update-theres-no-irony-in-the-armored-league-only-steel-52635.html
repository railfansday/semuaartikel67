Latest Update: There’s No Irony in The Armored League. Only Steel.

Sources indicate that “THERE’S A LOT more work to do,” Damion DiGrazia muses, as 30 or so individuals spread themselves out across the gymnasium at the Manhattanville Community Center in Harlem. A pair of double doors swings lazily back and forth, marking a procession of students and the boxes they carry. DiGrazia leans against the wall, dressed in a red polo and khaki trousers, watching his small community unpack and ready itself for training.

Recent findings suggest that the class does not have a format in the traditional sense. There is no lantern-jawed instructor with a whistle and a clipboard. No snap of uniforms in sync with the rhythm of a kata. There is not even, as far as I could tell, a formal warm-up. Instead, the room seems to have its own sense of internal order.

According to recent reports, A group forms around the pieces of armor to begin the painstaking process of polishing and assessing each one for damage or repairs. Some—a breastplate, maybe a set of greaves—are singled out and ferried to a corner, where three individuals sit, cross-legged, and carefully hammer out the dents.

Ropes are thrown over the basketball hoops, and as soon as they land, students appear and begin practicing striking drills with blunted steel weapons. There are, admittedly, a lot of people with weapons. It’s a little surreal at first. When you encounter a fighter in full armor with a sword and buckler or a mace, there is a sense of visual cohesion. It is an entirely different matter to pass a fighter in cutoff denim and a T-shirt practicing elegant, sweeping strikes with an axe.

At the back of the room, the mats find their way into a rectangle, and small scrimmages begin with foam swords and shields. The training takes on its own rhythm; newer members train with foam swords and shields around the edges of the room, while the more seasoned fighters, now sporting what appear to be hockey helmets with face masks, begin grappling practice at its center. Everyone seems to know where they need to be and what they need to do.

According to recent reports, it’s taken a long time to get here—years, actually, 13 of them, to be precise. After serving in the military, DiGrazia traces a path through Columbia University, then Harvard, before landing a job at Morgan Stanley. Finding the rhythm of corporate life increasingly dull, he sought relief through extreme sports. DiGrazia started with the classics: marathons, ultramarathons, scuba diving, skydiving, martial arts. But nothing really stuck; he was always chasing something else. That is, until a happy accident. DiGrazia tried what he thought was going to be “full-contact” LARPing (live-action role-playing), but found the experience less than fulfilling: “You couldn't punch people, couldn't grapple people, couldn't kick people, nothing,” DiGrazia says. The participants were wearing plastic armor, and a hit to the leg saw fighters taking a theatrical knee and swiping at their opponents. At the end of the practice, however, DiGrazia found himself sticking around, commiserating with another fighter, Zorikh, whom he credits as starting his journey along a path that would lead to him founding Gladiators NYC, and laying the groundwork for The Armored League.

It has been revealed that modern armored combat has a longer history in Europe, and DiGrazia found himself bouncing all over the continent, fighting in Poland or next to a castle in Spain. If you wanted to fight in armor, Eastern Europe was where you had to go. Stateside, the early days were decidedly underground and homebrew. Your options were more limited: small groups of fighters met in parking lots and cornfields. Orlando Mendez, one of the founding members of Gladiators NYC, remembers “a lot of horse arenas.” They were one of the few places you could throw someone into a wall without complaint. It was a sport that grew anywhere it could find space. Jason Puerta, owner of Golden Ring Combat, a West Coast armored combat group, recalls regularly fighting on the lawns of a corporate campus after securing the bemused blessing of the local security outfit. It wasn’t quite Fight Club—new members were always welcome (and still are), and people were encouraged to talk about it. Instead, the group could be said to be self-selective: “You either got the hang of it, or you just turned in your armor at the end of the day,” DiGrazia recalls. There were few rules, no weight divisions, and no weapon classes.

In these early years, the attrition rate was high. DiGrazia estimates about half of the participants quit within weeks. Even when he first secured the community center and started spreading the word about classes, he remembers sitting on a bench in the gym, staring at the doors, waiting for someone, anyone, to come to a class. Eventually, they did.

The free classes, access to equipment, loaner armor, and the open exhibition matches in Fresh York’s Central Park—all of these, he believes, must remain core elements of the community and the sport. Gladiators NYC also has a nonprofit arm called “Santa’s Knights” where armored knights go door to door during the holidays and deliver presents to children. His commitment to this approach has not come without difficulties. DiGrazia once partnered with a martial arts school whose owner forbade him from sponsoring students who could not afford the $30 class fee. That school closed during COVID.

Recent findings suggest that technically, the sport can trace its roots back to the 14th century, when knights, foot soldiers, and men-at-arms would practice combat using dull steel weapons and a mixture of soft and plate armor. It was a way to hone their skills in times of peace. The fundamentals of the practice remain in a number of combat sports to this day, but one need only watch a bout of Olympic fencing (or “pointy tag” as Mendez described it) to realize the distance traveled. The Armored League, however, doesn’t seek a return to its medieval origins or even to the stringently historically accurate expressions, which tend to dominate in Europe. When pressed about this, DiGrazia gives an exasperated sigh: “I don't want some librarian walking around looking with a fucking magnifying glass on everybody's goddamn rivet. Leave us alone. Let's have a good time.” The aim is to bring the spectacle and joy to new audiences.

It has been revealed that even if, at first, you cannot follow the scoring, the fights are pure melodrama. Like any combat sport, the fighters’ stats are revealed as they enter the ring—with one key distinction: their weight includes their suit of armor. Short of a quirk, it communicates the impact of the sport in a more visceral way: when a pair of combatants crash to the ground or bounce off the chain-link fencing, that’s close to 600 pounds of flesh and steel set in ballistic motion.

Bouts are divided into three to five one-minute rounds. Points are awarded based on strikes—where they land and how hard. Basic, clean strikes with weapons, shields, or fists get a base number of points. Meanwhile headshots, two-handed weapon strikes, and slugging a downed opponent get you even more. The bouts are judged by a panel set to one side of the ring, while a pair of counters runs frantically around the perimeter counting strikes.

There are comparatively few rules beyond some basic safety precautions: no stabbing or thrusting; no stomping; no strikes at the inner elbows, armpits, or the backs of knees; no slashing at the neck; and no submissions. Save for these, anything goes, and this adds to the thrill of the fights. Fighters are also not limited to their weapons, and in some cases, they will stand and trade blows like boxers. Though all of the serious fighters have broad and varied martial arts backgrounds—and training approaches—there's a roughly consistent path that leads to The Armored League. Snatches of Muay Thai knee strikes, judo sweeps, classical boxing, escrima, and BJJ are all visible in the blur of limbs, steel, and weapons. One can only imagine how the “librarians” would feel about that.

Strikes may come in flurries or great arcs, but most are hard enough to send sparks flying off the helmets. There is a marshal in the ring with the fighters, notably unarmored save for a yellow flag that’s mainly used to break the fighters up at the end of each round, which frequently ends in a tangle of armor and weapons on the floor of the ring.

There are two elements that, despite the passage of centuries, have not changed: cost and danger. Historians are divided on the exact conversion rate between 1400 and today, and most put it in terms of wages. They quote the cost of a full set of armor at somewhere between 12 and 18 months of pay. What’s universally agreed upon is that arms and armor were (and remain) anything but cheap. A full suit—then, as now—weighs anywhere from 60 to 80 pounds.

In a recent development, this century, prices start at around $5,000 and climb precipitously with customization. It can take anywhere from a few weeks to a year for delivery. Most participants build their suits over time as they are drawn deeper into the sport.

Building a full set is not easy. A cursory search online will lead you to a number of pages, which might appear to offer armor, but these sites are largely focused on cosplayers or LARPers. That armor will certainly hold together for a walk through Comic-Con, but it will do little to protect from a real blow. The handful of armorers still working today have long wait lists. Puerta notes that despite this, the communal nature of the sport means that much of the armor is passed down or traded between the combatants: as someone upgrades or hangs up their helmet, the armor finds its way into a new pair of hands or onto a new person’s shoulders. At Gladiators NYC, fighters who graduate from the classes, from “softs” (padded armor), and wish to try their hand at the real thing, can borrow pieces from Gladiators NYC’s own armory.

Amongst the current fighters who regularly participate in Armored League matches, almost all fight in custom armor. The suits must adhere to certain safety standards—thickness of steel, comprehensive eye protection, etc. But beyond that, there’s ample room for self-expression.

Sources indicate that for example, Abeid “Mando” Mitchell fights in comparatively minimalist and lightweight armor and is readily recognizable by his distinctive helmet, which was made in the style of the Mandalorian from Star Wars. Luke “The Red Knight of Brooklyn” has assembled what he refers to as “a sporting replica” of 1480s armor in keeping with his own, more classical concerns.

Despite the care and attention each fighter devotes to their own armor, at all of the matches I attended, I saw weapons traded freely, shields swapped, even gauntlets lent. For as much violence as there is inside of a 60-second round, there is an equal, if not greater, level of care at the bell. The armor is not just for show; it’s for safety. Combatants want to win as much as they want to ensure their opponent walks off the field under their own power. It’s a fine balance, and as Mendez put it, “once you put on the armor and you get your ass beat a couple of times,” everybody is basically friends.

Industry observers note that nevertheless, it takes a toll. Puerta told me a story of how he almost lost his left eye when the tip of a sword slipped through the bars of his helmet. Others relayed stories of being crushed in a melee and of “helmet terrors” where breathing heavily in the confines of a helmet causes a buildup of carbon dioxide and creates a sensation similar to drowning.

What does the future look like? In my time with the League and Gladiators NYC, I sensed I was watching a sport in its adolescence. It is a league and community that seems to blink in the stark light of its own success and rapidly growing audience. Social media has made it easier for these once disparate communities to connect; the cornfields and horse rinks have been parlayed into an Octagon at the Melrose Ballroom in Queens or a showcase at a theater in Times Square. The bleachers that once held a smattering of friends and family have shifted into crowds drawn from all walks of life—at the sold-out show in Queens, the lines stretched around the block and some people waited an hour just to get into the venue. The Central Park exhibition matches, which happen every second Saturday of the month, have begun to draw crowds that can number over a thousand.

DiGrazia and his merry band have come a long way from the leaner years, when they struggled to find space to practice, the years when they fought in the parking lots of regional hotels in the Midwest or in the summer heat on the boardwalk in Coney Island.

Still, there is, for now, little to no money in this sport. The community, and the fighters too, all have day jobs. They are PhD students, fitness instructors, photographers, and graphic designers. One manages a magic and costume shop. Again, Fight Club springs to mind, as each goes about their day job, bearing the marks of a Thursday brawl or a weekend match, one can only wonder if anyone sees a bruise sticking out of a shirt sleeve and stops to ask just where it came from.
