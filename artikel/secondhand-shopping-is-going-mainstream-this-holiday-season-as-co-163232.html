Secondhand shopping is going mainstream this holiday season as consumers seek value

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

As the high cost of living squeezes consumers' wallets this holiday season, more shoppers are turning to secondhand retail and thrifting.

It has been revealed that the shift comes as consumers look for better value, buyers put more emphasis on personalization and younger generations change the way they shop. The U.S. secondhand apparel market grew 13% in 2025, nearly four times faster than the overall retail clothing market, according to GlobalData.

That segment is expected to reach nearly $80 billion by 2030, growing more than 7% annually on average, GlobalData found. Much of that expansion — 40% — was driven by Generation Z, followed closely by millennials.

In a recent development, "I think this has been their version of the thrill of the hunt that millennials loved during the recession days," Katherine Cullen, a global senior retail analyst at EY, told CNBC. "It's a way to really express an individual style, find something unique, something that really fits their own vibe and personality.".

It goes beyond people shopping for themselves, Cullen said. With more emphasis on unique holiday gifts, shoppers are leaning more on secondhand retail than they have before, whether in clothing or in other retail categories.

They're looking for something that fits the personality of the person they're shopping for, she added.

In a recent development, for Vanessa Roberts, that meant finding two antique children's chairs that she notes will perfectly round out her nieces' play space. Roberts, 37, noted she's been thrifting for at least two decades, each time embracing the "thrill" of finding something unique or vintage.

Especially in fashion, at a time when the quality of mass-produced items has decreased, thrifted items offer more value beyond just the price tag, Roberts added.

"It's crazy that you can find better quality items at the thrift store right now from 20 years ago versus what you can find at stores right now," she stated.

Roberts, who lives in Los Angeles, said she began shopping for holiday gifts in September. As she prepares her haul for December, roughly 70% of her gifting will come from secondhand stores, she estimated.

Industry observers note that "It's been a fun little challenge, where it's like a little scavenger hunt when I go into the thrift store," she stated. "I'm definitely thrifting for the holidays – I do it every year.".

The trend coincides with an economy that has forced consumers to be more intentional about how they spend, as they face high inflation and elevated gas prices.

In a recent development, in October, the consumer price index rose a seasonally adjusted 0.4%, which was more than anticipated. Still, consumers are expected to continue spending through the holiday season, with sales projected to top $1 trillion for the first time — though some of that estimated increase is boosted by inflation.

"You have a consumer who, while they're willing to spend at the top level, they're willing to splurge selectively," Cullen stated. "Consumers are looking for value, which I think is something that is making secondhand resale and re-commerce more attractive.".

And while clothing is largely the entry point to buying resale, secondhand shopping is increasingly expanding into furniture, electronics and books, according to a report from consumer insights firm The Consumer Collective.

Its October report found that 34% of people surveyed remarked secondhand products offer better value than brand fresh ones.

Recent findings suggest that many secondhand items also have an inherent added value beyond the low price on the tag, according to eBay CEO Jamie Iannone. The uniqueness of those products, as well as the allure that vintage adds to them, means consumers may actually be seeking the benefits of secondhand in more ways than one.

"I can buy a product that's used, and it almost has cachet around it," Iannone told CNBC. "Consumers today, they're able to get these amazing values on products, and it's seen as very normal to be gifting pre-loved.".

According to eBay's Recommerce Report in October, 70% of global consumers surveyed said they consider secondhand shopping important for managing their budgets. That same report also found that 89% of respondents blueprint to spend the same amount or more on secondhand products next year compared with this year.

Iannone said he thinks the interest in secondhand this holiday season is "much stronger" than last year.

"It's becoming just a more established part of consumers' shopping and gifting routines," he mentioned.

At a time when algorithms are driving shopping trends, consumers are also looking for ways to distinguish themselves and their purchases.

That's stretching beyond buying for themselves to purchasing holiday gifts for others, too.

In a recent development, "Secondhand is where they're finding unique items to wear," Alon Rotem, chief strategy officer at ThredUp, told CNBC. The company's holiday survey found that respondents valued uniqueness nearly as much as the value of a thrifted piece.

"To me, that represents a pretty meaningful shift in consumer taste," he added.

It's partly why ThredUp, which is an online thrift marketplace, emphasizes that each of its products is "a snowflake," Rotem mentioned — something unique.

That's the appeal of buying preowned, rather than purchasing off the rack that hundreds of other people are reaching for, too.

It has been revealed that "Consumer tastes have changed, and they're much more into vintage," he added. "Uniqueness has a premium in their minds.".

Larger retailers are taking note, EY's Cullen said. More retailers, such as Lululemon, Patagonia and Madewell, are dabbling in preowned.

Recent findings suggest that wall Street is also increasingly watching the growing secondhand trend. Though shares of some secondhand and resale companies such as eBay and Savers Value Village have climbed this year, shares of both ThredUp and The RealReal have tumbled year to date.

"There's a lot of conversation around authenticity or the best find," Cullen remarked. "I think that interest and momentum, along with some of the economic factors that we're all facing, the idea that people are looking to be frugal is going to just continue.".
