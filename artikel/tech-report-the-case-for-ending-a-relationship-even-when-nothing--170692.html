📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a recent development, during my sophomore year of high school, in the throes of the COVID-19 pandemic, I started a relationship with my first love. While I am known to be a Type A planner, I tried to live in the moment, experiencing all the butterflies, first-times, and ups and downs of adolescent affection, just like my idols in the best coming-of-age movies.

It has been revealed that it wasn’t until the start of the college application process, two years later, that my love-blinded bubble popped and I realized we simply wanted different things. But looking back, I wonder how much of my break up decision was about the relationship itself, and how much was about the life I was looking forward to in college and beyond.

I think that’s common for a lot of young people in relationships. Many of us break up at young ages not because there’s something wrong with the other or the dynamic—there's no cheating, no explosive fight, and no obvious villain—but because we feel like we should. I’ve witnessed friends who are perfectly happy together in their early 20s call it quits for the fear of missing out on the future experiences that are a fundamental part of being young.

Industry observers note that if I’m being honest, I think it’s good to expand your dating portfolio before finding your forever love; there's great value in diversifying. Different relationships introduce us to different versions of ourselves, revealing what we need, what we're willing to compromise on, what we never want to experience again and simply what we do and don’t lie. And while I admire the people who are “one and done,” I can't help but wonder how you could know what you want forever if you've only ever known one version of someone—mainly yourself.

In relation to my first love, I knew I was ready to end things, but remained in limbo for months. Not only was that a waste of his time, but it was stupid to waste my own. Looking back, I don't regret the relationship, nor do I regret leaving it. Since then, I've loved and been loved by different people, for different reasons, and learned something new about myself each time. I'm not saying you have to date half of your city before settling down, or that those who stay with their first love are missing out. For some couples, growing up together is precisely what makes their relationship so special and I would never knock something like that. But I do believe there’s also value in choosing to leave a relationship even if there isn’t anything wrong with it—not for the reason that it’s all you’ve ever known, but because you realize you want to know more.
