In a recent development, fresh Jersey legislators are considering a bill that would be the first in the nation to define “Islamophobia.” It’s not merely descriptive, however. It is so overbroad that it would make it all but impossible to evaluate Islam and the culture it produces in any meaningful way.

That is because a provision in it mandates that public officials and law enforcement officers use the bill’s definitions when considering whether a civil or criminal law has been violated.

It is sure to run into American protections for free speech that are unusually strong even by Western standards. Adherents of every religion have learned over the centuries that hearing their faith smeared, even irrationally (Islamophobia means “an irrational fear of Islam”), is a price they want to pay.

The bill comes as the election of several Muslims to political office nationwide has refocused attention on the nature of Islam and especially its political dimension, or Islamism.

Television scenes of Muslim youth ransacking stores, government offices, and national symbols in France and throughout Europe have also drawn attention to the capacity, or even willingness, of Western societies to assimilate immigrants from the Muslim world. A political alliance between Islamism and the Left is another factor often discussed.

The Latest Jersey bill, A1146, is moreover championed by the Council on American-Islamic Relations, a pressure group devoted to increasing the number of Muslim elected officials, but which has been declared a “foreign terrorist organization” in Texas and Florida because of its association with the Muslim Brotherhood.

>>> Free Speech Under Siege in France.

All of this puts A1146 in the crossroads of a volatile debate growing in America and around the world—a debate which A1146 seems expressly made to shut down.

Sources indicate that the clause at the very end of the bill requiring that the definitions be used in investigations makes clear that what is at hand is not merely an exercise in informing the citizenry about what constitutes Islamophobia (already a loaded term). It claims in full that:.

In reviewing, investigating, or deciding whether there has been a violation of any policy, law, or regulation prohibiting discriminatory acts under the civil or criminal laws of this State, a public official or law enforcement officer shall take into consideration the definition of Islamophobia contained in subsection a. of this section for the purposes of determining whether the alleged act was motivated by Islamophobia and discriminatory anti-Muslim intent.

Subsection A has 13 different definitions that would catch in its nettle now viewed as legitimate. It starts by asserting that Islamophobia includes “hatred or severe disparagement towards Muslim persons or their culture.”.

The second would also chill debate, saying that Islamophobia includes “hostility and fear of Islam, and as a result, a fear of and aversion to all Muslims or the majority of Muslims.”.

Same for No. 5, which includes “making mendacious, dehumanizing, demonizing, or stereotypical allegations about Muslims, or of Muslims as a collective group.”.

Recent findings suggest that no. 11 carries no small degree of irony with regard to the Garden State. It stipulates that Islamophobia includes “accusing Muslim citizens of being more loyal to a transnational Muslim community or to their countries of origin, or to the alleged priorities of Muslims worldwide, than to the interests of the people of Upcoming Jersey or the interests of the people of the United States.”.

But it just so happens that Adam Hamawy, an observant Muslim just elected to the U.S. House of Representatives in Upcoming Jersey’s 12th District, was repeatedly asked on CNN’s Situation Room whether he views Iran, a country we’re at war with, as an adversary—and he declined to do so.

The astonished CNN journalist even reminded him that Iranians are made to chant “Death to America,” to which he responded, “We’ve been saying ‘Death to Iran’ for decades as well.” Supposedly, the bill in question would limit how we could talk about this.

Debate over different cultural practices and the societal outcomes that derive from them goes to the heart of policymaking. Making it illegal to discuss the results of any religious practice, whether Islamic, Catholic, Jewish, or Mormon, would manacle such debate.

Deliberation of this type is indeed seen as needed now more than ever, which is why the Heritage Foundation, where I work, last week published three papers on this very issue.

Sources indicate that one, by Victoria Coates, Jay Richards, and Roger Severino, cuts to the quick by asking, “How does our republic relate to—and depend on—the religious, legal, and cultural soil from which it emerged? And what domestic and international forces put it at risk? These questions have gained fresh currency amid rising tensions with an assertive Islam, both at home and abroad.”.

Sources indicate that as the paper points out, religious doctrine shapes individual and group behavior, and includes premises and conclusions that the Newly introduced Jersey law could make illegal. For example, it states that “hundreds of millions of Muslims hold views that both guide behavior and contradict key principles of the American system.”.

The Heritage report makes another vital point when it observes that “Western European countries, including the United Kingdom and France, are now experiencing recurring conflicts tantamount to a national identity crisis related to large, unassimilated Muslim populations.”.

Yet the authors of the Upcoming Jersey bill explicitly admit they took their cue from the United Kingdom, writing that their definitions were “modeled in part after the All-Party Parliamentary Group (APPG), which is composed of British Parliament Members.”.

The All-Party Parliamentary Group’s 2018 report defining Islamophobia was adopted by several political parties and institutions, despite its potential infringement on free speech.

As our paper made clear, “Britain provides a warning of what happens when a Western state lacks the courage to prevent fully parallel Islamic structures that ultimately compete with, if not displace, civil law.”.

The APPG report was strongly influenced by the Muslim Council of Britain—which, according to another of the papers we published last week, was established and is run by the Muslim Brotherhood.

The Brotherhood, writes French anthropologist Florence Bergeaud-Blackler in that other paper, “has engaged in a century-old project to ‘Islamify modernity’ through gradual, lawful, multisector action” and it is doing so “across Europe, and now across the United States.”.

Which makes CAIR’s involvement with the Newly introduced Jersey bill all the more attention-grabbing, given its links with the Brotherhood.

Founded in Cairo, Egypt, in 1928, the Muslim Brotherhood is a transnational Islamist terrorist group that is banned in its home country as well as others throughout the Middle East.

In 1987, the Brotherhood created as its Palestinian branch the terrorist group Hamas. The Brotherhood also has ties to the Lebanese terrorist group Hezbollah.

How is CAIR tied to the Brotherhood? In 2007, the U.S. Justice Department named CAIR “an unindicted co-conspirator” in the Holy Land Foundation terrorism financing case.

Two CAIR co-founders, Omar Ahmad and Nihad Awad, were respectively the president and public relations director of the Islamic Association of Palestine, the public-relations arm of a Palestinian committee founded by the Brotherhood. Another co-founder, Rafeeq Jabar, was IAP president at one point.

All of this would be very difficult to discuss in the Garden State if the bill becomes law. “If this law passes, accusations of Islamophobia could be used to equate any criticism of Islam or Islamism with an attack on Muslims,” Bergeaud-Blackler texted me. “Fear of being accused of Islamophobia could lead journalists, researchers, and anyone who speaks critically about Islam in public debate to censor themselves.”.

Yes, we can expect that A1146, if passed, will face First Amendment challenges in the courts, but these are not sure to succeed.
