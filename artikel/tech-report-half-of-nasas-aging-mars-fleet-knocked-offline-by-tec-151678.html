Tech Report: Half of NASA's aging Mars fleet knocked offline by technical issues

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Two of NASA’s four missions operating at Mars have suspended science operations after encountering separate technical problems in recent weeks.

Recent findings suggest that the missions affected by the outages—hopefully temporary—are NASA’s Perseverance rover and Odyssey orbiter. Both are operating beyond their original design lifetimes. Perseverance is in its sixth year exploring the surface of Mars, having collected rock and soil samples for eventual return to Earth. The rover is now studying the rim of a crater that once contained an ancient lake of liquid water. Odyssey arrived in orbit around Mars in 2001 and is the longest-lived Mars mission in history.

It has been revealed that the problem on Perseverance stems from the rover’s communications system. The rover has two redundant UHF radios for high-rate communications with a fleet of orbiters that relay data to Earth. A separate X-band system is available for direct-to-Earth communications at a lower data rate.

Recent findings suggest that one of the rover’s two UHF radios was unresponsive when it powered up on October 30, triggering an automatic switch to the backup radio and placing the rover in safe mode. Ground teams at NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory in California later discovered the backup UHF radio was transmitting at about half of its expected power output, NASA mentioned in a statement last week.

“The team has stopped using both radios until they can evaluate potential causes of the challenges and one or both are deemed safe to resume operating,” NASA remarked.

In a recent development, perseverance is now transmitting signals directly to Earth in X-band, but the connection provides a “significantly lower data rate” than relaying signals through orbiters using the UHF radios. Mission managers paused the rover’s science operations “out of an abundance of caution” as they investigate the radio problem. Their initial focus is on getting the backup UHF radio running before turning their attention to recovering the primary radio, NASA noted.

“The team is hopeful the rover could return to science by the end of November.”.

The six-wheeled robot broke a record last month for the farthest any vehicle has driven on another planet. The Perseverance rover surpassed the 28.06-mile (45.16-kilometer) mark set by NASA’s Opportunity rover, which stopped working in 2018. Perseverance’s odometer now sits at twice the rover’s original goal for distance traveled.

The situation with the Odyssey orbiter seems more dire. One of the four reaction wheels used for pointing the spacecraft stopped spinning in early November, leaving Odyssey with two good wheels after the first one failed in 2013. The orbiter needs at least three wheels to keep it oriented correctly.

Recent findings suggest that “Without the three reaction wheels, the spacecraft must rely solely on its hydrazine thrusters to remain oriented,” NASA remarked. The orbiter requires stable pointing to turn its solar arrays toward the Sun and its science instruments toward Mars.

Odyssey was running low on propellant before the reaction wheel malfunction, but managers were optimistic that with “extremely low” fuel usage, the orbiter would have enough to continue operating through at least the end of 2028, said Jeffrey Plaut, Odyssey’s project scientist at JPL, in a presentation earlier this year.

It has been revealed that that assumed no failures in Odyssey’s three remaining reaction wheels. Without three healthy wheels, engineers estimate the spacecraft has enough hydrazine fuel to last one to two months, NASA said. The good news is that ground teams had some initial success troubleshooting the balky reaction wheel. Further testing is necessary to determine whether the wheel is recoverable.

Odyssey is one of four orbiters NASA uses to relay data between Earth and the Perseverance and Curiosity rovers on the Martian surface. Curiosity is still driving and collecting data after 14 years on Mars. The other three relay craft are NASA’s Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter, which reached the red planet in 2006, the European Space Agency’s 10-year-old ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter, and ESA’s 23-year-old Mars Express spacecraft.

NASA lost contact with its MAVEN orbiter last December after 11 years of operations at Mars when the spacecraft suddenly spun out of control. An investigation could not identify a specific root cause, but engineers concluded that a radiation-induced single event upset or a software concern caused MAVEN’s flight computer to reboot, according to Tiffany Morgan, director of NASA’s robotic Mars Exploration Program.

MAVEN’s inertial measurement unit failed to provide the computer with correct information on the spacecraft’s orientation, causing it to spin in response to the faulty data, Morgan noted in a November 9 presentation to the Mars Exploration Program Analysis Group.

Industry observers note that NASA is preparing to send reinforcements to renew the agency’s presence at Mars. In September, NASA awarded Blue Origin a $700 million contract for the Mars Telecommunications Network (MTN), an orbiter dedicated to providing high-bandwidth connectivity between Earth and other missions operating on and around the red planet.

Industry observers note that NASA wanted Blue Origin to complete the telecom orbiter by the end of 2028 and have it operational at Mars in 2030. But the Mars Telecommunications Network is on hold after Rocket Lab, which lost its bid to win the MTN contract, filed a protest with the Government Accountability Office. The GAO is expected to rule on the protest next month, at which time Blue Origin could start work on the NASA contract if the government rejects Rocket Lab’s petition.

Recent findings suggest that another Mars mission, SkyFall, could also launch in 2028. NASA aims to send three helicopter drones to Mars to explore the planet’s geology and weather from the air.

Recent findings suggest that A perennial priority for Mars scientists is replacing the unique high-resolution mapping camera on the aging Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter. Such a capability will help identify landing sites for future robotic and human missions, including a potential mission to return Perseverance’s rock specimens to Earth.

In a recent development, NASA is in the very early stages of developing a mission concept that could take over MRO’s imaging capability, but there’s no firm schedule or budget for such an undertaking. NASA will likely rely on MRO for landing-site reconnaissance well into the 2030s.
