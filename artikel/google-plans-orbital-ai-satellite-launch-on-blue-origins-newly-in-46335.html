📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that google declared on November 19, 2026 that Blue Origin’s Upcoming Glenn will conduct Project Suncatcher’s first dedicated heavy-lift launch, targeted for the first quarter of 2029. The companies’ launch services agreement covers deployment of solar-powered satellites carrying Google’s Tensor Processing Units, or TPUs, into sun-synchronous orbit.

Each satellite is designed to provide computing capacity roughly at the scale of a data center rack. The mission is part of Google’s research into whether an interconnected network of solar-powered satellites could scale machine-learning compute in space. The dedicated launch also builds on Google’s continuing work with other satellite and launch partners.

In a recent development, blue Origin’s Newly introduced Glenn specifications give the vehicle general lift ratings of 45 metric tons to low Earth orbit and more than 13 metric tons to geostationary transfer orbit. The seven-meter payload fairing provides twice the volume of smaller five-meter-class fairings, while the reusable first stage is designed for a minimum of 25 flights.

Jordan Charles, senior vice president of New Glenn at Blue Origin, said the rocket’s “heavy-lift capacity and large payload volume give customers the flexibility to bring new capabilities to orbit.” For Suncatcher, that combination is intended to serve a multi-satellite deployment, moving beyond the single-prototype stage of the research.

The agreement follows Google’s September 2026 Project Suncatcher prototype preview and its subsequent in-orbit TPU response. A prototype satellite launched on October 1, 2026; by October 30, 2026, Google had powered the host and TPU trays and run early machine-learning tests during commissioning. Its onboard Gemma 4 model generated a haiku, which Google described as the first AI response from a data-center-class TPU in orbit. The disclosed plan continues with thermal-stress and radiation testing, followed by a planned 2027 milestone of two interconnected satellites.

Google indicates satellites in low Earth orbit can access near-constant sunlight and generate up to eight times more solar power than on Earth, forming Project Suncatcher’s energy rationale. Proposed future designs place dozens of TPUs on each satellite and use lasers for high-bandwidth communication over short distances between spacecraft orbiting in clusters.

Beyond power generation, the proposed cluster poses cooling and communications problems. In a vacuum, chips cannot rely on airflow; engineers have tested heat pipes and radiators in a thermal vacuum chamber. The laser links must maintain high bandwidth over short distances between moving spacecraft, requiring precise knowledge of each satellite’s position relative to its neighbors.

Sources indicate that the agreement supplies a heavy-lift path for a larger research deployment, while the prototype and twin-satellite work must still establish the essentials of orbital computing: reliable chips, effective cooling and precise intersatellite communication. That sequence makes the upcoming tests central to whether Suncatcher can progress from individual machines running in orbit to a networked machine-learning system.
