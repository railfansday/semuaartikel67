Latest Update: OpenAI bugging case defendant Alfozan set for trial in January

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a recent development, A federal judge on Tuesday set Jan. 20 for the start of the trial for Saudi national Abdulrahman Alfozan, who is accused of planting a recording device in an OpenAI conference room when he worked for the artificial intelligence manufacturer as an engineering lead.

It has been revealed that if the trial starts on that date in U.S. District Court in San Francisco, it would be after an unusually short lag since Alfozan's arrest in October. Federal criminal cases, as a rule, do not go to trial so quickly.

But Alfozan's attorney, David Rizk, sought the speedy trial after complaining at a court hearing that prosecutors have been slow in turning over evidence in the case.

Rizk also said prosecutors have not given him any evidence that Alfozan engaged in espionage, after they argued successfully last month that he should be detained without bail, and after saying his alleged bugging "could pose a danger to the national security of the United States.".

Among other things, prosecutors had noted that Alfozan is a venture partner at Saudi venture firm Raed Ventures alongside a member of the Saudi royal family, Prince Khalid bin Bader Al Saud.

It has been revealed that alfozan, who has pleaded not guilty in the case, is charged with one count of intercepting oral communications. He faces a maximum of five years in prison if he is convicted.

Prosecutors have stated Alfozan was arrested after staff at OpenAI found a recording device wrapped in toilet paper and installed in the back of a television in the conference room at its San Francisco headquarters in September. A surveillance camera captured Alfozan looking at the gadget in the room.

Assistant U.S. Attorney Nick Parker told Judge Vince Chhabria on Tuesday that government investigators have been locked out of Alfozan's phone, but they have also collected one other phone, a tablet, and two laptops.

Prosecutors also obtained 160 hours of recordings from meetings in a conference room.

The recording device itself is encrypted, but if data was backed up to another device, investigators would have been able to obtain it, Parker mentioned.

According to recent reports, chhabria told prosecutors that they must turn over all reports the FBI has created to Alfozan's attorney by Nov. 24, and that they must also move promptly to give the defense other evidence for use at trial.

"We need to make sure that you get whatever discovery you're entitled to promptly," Chhabria told Rizk.

OpenAI, which fired Alfozan after his arrest, has mentioned it lacked proof of him taking source code or customer data.

Chhabria also told Parker he wants to see, within seven days, any evidence that might exist showing Alfozan carrying out economic espionage, stealing trade secrets or acting as an unregistered foreign agent.

Parker remarked he was not aware of any such evidence at this time.

"At this point, I'm not suggesting the defendant stole anything," Parker remarked.

The prosecutor told the judge that his team had a "serious concern" about Rizk's looks to review evidence turned over to the defense with an AI tool that competes with OpenAI. Parker noted that among the seized evidence is a laptop that might have proprietary OpenAI data.

Chhabria replied, "I'm a lot less concerned about that than you are.".

The judge ordered Parker to submit a motion by Friday explaining why the defense's use of a competing AI to analyze evidence might be a problem.

Rizk told CNBC he intends to use Anthropic's Claude. Anthropic, which aims to go public on Nasdaq this week, was founded by OpenAI employees.

— CNBC's Dan Mangan contributed to this report.
