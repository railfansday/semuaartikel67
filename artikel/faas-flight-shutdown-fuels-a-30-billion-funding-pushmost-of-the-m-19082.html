📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a recent development, after the FAA communications breakdown brought departures across the country to a halt on Friday, the travel industry has its answer: give air traffic control another $30 billion. Most of the money in the bill they’re pushing wouldn’t even go to air traffic control. The U.S. Travel Association says Friday’s ground stop demonstrates that the FAA “lacks the resources necessary to modernize America’s aging aviation infrastructure.” It wants Congress to pass the Modern Skies Act sponsored by Senator Eric Schmitt (R-MO) before the end of this Congress. That legislation would provide $30.1 billion, including $15 billion for airport terminals. It would leave the FAA running air traffic control under essentially the same institutional structure that’s spent decades failing to deliver promised improvements. We need modern equipment. We also need to modernize the organization buying and operating it. Using this failure to sell airport terminal subsidies does neither particularly well. A Local Equipment Change Disrupted Most Of The National Network The nationwide departure stop began at 3:54 p.m. Eastern Friday and lasted until 8:15 p.m. The airline recovery took much longer. There were over 18,000 cancellations from Friday through Sunday. Planes and crews wound up in the wrong places. Crew duty limits and missed connections kept the disruption going after communications stabilized. The FAA identifies two sites: a radio facility in El Dorado, Arkansas, where contractor L3Harris was installing a router, and a radar facility in Amarillo, Texas. Excessive connection attempts flooded their ancient private network, disrupting aircraft communications and radar feeds at 17 of 22 en route centers and spreading into approach control facilities. Blocking the two sites stabilized the network. Officials also disabled the broadcasting function on about 60 similar routers. Some radio frequencies and radar feeds kept working, allowing aircraft already airborne to land. FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford describes the work as routine maintenance, separate from the new $12.5 billion modernization program. That makes the question about managing the system day to day even more pressing if it can destabilize the whole system with only the third nationwide ground stop in U.S. history.
