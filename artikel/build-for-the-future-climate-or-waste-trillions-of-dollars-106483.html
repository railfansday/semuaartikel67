Build for the future climate — or waste trillions of dollars

“Prepare for El Niño”, the World Meteorological Organization warned earlier this year. Governments are responding by adjusting agricultural practices and securing water supplies. But climate measures should not begin when a hazardous event approaches, they should be part of a wider strategy of planning for a warmer future.

Sources indicate that repeated disasters cause immense disruption to people’s lives and livelihoods, and the economic loss and damage can add up to trillions of dollars. Climate warnings and actions taken in anticipation of disasters can help to reduce the impact on people and economies. But their effectiveness is constrained by existing limitations, such as a community’s access to resources and infrastructure.

Such vulnerabilities must be addressed at their source, when decisions about development and infrastructure are being made, so that climate risks are reduced through better planning rather than through reactive responses to individual events. Decisions about the location and capacity of reservoirs, agricultural drainage systems and the design of cities and transport infrastructure, for instance, cannot be changed easily once they have been made.

According to recent reports, much of what is being built now will shape societies for decades. To meet global climate and development objectives by 2030, nearly US$7 trillion of infrastructure investment is needed annually1. Global annual infrastructure spending is projected to increase from $4.4 trillion in 2024 to $6.9 trillion by 20502. But it will be wasted if projects are designed inadequately for the climate conditions that people will face.

In a recent development, decisions for development projects are being hampered because climate projections are often not integrated into planning processes. Another challenge is the limited availability of reliable, decision-relevant climate information for the 5–30-year period — an intermediate timescale between seasonal forecasts and long-term climate projections that is particularly relevant for planning major infrastructure such as roads and reservoirs3.

Sources indicate that climate services can provide this information. Scientists can improve climate projections. Development planners can assess projects. But unless these functions are connected within the development process, the system as a whole remains reactive.

A common operating system would change that. Government ministries that oversee development would determine which planning decisions need to consider climate information. Planning institutions would ensure that climate assessments are undertaken and considered as part of investment approval for climate-sensitive projects. Climate agencies would provide the scientific information, which would improve both planning and science. Here, I outline how such a system would work.

According to recent reports, A central government planning institution should establish a small climate-planning unit within it, not separately. Corresponding units could be set up in state or subnational planning institutions when appropriate. In countries without central planning institutions, the same functions could be assigned to existing government institutions.

The central unit would set common standards, consolidate information needs across sectors and jurisdictions, coordinate with national climate agencies and ensure that climate considerations are incorporated into the planning and appraisal of projects of national significance. Subnational units would apply the framework to local projects and feed emerging requirements back to the central unit.

Responsibilities should be made explicit. Ministries should identify the climate information needed to support various sectors. Planning institutions would coordinate those needs and ensure that such climate information informs investment decisions. Climate agencies would produce the underlying scientific information and ensure its technical accuracy and quality.

This division matters. The proposal is not to make development planners become climate scientists, nor to make climate agencies become development authorities. It is to give them a permanent institutional connection.

The rules governing public investment in development should be changed to follow this principle: funding for a major climate-sensitive project should not be approved without a demonstration that it has been designed for the climate it is expected to face.

Governments should request climate assessments for large, long-lived and climate-sensitive projects before approving funds. Assessments should be provided early enough in the process to influence the location, technology, scale and design of a project. For a new reservoir, for example, the relevant planning unit would identify the climate conditions that the reservoir is likely to face during its operational lifetime, with implications for where and how it is built.

Industry observers note that A climate-planning unit would not decide whether the project should proceed; it would certify that the relevant climate risks have been assessed and that reasonable alternatives have been considered. Its assessment would be considered as part of an investment appraisal alongside economic, technical, social and environmental factors.

To be effective, the system must connect three functions: development demand, climate analysis and investment approval.

Recent findings suggest that ministries should periodically identify the climate information needed for their forthcoming development decisions. The central planning unit should consolidate these requests and work with climate agencies to determine what information can be provided, what projects require fresh analysis and when investment for climate observations, modelling or scientific capacity is needed.

According to recent reports, this comprehensive approach would provide climate scientists with clear directives from the development system. It would encourage them to improve climate projections for specific regions, over a 5–30-year typical planning horizon, and for distinct sectors. It would also reduce the need for individual development projects to commission their own climate analyses, which can be patchy and inconsistent.

Industry observers note that an analysis of six generations of assessment reports from the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change found, for instance, that inconsistent climate projections across many low- and middle-income countries were complicating adaptation and long-term planning efforts4. For example, in some regions, precipitation was projected by one assessment to increase, and by the next to decrease, with successive views bouncing back and forth.

Such disagreements undermine a region’s ability to make long-term investments. They increase the likelihood of costly misallocation of funds, maladaptation to climate risks and infrastructure being developed that is ill-suited to future climate conditions. Dams, cities and transport corridors cannot be easily redesigned or rebuilt once constructed.

This challenge is compounded by the fact that climate models perform poorly across many climate-vulnerable regions. In South Asia, for example, simulations of temperature and precipitation can differ from observed values by as much as 25% and 100%, respectively5. Such biases limit the reliability of model-based climate information for guiding long-term development decisions.

That’s why the proposed climate-informed planning units would ensure that climate agencies’ work is guided by clearly articulated development needs and that the information they provide is reliable and fit for development planning. Importantly, they would not be a replacement for other climate services, which can remain focused on providing accurate information for a wide range of sectors6,7. The aim is to bridge a specific gap between climate information and development.

Industry observers note that A climate assessment for a development project should not become obsolete as soon as the work is approved. Both central and subnational planning units should regularly monitor whether new climate observations or scientific assessments change the climate risks identified. If they do, an updated assessment should be returned to the planning authority.

Industry observers note that the units should evaluate whether the climate information generated by the system is useful. Regular technical reviews would compare climate projections with observations and assess whether the information remains fit for planning purposes. Institutional reviews conducted by climate-planning units could also examine whether substantial projects have incorporated climate foresight and whether it influenced their designs.

According to recent reports, these reviews would create a feedback loop. Development decisions would determine what information is needed, which could be satisfied by climate-science and investment-appraisal processes. Data from implemented projects would then be fed back into the system to upgrade climate information and plans going forwards.
