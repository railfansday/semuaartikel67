The narwhal’s legendary “unicorn horn” holds a remarkable engineering secret

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that in the 17th century, Danish King Frederik III already knew that the legendary unicorn horn came from a real animal. However, this creature had fins instead of hooves and inhabited the icy waters of the Arctic. The king dispatched an expedition to Greenland to investigate the source of these highly prized objects. The successful mission earned him a throne fashioned entirely from "unicorn horns," enhancing his power and prestige across Europe. Yet the treasured horn was not actually a horn, but "merely" a tooth.

In a recent development, the narwhal's tusk is unlike almost any other tooth in nature. Whereas elongated teeth, including elephant and walrus tusks and beaver incisors, grow in curved shapes, the male narwhal develops a remarkable corkscrew-shaped tooth. Typically, the upper left canine grows forward through the upper lip, twisting counterclockwise as it extends. It can reach two meters (6.6 feet) in length, equivalent to as much as half the animal's body length.

This unusual biological structure caught the attention of an international team of researchers from several scientific disciplines. Using advanced X-ray imaging techniques, they set out to determine whether the arrangement of the tusk's microscopic building blocks could explain the direction of its visible spiral.

Sources indicate that what they discovered was even more remarkable than expected. Rather than forming a single spiral, the tusk's tiny structural components create two intertwined helices that twist in opposite directions. Together, they produce an exceptionally stable structure.

The research was conducted at three major European synchrotron facilities: the Swiss Light Source SLS at the Paul Scherrer Institute in Villigen, the MAX IV Laboratory in Lund (Sweden), and the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility ESRF in Grenoble (France). The findings were published in Nature Communications.

A Giant Tooth Built Like Reinforced Concrete.

In a recent development, narwhal tusks continue growing throughout the animals' lives and can easily reach 70 years of age. Despite their striking appearance, the precise purpose of these enormous teeth remains uncertain. Evidence suggests they are primarily involved in mate selection. Narwhals have also been observed crossing their tusks in playful "duels," supporting the idea that the structures are important for reproduction and social interactions.

Although questions remain about how narwhals use their tusks, scientists now have a much clearer picture of how they are constructed.

"Like bones or other teeth in the animal kingdom, the narwhal tusk is a complex composite material whose structure extends from tiny nanoscale building blocks to the visible form of the entire tooth," indicates Marianne Liebi, a PSI researcher and co-author of the study.

Composite materials combine different substances to achieve properties that the individual components cannot provide alone. Reinforced concrete is a familiar example. Concrete made from cement and sand can withstand considerable pressure, while an internal steel framework helps resist pulling forces and provides additional stability.

Industry observers note that "The narwhal tusk, too, must be both hard and flexible," Liebi explains. "Only then can it withstand the strong hydrodynamic forces that swimming generates.".

The Remarkable Microscopic Structure of Narwhal Tusks.

According to recent reports, most of the narwhal's tusk consists of dentin, the same hard tissue that makes up much of the interior of human teeth. A thinner layer of cementum covers its exterior. In other mammals, cementum is typically confined to the roots of teeth.

Industry observers note that both materials contain extremely fine collagen fibers strengthened by tiny mineral crystals. Much like the steel framework inside reinforced concrete, the collagen fibers provide internal reinforcement, while the mineral crystals supply hardness. This combination allows the tusk to possess both strength and flexibility.

Understanding how this microscopic arrangement produces the much larger spiral presented a significant scientific challenge.

"The collagen fibers and mineral crystals are on the nanometer scale, while the spiral of the tooth only becomes visible at centimeter and meter lengths," Marianne Liebi explains.

Sources indicate that A nanometer is one billionth of a meter. To investigate how structures at this incredibly small scale relate to the shape of an entire tusk, the researchers turned to a sophisticated X-ray imaging method known as tensor tomography.

Advanced X-Rays Reveal the Tusk's Hidden Architecture.

Conventional dental X-rays produce images because teeth absorb more radiation than the softer tissues around them. The resulting image is essentially a shadow that reveals the shape and density of the tooth.

However, the powerful X-rays generated at synchrotron research facilities can provide far more detailed information. When these beams encounter regularly organized structures inside a material, the X-rays scatter in distinctive patterns. Scientists can analyze those patterns to determine how microscopic components are arranged.

"From these patterns, we can calculate how the nanometer-sized mineralized collagen fibers inside the material are oriented," explains first author Adrian Rodriguez-Palomo.

It has been revealed that rodriguez-Palomo began working on the project while pursuing his PhD at Chalmers University of Technology in Sweden. He subsequently continued the research as a postdoctoral researcher at Aarhus University in Denmark.

Sources indicate that during tensor tomography, researchers rotate the tooth within the synchrotron's X-ray beam while collecting measurements at individual locations. Millions of these measurements are then combined to reconstruct a three-dimensional representation of the tusk's internal organization, connecting its microscopic components with the much larger structure.

An Unexpected Discovery Hidden in the Data.

When the researchers first examined their extensive imaging data, they could not find the spiral structure they had anticipated.

"That puzzled us quite a bit," recalls Marianne Liebi.

Earlier mechanical experiments had indicated that the tusk's characteristic twist should be reflected in the orientation of its collagen fibers and mineral crystals.

"But instead of a helix, all we saw was a regular pattern.".

According to recent reports, the breakthrough came when the scientists changed their approach. Rather than examining individual points in the three-dimensional images in isolation, they investigated how the orientation of each point related to its neighbors.

"When we compared how these points were oriented relative to each other, a directional trend began to appear," recalls Palomo. "All we had to do then was connect the dots - as in a children's paint-by-numbers kit - and suddenly the structure became visible: two intertwined spirals.".

Two Opposing Spirals Give Narwhal Tusks Their Strength.

Industry observers note that further examination revealed that the two spirals occupy different regions of the tooth. The thin outer cementum layer forms a left-handed helix, whereas the mineralized collagen structures within the underlying dentin spiral in the opposite direction.

Sources indicate that this sophisticated natural design provides substantial mechanical advantages. Much like the intertwined strands of a rope, the two opposing helices work together to create an exceptionally stable structure. Their arrangement helps the tusk resist twisting forces, known as torsion, as well as other forms of mechanical stress.

The same microscopic architecture also produces the narwhal's recognizable corkscrew shape, linking the extraordinary appearance of the legendary unicorn horn to structures far too small to see with the naked eye.

Narwhal Tusks Preserve Decades of Arctic History.

The study forms part of a research project funded by NordForsk called "Narwhal tusks - a tale with a twist." It also received support from the European Union's Horizon 2020 research and innovation program under the Marie Skłodowska-Curie program.

Under the leadership of Henrik Birkedal of Aarhus University in Denmark, the international and interdisciplinary research team is exploring both the internal architecture of narwhal tusks and the relationship between tusk growth, environmental conditions, and climate.

As a narwhal's tusk grows, it develops successive layers that function much like the annual growth rings of a tree. Scientists can examine these structures to reconstruct past Arctic environmental conditions, including changes in climate and food availability.

During a lifespan that can approach a century, a male narwhal effectively builds its own environmental archive within its remarkable spiral tooth.
