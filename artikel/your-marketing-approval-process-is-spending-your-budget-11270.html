Your marketing approval process is spending your budget

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

“Can we get one more person to weigh in?” If you work in marketing, you know where this can lead. The campaign is ready, the team has worked through the feedback, and the launch date is approaching. Then another reviewer joins the conversation and questions a decision everyone thought was already settled. Back it goes. Newly introduced copy, updated graphics, another round of approvals. Meanwhile, the next project is waiting. No one approved a bigger budget. But the team is spending more time on the same work. In June 2026 research from Bloomberg Media and Lippincott, nearly eight in 10 CMOs said bureaucracy frequently interferes with decision-making. Corporate leadership was the primary source of delays for major marketing initiatives. As leaders, we need to look at our part in that. We expect teams to deliver on time, but every additional review affects their ability to do it. “ONE MORE REVIEW” IS MORE WORK THAN IT SOUNDS Changing a headline might take a few minutes. Updating it across a landing page, emails, ads, and a sales deck takes longer. Someone also has to check those changes and send the updated materials back for approval. That rework has to fit somewhere, and it’s rarely planned for in the workback schedule. So it takes time from a campaign already in production. It forces the creative department to stay late (again), or pushes the deadline to next week. Your team feels overworked and stressed, so it may appear to be a resource problem. But the real problem is in the process. The solution is to take a hard look at your marketing workflow, both documented and unspoken. START WITH APPROVALS If you find your campaigns are consistently missing deadlines, look at how many review rounds you typically have, and how many stakeholders must sign off. Understand who is involved, why they are involved, and how you can make sure they’re providing that value at the right time. Is the CMO signing off on the campaign messaging before any creative is designed, or are they seeing the campaign messaging the night before launch? Is legal being asked to approve photos from the expensive product shoot without being asked to provide any legal guardrails in the brief? Carefully sequencing sign-offs based on dependencies, and allotting time in your workback schedule for their review, will ensure you’re setting your team up for success from day one. GIVE REVIEWERS CLEAR EXPECTATIONS Often, the root of friction is in how we request feedback. Send a draft with “Let me know your thoughts,” and you may get a rewrite when you just needed someone to check the facts. Be specific about each person’s role. Product experts review accuracy. Legal reviews claims. The marketing lead evaluates whether the message speaks to the audience and aligns with the campaign goal. Establish who makes the final call when those perspectives conflict. A project manager can gather comments and chase responses, but someone with decision-making authority needs to resolve any disagreement. Asking the team to satisfy incompatible feedback just sends it around again. RANK FEEDBACK BY ITS IMPACT There are good reasons to reopen approved work. A product feature changes, a claim turns out to be inaccurate, or legal identifies a concern. If someone wants a late change, bring the conversation back to the campaign’s goal. Will it help the audience understand the message or correct something important? If it doesn’t clear one of those hurdles, stick with the approved version. As a reviewer, before requesting a change, find out what that change will impact. That includes who must review it again and what will have to move or be sacrificed to accommodate it. You need to understand its impact on the schedule, the team capacity, and ultimately the budget. Make sure it’s worth the cost. As leaders, we need to trust our team to make the right decisions. But when there are too many decision-makers on a project, nothing gets resolved. Before asking your marketing team to move faster, look at how approvals are handled in your organization. Every review has a cost, and those review cycles may be eating up your budget. Lisa Larson-Kelley is president and CEO of Quantious.
