This month, we sat down with Dancing with the Stars judge and dancer Carrie Ann Inaba. For the uninitiated, Inaba has been the sole female judge on the hit dance competition show since its inception, but her ambitions in entertainment began long before she arrived in the ballroom. After being discovered at a talent show in Hawaii, Inaba moved to Japan to pursue her dreams of performing, a leap of faith that catapulted her career to new heights. From choreographing and staging shows like American Idol and So You Think You Can Dance to bringing ballroom dance into the homes of millions, her career has been full of massive risks and what she calls the “healing art” of dance. Ahead of the season 35 finale of Dancing with the Stars, Inaba reflects on how she got her start in the biz and the moment that changed everything in her career, then pulls back the curtain on what makes Dancing with the Stars a true feat of live television.

My very first job was working for my father when I was about 10 or 11 years old. He was an entrepreneur and, at the time, a real estate developer with an office in the heart of Waikiki. After school or on weekends, I would go to his office, and he would always put me to work. I think those early experiences are why I’ve always had such a positive association with work.

I was “discovered” by a Japanese producer at a talent show in Hawaii called Search for Talent. I was dancing with my friend Tina Horii, and we were offered a recording contract with Pony Canyon Records, with the intention of eventually returning to the U.S. as crossover artists. At the last moment, she decided not to go, but I felt compelled to honor the commitment. As part of my recording deal, I negotiated a contract to perform as a dancer on one of Japan’s most popular television shows, Yoru no Hit Studio DX. My love of dancing was still my first priority. I loved that chapter of my life. I was a member of the Dancing Dolls, much like the Solid Gold Dancers or the troupe on Dancing with the Stars. We performed alongside the musical guests, and it was a dream come true. My recording career was more challenging. I was uncomfortable being the center of attention, and everything felt rushed. It felt more like a business than a creative endeavor. The day after I performed my first single, “Party Girl,” on Fuji TV, I was mobbed by fans in Harajuku. It was an incredible introduction to the world of live television, which has become a thread throughout my career.

When I booked Dancing with the Stars, suddenly an entirely new world of opportunities opened up to me, and I wasn’t necessarily ready for all of them. Until then, I had been very intentional about the direction of my career. I was focused on dance, choreography, and producing. Everything had dance at its center. I had produced some live reveals but really wanted to produce television. So the job of judging on Dancing with the Stars felt like a good fit toward that goal. What [the show] gave me was the opportunity to learn my own voice.

At 18, I moved to Japan, a country where I didn’t speak the language. I asked to be released from my recording contract when I realized the direction of my career was no longer aligned with who I was. I’ve never been afraid to walk away from an opportunity when staying would mean compromising my values.

Sources indicate that later, I chose to accept the job of opening Madonna’s Girlie Show Tour by doing something that had never been done before: descending a 50-foot unsecured fire pole with a non-weight-bearing safety. It was basically a live stunt that I performed every night of that tour. I had to go through intense training for it. It was controversial, but I really liked the idea of it and the statement it was making, so I mentioned yes. It was one of the scariest things I’ve ever done; there was real risk every time I got on that pole. But it was very rewarding and I believed in what I was doing. As an Asian American woman, I wanted to break down stereotypes of what we could do.

I also think I made a very bold move this year by standing up for myself amid some workplace drama. I’m still processing what happened, and since the competition is still going on, I will leave it at that. But it was a profound moment for me in my career and life.

Industry observers note that to trust myself, trust my choices, and always speak my truth no matter how uncomfortable it is to do so. I live by that.

It’s so much more than just steps and technique—it is a healing art form that empowers people. I encourage and notice the aspects outside and around the technique because that is what I know to be vital from my personal and professional experiences. After a dance is done, most people will not remember the steps themselves or how many heel leads they had or didn’t have; they will remember how the dance made them feel. Of course, they need to do the correct style we have asked of them and follow any rules we have established, but we aren’t creating professional dancers to compete in Blackpool. We are an entertainment show that really celebrates the human spirit and the courage to try something fresh. That is what makes our show so special.

I can personally say that in 35 seasons, I have never been told what score to give a performance. Not once. I don’t think I would be able to continue judging if that were ever to happen. It would go against my core values and everything I believe about the responsibility of being a judge.

I don’t think people fully understand the scope of what we accomplish each week. The sheer amount of creative and physical energy it takes to bring this show to life is extraordinary. Every week, we essentially make the impossible possible. From choreography, performance, production, storytelling, and costume design to social media strategy, brand partnerships, publicity, security, catering, and transportation, there are so many moving parts. And once we start that first episode, it never stops until the Tuesday before Thanksgiving. It’s a feat on its own, but it’s also that we do it live every week.

There are no do-overs. Live television is high-stakes and high-pressure, and Dancing with the Stars is unlike anything else on television. The production is so elaborate that I sometimes wonder if people realize it’s all unfolding in real time.

In a recent development, it’s an honor to work on a show that is as demanding as it is fulfilling. We create compelling television that brings people together. People throw watch parties, and families across generations have something they can enjoy and talk about together. In a world where it’s increasingly easy to become isolated in our own individual experiences, Dancing with the Stars gives us a shared moment.

For this finale, viewers on both coasts will have the opportunity to watch and vote live, making that shared experience even more special.

When a dance transcends the steps, the technique, and the choreography, and somehow becomes greater than the sum of its parts, that’s the magic that makes it a ten for me. I can feel it. And that magic is what keeps bringing me back season after season.
