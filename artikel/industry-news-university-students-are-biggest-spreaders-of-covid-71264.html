Universities have become hotbeds of SARS-CoV-2 transmission, according to a massive analysis of more than 85,000 viral genome sequences and other data collected from people with COVID-19.

The study, published on 19 November in the journal Cell1, finds that viral transmission between 2021 and 2023 was highest among adolescents aged 15 to 18 in schools, undergraduates at university, and residents in nursing facilities. Undergraduates, in particular, were consistently the first group to start spreading emerging genetic variants of SARS-CoV-2.

Surprisingly, however, staff members at all three types of institution had about the same risk of contracting COVID-19 as did those in their surrounding communities. So, for example, professors were about as likely to get COVID-19 from the coffee baristas in their university towns as they were to get it from a student at their university.

Sources indicate that the analysis is among the largest to bring together viral genomic sequences with medical data from people with COVID-19. “The scale is truly impressive,” says Bill Hanage, who studies the evolution of infectious diseases at the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health in Boston, Massachusetts. “It’s an example of the depth and the detail of the picture that can be painted using the medium of population genomics.”.

Industry observers note that the findings were based on data collected from COVID-19 tests taken by people in Massachusetts, between November 2021 and January 2023. The genomic data allowed researchers to trace the path of SARS-CoV-2 variants that emerged over time, and to link this with epidemiological data about the study participants, such as their age and whether they had been vaccinated against COVID-19.

Overall, the team found that booster vaccinations helped to reduce SARS-CoV-2 transmission. This was true even for BA.1, an Omicron variant that started to circulate in Massachusetts in late 2021 — a striking result because COVID-19 vaccines at that time hadn’t yet been updated to protect against BA.1, Hanage says. “That’s an key illustration of how vaccines, even when they’re not perfect, can still be beneficial,” he says.
