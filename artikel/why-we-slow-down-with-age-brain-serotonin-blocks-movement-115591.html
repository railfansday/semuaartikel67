Why We Slow Down With Age: Brain Serotonin Blocks Movement

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Researchers have uncovered the specific brain circuit responsible for age-related declines in physical activity. The study reveals that aging increases serotonin signaling in motivation and movement centers, acting like a biological brake that suppresses dopamine-driven urges to move, while blocking this serotonin pathway restores youthful activity, speed, lean muscle mass, and grip strength in older mice.

Physical inactivity is one of the strongest modifiable risk factors for chronic disease, frailty, and premature mortality. Yet across human populations and animal species alike, advancing age brings a predictable, progressive decline in spontaneous daily movement, a biological phenomenon whose root neural cause has remained largely unexplained.

In a fresh study published via ScienceDirect and supported by the UKRI-Biotechnology and Biological Sciences Research Council (BBSRC), researchers led by Professor Lora Heisler have identified a dedicated neurochemical circuit in the brain that actively dials down the natural drive to move as we grow older.

To establish how movement patterns shift across the adult lifespan, the research team first compared physical behavior in adults aged 50 to 70 against younger adults aged 17 to 30. Older adults walked more slowly, covered less daily distance, and carried higher levels of body fat, a behavioral and metabolic trajectory closely mirrored by aging mice.

Probing the neural circuits governing motivation and motor initiation in the mouse brain, the investigators uncovered a fundamental shift in neurochemical balance:.

“Think of the brain’s movement system as a bicycle. The brain chemical dopamine helps push the pedals, driving the urge to move, while another chemical called serotonin puts a brake on this pathway. With age, that brake becomes increasingly engaged, slowing voluntary movement. We found that releasing the brake restored more youthful levels of activity in older mice,” explained Professor Heisler.

“Rather than the aging brain running out of fuel, our findings suggest it may be applying too much brake. By reducing signalling through this serotonin pathway, we were able to release that brake and restore more youthful patterns of movement in older mice.”.

When the researchers experimentally blocked this age-amplified serotonin pathway in older mice, the physiological transformation extended well beyond increased step counts.

Releasing the neurochemical brake restored youthful spontaneous activity and movement speed, leading to measurable improvements in systemic physical health: treated older animals accumulated less body fat, gained lean muscle mass, and demonstrated stronger physical grip strength.

By demonstrating that age-related physical slowing is actively programmed by a modifiable brain circuit rather than inevitable muscular exhaustion, the research opens promising therapeutic avenues for extending healthspan and physical independence.

“Going forward, our findings suggest that targeting serotonin signalling in this pathway could one day provide new approaches to maintain mobility, independence, and healthy aging in an increasingly older population,” Heisler remarked.

“We all tend to slow down as we get older, but until now it hasn’t been clear why,” added Dr. Amanda Collis, Executive Director for Strategic Research and International at UKRI-BBSRC. “This BBSRC-supported research highlights that part of the answer lies in the brain, identifying a pathway that appears to dial down our natural drive to be physically active with age.”.

Why do people naturally become less physically active as they get older?

According to recent reports, researchers discovered that aging increases signaling in a specific serotonin pathway in the brain’s movement and motivation centers. This heightened serotonin acts like a brake that suppresses dopamine, the brain chemical that drives the natural urge to move, causing voluntary physical activity to slow down.

Can blocking this brain pathway reverse age-related slowing?

According to recent reports, in studies with older mice, blocking this overactive serotonin pathway released the biological brake on dopamine. As a result, older animals regained youthful levels of physical activity and movement speed, accumulated less body fat, gained lean muscle mass, and improved their grip strength.

What does this discovery mean for healthy aging in humans?

Because the decline in daily movement is driven by an active brain circuit rather than the body simply “running out of fuel,” targeting this specific serotonin pathway could eventually lead to newly introduced therapies that help older adults stay active, independent, and healthy longer.

Brain serotonin circuit reverses decline in physical activity with age.

Physical inactivity increases with age and is the fourth leading risk factor for mortality. Establishing the mechanisms underpinning declining activity with age has remained both elusive and difficult to overcome. Older (50–70 years old) compared with younger (17–30 years old) United Kingdom participants highlighted reduced physical activity, and this profile was effectively modelled in mice.

Analysis of brain serotonin neurons in the dorsal raphe (5-HTDR) revealed altered firing activity in older mice. Chemogenetically mimicking this 5-HTDR tone in young adult mice produced an older adult physical activity profile.

Genetically blocking 5-HT activity at 5-HT2C receptors prevented the decline in physical activity with age. Importantly, barring 5-HT action at 5-HT2C receptors specifically within the ventral tegmental area restored youthful physical activity levels and strength in older mice.

These data fill a longstanding knowledge gap by defining brain circuitry programming the decline in physical activity with age, and importantly, a means to reverse it.
