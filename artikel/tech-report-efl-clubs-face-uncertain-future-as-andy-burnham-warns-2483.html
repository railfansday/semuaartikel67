Prime Minister Andy Burnham has urged caution over scrapping the 3pm blackout, and EFL clubs may have good cause to back him over this.

With rates of the illegal streaming of matches continuing to rise, there has been much conjecture in recent times over whether it is time for the ending of the 3pm blackout for televised matches.

The blackout, which prevents the broadcasting of live matches from the Premier League and the Championship between 2.45pm and 5.15pm on a Saturday, has come in for increasing criticism in recent years from fans of clubs who are unable to legally watch matches between these times while their counterparts have the ability to do so.

But now an interjection from Prime Minister Andy Burnham has cast fresh doubt over whether the blackout will be lifted in the near future, after he explained that such a decision needs to be handled carefully in order to protect smaller clubs.

Recent findings suggest that the BBC have reported that Prime Minister Andy Burnham saying that he is sceptical about the removal of the 3pm blackout. Burnham has said that he believes that any removal of the blackout should be handled extremely carefully in order to protect the revenues of smaller clubs.

Burnham has stated that, "I would urge a bit of caution [on the blackout], because it does protect the income of lower-league clubs," adding that, "If you have effectively wall-to-wall Premier League football at any time, it takes away perhaps the incentive to go and watch the football league or non-league.".

Burnham, who is a long-standing supporter of Premier League club Everton, remarked that he wouldn't be taking a solely consumer-orientated view of removing the blackout: "I think it has to be considered carefully. I'm not going to take the pure consumer view here. I'll take the health-of-football approach to this particular concern.".

According to recent reports, and he also confirmed that his main aim with regard to this glitch is the protection of English football's unique pyramid system: "I think it's about protecting the experience with the paying supporter, protecting the football pyramid. Those complications matter to me, first of all.".

The 3pm blackout was first introduced in the 1960s, at a time when the televised coverage of football was first really starting to proliferate and the attitude of clubs towards the broadcasting of matches was very different to today.

At a time when match-day revenue was how clubs made the majority of their incomes, there was deep suspicion on the part of owners and chairmen about what sort of effect the widespread televising of matches might have on revenue coming into clubs.

Sources indicate that an attempt to show live Football League matches had failed in 1960 after just one match was shown, but with England hosting a World Cup in 1966 there was increased interest from broadcasters in showing matches. The Burnley chairman Bob Lord spearheaded the opposition, which resulted in the blackout being introduced. Live televised football from the Football League didn't return until October 1983.

Industry observers note that critics of the blackout argue that it is a relic from a bygone age. In an era during which matches can be illegally streamed from elsewhere in the world, they argue that allowing all matches to be shown on the television regardless of kick-off times would be beneficial to clubs, who could charge more for their rights, and for fans of massive clubs who want to see all of their teams' matches live.

The counter-argument to this is that, while no-one knows for sure what would happen were the blackout to be lifted, it's difficult to imagine a scenario in which it would be good for smaller clubs. Anecdotal evidence has long suggested that smaller clubs take a hit when they have matches scheduled for the same time as bigger matches.

Sources indicate that and it remains the case that smaller EFL clubs and non-league clubs remain more dependent on match-day revenues than bigger clubs. This isn't to say that it doesn't matter to Premier League clubs, for whom it remains their third-largest source of income. But without television deals of the value of the Premier League's or vast commercial contracts, the lower you travel down the football pyramid, the more dependent clubs are on supporters turning out for matches. A YouGov poll in 2023 found that 14% of match-going fans would be more likely to stay at home in the event that matches were shown on the television at 3pm on a Saturday.

Furthermore, supporters of retaining the ban argue that removing the blackout would be akin to letting a genie out of a bottle. The likelihood of removing the ban would be a one-way street, and even if the effects of it were more severe for smaller clubs than anticipated, the likelihood of it being reinstated would be extremely thin. And with the rights to Premier League or Championship matches still being extremely valuable, big increases to the amounts being paid for matches at that level would only further widen the financial gap between the wealthiest clubs and the rest.

Recent findings suggest that the Premier League and EFL could, if they wanted to, remove all of their matches from the period covered by the 3pm blackout and ensure that all matches could be shown live on the television. But with matches already routinely being shown at lunchtime, early in the evening, and increasingly on Saturday nights, it seems unlikely that the bigger clubs would do anything but fill in the gaps with more live televised football in the event that it was removed.

According to recent reports, andy Burnham's comments will cause a mixed reaction. The 2023 YouGov poll found that 25% of all fans supported the blackout while 52% were against it, though these numbers changed to 44% in favour and 45% against when it came to match-going fans. Balancing these opposing interests could prove tricky for the Prime Minister, given the conflicting nature of the desire of bigger clubs to maximise their revenues and smaller clubs' fears over what effect loosening or abolishing it could have upon them.
