Every Cure confirmed a partnership with OpenEvidence on November 16, 2026, to advance latest uses for existing medicines where commercial incentives are lacking, linking AI-supported drug evaluation with specialist input, clinical-trial recruitment and evidence distribution to physicians.

Industry observers note that every Cure estimates that roughly 18,000 diseases are recognized but only about 4,000 have approved treatments, and that more than 300 million people globally have a disease with no FDA-approved treatment. Those figures frame the partnership’s target: promising uses for approved drugs, including generics, that a nonprofit can pursue without a conventional commercial return.

Repurposing builds on existing safety data and manufacturing capacity rather than starting with an entirely new compound. The announcement contrasts that foundation with new-drug development, which can require billions of dollars and more than 10 years. That rationale explains the attraction of repurposing, but it does not establish that any particular candidate will work in a new disease.

Recent findings suggest that every Cure applies data science and AI to evaluate approved drugs against diseases systematically. OpenEvidence will contribute proprietary models and biomedical sources and data, including content from its strategic partnership with Nature journals, to help assess repurposing hypotheses.

Industry observers note that openEvidence will also match individual repurposing programs with physicians who have cared for patients with the relevant disease. For many rare and underserved diseases, only a small number of these physicians exist, making that access a central part of the collaboration.

Sources indicate that “OpenEvidence can help us reach the physicians who care for these patients. Moreover, their firsthand experience will help us ask better research questions and design studies that address what matters most to patients and physicians,” stated Matt Goddeeris, Every Cure’s chief scientific officer.

Industry observers note that once Every Cure identifies a promising opportunity, it remains responsible for generating evidence on whether the treatment is safe and effective in the upcoming disease and for addressing barriers to patient access. OpenEvidence’s role continues as programs move from research into the clinic.

Recent findings suggest that for programs entering clinical trials, OpenEvidence will alert treating physicians when a patient may qualify, leaving the physician to decide whether to pursue a referral. That recruitment support is particularly relevant to rare diseases, where eligible patients can be difficult to locate. As trial results emerge, OpenEvidence will disseminate the findings to physicians at the point of care.

Isabel Ostrer, OpenEvidence’s director of primary care initiatives, described the evidence gap: “Physicians come to OpenEvidence for the best available evidence, but for thousands of diseases, that evidence doesn’t exist yet.”.

In a recent development, every Cure says its approach grew from co-founder and CEO David Fajgenbaum’s experience with idiopathic multicentric Castleman disease. After being told that no options remained, he identified an overactive immune pathway in his blood and tested sirolimus, a drug approved to prevent kidney-transplant rejection that targets the same pathway. He has been in remission for more than 11 years and has advanced 14 additional repurposed drugs.

Sources indicate that openEvidence already works with the National Organization for Rare Disorders on more than 3,000 expert-reviewed rare-disease summaries. The organization claims its platform is used daily, on average, by a majority of U.S. physicians and is free for all verified U.S. clinicians, providing the reach through which it looks to support specialist matching, referrals and dissemination.

In a recent development, the partnership joins that repurposing experience to a national clinical network. Its central test is whether AI-assisted hypotheses, paired with specialist knowledge and physician reach, can become credible trial evidence and accessible treatments for patients who otherwise have few options.
