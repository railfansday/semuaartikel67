Apple’s ‘just works’ approach is great, but goes a little too far

According to recent reports, “It just works” is one of the phrases most commonly associated with Apple. Interestingly, it appears to have been coined by the company’s customers and then used by reviewers before it was later picked up by Steve Jobs.

Of course, it’s now commonly used ironically when Apple products don’t do what they are supposed to (Apple Home, I’m looking at you), but I do think that it’s absolutely the right philosophy and goal – most of the time ….

Recent findings suggest that macworld referenced an Apple brochure from 1996 entitled “Why do people prefer Macintosh?” This is a collection of testimonials from customers, a number of whom use the “it just works” phrasing. It was subsequently used by a number of reviewers. The earliest example I can find of Steve Jobs adopting it was in 1999, when discussing the rollout of FireWire to the Mac in the PowerMac G3.

It’s hot-pluggable. You don’t have to turn your computer off to connect and disconnect peripherals, you can just plug them right in. Again, the drivers are dynamically loaded. There are no terminators and there are no switches. It just works.

(If you have a link to any earlier example, please do let me know in the comments.).

Sources indicate that I’m a significant fan of the philosophy. Most of the time, users should just be able to get on with the tasks they want to execute without having to worry about the technology. The tech that makes it all possible should sit there quietly in the background.

However, there are cases where I think Apple takes this approach a little too far, and where tech-savvy consumers at least would like a little more transparency and control.

AirPods firmware updates are a good example. Back in September, there was quite a significant update in the form of custom EQ. It’s a rather basic version, but still a feature many had wanted for a long time. Instead of being able to manually trigger the update, users had to wait until it “just worked.”.

Apple’s support document has a how-to guide that is essentially an outline of the conditions required to permit an revision, and an instruction to simply wait until it happens. Which you will only know by repeatedly checking.

Sources indicate that make sure your AirPods are connected via Bluetooth to your iPhone, iPad or Mac. Connect your charging case to a power source. Put your AirPods in their charging case and close the lid. Keep the lid of the charging case closed, and keep your AirPods in Bluetooth range of your iPhone, iPad or Mac. Wait at least 30 minutes for the firmware to be updated. Check the firmware version again.

In a recent development, another example, and one I’ve complained about before, is that there is no way to manually trigger an iCloud document sync, nor any progress indicator when it does finally happen. In the best of cases, it takes seconds; in the worst, many hours. And again, the only way to find out is to repeatedly check.

It’s the same story when shooting a video on your iPhone. Will it still be there an hour later, available to instantly edit and view? Maybe, or maybe it will have been offloaded to the cloud, requiring it to be downloaded before you can do anything with it – while a video you shot months earlier is still stored locally on the device! You can, of course, switch off optimization altogether, but if you choose to use it, you get no control over it. I want a setting that allows me to do things like specify local retention of all photos and videos taken within the past fortnight.

It has been revealed that will that video be available in the Mac Photos app when you check half an hour later? Maybe, maybe not. You can click the Sync Now button as many times as you like, and it will apparently have no effect, claiming it’s up to date despite the video being entirely absent. It will be there when it’s there.

Once it is in the Photos app on your Mac, it’s a similar story when exporting it to a folder. You can drag and drop the video into the folder, and this time you are manually triggering the transfer (yay!), but there will be absolutely no visual indication that it is in progress. No grayed-out icon, no progress bar, nothing. So far as the user interface is concerned, it appears that your drag and drop had no effect – until the video file finally appears.

I think with all of these things and more, Apple needs to offer options. Yes, most non-tech customers may be perfectly happy to just wait until things happen, but there ought to be a standard way to trigger things that right now happen in their own sweet time, as well as progress indicators and confirmation that a process is complete.
