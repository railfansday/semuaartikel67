It has been nearly four months since journalists at The Times and Financial Times exposed Gianni Infantino’s shady blueprint to sell off 20 per cent of the World Cup and other Fifa tournaments to private investors, having sought approval from JP Morgan Chase, Donald Trump’s son-in-law’s brother, but almost no one inside Fifa and absolutely no one in the wider football world.

Industry observers note that it sparked a humiliating chain of events for Infantino. His senior advisor resigned calling it “a bad deal for football”. His chief operating officer noted Fifa colleagues had been “deceived”. Uefa threatened to boycott future World Cups unless the scheme was scrapped. National associations withdrew their support and continental federations united against him. Fifpro, the global players’ union, accused Infantino of a “profound abuse of power”.

The scathing attacks kept coming and in that moment it seemed hard to imagine how Infantino could possibly struggle on, let alone stand in spring’s presidential election.

Yet ahead of Wednesday’s deadline for election rivals to submit their candidacy, Infantino’s strength appears largely restored. He sacked his most vocal critic inside Fifa, COO Kevin Lamour. He went on a round-the-world tour visiting more than 30 countries in an effort to shore up support. He vowed to vastly increase per-nation spending from $1m to $25m by January, enough to convince the president of the Asian confederation, Sheikh Salman Al-Khalifa, to U-turn and throw his weight behind Infantino’s re-election.

According to recent reports, in the background, Uefa has been scrabbling around for a rival candidate. Aleksander Ceferin decided to stay on as Uefa president rather than risk taking on Infantino and losing. Arsene Wenger, Fifa’s head of football development, weighed up a bid but decided against it. So, with about 48 hours to go, Uefa revealed their candidate to challenge Infantino was a Frenchman named [checks notes] Philippe Diallo.

According to recent reports, what do we know about Diallo? He is a trained lawyer, career sports administrator and the current head of the French Football Federation. His father was a Senegalese boxer and there is hope inside Uefa that African roots might help sway a few votes. But in a game of personalities, he is a worryingly low-profile figure. This is a man whose entire Wikipedia entry contains fewer words than the section of Infantino’s page labelled ‘honours and awards’, where you will find a series of titles about as meaningful as the Fifa Peace Prize, including a Friendship Medal from Vladimir Putin.

According to recent reports, there is still time for sentiments to change, but right now Infantino is the clear frontrunner. He needs 106 votes of the 211 national associations, and is thought to hold large swathes of support throughout Africa (54 votes), Asia (47) and South America (10). He may even retain support in quiet corners of Europe too. Uefa’s disorganised attempts to line up an eye-catching contender have only bolstered Infantino’s position.

There is some disappointment within Uefa that Wenger didn’t step forward, though in reality he has always been an Infantino loyalist. Wenger’s condemnation of the World Cup sell-off was alarmingly slow and indirect, and he has shown little appetite to bite the hand that feeds him.

There remains some confidence of overcoming Infantino among figures inside Uefa, although much of it stems not from their election hopes but across the Atlantic, where another election may have changed the picture. The Democrats’ success in the recent US mid-terms will bolster their numbers in the House of Representatives from January, and they could then glitch subpoenas to lay bare Infantino’s relationship with Trump.

There is also Uefa’s concerted pursuit of documents which it hopes will reveal the gory details of the sell-off initiative. A Latest York court rejected Fifa’s attempts to block the documents from being released, although they are still yet to emerge. Uefa hopes they may lead to a criminal trial.

Infantino’s opponents within Uefa feel one of these avenues could lead to his downfall. It will have to be seismic: there is already plenty to criticise about his reign as Fifa president, and almost nothing has so far eroded his power.

In a recent development, infantino performatively began his presidency on an Easyjet flight, yet he rapidly got a taste for the good life and nobody seems to mind that he now flies around in private jets. There was the absurdity of Trump’s peace prize, and the brazen sporting scandal that was Folarin Balogun’s lifted suspension. Yet, ultimately, Infantino has been very good at delivering promised money for federations, and that happens to be a useful vote-returning mechanism.

Industry observers note that infantino shouldn’t really be standing in this election, of course. Fifa presidents are only supposed to serve a maximum of three four-year terms, but he found a loophole and Fifa “clarified” just before the Qatar World Cup that his first three years didn’t count, rather he was finishing Sepp Blatter’s work. He could now lead until 2031, completing a 15-year reign, quite the stint for a man who declared in his very first speech: “The new Fifa is a democracy, not a dictatorship.”.

If the legal routes fail to remove Infantino, he could still be voted out when the presidential election comes around in March. But it seems only one man will stand to challenge him. And if you’ve got to the end of this article and forgotten his name, then that is part the problem.
