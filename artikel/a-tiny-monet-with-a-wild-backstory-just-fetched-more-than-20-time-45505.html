The auction houses’ marquee evening sales get all the headlines, with high-eight-figure artworks and high-nine-figure totals, sometimes approaching the billion-dollar mark when the market is flush.

Recent findings suggest that but the more modest daytime sales of lower-priced materials occasionally contain real treasures. When I wrote about them for Artnet News in 2015, experts told me that this is where the houses make a lot of their profits; dealers haunt these sales to stock up on inventory; they are far less stage-managed than the evening events; they may be more indicative of the actual health of the market; and you can find better deals. And they can occasionally spark serious bidding wars. On Wednesday, a tiny artwork by a titan of French Impressionism became the latest example.

According to recent reports, claude Monet’s La Barque (ca. 1874), which came under the hammer at Christie’s Rockefeller Center saleroom at a day sale of Impressionist and modern art, was estimated at $300,000 to $500,000, but fetched just shy of $7 million. Its modest estimate matched its diminutive size: it measures only 2¾ by 3½ inches, so it sold for $722,285.71 per square inch.

More than 20 bidders fought for the tiny panel, some in the room and some bidding on the phone via Christie’s specialists, says the house; the contest went on for six minutes and 45 seconds, and Rachel White Young, head of the 20th-century evening sale, won the prize for her client.

Even before making a little auction history on Wednesday, the painting had an unusual backstory.

Sources indicate that paris art dealer Georges Petit, a collector and supporter of the Impressionists, commissioned it from the artist; the magazine L’Art Moderne wrote about it in June 1902, relating how the collector carried around small pieces of wood, cut to identical sizes, which he handed out to artists he admired. He would offer them 100 francs and ask if they would return the petite panel as a painting. He did the same with Camille Corot, Édouard Manet, Camille Pissarro, and Pierre-Auguste Renoir, all of whom obliged.

All five would come into the collection of Thérèse and Frédéric Humbert, notorious figures in French high society. She claimed to have been willed a fortune by an imaginary American millionaire, Robert Crawford, whose life she had supposedly saved. They borrowed against an imaginary fortune, buying a grand home and occupying the center of Parisian social life for two decades, until bank officials started asking questions and her creditors sued and the tissue of lies was shredded.

In a recent development, funny enough, the resulting sale of their possessions would take place in 1902 at none other than Galerie Georges Petit. Galerie Bernheim-Jeune bought it and it remained with that family until 2013, when the ollectors Marlene and Spencer Hays got it at Paris auction house Hôtel Drouot, for the hammer price of €200,000. They began selling books door-to-door as a summer job for Southwestern Manufacturer and later went on to run the busines and start a clothing company.

According to their foundation, when asked what was the most fun part of their lives, they would say, “learning about art and building a collection that we treasure,” but would also quote Sir Richard Attenborough to the effect that art collectors are “simply its temporary, fortunate and delighted custodians.” Their collection included works by Pierre Bonnard, Edgar Degas, Henri Matisse, Amedeo Modigliani, and Édouard Vuillard.

They were as good as their word; they granted their collection to Paris’s Musée d’Orsay in 2016. It was the largest foreign collection donated since World War II, with some 600 masterworks from the late 19th and early 20th centuries. He died the next year, survived by Marlene.
