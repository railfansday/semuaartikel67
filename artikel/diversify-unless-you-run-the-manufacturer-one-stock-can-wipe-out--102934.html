📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Keeping most of your savings in a few stocks makes sense for firm founders and full-time investors. For everyone else, one company’s collapse can erase years of saving. Diversify by owning thousands of stocks of every size and sector through one low-cost total-market or total-world index fund, and skip extra funds that hold the same companies.

Enron, Lehman Brothers, and General Electric were household names, and each manufacturer’s stock fell 90% or more from its high. Anyone holding little besides one of them had lost most of their savings at the low, and Enron and Lehman went bankrupt.

Recent findings suggest that A founder can keep a fortune in one stock because they run the tech giant. Warren Buffett’s case against diversifying is for experts. In a 1993 shareholder letter, he wrote that owning many stocks makes no sense for investors who understand businesses well enough to find five to 10 fairly priced companies with lasting advantages. Everyone else, he wrote, should own many stocks, such as through an index fund.

Sources indicate that finance professor Hendrik Bessembinder found that four of every seven U.S. stocks since 1926 earned less over their lifetimes than one-month Treasury bills, short-term loans to the government. The best-performing 4% of companies produced all of the market’s gain over those bills, since the rest together only matched them. A handful of stocks will probably miss that 4%, and a fund that owns the whole market holds all of it.

In a recent development, diversifying protects you from one firm, industry, or country going bad, not from a falling market. The S&P 500, an index of 500 large U.S. companies, lost about 37% in 2008 including dividends.

According to recent reports, diversifying costs almost nothing. One fund, Vanguard Total World Stock ETF, holds more than 10,000 stocks in the U.S. and abroad for 0.06% a year, or $6 on every $10,000. A total-market fund covers thousands of U.S. stocks.

Recent findings suggest that choose an index fund, not an actively managed fund whose manager picks the stocks, and keep any stocks you buy on your own to a small slice of your portfolio. If your job gives you firm shares, cap your employer’s stock too.

The opposite mistake is owning several funds that hold the same companies. FINRA, the brokerage industry’s regulator, suggests checking whether your funds hold similar companies. Look up each fund’s 10 largest holdings on its own page, and when the same names top two lists, one total-market fund can replace both.
