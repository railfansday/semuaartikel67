Tech Report: Injured locusts heal or are cannibalized depending on levels of a bacterial odour molecule

This is a summary of: Han, W. et al. Injury-induced signalling coordinates immunity and cannibalism in locusts. Nature https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11297-7 (2026).

Locusts are infamous for their ability to form giant swarms that can devastate plants, including crops, across vast areas1. One of the forces thought to drive swarms of young, flightless locusts in the ‘hopper’ life stage is cannibalism: individuals at the back eat those in front if they fail to move fast enough. Cannibalism is thus an important factor that underpins the collective behaviour of locusts2.

Three years ago, we discovered an anti-cannibalism pheromone — an odorous chemical (odorant) that conveys a message between members of a species — that protects migratory locusts (Locusta migratoria) from being eaten3. We wanted to know whether other odorants might act as attractants. We had observed that wounded locusts were particularly likely to be attacked and consumed, so we started our investigation there.

Sources indicate that initially, we used behavioural experiments to investigate whether hungry locusts are indeed attracted to wounded individuals of the same species (Fig. 1). Next, we collected odorants emitted by wounded locusts and performed chemical analyses to identify attractive compounds, which we then tested in further behavioural experiments. Earlier work had shown that the odorants we identified are produced by bacteria living in or on locusts4, so we asked whether bacteria were responsible for producing them in injured locusts as well.

Figure 1 | Locust cannibals devouring a wounded locust. Four migratory locusts (Locusta migratoria) in the fifth-instar (final) developmental stage attack and devour a wounded fourth-instar individual of the same species.Credit: Han, W. et al./Nature (CC BY 4.0).

For an odorant to function as a cue, it must first be detected. We therefore investigated the locust’s smell-sensing (olfactory) system, located in the antennae, and found receptor proteins that detect the compound we had identified. Using gene editing, we knocked out the genes that encode these receptors to investigate their role in attracting cannibals. We also disrupted odorant production in the implicated bacteria. To understand why wounded locusts emit these odorants, we used various behavioural and molecular methods to investigate changes in the expression of genes involved in the locust immune system.

Our experiments revealed that bacteria that proliferate around wounds in locusts produce the odorant guaiacol (which smells like single-malt whisky). It is detected by receptor proteins in the antennae of other locusts, attracting potential cannibals. Guaiacol also activates the immune system of the wounded locust, helping to protect the wound against infection by disease-causing agents (pathogens).

Sources indicate that at the relatively low concentrations typically associated with small wounds, the compound did not attract cannibals, and the wound often healed. However, at higher concentrations, guaiacol reached levels that attracted other locusts, leading to the wounded insect being attacked and eaten. The system therefore involves a trade-off between fighting pathogens and attracting hungry members of the same species.

The processes that underlie the formation and movement of giant locust swarms remain unclear. However, by revealing a trade-off between immunity and cannibalism, we have added another piece to the puzzle. A deeper understanding of chemical communication in locusts might also inform the development of environmentally sustainable approaches to locust management. More broadly, the finding that a bacterially produced compound can act as a signal to activate genes involved in the immune system is interesting from biological and medical perspectives.

The main limitation of our study is that we have not yet uncovered the mechanism by which particular bacteria are recruited to wound sites. How does the number of bacteria increase, and how do they populate the wound?

Recent findings suggest that the natural next step is to conduct experiments addressing these questions. By introducing specific markers, such as fluorescent proteins, into the bacteria, it should be possible to track their movement through the body and investigate their distribution across organs before and after a locust is wounded. — Hetan Chang and Bill S. Hansson are at the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Shenzhen, China. B.S.H. is also at the Max Planck Institute for Chemical Ecology, Jena, Germany.

“This paper describes how cannibalistic behaviour in a migratory locust is stimulated by recruitment of symbiotic bacteria to an injury site, where the bacteria release a volatile chemical perceived by conspecifics as an indicator of an incapacitated peer, triggering cannibalistic consumption. The work is extremely convincing, and the underlying biology is exciting. The combination of approaches the authors use to determine the basis for the attraction and cannibalistic behaviour is outstanding.” — Brian Lazarro is at Cornell University, Ithaca, Latest York, USA.

In a recent development, we are fascinated by insects and especially by swarming locusts. What holds a swarm together? Which mechanisms drive it forward? How do individuals interact in the swarm? And how does the swarm eventually break down into individuals? When we discovered the anti-cannibalism pheromone3, we wondered whether there might also be attractant odorants that prompt locusts to attack one another. When we observed that wounded locusts were often attacked, we decided to collect odorants specifically from them. A key moment came when we realized that guaiacol was produced not by locusts, but by bacteria. This meant that we could identify the guaiacol-producing bacteria and determine which biochemical pathways they used. We were accustomed to studying pheromones produced by moths, flies and beetles. Here, however, we had an animal carrying a microbiome-based odorant factory. When we discovered that the odorant also boosted immune function in the wounded animal, our eyes were opened even wider. — B.S.H.

According to recent reports, at low concentrations, a chemical called guaiacol prompts locusts to flock together, and encourages pathogen resistance and healing. Synthesized by a bacterial symbiont, Enterobacter hormaechi, higher concentrations turn locusts nasty, prompting them to eat their wounded comrades, overcoming the cannibalism-suppressing pheromone phenylacetonitrile. — Henry Gee, Senior Editor, Nature.
