It has been revealed that on the quest to age smarter and slower, there’s a lot that your skin care can do. But perhaps one of the greatest contributions lies in the foods you consistently eat. Of course, diet and exercise are the pillars of good health, proper body function, and longevity, but a recent study done by scientists at the German Center for Neurodegenerative Diseases (DZNE) also reveals that 10 specific diets are linked to slower aging. Experts unpack the findings for ELLE and break down which foods you should incorporate or ditch from your diet to help improve skin health.

In a recent development, to understand the connection between diet and skin health, Caitlin Hosmer, MS, RD, LDN, a registered dietitian and co-founder of MenoPantry, notes it’s important to define the notion of slowing down aging. She points out that this particular DZNE study didn’t look at wrinkles or how old people feel. “It looked at biological aging, measured with epigenetic clocks. These read chemical marks on our DNA (methylation) that shift as our cells age. In about 7,500 adults, the researchers found that following any of 10 healthy eating patterns more closely was associated with slower biological aging,” Hosmer explains. The 10 eating patterns or diets included in the study results are Mediterranean, Nordic, DASH, MIND, and plant-based. Hosmer says that while the effect was modest, this kind of study shows an association, not causation—proof that diet is the reason for the positive outcome—so it is still a meaningful signal.

Additionally, Mark Gilbert, nutritionist from The 1:1 Diet by Cambridge Weight Plan, tells ELLE that no single food has been satisfactorily studied and shown to be likely, on its own, to slow aging. “Of all the predictors of a long, healthy life, two of the best, which can be improved with diet, are muscle mass and strength, but this leads to a controversial conclusion,” indicates Gilbert. “The best nutritional intervention to increase muscle and strength is eating a high-protein diet. However, many scientists argue that high-protein diets have the opposite effect on longevity. So, if we can’t even agree on that, you can see that science has a long way to go to try and figure this out.”.

Recent findings suggest that one stat Hosmer found particularly striking was that over 70 percent of the biological pathways affected were the same across all ten diets. “I interpret that to mean that no single superfood is doing the work, and it affirms that [a combination of] food, diet, and nutrition impacts us on the cellular level. The foods these patterns share are what matter,” says Hosmer, who, along with Gilbert, breaks down the standouts:.

In a recent development, alongside individual foods, Hosmer says certain diet components have the greatest impact on aging. For starters, protein is a must. “As we age, our muscles respond less efficiently to the protein we eat. This is called anabolic resistance, and in women, the drop in estrogen makes it worse,” she explains. Building and retaining muscle isn’t only about strength; Hosmer says it’s our largest site for handling blood sugar and a strong predictor of independence later in life. “Many women eat less protein just when they need more.”.

Gilbert adds that while protein is an vital part of a balanced, longevity diet, there are some considerations to keep in mind before adding it to your meal. “I believe that because muscle and strength are so strongly associated with longevity and that protein, particularly later in life, supports them,” he indicates. The caveat, however, is that there is a potential mechanism (which has to do with the amino acid methionine) whereby too much protein could have negative effects. “Many scientists argue that, because in insects and some other animals, less protein along with calorie restriction increases lifespan, humans would similarly benefit. Also, some cite the so-called ‘Blue Zones’, alleged to show that plant-based, low-protein diets are superior. In my opinion, all of these have been shown to be invalid,” says Gilbert.

Industry observers note that fiber is another essential nutrient that you’ll reap from plant foods and mushrooms. Meat, fish, dairy, and eggs contain no fiber, and because our enzymes can’t break them down, Hosmer indicates that fiber travels intact to the large intestine to assist. Once there, fermentable fibers become food for the gut microbiome; other fibers mainly add bulk and keep things moving. “As gut bacteria ferment fiber, they make short-chain fatty acids, which nourish the cells lining the gut, help keep that barrier strong, and keep inflammation in check. Fiber also steadies blood sugar and helps the body excrete estrogen the liver has already processed, supporting healthy hormone balance. Yet more than 90 percent of American women don’t reach the recommended 21 to 25 grams a day,” Hosmer explains.

According to recent reports, plant diversity matters, too, and not just quantity. Hosmer points to research from the American Gut Project that found people who ate 30 or more different kinds of plant foods a week had more diverse gut microbiomes than those who ate 10 or fewer. “That’s because each plant brings its own mix of fibers and polyphenols, and some bacteria have preferences, so a varied diet feeds a broader community of microbes. A broader community does more jobs. It makes short-chain fatty acids that protect the gut lining, helps regulate immunity and inflammation, turns plant compounds into forms the body can use, and even helps process estrogen.” The variety of plants Hosmer adds seems to matter more than any single “perfect” plant.

Industry observers note that you’ve likely heard of healthy fats, meaning omega-3s from fish and the polyphenol-rich fat in olive oil. Adding these to your diet can support cell membranes and help settle inflammation.

Industry observers note that also on the list for Gilbert is vitamin D, which he says studies have shown to be inversely related to the risk of mortality. “This does not prove that low vitamin D is the cause, but it is compelling evidence. Although some results are controversial, low [vitamin D] levels have been linked to all-cause, cardiovascular, cancer, and infection-related mortality,” he adds. Vitamin D is intimately involved in immunity, too. It protects T-cell immune response.

Although Gilbert claims, “The only thing all good nutritionists agree on is that there is no one ‘best’ diet or ‘best’ food,” he warns that for most people, minimizing the intake of sugar and refined, starchy grains and increasing protein intake is, in my opinion and experience, the best approach, but, again, different people can thrive on different diets. “As in the examples I gave, many of these foods contain phytochemicals—plant chemicals like polyphenols, flavanols, cyanidins, and others – which affect disease risk or support systems in the body that are likely to serve you well in old age.”.

Hosmer notes several other red flags as contributing to unhealthy skin: Diets dominated by refined carbohydrates and added sugars, heavily processed meat, and regular alcohol. “Each of these drives the chronic, low-grade inflammation researchers call “inflammaging,” and blood-sugar instability.”.

Recent findings suggest that having seen so many gaps in these crucial nutrients in her practice, and understanding how women’s bodies change in midlife, led Hosmer and her team to build Menomix at MenoPantry. “One serving adds meaningful protein along with ground flaxseed and psyllium fiber, plus a range of plant ingredients. It’s made to help close the protein, fiber, and plant-variety gaps that so many women are carrying,” she adds.

To dive deeper into some of those gaps or bad habits that can send skin aging off the rails, Hosmer offers some eating patterns that push biology in the wrong direction:.

The information can be overwhelming, but instead of purging everything in your fridge, Hosmer suggests first focusing on adding good foods instead of restricting less healthful options. “In my experience, that’s more sustainable, both because it feels less like deprivation and because the “good” foods can displace the ones that speed aging up instead of slowing it down. You don’t need to pick the “right” diet and follow it perfectly.”.

She highlights that the DZNE study found few people scored well on all ten diets at once, yet better adherence to any of them helped. What matters is the common ground. Mediterranean, Nordic, DASH, MIND, and plant-based diets vary on the plate, but they have a common core: more fruits, vegetables, and whole grains, and less red and processed meat, and added sugar. “That shared core is where I’d start,” claims Hosmer, offering this meal planning guide:.

In a recent development, increase your familiarity with, and use of, plant-based proteins. Plant-based proteins offer an excellent package of beneficial nutrients, including fiber (not found in animal products). If you aren’t already familiar, learn how to include whole soy foods (tofu, tempeh, edamame) in your diet; add beans and peas and lentils to dishes here and there. Nuts and seeds and nut and seed butters count here also.

In a recent development, build meals around vegetables and fruit. Fill half your plate with them, and choose deep colors like leafy greens, berries, beets, and cabbage-family vegetables. Those colors come from polyphenols, plant compounds that act as antioxidants and feed beneficial gut bacteria.

In a recent development, make your grains whole and varied. Oats, rye, barley, brown rice, and whole-wheat bread are staples of the Nordic and DASH patterns, the two most consistently linked to slower aging in this study. Try a grain you haven’t eaten before—quinoa? Farro? Millet or teff? They are all cooked the same way—in boiling water, just with different grain-to-water ratios. Note: raise fiber gradually, with plenty of water, so your gut has time to adjust.

It has been revealed that count your plants, not your calories. Aim toward 30 different kinds of plant foods a week. Veggies, fruits, nuts, seeds, whole grains, beans, peas, herbs, and spices all count. A homemade trail mix, an interesting salad, or a smoothie with a handful of fruit and veggies can add three or four at once.

Industry observers note that choose your fats deliberately. Most of these patterns favor unsaturated fats over saturated ones. Use extra-virgin olive oil as your everyday fat, and eat oily fish two or three times a week.

Sources indicate that keep red and processed meat occasional, and keep added sugar low. Every one of these diets limits them.

Quality matters. Note that “plant-based” isn’t automatically healthy. A version of the plant-based diet built on refined grains, sweets, and sugary drinks tended to be associated with faster aging, as did a pro-inflammatory eating pattern overall. Food quality matters, not just food category.

In a recent development, diet is the foundation the other efforts rest on. Collagen supplements and skin care can help, but they can’t make up for a body that lacks what it needs to build and protect tissue. According to Gilbert, collagen supplements contain very high levels of the amino acid glycine, which he indicates can balance the potential harms of proteins that are high in methionine.

Hosmer further explains that collagen peptides break down during digestion into amino acids and small peptides. Your body then decides where to use them. “To actually build collagen, it needs enough total protein, plus vitamin C. Vitamin C is a required helper for the enzymes that stabilize the collagen structure. It also needs minerals like zinc and copper. So a collagen supplement taken on top of a protein-poor, micronutrient-poor diet is not going to be optimized,” Hosmer says. “Collagen is also an incomplete protein: it’s low in leucine and lacks tryptophan entirely. It can add to your protein intake, but it isn’t a substitute for complete proteins when it comes to muscle.”.

Industry observers note that on the surface, what we eat affects the skin from the inside. Hosmer notes that polyphenol-rich foods and healthy fats support the skin’s own defenses against oxidative stress, and high blood sugar leads to glycation, which stiffens collagen fibers. The gut matters here, too. “Researchers call the connection the gut–skin axis. When the gut lining is healthy, and the microbiome is well fed, inflammation throughout the body tends to stay lower, and the skin reflects that. External products can only do so much if we aren’t supporting our skin from the inside,” Hosmer says.

Recent findings suggest that diet plays a major role in overall skin health and slower aging, but Hosmer argues that it’s not the full picture. To optimize more youthful-looking skin, consider other factors that may be equally as important as diet.
