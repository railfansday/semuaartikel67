Industry observers note that if you bought Ethereum (ETH -1.61%) expecting the fees it uses to shrink its coin supply and lift its price, it might be time to sell. Its co-founder, Vitalik Buterin, just remarked not to count on that premise. In a Nov. 8 thread on X (formerly Twitter), Buterin said that his vision for the coin is that its value should be assessed by a handful of factors that are distinct from the fees owners pay to make transactions on its blockchain.

Industry observers note that instead, he thinks it should be considered as a store of value, which implies different things about what makes it valuable. Here's why that newly outlined philosophical position could be grounds to sell Ethereum if you hold it.

Buterin's view is that rather than maximizing fee revenue, Ethereum should instead prioritize a group of properties that he calls CROPS: censorship and capture resistance, open-source code, privacy, and security.

The CROPS properties, he argues, will imbue the token with a greater value than what would be possible by valuing the asset on the basis of what people are willing to pay to interact with it on the Ethereum blockchain. That's somewhat plausible, as they're rare traits that are hard to imitate, and that Ethereum is already well positioned to deliver.

Industry observers note that buterin additionally claims that to add weight to the proof of the coin's value, the network should try to host more tokenized real-world assets (RWAs), like Treasury bills held as a crypto token. Because those assets would be held on Ethereum, and because they could be exchanged for Ethereum or vice versa, the coin would be a medium of exchange, which would give it value even if the actual volume of exchanges taking place might be low.

But that portion of his argument is not very persuasive. On Jan. 1 of this year, there were $16.1 billion in tokenized assets on Ethereum's chain; as of Nov. 13, there were $15.1 billion. It doesn't appear as if the blockchain is a favored destination for fresh inflows related to tokenization, even though it's the crypto sector's leader in terms of its base of tokenized assets.

The wake-up call here is that Ethereum's most senior leadership has formally let go of the idea that the coin's price could be boosted over the long run by incurring transaction fees for its utilization and then burning some of those fees to destroy coins and constrain its supply.

It has been revealed that on some level, that makes sense, because the network's average fees per transaction have fallen by 96% during  the past five years as a result of tech upgrades. But for investors who bought the coin expecting fee burns to shrink its supply, it's now time to sell Ethereum. Adoption will still be pursued, but not as a way to maximize fee revenue; the CROPS factors are the priority, and their contribution to Ethereum's value is far harder to quantify than fee revenue and coins eliminated from the circulating supply.

For investors whose investment thesis was broader, it's also getting harder to argue for holding on, but the scale hasn't tipped yet, as Ethereum's financial weight within crypto still gives it a tremendous amount of staying power as well as growth potential.
