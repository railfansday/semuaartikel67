Why are the spiral arms of this galaxy glowing?

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Webb’s infrared portrait of NGC 2283 reveals where young stars light up clouds of hydrogen gas in a galaxy 45 million light-years away.

Today’s Image of the Day features NGC 2283, a spiral galaxy in the constellation Canis Major.

A few bright white stars catch the eye first. Behind them, reddish clouds wind around a golden center, tracing the arms of a galaxy about 45 million light-years away.

The James Webb Space Telescope collected the data for this portrait in just 17 minutes of total exposure time.

What makes the image worth a longer look is how much is happening inside those arms.

The bright knots scattered along them show where young stars are already changing the clouds they formed from.

Elsewhere in the same galaxy, a massive star exploded in 2023, returning material to the space between stars.

The large white stars with long, sharp spikes belong to our own Milky Way.

They lie between Earth and NGC 2283 and appear in the picture because they happen to sit along Webb’s line of sight.

Their spikes come from the way light bends around parts of the telescope. Once you look past them, the galaxy’s shape is easier to pick out.

Two loosely wound arms curve away from an elongated band of stars that crosses the center.

Astronomers call that central structure a bar. Our own galaxy has one, too, but we observe the Milky Way from inside its disk.

Webb sees NGC 2283 almost face-on, giving us a clear view of how its bar and arms fit together.

Webb built this view from six exposures taken through different infrared filters.

Its Near-Infrared Camera and Mid-Infrared Instrument recorded different parts of the light coming from the same patch of sky.

According to recent reports, combining those observations lets astronomers see several things at once. Some filters pick up light from stars or from clouds of hydrogen energized by young stars.

Others detect the glow of carbon-rich molecules called polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, which are also found in soot on Earth.

Image processors assign visible colors to those infrared measurements.

The result lets us follow details that would otherwise be invisible to our eyes, including the uneven distribution of gas and dust along the arms.

The spiral arms look almost continuous from a distance. Look closer and they break up into bright clumps, wisps, and darker gaps.

The brightest knots include dense clouds of gas lit up by young stars.

In a recent development, A star begins forming when part of a cold cloud becomes dense enough to collapse under its own gravity. As material falls inward, the growing star heats up.

Some of the most massive young stars release enough ultraviolet light to energize the hydrogen around them, making the surrounding gas glow.

That glow gives astronomers a way to find places where star formation has happened recently.

In NGC 2283, the glowing clouds and young clusters make it possible to trace the arms even where dust hides some of the stars.

On January 28, 2023, astronomers discovered a supernova in NGC 2283.

Known as SN 2023AXU, it was a Type II supernova, the kind that can occur when a massive star runs out of fuel in its core.

Once the core can no longer support itself against gravity, it collapses. The resulting explosion throws the star’s outer layers into space.

Over time, material from explosions like this can mix with surrounding gas and become part of clouds that form latest stars.

That process changes the ingredients available to later generations.

Elements such as oxygen and sodium can travel far from the star that released them, then end up in another star or in the material that gathers around it.

NGC 2283 is one of 55 nearby galaxies Webb observed in a program designed to study how stars, gas, and dust interact.

These galaxies are close enough for Webb to distinguish individual star clusters and gas clouds, allowing astronomers to compare conditions from one part of a galaxy to another.

In this portrait, the spiral arms may be the first thing you notice. The small knots along them are what make the scene worth coming back to.

Each marks a place where young stars are already leaving a visible imprint on the material around them.
