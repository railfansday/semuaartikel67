Science’s greatest failure is not getting things wrong, but by sometimes pretending that there is more certainty than there really is, indicates social scientist Andy Stirling.

This can happen, he indicates, when researchers face political pressure to provide a single, definitive answer, when the evidence is far more complex.

In the final episode of a six-part podcast series about failure in science, Stirling, an emeritus professor at the University of Sussex in Brighton, UK, draws on examples from climate change, the COVID-19 pandemic, environmental campaigns and energy policy.

“In those areas, science just does not convey the degree of uncertainty and of variability and of how many potentially viable options are being cut out when you just give one clear answer,” he states. “It thinks it’s helping society by just giving one picture of the truth. And in fact, it’s undermining both itself and democracy when it does that.”.

Stirling, whose research includes science and technology policy and environmental politics, claims that on occasions politicians have almost literally hidden behind scientists on a podium, who have relied on their authority and expertise to protect them.

Earlier this year, Stirling spoke at a one-day conference on failure in science, with a focus on careers and innovation. It was held at Imperial College London in partnership with Nature.
