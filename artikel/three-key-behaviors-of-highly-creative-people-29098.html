In a recent development, it may well be one of the most frequently asked questions by people from a wide range of occupations: “What do I need to do in order to become more creative?” Suffice it to say that there are several intertwining characteristics of those deemed to be innovative and imaginative. But, for our purposes, let’s take a look at three of the most-cited and most-recommended behaviors that positively influence our creative expression.

According to recent reports, curiosity is ingrained in our DNA. The mysteries of the universe have always been an impetus for us to peek and poke and prod for.

Industry observers note that answers - learning something latest about our surroundings as well as about ourselves. We want to know more than we know; we want to expand our horizons, try out the new and undiscovered, and pursue experiences that deepen our comprehension (and appreciation) of the world in which we live.

But curiosity is much more than a simple search for answers. It is also the generation of possibilities. When we look at the world from multiple perspectives, we give ourselves permission to examine a wider range of resolutions and remedies. We open up our minds to explore the unexamined and unknown. Curiosity becomes a propellant for fresh opportunities and new options. Ian Leslie explains it this way: “…curiosity is essential to an exploring mind; it opens our eyes to the new and undiscovered, encouraging us to seek out new experiences and meet new people.”.

It was Albert Einstein who famously said, “I have no special talents. I am only passionately curious.” For Einstein, curiosity was the engine that drove his creativity. Curiosity was his catalyst for questioning, and questioning is what propels us to seek out the unfamiliar and the unknown. For, without curiosity, knowledge becomes stagnant and immovable. It does not move us forward, nor does it have sufficient power to examine what may lie just beyond our experiences.

In conducting research for my latest book (see below), I came across some compelling insights underscoring the fact that creative thinkers tend to ask more open-ended questions. For example, a 2023 study in the Journal of Creativity took a look at the types of questions posed by 165 undergraduate students during select creative-thinking tasks. Individuals whose responses showed greater originality and fluency tended to ask more open-ended questions. The researchers also discovered that questions involving possibilities (rather than absolutes) were associated with originality in various tasks.

Consider the difference between these two questions: “Is this a good idea?” (This type of query invites a yes-no response. As such, it encourages evaluation before any exploration takes place.) and “What are some different ways this particular idea could be improved?” (This type of question, on the other hand, invites alternatives, experimentation, and the possibility of multiple solutions.) Additionally, there is evidence to indicate that open-ended questions may support creativity through several inter-related processes:.

Perhaps most compelling are the open-ended questions we ask ourselves. These inwardly-directed queries often shape what we pay attention to, which alternatives we may conjure, and how we interpret our experiences. They give us a framework to muse, ponder, and think in several different directions. For example, contemplate these possibilities: “What could I learn that would help me improve?” “What did this attempt teach me, and what could I try next?” “What are six possible solutions, including unconventional ones?” and “What other approaches might work just as well – or better?” Note that these examples illustrate divergent ways of thinking – contemplations of what might be rather than what is. Or, in the words of Eugene Jonesco, “It is not the answer that enlightens, but the question.”.

It has been revealed that taking risks is essential in our overall growth and development. We all took some risks when we learned how to walk. By the same token, we took a risk when we got our driver’s license and took the family car out for a spin, and many of us took a risk when we joined up with another individual and stated that we would spend the rest of our lives with that person. Basically, taking risks is an essential element of our learning curve. Yet, what we often practice in the real world may be altogether different—minimize the risk, and you can minimize the potential defeats. But what results is often stunted creative expression. Innovation is all about taking a chance; all about venturing into unfamiliar territories with the possibility of discovering something fresh or something different. That’s not possible without risk-taking.

Industry observers note that creative individuals are known for taking chances. They examine the unknown – not necessarily because it will lead to something successful but rather because it’s new (Cue Leonardo da Vinci, Marie Curie, Pablo Picasso and Steve Jobs). Those individuals were happy to move beyond what they knew in order to play with unexplored ideas. Most importantly, they often envisioned the inevitability of failure as a creative learning opportunity…a chance to regroup and refocus.

In a recent development, by and large, creativity involves the freedom to move into uncharted territories and unfamiliar vistas. As such, risk-taking is an essential element of the creative life simply because it gives us license to step outside our comfort zone, challenge how we think, and explore a plethora of possibilities. When we give ourselves permission to experiment (and make mistakes), we open a door to unexpected discoveries and innovative ideas. Or, as Erich Fromm so eloquently put it, “Creativity requires the courage to let go of certainties.”.

What is most key – especially for those who wish to enhance their creative spirit – is to constantly pursue, refresh, and master the three attributes above. Every day!
