In a significant development in Apple’s Supreme Court appeal of a civil contempt ruling, Epic Games has received backing from 32 states and the District of Columbia, along with major developers, legal experts, former federal judges, and more. Here are the details.

Apple’s ever-sprawling fight against Epic Games over its App Store policies is presently being fought on two fronts: in the Supreme Court, where Apple is seeking to overturn a civil contempt ruling, and in the district court, where the companies are disputing what commission, if any, Apple can charge on purchases made through external payment systems.

The civil contempt ruling came after Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers found that Apple had violated an earlier injunction issued in its App Store dispute with Epic.

In a nutshell, after she ordered Apple to allow developers to direct owners to alternative payment methods outside the App Store, the company responded by allowing external payment links, but imposed a commission of up to 27% on purchases made through them.

Since then, Apple has been trying to overturn the civil contempt ruling, basically arguing that the original injunction did not explicitly prohibit the commissions and other restrictions it imposed on external purchases.

After the Ninth Circuit upheld the contempt finding and denied its request for rehearing, Apple took the case to the Supreme Court, which agreed to hear its appeal.

Importantly, the Supreme Court will only consider the legal standard used to uphold Apple’s civil contempt ruling, not the separate question of what commission, if any, the firm can charge on purchases made through external payment methods.

That will still be determined in the district court, where proceedings have continued despite Apple’s attempts to put them on hold pending the Supreme Court’s decision.

With oral arguments scheduled for January 11, 2027, the case has recently seen a flurry of activity, including Apple’s opening brief in September and Epic’s response earlier this month.

Recent findings suggest that apple also received support from several organizations in September, including the Washington Legal Foundation, the International Center for Law and Economics, the Computer & Communications Industry Association, and the U.S. Chamber of Commerce. The US government also submitted a brief supporting neither party, arguing that the contempt finding was unjustified over Apple’s 27% commission, but warranted over its restrictions on button designs.

Which brings us to the latest developments: a series of amicus briefs filed in support of Epic Games.

Last week, a total of 9 amicus briefs were filed in support of Epic Games, including one led by Utah and joined by 31 other states and the District of Columbia.

This particular brief notes that “the district court correctly held Apple in contempt for continuing to engage in anticompetitive conduct barred by a prior injunction,” adding:.

According to recent reports, “The court found that Apple engaged in unlawful anti-steering restrictions and required Apple to lift those restrictions. Apple then lifted them in name only, imposed newly introduced (and substantially similar) strictures on developers that mirrored the old ones and produced the same result. Worse still, Apple did all this in bad faith: Apple knew its behavior was wrong and actively hid that fact from the court, including by providing false testimony. Under any reasonable reading of the district court’s injunction, Apple’s conduct is worthy of civil contempt.”.

The states also argue that “Injunctions in competition cases cannot afford complete relief without addressing the ongoing effects of anticompetitive behavior,” and that “[c]onstruing competition injunctions narrowly defeats their purpose and prevents meaningful redress.”.

Similar arguments appear throughout the other briefs, which include submissions from Spotify, Match Group, and Microsoft, as well as nonprofit organizations such as the American Antitrust Institute (AAI), Public Knowledge, and the Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF).

In their joint brief, Spotify, Match Group, and Microsoft argue that they “have first-hand experience with the effects of Apple’s conduct and evasion efforts,” adding that “Apple’s newly introduced restrictions were no different from the unlawful rules they replaced.”.

Industry observers note that as for the brief filed by former federal judges, they warned that “[i]n cases like these, where enjoined parties seek to circumvent an injunction through reliance on its literal text, courts must have the power of contempt to deter and remedy evasion. Otherwise, courts would be forced into a game of whack-a-mole, trying to anticipate every possible means of evading an injunction […]”.

Arguments by the Little Tech Association, AAI, Public Knowledge, and the EFF all follow a similar line, with some also addressing how Apple’s restrictions on external payment links could affect consumer choice, pricing, and competition.
