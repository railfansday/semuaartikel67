Tech Report: How did Coinbase stop deepfake Elon Musk’s promise to double your crypto?

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Scammers recently used a fake video of Elon Musk to get people to send them crypto. It didn’t go to blueprint.

Especially since according to Coinbase, it blocked a massive chunk of those transfers before the money could disappear.

… and Coinbase stopped $4.7 million from reaching scammers.

Someone put out a YouTube livestream made to look like a Tesla flying car demo. On screen was an AI-made Elon Musk, and the pitch was to send crypto and get 2x back.

According to recent reports, more than half a million people tuned in. The stream flashed wallet addresses and QR codes. So, sending money took just a scan.

It has been revealed that in a blog post, Coinbase laid out what happened. In about two hours, hundreds of its users tried to send funds to the scammers. Its automated fraud system caught about 91% of that.

The real Musk had nothing to do with any of it.

Here, it’s worth pointing out that Coinbase hasn’t said how much of the remaining 9% actually got through.

Its fraud agent watches outgoing transfers as they happen.

Recent findings suggest that if any transfer looks off or iffy, like a payment to a freshly created wallet or something tied to a “giveaway,” the agent in question steps in. In such a case, the agent opens a chat with the user, explains why the transfer might be risky, and prompts them to think twice before proceeding.

It even checks its own replies before showing them. According to Coinbase, no person was involved at any point. Nobody’s account got hacked either. No passwords leaked. People (some of them long-time users) chose to hit send because the fake Musk was convincing.

Our systems can see risk signals in real time. They can’t see intent.

That is exactly what makes these scams tough.

According to recent reports, it isn’t the first fake Elon Musk either. A doctored video in 2023 reportedly cost victims around $160K. In June 2024, deepfake giveaways popped up on more than 35 YouTube channels during a Starship launch.
