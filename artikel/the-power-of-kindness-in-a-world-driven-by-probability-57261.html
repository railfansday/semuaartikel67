Posted November 19, 2026 | Reviewed by Jessica Schrader.

"We place life’s bets by countless calculations of probability, conscious and unconscious, only to discover over and over how short they fall of the wildest reaches of the possible, which always includes but exceeds the probable." —Maria Popova.

“I’ve been searching for ways to heal myself, and I’ve found that kindness is the best way.” —Lady Gaga.

In a recent development, "The question is not: what have I achieved? But rather: who can I be when I am with you? Who can you be when you are with me?" —Nora Bateson.

It has been revealed that we live in a world, at least in our hectic Western-based culture, that is driven by probability. What is probability? How does it taste, smell, or feel? Does it just linger in our complexity, waiting for a never-achieved form? We calculate within probability both with our awareness and unawareness. It penetrates our educational, economic, political, and medical systems. It assumes that we will automatically accept models of certainty. Yet our problems still exist in the domain of probable content.

Industry observers note that probability is all-powerful and designed to make you believe its outcomes are absolute. It waits for our lives to pass through it with definitive rules to fit all. However, it can never seem to guarantee success. It tries to be the basis of caution, yet it can also be the source of the other side of safety. It can, in many instances, be the origin of violence and discrimination.

It has been revealed that why? Probability measures the likelihood or chance that a specific event will occur. It survives on unresolved paradoxes of this or that, and that misses the yin/yang, messy/beautifulness of life in all its complexity. It can be funny, as in humor, and puzzling with choices, but when not harnessed, it feels separate and fragmented, avoiding the ecological process of how all patterns connect.

Industry observers note that probability evades the wisdom of understanding how love, altruism, and empathy can breed kindness, which some might consider the regulating homeostasis basis of living systems. Anthropologist Gregory Bateson more than hinted at the misunderstanding of probability’s faults by suggesting that absolute outcomes hide how "Human actions involve play, context, and the probability of disaster or risk within a relationship." Probability makes us too busy to savor what happens between relationships, yet when we do, it can help us step back from absolute outcomes and certainty and find improvisational potential. When we do, with encouragement, this can create all kinds of possibilities. Maria Popova, for instance, mentions in the first stanza of her poem "The Wildest Bet Is the Winning Bet":.

"You wouldn’t have bet on it,the battered rockorbiting a starfrom the discount binof the universe".

Probability dissolves when you zoom outward with your personal lens and widen your perspective, making room for kindness to be born. Probability, in this case, begins to blend with how we share our possessions. It becomes an advocate for tending to others' needs, which gives ultimate meaning to who we are as humans. It becomes the terrain of kindness, the quality or state of being friendly, generous, considerate, and genuinely concerned for the well-being of others. Through kindness, we can choose to blend with the paradoxes inherent in unchecked probability to see an ecological unity in a manner felt by C.S. Lewis, who wrote in Surprised by Joy: "Only when your whole attention and desire are fixed on something else—whether a distant mountain, or the past, or the gods of Asgard—does the 'thrill' arise." That is the prerequisite for kindness.

Recent findings suggest that using kindness as an ecological framework supports a sense of order in contrast to probability’s search for finite answers. This demand for finitude is what excludes kindness because it depends on producing outcomes. Kindness, however, is driven by understanding the constraints and interdependency of any living system. This is why it is woven into global popular symbolism and metaphors. It is based on maintaining what may be good for humanity rather than accumulating something. It is forming a kinship based on forgiveness, not to condone past pain but to move forward with altruism and empathy. Kindness emerges in what poet Allen Ginsberg poetically called a state that "stretches thru every probability." It is the beginner's mind of temporary emptiness, which can at first seem conceptually liable, mistaken for sheer nothingness, yet is in fact the improvisational potential for possibilities of kindness.

According to recent reports, we must recognize this need for kindness, especially in today's struggle to accept diversity. This underscores a profound reminder, particularly useful as we celebrate Thanksgiving, that the Anglo experience in North America began as a massive mistake in probability, as Garrison Keillor reminds us: "...we survived only by the kindness of strangers who had every reason to be cruel." In a quote often attributed to Mark Twain, this type of innate empathy is "...the language which the deaf can hear and the blind can see.".

When the dictates of probability, with seemingly good intentions, succumb to the dark side of fragmented content in our educational systems, isolated profit-driven health mandates, and sociopolitical polarization, the antidote is massive acts of kindness. This releases feel-good neurotransmitters like dopamine, serotonin, and endorphins. It activates the brain's reward center, creating euphoria and emotional warmth. It further reduces stress by soothing the vagus nerve, which recharges and rejuvenates when it feels secure.

Industry observers note that it is interesting how unchecked probability is the cloak of "science" that constantly tries to quantify or refute the effects of kindness, when it only needs to observe how it strengthens social bonds like a welcome adhesive. Kindness can increase trust and collaboration, and encourage greater equity in the distribution of wealth, security, and opportunity, allowing for our innate interdependency with each other and nature. Another worthwhile attribute is the resulting togetherness that correlates with lower rates of anxiety and depression. Consider this in contrast to the array of physical ailments that can occur with unchecked probability, and it's a choice worth exploring.

Here are some prompts to help you sustain kindness as an antidote to probability. Use them with someone else or in groups. After each response, follow up with at least five “How did that affect you?” questions and ask how you can make a difference. Then discuss using paraphrasing to provide feedback about what was heard.
