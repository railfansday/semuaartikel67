Why there are still no non-WebKit browsers on iOS

🌐 Source: Mozilla • 📅 20 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 3 min read (536 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that firefox on iPhone is growing fast across the world. This is especially true in the EU since 2024, when the EU Digital Markets Act (DMA) started requiring Apple to let people choose their default browser; millions have picked Firefox, and daily use on iOS has doubled. People want to choose the browser on their phone.

But there remains an key limitation to what is available on iPhones: Apple forces all browsers on iOS to be built on Apple’s own browser engine.

Industry observers note that this has meant that companies like Mozilla cannot easily bring the full range of Firefox features and functionality to iPhone customers because many of these are built within our browser engine, Gecko. iPhones and iPads are unique in having this restriction. On all other major operating systems, including Apple’s macOS, people can access the Gecko-powered version of Firefox.

According to recent reports, as a result of the DMA, Apple began allowing alternative browser engines on iOS in the EU. It published a set of conditions that companies like Mozilla must comply with to bring their own browser engine. At the time we said Apple’s rules made it “as painful as possible” for alternatives like Gecko. In the meantime, Japan also introduced new rules requiring alternative browser engines on iOS. Apple applied very similar conditions in Japan to those in the EU.

Industry observers note that nearly three years since Apple introduced these conditions in the EU and almost a year since Japan, not one developer has been able to launch a non-WebKit browser on iOS. The reason is simple: Apple’s conditions remain unworkable. The real test of any rule is whether it delivers the change it was meant to achieve in practice. Compliance on paper alone falls short of that test.

In a recent development, since the DMA came into effect we’ve been working behind the scenes to test and improve Apple’s conditions so that we can bring Gecko to iOS owners. Those efforts have so far largely failed.

One of the conditions requires us not to disclose non-public information related to the project, but here’s an example of what we are up against: to use Gecko, we can’t simply upgrade the Firefox app people already use and love. We’d have to build a whole separate app, a second Firefox. Then we’d have to ask every Firefox user to download it, move their bookmarks, passwords and history across, and manually switch over, step by confusing step.

Recent findings suggest that that’s a lot to ask of people who already made their choice. A significant proportion of people would understandably give up, including many who picked Firefox from the EU’s choice screen.

The conditions Apple imposes, including a level of user friction Apple would never accept for its own apps, mean that the open door feels more like a trap door.

According to recent reports, firefox users on iPhone chose an independent browser. They should get everything that comes with it, including Gecko, the last major browser engine not developed by a big tech company. We’ll keep pushing for a real level playing field, not an opening that drops independent browsers through the floor.
