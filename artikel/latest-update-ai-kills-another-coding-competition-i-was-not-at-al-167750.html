The Atari Homebrew Awards, an annual celebration of fresh games developed for old systems by the people who love them both, has been cancelled after eight years of operation—and yes, it's because of AI.

Industry observers note that "As you may know, the landscape of retro game development changed dramatically this year with the massively increased capabilities of LLMs. More and more games have incorporated AI elements into their development chain and now have the capability to create full games without writing a single line of code," Atari Homebrew Awards host James, aka ZeroPage Homebrew, wrote on the Atari Age forums.

It has been revealed that "Keeping up with the large number of AI-assisted game development had become increasingly difficult as this year wore on, with some developers even releasing multiple games a week with the aid of AI. This flood of new AI games forced me to re-evaluate the way games were qualified and judged for the upcoming AHAs.

According to recent reports, "The Atari Homebrew Awards were now challenged to make sure the work of human developers were still honoured amongst the new fully computer-coded games. I was not at all interested in giving awards to the output of large language models, only to humans who coded their own games, but I rapidly discovered that this was going to be more difficult than I thought.".

James remarked he discovered last week that "a large majority" of developers were using AI in some capacity to make their games, from testing to wholesale game creation, "without one typed line of code.".

That left two options: Disallow any and all AI usage in the Atari Homebrew Awards competition, which James said would disqualify a large number of "extremely talented coders" who used AI in some very minor way, or let it slide and either choose an arbitrary dividing line between what is and isn't allowed or allow everything, including fully vibe-coded games.

It has been revealed that james mentioned he wasn't interested in that, which meant he needed to settle on how much AI usage would be allowable—and also how to determine if AI was used at all, since some developers are better at disclosure than others. That, naturally, led to the worry that developers could be subject to "false positives and possible witch hunts," something that's already a problem in mainstream game development.

Ultimately, James decided there was no practical way forward, although he did leave open the possibility of a return someday. "This is an extremely upsetting conclusion for this event because honouring all the hard work the community put into these games is always a highlight of my year," he wrote. "I know many others feel the same way and I hope to be able to continue this tradition in the coming years as things settle into a fresh normal.".

As we noted in July, debates over "acceptable" levels of AI use are cropping up all over the open source development scene: Some are drawing hard lines against it for principled and/or practical reasons, yet its popularity is growing explosively because, frankly, you don't need to know what you're doing in order to do it—as long as you're not too concerned with doing it well.

According to recent reports, the Atari Homebrew Awards isn't the first such event to fall victim to the AI flood. Earlier in November, the Australian STEM Videogame Challenge, a "free national videogame development competition for students," announced that the 2026 event will be its last because "the rapid and complex rise of generative AI in game development [made] it harder to judge what students made themselves," and thus undercut the whole purpose of the program: Encouraging kids to learn how to code.
