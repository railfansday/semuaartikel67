The Rise of the House Manager: Why Busy Families Are Outsourcing Everyday Tasks

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Between managing the demands of work and keeping up with everything at home, it can feel like there are never enough hours in the day.

For busy, overscheduled families, hiring a house manager may be the key to reclaiming valuable time, reducing daily stress, and bringing a little more balance to everyday life.

For those who are unfamiliar with this trending form of home support, a house manager is basically someone who keeps track of things behind the scenes so you can off-load some of your never-ending to-do list.

"A house manager takes on the parts of the household a family chooses to hand off—that means both the everyday work and the planning behind it, from start to finish," indicates Kelly Hubbell, founder and CEO of Sage Haus, a thriving house manager placement tech giant. "Think meals, laundry, errands, calendars, pet care, and home projects. A house manager doesn't just complete tasks ... they anticipate what's needed and handle it without being asked.".

In other words, a house manager is an added support system that helps keep a household functioning efficiently.

The simplest definition of a house manger is someone who helps facilitate everyday life behind the scenes.

"A housekeeper cleans, a nanny cares for the kids, a house manager owns whole areas of the household, from planning to follow-through," claims Hubbell.

There can be overlap. A house manager might tidy, do laundry, or prepare meals, depending on what the family hands off. The difference is ownership.

In a recent development, "Take laundry: A housekeeper might wash what's in the basket, whereas a house manager who owns laundry knows the soccer uniform has to be clean by Saturday, without a reminder," explains Hubbell. "The work that's different is the thinking behind the household.".

The best house managers have sharp judgment and the ability to plan ahead, juggle several schedules and tasks at once, manage vendors, and make good decisions within the scope a family has agreed to, according to Hubbell.

They're proactive and notice what needs attention before anyone asks. And because they're in someone's home, trust, reliability, and discretion are essential.

"People come to it from lots of backgrounds: nannies whose kids aged out of needing a nanny, teachers, hospitality and operations managers, military spouses who have set up a household in four cities, and moms coming back into the workforce after years of running their own homes—frankly, the hardest training there is," says Hubbell, whose network now includes over 7,000 house managers in the U.S. and Canada, though Sage Haus accepts only about 5% of applicants to ensure quality.

"As AI changes the jobs available, this work depends on what technology can't supply: human judgment, trust, and care," she explains.

Basically, house managers are gaining traction because people are burning out, especially women.

Sources indicate that "Most families hit a point where their life, career, or ambitions can't grow any further without more support—I call that the support ceiling," says Hubbell. "They don't need to scale back their dreams. They need to scale up their support.".

Many families already have some help, like a cleaner every other week, a nanny/sitter, or groceries on autopilot. But someone still has to hold it all together—the noticing, planning, and following up.

"That's the mental load, and research shows it usually stays with mom, even as her career and earnings grow," notes Hubbell.

In a recent development, according to a 2024 study from the University of Bath and the University of Melbourne, mothers in the U.S. carry 71% of mental-load tasks, and while women’s employment and earnings reduce their physical household labor, it does not shrink their cognitive burden.

According to recent reports, "Real estate has always recognized the value of a well-maintained home. What we're only beginning to recognize is the value of the work required to maintain it," states Laura Marie Rivera, a gender equity and unpaid labor researcher and host of the "Motherhood Advantage" podcast. "The rise of professional house managers puts a job title and salary on responsibilities that mothers have managed for generations—largely without either.".

Unpaid household work in the U.S. is worth up to $6 trillion a year, and none of it highlights up in the GDP. However, the rise of house mangers is finally putting a dollar amount on the mental load of keeping a home operational.

Sources indicate that "Paying someone to manage a household doesn't just reveal the economic value of the work, it reveals the scope of the work," claims Rivera. "We're not witnessing the invention of a fresh profession so much as the professional recognition of work that has been hiding in plain sight.".

The good news is that having a house manager isn't only for the wealthy. In general, cost depends on where you live and what you need the house manager to take on.

"Families hire and pay house managers directly, and pay ranges from $25 to more than $50 an hour depending on location and scope," states Hubbell. "It doesn't have to be full time: 80% of families start with part-time help, averaging 15 to 20 hours per week.".

Recent findings suggest that for many families, a house manager can be a more practical investment than several separate kinds of help, because it brings that support together in one role. However, there are still options when budgetary constraints prohibit the employment of a house manager.

"Support doesn't have to start with a hire," says Hubbell. "It can begin with mapping out everything it takes to run your home and deciding what to drop, what to share with a partner, and where you need help most.".

That being remarked, for those who can swing it, having a house manager can be life-changing.

Industry observers note that in theory this all sounds amazing, but what's it like in practice? We checked in with a house manager and a family that's hired a house manager to get the inside scoop. But the short version is, both are very happy with their decision.

Before becoming a house manager, Terry Boch worked as an executive assistant, but also spent years as a stay-at-home mom raising her three children.

Industry observers note that once they were grown and she reentered the work force, she was enjoying being an executive assistant again but realized she wanted something less computer-focused and more hands-on. That's when she came across a job post seeking house managers. Intrigued, she pursued the opportunity, was matched with a family in New Jersey, and found it was a perfect fit for her skill set.

Sources indicate that "I love transforming an already beautiful house into a warm, welcoming home that looks and smells inviting every day," indicates Boch, whose primary responsibilities include meal planning, grocery shopping and meal preparation, laundry, resetting bedrooms and living spaces, and plant care. "I work approximately 15–20 hours per week, but I like to say that I get to play house in a home I could never afford.".

In reality, however, Boch indicates she sees what she does as much more about creating a home than maintaining a house.

"Although my interaction with the children is limited, knowing that I can contribute to an environment where they feel comfortable, cared for, and happy brings me tremendous joy," says Boch. "I love making a home feel welcoming, anticipating what a family needs, and taking pride in all the little things that make everyday life more enjoyable. I'm not a house cleaner; I'm a homemaker at heart.".

On the flip side, Krissel Varela found her way to the idea of hiring a house manager online.

In a recent development, "Following Sage Haus on social media really opened my eyes to what household support could look like," notes Varela. "I had always thought about help in a more traditional sense, like a nanny or someone full-time, but started realizing we could shape the support around what our family actually needed.".

Recent findings suggest that though she'd had a nanny when her kids were younger and she was working a corporate job, once they reached school age, they didn't need that kind of assistance anymore. However, their lives didn't get less busy.

"Between school, sports, activities, keeping our household running, and now building a business of my own, there are just so many moving pieces," indicates Varela. The idea of a house manager made sense for her family, though they didn't need someone full time.

According to recent reports, "Right now, we have help about 8 to 10 hours a week, and even though that's not a huge number of hours, the impact feels so much bigger," states Varela. Her house manager is usually with them on Mondays and Thursdays, with some flexibility predicated on their needs.

"Between laundry, household organization, errands, and all those little tasks that add up throughout the week, having someone share those responsibilities makes such a difference," she adds.

Though it's been only a few months, Varela says she can already feel a huge life shift.

"It's not just the actual hours saved; it's also the mental space that comes from knowing someone else is helping keep things moving without having to manage every little detail," notes Varela. "It's also given me more room for the things that are easy to push aside when life gets busy, whether that's spending quality time with my husband, enjoying our weekends, or even having a little time for myself. Having that extra support has really helped me be more intentional about where my time and energy go.".

And that is exactly what a house manager is supposed to do, open up space for life at home to be about more than just getting by every day.

Sources indicate that "People should also know there's a cost to getting support and a cost to going without it, whether that's your health, your relationships, or your connection with your family," says Hubbell. "The goal isn't to outsource your life; it's to get enough support to be present for it.".
