Industry observers note that A “situationship” is more than a casual hookup, but it’s not exactly a committed relationship, either. While there may be sex, emotional intimacy, and even regular dates, it stops short of an established relationship because the partners involved don’t have a clear or mutually agreed understanding of where things stand.

Industry observers note that situationships appear to be on the rise, but what do we actually know about them from a scientific standpoint? For example, do men and women tend to approach them differently? And do they ever turn into romantic relationships?

New preliminary research offers intriguing insight into the nature of situationships.

In this study, researchers surveyed 390 adults, most of whom were from the United States, United Kingdom, or Canada. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 75 and were asked to report on relationship experiences that “exist between casual dating and committed partnerships” that “typically lack clear labels or commitment.”.

It has been revealed that they were asked about their history with situationships, including how many they’d had, as well as their motivations for pursuing such arrangements and their expectations around sexual exclusivity. They were also asked to define what a situationship means in their own words.

What these findings tell us is that most people seem to understand situationships as sexual and intimate connections that feature a lot of relationship-like behaviors, but exist in a very ambiguous and unclear state.

According to recent reports, the results also indicated that men and women differed in terms of how they approached situationships. For example, women were significantly more likely than men to report entering these relationships with the hope that they would eventually develop into a committed partnership. They were also more likely to see long-term romantic potential.

Industry observers note that however, women were especially likely to describe situationships as emotionally confusing or characterized by mixed signals. In fact, 77 percent of women compared to 50 percent of men noted that situationships involve moments of closeness followed by distance. Likewise, 65 percent of women (vs. 44 percent of men) described situationships as carrying on without really progressing toward anything.

These findings suggest that, on average, men tend to approach situationships more casually, whereas women are more likely to view them as a possible stepping stone to a full-fledged romance.

Recent findings suggest that it’s important to reiterate that these are average (not universal) sex differences. However, whenever people enter a relationship with different expectations, there’s extra potential for disappointment.

One of the most striking findings was just how rarely situationships actually turned into romances.

Overall, 56 percent of participants said they had entered a situationship hoping it would later become a relationship. However, just 9 percent of past situationships eventually made the transition.

In other words, if you’re hoping for more from a situationship, odds are that it probably won’t work out, despite the fact that these relationships aren’t necessarily brief and are often recurring. In fact, about 41 percent of past situationships lasted at least seven months, with about one-quarter lasting for a year or longer. Also, half of participants reported that they had returned to a previous situationship at some point.

In short, situationships are often prolonged, recurring connections, even when they aren't progressing toward commitment.

Recent findings suggest that despite the lack of clear labels and expectations, 55 percent of participants assumed their situationship was sexually exclusive. Moreover, 68 percent of women and 58 percent of men agreed that they would feel betrayed if their situationship partner was seeing other people.

This suggests that just because a relationship is undefined, it doesn’t necessarily mean that people enter it free of expectations. Although some might assume that regular sex, emotional intimacy, and going on dates imply exclusivity or growing commitment, their partners may be interpreting the same behaviors very differently.

As with any study, this one has its limitations. In particular, because it involved an online convenience sample, it’s not clear how representative the findings are of the broader population. Also, all participants reported on different-sex situationships, so we can’t say whether the findings would apply to people in same-sex contexts.

Sources indicate that that remarked, the findings still tell us something crucial, which is that situationships aren't necessarily problematic because they lack traditional relationship labels. The core tension is that partners often don’t understand or agree upon what their connection really means.

According to recent reports, while freedom from labels and expectations is part of the appeal of a situationship, it seems that many are still bringing certain assumptions into them. Further, escalating intimacy doesn’t necessarily signal that each partner sees a progression toward commitment.
