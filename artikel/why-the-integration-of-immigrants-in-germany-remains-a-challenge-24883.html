Why the integration of immigrants in Germany remains a challenge

Sources indicate that few topics in Germany spark as many heated debates as integration. How successful have efforts been to integrate newly introduced arrivals? What is needed to enable successful integration? And what are the biggest problems?

Every two years, the government's Commissioner for Integration presents a comprehensive set of statistics on the matter. Since last year, that role has been held by Natalie Pawlik, a parliamentary representative from the center-left Social Democratic Party (SPD).

Speaking in Berlin on Wednesday, Pawlik offered an overall positive assessment: "Integration is improving steadily, despite ongoing hurdles and challenges." She continued: "The people of our country want integration to succeed. They rightly expect that people who live in Germany permanently will learn our language and abide by our laws.".

The Commissioner for Integration is part of the Federal Ministry of Labor because ensuring that newcomers find work quickly is especially key.

In a recent development, the integration reportwas created by a team led by Frank Kalter, director of the German Center for Integration and Migration Research (DeZIM), drawing on official government statistics, surveys and independent studies. They found that when all immigrant groups are counted, about 30% of the population today has some sort of migration background — even if that comes down to only one parent.

Pawlik commented: "This is an notable message at a time when the discussion often focuses primarily on problems related to supposedly failed integration." Kalter also pointed out that the employment rate among immigrants in Germany is higher than in most other EU countries — nearly 70%.

Sources indicate that and they work primarily in one sector: "In recent years, the biggest increase of workers without German citizenship has been in the health and social services sector." According to the data, there were about 1.8 million people without a German passport working in Germany in 2009. By 2025, this number had more than tripled to 5.8 million.

According to recent reports, however, problems remain. In nearly all immigrant groups, women have difficulty finding employment, especially if they have children and their husbands are employed. Only about 60% of women in such families are employed, whereas nearly 88% of women in families with German passports are employed.

According to recent reports, there are also immigrant groups that face barriers to accessing educational opportunities and the job market. These are mostly young men who came to Germany as refugees. And if one considers citizenship as a distinguishing factor, the percentage of convicted criminals who are not German citizens is far higher — around 40% — than their share of the population. And they are also reported to the police far more frequently. At the same time, individuals without a German passport are also more likely to become victims of crimes.

Pawlik emphasizes that language acquisition is the foundation and starting point of any successful integration. But this is precisely where the government tightened its purse strings earlier this year to save money. Many integration courses are now no longer offered at all or receive less government funding. Pawlik made it clear that she is strongly critical of these cuts, even though she herself is part of the federal government.

It has been revealed that most recently, Pawlik and her fellow Social Democrats have been advocating for people who have been working in Germany for some time to receive more secure residency permits — especially those from Syria and Ukraine. On Tuesday, SPD lawmakers adopted a position paper on the matter. In the paper, they propose that existing legal rights that allow workers to remain in the country should also apply to refugees. In other words, it would give working refugees a more secure status.

But will the conservatives from the Christian Democratic Union (CDU) and Christian Social Union (CSU) — who lead the coalition led by Chancellor Friedrich Merz (CDU) — support this? "You'll have to ask the interior minister," Pawlik replied when asked by DW. Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt (CSU) is known for being a hardliner on immigration policy.
