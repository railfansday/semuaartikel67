Latest Update: Delta Air Lines CEO Called Wi-Fi Essential, Now Indicates Passengers Don’t Choose Airlines For It

🌐 Source: Liveandletsfly • 📅 19 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 6 min read (1231 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways
  • Delta Air Lines CEO Ed Bastian once called connectivity essential to daily life and promised an onboard experience resembling what passengers enjoy on the ground.
  • Speaking with Fortune Editor-in-Chief Alyson Shontell at the Fortune 500 Innovation Forum on November 16, Bastian defended Delta’s connectivity strategy.
  • “This is about the total experience, and customers are not choosing airlines based on the Wi-Fi.”.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Delta Air Lines CEO Ed Bastian once called connectivity essential to daily life and promised an onboard experience resembling what passengers enjoy on the ground. Now, defending the decision to wait for Amazon Leo rather than install Starlink, he notes customers do not choose airlines based on Wi-Fi. I understand the argument he is trying to make, but Delta spent years teaching passengers to expect more from their internet connection. Some of us listened.

Speaking with Fortune Editor-in-Chief Alyson Shontell at the Fortune 500 Innovation Forum on November 16, Bastian defended Delta’s connectivity strategy.

“This is about the total experience, and customers are not choosing airlines based on the Wi-Fi.”.

He acknowledged enjoying Starlink himself, explained that Delta and SpaceX could not agree on a business model, and expressed confidence that Amazon could deliver a competitive alternative. Bastian also defended the existing Viasat product, describing it as “pretty darn close” to Starlink.

We can debate his last point, but his broader claim about how customers choose airlines strikes me as far too dismissive, especially considering what he has said before.

Industry observers note that when Delta announced its free Wi-Fi initiative in January 2023, Bastian described connectivity in very different terms. Back then, he said:.

“At work, at home and everywhere in between, connectivity is essential to daily life, and your journey on Delta should be no different.”.

In the accompanying T-Mobile announcement, he added:.

“The ability to stay connected while seeing the world is simply foundational.”.

It has been revealed that I agreed with him. Delta made fast, free Wi-Fi part of its premium proposition. The message was that passengers could expect a more useful and enjoyable flight because they could remain connected.

Sources indicate that the airline has continued making that case. Its own explanation of why travelers should choose Delta includes fast, free Wi-Fi among the benefits it promotes.

Now that its primary competitor, United, is rapidly approaching a point in which it will have a superior internet product, the message sounds more like passengers should focus on everything else.

I tend to give people the benefit of the doubt and Bastian is no exception.

In a recent development, saying connectivity is essential does not necessarily mean passengers will select the airline with the fastest connection. Bastian could reasonably argue that Delta already provides sufficiently good Wi-Fi and that another improvement in speed will not overcome differences in schedules, service, or loyalty.

It has been revealed that but saying customers do not choose airlines based on Wi-Fi goes considerably further. Some certainly do. I am one of them. Delta cannot spend years promoting connectivity as part of what makes its product worth choosing, then expect no scrutiny when its CEO downplays connectivity as a reason to choose a competitor.

Perhaps Bastian has data showing that relatively few passengers have changed their booking habits. I would be interested in seeing it. His statement, however, leaves little room for customers whose behavior already contradicts it. He’s essentially inviting them to fly to United instead.

Not that I fly Delta often, but unlike some of my colleagues I’ve had very good experiences with Wi-Fi on Delta.

Recent findings suggest that the Viasat Wi-Fi I have used on both American Airlines and Delta has generally been fine for the work I do. I can work on the blog, handle email, and complete other tasks without needing the fastest connection available.

Starlink or Viasat, I just need my connection to work and I should not have to wonder whether it will (one reason there are many aircraft on United, like the 757-300, that I avoid like the plague). If Viasat lets me accomplish what I need to accomplish, that flight remains productive and I’m a happy passenger. I am not going to pretend otherwise simply because I prefer Starlink.

It has been revealed that but Starlink has raised my expectations. It is objectively faster and more responsive and it more closely resembles working from the ground…always. When I have comparable options, that influences my decision. I have already explained how I try to improve my odds of finding Starlink on United Airlines.

That does not mean I would accept any fare or routing to get it, but it means connectivity enters the calculation alongside departure time and price, particularly on a daytime flight. For someone whose work travels with him, several productive hours onboard have immense value…and I cannot relax on a plane if there is work to be done (that miserable United 777 flight from SFO-EWR comes to mind).

Bastian is right that passengers evaluate the total experience, but the internet connection is part of that experience and increasingly a critical part of the experience as passengers expectations evolve.

A good crew and comfortable seat are welcome and in some ways foundational, but so is being able to finish work before landing rather than spending the evening catching up instead of spending it with my family.

Sources indicate that different passengers will assign different values to those things. Someone taking a vacation may be delighted to disconnect. Someone traveling during the workday may be far more concerned about whether the connection will support the tasks waiting on a laptop. I do not expect Bastian to concede that every passenger should leave Delta for United, which is just a stupid argument. But recognizing that connectivity can influence a booking would hardly undermine the rest of Delta’s product…Bastian suddenly sounds defensive and tone deaf.

It would simply acknowledge how some customers use their time onboard….

I have defended the commercial logic behind Delta choosing Amazon Leo. An airline has legitimate reasons to consider control over its passenger interface and the broader business relationship when selecting a technology partner. We should also welcome competition for Starlink. Having another capable provider would be good for airlines and passengers.

According to recent reports, but Delta prepares to begin installing Leo on an initial 500 aircraft in 2028. That leaves time for competitors to establish new expectations, even if Amazon ultimately delivers an excellent product. As I discussed earlier this week, the failed launch carrying 40 Amazon satellites illustrates the execution risk involved in waiting for a network still being built, even if Delta has multiple back-up options.

Sources indicate that delta may have made the right long-term choice: only time will tell. But that does not mean passengers will ignore what competitors offer in the meantime and I’m fairly comfortable in saying Delta will lose a fair number of customers due to its Wi-Fi disparity with United.

Bastian once described connectivity as essential and foundational. He was right then, making his recent remarks puzzling, even when taken in context. I also concede that the Viasat service on American and Delta has generally worked well for my needs. This is not a case of Starlink being the only usable internet in the sky.

But reliable Wi-Fi now influences which flights I book, and Starlink increasingly gives me another reason to choose one departure over another and specifically, to choose United Airlines over others. Delta helped make connectivity something passengers expect. It should not be surprised when passengers begin choosing airlines based on who delivers it best.
