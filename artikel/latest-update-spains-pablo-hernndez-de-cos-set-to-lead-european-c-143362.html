Latest Update: Spain’s Pablo Hernández de Cos set to lead European Central Bank

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Pablo Hernández de Cos is set to succeed Christine Lagarde as president of the European Central Bank next year.

He received backing from euro zone leaders at the European Council Summit, multiple media reported on Thursday.

The role still requires formal appointment after consultation with the European Parliament and the ECB's Governing Council, but neither has veto power and the decision follows intense political wrangling to reach a consensus on who should serve the eight-year term.

Reuters reported that de Cos was set for the nomination after the Netherlands agreed to back Klaas Knot, the only other candidate, citing European diplomatic sources.

De Cos is a 55-year-old economist and career central banker who rose through the ranks of the Bank of Spain, starting as a researcher in the 1990s and working in various departments before becoming governor from 2018 to June 2024.

He chaired the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision from 2019 to 2024, and has been General Manager of the Basel-based Bank for International Settlements since July 2025.

Both de Cos and his main rival for the ECB top job, Knot, were widely seen as candidates with deep knowledge and experience in monetary policy and financial stability — attributes that took on particular importance at a time of resurgent inflation, fragmentation and political uncertainty.

Lagarde had extensive experience in financial institutions but was criticized by some early in her tenure for a lack of central banking experience.

Sources indicate that de Cos gained sufficient political backing in closed-door discussions and negotiations since Lagarde noted she would step down on Feb. 7, 2027, over seven years after she became the first woman to helm the ECB. That was despite a final push by the Netherlands at the European Council meeting this week backing Knot.

Representatives from the euro area's biggest economies — Germany, France, Italy, Spain and the Netherlands — have been horse-trading as they look to secure representation on the central bank's six-member Executive Board.

Influential roles as chief economist, expected to go to Germany's Markus Brunnermeier, and head of the Markets Portfolio, for which France's Laurence Boone has reportedly been tapped, also change hands in 2027.

Analysts say de Cos's commentary and actions over more than two decades at Spain's central bank paint him as moderately dovish — inclined to favor lower interest rates to support economic growth — but also as a pragmatist who has been willing to adapt.

That has included support for strong rate hikes during the energy price shock that followed Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, but also giving staunch backing for rates below zero and the huge stimulus package introduced by then-ECB President Mario Draghi to reboot the euro zone economy in 2019.

De Cos quipped in 2024 that he was an "owl" after being asked if he was a hawk or a dove, emphasizing his focus on data and the situation at hand.

Sources indicate that "It is true that Hernández de Cos supported the ECB's uber-dovish monetary policy during the period when inflation was persistently below target. However, his monetary policy views may well be regime-dependent," Christian Kopf, head of fixed income and foreign exchange at Union Investment, said in emailed comments.

"We are now clearly in a higher-inflation regime, with core inflation in the euro area expected to remain above the ECB's target through 2029.".

Kopf added that de Cos was the candidate with the "strongest technical understanding of monetary policy and central banking," which he said was vital at a time when the world is "witnessing an assault on central bank independence, not only in the United States but also in countries such as Japan.".

"By appointing a career technocrat such as Hernández de Cos as ECB President, the European Council would send a strong signal that Europe will not waver in its commitment to price stability and that the euro will remain a hard currency," Kopf remarked.

Sources indicate that spain's de Cos as president, Germany's Brunnermeier as chief economist and France's Boone as head of market operations represent a "carefully crafted package deal," Kopf also noted, because it balances representation from northern and southern European countries. The result was partially due to the knock-on effects of the German government pushing hard for the chief economist position, he stated.

Along with a desire among politicians for influence, a north-south balance is seen as important as the northern countries are seen as leaning more hawkish — more inclined to opt for rate hikes and restrictive policy to tame inflation — while southern are perceived as more dovish.

John Orchard, chairman of the Digital Monetary Institute at think tank OMFIF, said that as a "central banker's central banker," de Cos had "managed to earn respect from typically hawkish northern observers.".

Another supportive factor of de Cos's candidacy was the stellar growth rate of many of those southern economies in recent years.

Once the "sick men" of the euro area's 2010s sovereign debt crisis, Portugal, Italy, Spain and Greece have become some of the bloc's strongest growth drivers in recent years.

Industry observers note that factors such as strong tourism, relative political stability, increased foreign investment and strengthening public finances have boosted southern economies. Meanwhile, the euro zone's traditional powerhouse Germany has stuttered amid soaring industrial costs and a struggling autos market.

Orchard noted that some believed that Lagarde's presidency had ushered in an "esprit de corps" at the ECB, with policymakers focused on the interests of the whole European economy.

Industry observers note that that would, in theory, reduce the importance of nationality among leadership and of traditional notions of hawkish northern nations and dovish southern ones. However, Orchard also noted the recurrent theme of national representation in the recent debate and the perception of Knot as a hawkish choice.

Diverging performance is a core challenge for any leader of the ECB, which must set monetary policy for 21 economies all growing at different speeds and with their own domestic priorities and inflationary pressures.

But it has become particularly pressing this year as the bloc's second-biggest economy, France, is teetering on the edge of its own potential debt crisis.

Moves in French markets and whether the ECB needs to take more extreme measures beyond continuing to urge fiscal discipline will remain in focus for de Cos.

The ECB Executive Board's view of what measures could be taken to prevent market disorder and under what conditions was a key topic under discussion when choosing who should fill the roles, according to insiders.

Despite political efforts to pass a budget that will reduce its budget deficit to around 5% of GDP, investors have been driving up French borrowing costs on concerns over its mounting public debt-to-GDP ratio, high budget deficit and contracting economy.

Risks are being compounded by political uncertainty ahead of France's presidential election in April, which is stymying business activity and raising wider questions about EU unity as far-right and far-left candidates pull ahead.

Rising French yields and the widening spread in France's borrowing costs over Germany's have led to speculation over whether the ECB might need to deploy tools such as liquidity injections or its never-used Transmission Protection Instrument to protect other countries from contagion and support the euro.

This would be politically controversial unless France agreed to stringent fiscal discipline measures akin to those that southern nations signed up to during the Sovereign Debt Crisis — which could be complicated if far-right presidential frontrunner Marine Le Pen, who has a combative relationship with EU institutions, wins.

Ken Egan, head of European sovereigns at ratings agency KBRA, stated fragmentation was always a challenge for the ECB, which does not want to see divergent financing conditions and high sovereign spreads across the bloc.

However, he remarked that in the case of France he believed we were "some way from having a serious conversation about ECB intervention.".

"If anything, sovereign spreads have pulled back a bit and tightened, and some measures of market liquidity have also improved in recent weeks," he mentioned by email.

"That doesn't mean the underlying risks have disappeared, but I think it's notable to distinguish between periods of market volatility and a more fundamental breakdown in monetary policy transmission.".

De Cos also takes up the mantle as Europe faces yet another surge in price pressures.

Industry observers note that euro area inflation rose to 4% in October, its highest level in more than three years. Energy was the biggest contributor, as the Iran war drives up oil and gas prices and tightens the supply of refined fuels.

That leaves the central bank facing a difficult policy balance, preventing higher energy costs from spreading into wages and wider prices while avoiding unnecessary damage to growth.

According to recent reports, de Cos will inherit major supranational projects, including prepares for a digital euro and Europe's long-delayed capital-markets integration. In a recent speech, Lagarde warned that each year without capital markets integration meant "more of our savings are invested abroad and more of our growing firms look elsewhere for funding.".

France is not the only euro area country heading for a pivotal election next year, with votes in Finland, Greece, Italy, Estonia, Slovakia and Slovenia also expected, adding to the politically uncertain climate.

It is also not the only country grappling with higher government borrowing costs, public debt and a struggle to find fiscal headroom, a growing problem among many developed countries.

Recent findings suggest that the ECB's Financial Stability Review meanwhile warned this month of mounting risks from European investors' growing exposure to the U.S. economy, and equity and credit markets' vulnerability to AI.

Outgoing head Lagarde, meanwhile, told the European Banking Congress in Frankfurt this month that her biggest concern was now cyber threats, an area she said policymakers had not paid enough attention.

Industry observers note that "The one [worry] that keeps me awake, and many of you awake ​as well ... ​is ⁠AI applied together with cyber threat," she said.
