Welcome back to World Brief, where we’re looking at delayed U.S. Patriot air defense missile shipments to Ukraine, Israeli forces killing a top Hamas official, and yet another purge within China’s leadership.

As Russia batters Ukraine’s cities with missile strikes, U.S. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth has delayed sending scores of Patriot air defense missiles to the embattled country, including those held by European nations, according to a Financial Times report.

In a recent development, in total, the U.S. has held up around 60 Patriot air defense missiles, including more advanced PAC-3 missiles as well as around 20 PAC-2 missiles. The missiles include those that are already held in European stockpiles but require U.S. sign-off before their transfer.

The missile transfers were originally set to occur in September, October, and November in batches of around 10 missiles each. A further delivery is set for December. Ukraine began sounding the alarm publicly in October.

Patriot air defense missiles are Ukraine’s primary defense against Russian ballistic missiles, which Russia has used in an accelerating campaign to make Ukraine’s major cities unlivable.

Recent findings suggest that since September, Russia has attacked Ukraine’s energy infrastructure more than 350 times, frequently resulting in days with no regular supply of power or water, a growing concern as winter approaches. The Pentagon is separately delaying approving a initiative to allow Ukraine to produce its own Patriot missiles domestically.

According to recent reports, the slowed deliveries follow interruptions in recent weeks to the functioning of Starshield, a version of the Starlink satellite internet service that Ukraine relies on for battlefield communication. Ukraine uses Starshield in its mid-range strike drones, as well as in systems used to defend against jet-powered Russian attack drones. Starshield is owned by Elon Musk’s SpaceX. Musk and the Trump administration have traded blame over who is responsible for the throttling of Ukraine’s Starshield access.

Even as Russia scores wins in its missile campaign against Ukraine, however, recently leaked data and Ukraine’s own strikes illustrate the high price Moscow is paying.

Russia has lost at least 490,000 soldiers, according to data derived from a leaked Russian government database and analyzed by independent Russian news outlet Meduza. Even that may be an underestimate—a further 230,000 are listed as missing in action, many of whom are likely dead but not yet officially recorded as such. That means that as many as 720,000 may have died in Ukraine. The losses place the war in Ukraine as the deadliest conflict for Russia since World War II.

Recent findings suggest that russia primarily sends volunteers to fight in Ukraine and pays large bonuses as an incentive. While Russia has so far been able to find enough volunteers to staff its army, the losses are an economic and social strain on the country. Data compiled by Janis Kluge, an expert on Russian recruitment, demonstrates a slowdown in recruiting for October as compared to last year.

It has been revealed that meanwhile, Ukraine’s long-range strike capacity continues to develop. On Tuesday, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky confirmed that Ukraine was behind a strike on a Russian cargo ship in the Gulf of Finland. Ukrainian drone also recently attacked a Russian warship docked at a port in Libya. Ukraine on Tuesday separately launched yet another mass wave of strikes on Russia, with Russia stating that it shot down 699 drones.

Industry observers note that deadly airstrikes. Israeli forces have “eliminated” Naim Abu Nahal, the head of Hamas’s internal security apparatus, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and Defense Minister Israel Katz revealed in a joint statement posted on X on Tuesday. Nahal took on the role in September, after Israeli forces seized his predecessor in a Gaza ground operation and took him to Israel. “No terrorist will succeed in evading the long arm of the State of Israel,” the statement added.

Recent findings suggest that the Israeli military also launched two airstrikes that killed six other people in Gaza on Tuesday, including a teenage boy and two police officers, who were killed at a police station, according to Palestinian health officials and first responders. Gaza’s police force slammed what it called “an ongoing campaign targeting the police apparatus aimed at sowing chaos,” the BBC reported; Israeli forces said they struck “military operatives.”.

In a recent development, corruption probe. A top Chinese Communist Party official once considered a possible successor to Chinese leader Xi Jinping is being investigated “for suspected severe violations of discipline and laws,” Xinhua reported on Tuesday, in the latest example of ongoing purges of China’s leadership.

It has been revealed that the official, Chen Jining, is the head of the Communist Party in Shanghai, China’s most populous city and a key economic center. He is also a member of China’s Politburo, an elite group of 24 officials that help lead the country. He had been seen as likely to rise within the ranks of China’s government, having previously served as mayor of Beijing.

The move is part of Xi’s long-running anti-corruption campaign that has seen hundreds of top officials, including many within the military, convicted. Since 2025, three other members of the Politburo have also faced corruption charges, and in the last two months, corruption charges were confirmed against both Beijing’s deputy mayor and the former mayor of major city Chongqing.

Xi’s consolidation of power comes ahead of a likely fourth term as president beginning next year, meaning that he will hold power until at least 2032.

Money up front. Japan will pay its full contribution to the International Criminal ​Court (ICC) ahead of schedule following an appeal by the court after the United States sanctioned it, according to Prime Minister Sanae ‌Takaichi.

Japan is one of the largest contributors to the court, accounting for around 15 percent of its funding. The early payment will give the court a larger financial cushion after U.S. sanctions take effect in April 2027.

Recent findings suggest that the Trump administration introduced a campaign against the ICC in July, starting with pressuring other nations to withdraw from it. That effort has been largely unsuccessful, with only four countries—Tajikistan, Chad, Venezuela, and Naoero—leaving the court. In October, the United States sanctioned the ICC itself, which U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio called a “rogue court.”.

The United States has long been concerned about the ICC’s ability to prosecute U.S. soldiers. However, the Trump administration’s pressure on the ICC represents the sharpest attack yet on the body.

In North America, a full moon that falls after Nov. 7 is known as a “beaver” moon, as it usually occurs around when beavers are building their dams and stocking up on food. If you happen to miss this month’s supermoon, though, don’t worry: There are three more coming in a row in December, January, and February, according to NASA. Happy moongazing!
