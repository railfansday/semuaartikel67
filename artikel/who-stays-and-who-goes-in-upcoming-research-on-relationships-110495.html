Who Stays, and Who Goes, in Upcoming Research on Relationships

It has been revealed that your neighbors have been married for years, almost to the point where you can’t imagine them as anything but together. Much to your surprise, they come by to let you know that they're done. Here you thought you were a pretty good judge of character, if not love, but clearly you’ve missed some clues.

According to recent reports, as it turns out, relationship researchers can be in the same thorny situation. The holy grail in investigations of divorce is to find the magic formula that predicts who sticks it out and who moves on from a long-term partner. A newly published study provides some hope that the grail may yet be achieved.

University of California Los Angeles’ Molly Smith and colleagues (2026) took advantage of a 12-year longitudinal data set involving 368 newlywed couples who were studied 10 times on a yearly or near-yearly basis. The purpose of the study was to contrast three perspectives on the development of married couples, including the possible outcome of divorce. The authors contrasted these three theoretical perspectives on relationship development to see which would prove strongest:.

In a recent development, over the course of the 12-year period, couples completed individual interviews as well as participated in behavioral observations during discussion of a contentious topic, chosen by the couples. The topics they discussed included finances, household chores, family obligations, and communication during conflict management.

It has been revealed that coders rated the videos of each component of the behavioral observations along the dimensions of positive and negative adaptive communication. Positivity included such observations as positive mood, warmth/support, physical affection, humor, enjoyment, endearment, and responsiveness. The negativity ratings included disruptive responses, hostility, contempt, denial, angry coercion, dominance, verbal attack, interrogation, and externalized negative behavior. A third set of ratings assessed effective communication (e.g., coming up with good solutions).

Sources indicate that in the statistical model testing these contributions to divorce, the main finding to emerge was that the vulnerabilities of wives was the strongest contributor. This included adverse childhood experiences, parental divorce, history of abuse, negative emotions, and problematic substance use. Indeed, wives’ vulnerability was the only significant predictor of time to divorce.

In interpreting this finding, one possibility the authors offer is that women are more tuned in to relationship problems so that, when something bad occurs, they will be the first to leave. Social role theory proposes, in contrast, that women are expected more than men are to take on the larger share of labor in the household as well as to maintain intimacy. Vulnerability adds to the likelihood of seeking divorce because, in the words of the authors, “vulnerable women may more readily reach a threshold of dissatisfaction that prompts a decision to exit.”.

Sources indicate that these explanations can only go so far, however. Even the best predictive model only performed at 34 percent accuracy. Sometimes couples just decide to split, which the authors suggest may not be the worst outcome in a bad relationship.

Even though the outcome of divorce was not predicted with a high degree of certainty, there were mitigating effects of good communication on relationship outcome for vulnerable wives. If a couple communicated well, this could “neutralize the risk conferred by wives’ vulnerabilities.”.

Sources indicate that looking at your own relationship, the findings suggest you might want to look to external threats to your relationship rather than communication strategies alone. Returning to the example of your neighbors, couples who seem to get along well may still face an uphill battle in cases when the vulnerability of the wife due to external factors becomes overly burdensome.

The findings can also help you gain perspective on the difficulty of peeking inside a marriage or other long-term committed relationship to know what’s going on. Even with as meticulous a method as Smith et al. used, it wasn’t possible to overcome low odds in predicting who divorced. The authors also point out that what plays out in the lives of newlyweds may be very different than what occurs over the course of time. Stressors such as financial difficulties can develop over time to erode what may have looked like a great relationship at the outset.

To sum up, foretelling long-term couple outcomes is a science fraught with difficulties. The UCLA study suggests that looking outside as well as inside the relationship may ultimately improve that science.
