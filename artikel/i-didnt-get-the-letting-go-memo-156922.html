I Didn't Get the “Letting Go” Memo

Posted November 25, 2026 | Reviewed by Monica Vilhauer Ph.D.

Recent findings suggest that I never got a specific memo about how to let go of my children. My parents, as Australians, were more open to this idea than the European parents of many of my contemporaries, most of whom had recently endured the trauma of the holocaust. But even with a different cultural background, I've felt that mothering and letting go is, on so many levels, a contradiction in terms. Mothers receive an intuitive message: You are meant to stay close to your children, to protect them, and to look after them. We assume this means always.

Recent findings suggest that there is a well-worn path of letting go, which surprisingly begins when a child is a few hours old. Lisa Baraitser1 opened my mind to this in her description of the newborn falling asleep. It describes the mother’s response, her feelings and tears as she realizes that her infant’s “first sleeps” hold a deeper meaning as he/she goes “away on their own for the first time.” It’s almost like a jolt, an awareness for the mother that her infant, a mere few hours old, drifts in sleep to a place that she isn’t part of. In the full swing of post-birth, we aren’t really tuned into receiving this message.

This realization would have helped me. It would have informed my understanding of mothering: that one’s child, even at a few hours old, is a separate being. It's a startling and humbling recognition that, as mothers, we don't own our children; we are “there to be left,” as Erna Furman2 puts it.

We get these messages continually from our children. The child crawls away from us to experience the world, then walks, then goes to preschool. But for the first 11 or so years, we are the ultimate hands-on caregivers. We feed, rock, and change our infants. When they are toddlers and young children, we kiss their skinned knees, and we are at their service all day and during the night to calm their nightmares and their sore tummies. To them, mom is the person who knows how to fix things—everything. We are their person.

This changes when our adolescent teenager surfaces. We become sparring partners, and if we are lucky, we get more than a contemptuous look or that stare. We return to nights of interrupted sleep, as we stay awake all hours waiting for them to come home. We spend a lot of time reflecting on and talking about what’s gotten hold of our child. Why is there no conversation? Why are they so abrupt and rude? Why is it all about their friends? It’s so difficult to reframe this as a process of healthy separation, a letting go, as they wear us out in a new way— with emotional exhaustion. Those of us who want to hold onto the earlier status quo are in trouble.

It has been revealed that the teenage years give us pause for thought. How do we receive the message to back off, that we need to let go? One of the wisest comments that I ever heard about this stage is that a child is like this because he/she can be, because he/she has a mom who can bear it. The child intuits this through continual testing. It’s all part of the separation. It requires us to hold onto the sides of the roller coaster driven by our child, to stay on board and go with it while insisting on some guardrails, believing that our child will return to us one day, albeit in a different form.

Then, as our children approach late teens and early 20s, there are fresh challenges and shifts as they hopefully enter the workforce, continue their education, train for a trade or technical career, or go out on his/her own. We become more like roommates on different programs as we navigate home life with another adult. Or they leave home.

The next stage may include our child partnering, and the cycle begins again with their own children. Our next lesson is that it's their turn to make their own family. Are we able to watch and learn with curiosity and gratitude?

Industry observers note that as we look back at our mothering, it’s clear that one of the most vital parts of mothering is to intuit when to let go and how: to know that each child is different, and to ask ourselves whether we've been adaptive, accepting, and understanding enough. Have we tried to hold on too long or let them go too soon? Have we used guilt, been a victim, or judged them openly and harshly? Have we encouraged our child at each stage to learn their own place in the world?

Recent findings suggest that how do we get there? Letting go of one’s children is an art form. It takes sensitivity, a lack of ego, and not putting ourselves first.

My work on ambivalence has guided my understanding of this. Mothering is full of contradictory feelings that can be difficult to bear, that often seize us at any one time, and also renew our love and give us the strength to separate.

Saying goodbye to our 4-year-old at preschool is a bittersweet love. We feel both fury that our 15-year-old arrives home at 4 am without calling and absolute love when he/she walks through the door in one piece. We feel pride and joy when we see our child commit to a partner, but also loss; they belong to someone else now. All of these feelings mean separation.
