Ice baths don’t help your muscles recover after a workout any better than heat-based methods, according to a new study

Despite the popularity of ice baths for post-workout recovery, latest research finds that cold-water immersion helps restore muscle strength no better than heat-based methods.

It has been revealed that enduring a freezing ice bath after a hard workout feels like a steep price to pay for better muscle recovery. Now, results from a new study show that cold-water immersion restored strength no faster than sitting in a chair with a hot towel around your neck.

In a recent development, for this research project, forty people wrecked one thigh muscle on purpose. They then spent 15 to 30 minutes in cold water, hot water, hot air, or an ordinary room. Three days later, their strength had come back at the same pace in every group.

The hot water did change the body in ways the ice bath didn’t. Blood markers of muscle damage settled faster after a hot bath, and a protective protein rose more.

Those changes never indicated up as extra strength or less soreness. So, for now, the finding is that heat and cold are different, not that either one is better.

According to recent reports, freya Bayne, a lecturer in sport and exercise science at the University of East London, led the study. Her co-authors came from London South Bank University, Maastricht University, and the University of Montpellier, among others.

Each person got one session of one treatment, and the follow-up stopped at 72 hours, so the results say nothing about weeks of regular ice baths.

“Cold-water immersion remains one of the most popular recovery strategies used by athletes and recreational exercisers alike,” Bayne said.

“Our findings suggest that heat-based recovery methods produce different physiological responses following strenuous exercise, particularly in relation to markers associated with cellular protection and muscle repair.”.

The volunteers were 40 adults, 31 of them men, averaging 26 years old, who already lifted weights three to five hours a week.

Each one sat at a machine that forced the dominant knee to bend while the thigh muscle fought it, 210 times in all. Contractions like that are what make muscles sore a day or two later.

It has been revealed that right after the session, the thigh could push with about 36 percent less force than before. That loss is what the recovery treatments were meant to undo.

Each person was assigned at random to one of four treatments, ten to a group. One group sat chest-deep in water at 108°F (42°C) for 30 minutes, and another sat in an ice bath at 48°F (9°C) for 15 minutes.

A third sat in a room heated to 86°F (30°C) for 30 minutes, and the last sat in a room at 64°F (18°C) in their gym clothes.

In a recent development, everyone came back at 24, 48, and 72 hours to push against the machine again. They also gave blood and rated their pain and soreness.

In a recent development, by 72 hours, the hot water, hot air, and plain room groups had all recovered about 88 to 90 percent of their starting strength. The cold water group was at about 82 percent.

Recent findings suggest that the gap between cold water and the rest looks like something, but with ten people per group the uncertainty around it is wide. The statistics could not separate any group from any other.

The authors wrote that the pattern fits other recent work suggesting cold can slow the signals that rebuild muscle, while stopping short of calling it a result.

Three things in the blood told a different story from the strength test. One was a protein called creatine kinase, which leaks out of damaged muscle fibers.

Sources indicate that it was back to normal 48 hours after the hot bath and 72 hours after hot air or a plain room. After the cold bath, it was still about 25 percent above normal at 72 hours.

Recent findings suggest that the second was heat shock protein 72, a protein that cells make under stress to protect and repair themselves. Right after the treatment, the hot water group’s level was more than three times its start. The cold water group’s was about one and a half times.

The third, an immune messenger called interleukin-6, spiked highest after the hot bath and then dropped fastest, back to near normal within a day.

In a recent development, only the hot bath warmed the muscle itself, from 98°F to 102°F (36.6°C to 38.8°C). The cold bath cooled it to 93°F (34°C).

Pain and soreness rose in every group by the next day and were still up at 72 hours. No treatment brought them back to the starting point. The hot air group reported the most pain on days one and two and the most fatigue on days two and three.

Cold water held soreness down on the first day, but it climbed on the second before easing on the third.

“It is notable to recognize that these responses did not result in significantly greater recovery of muscle strength or lower levels of soreness over the following 72 hours,” Bayne noted.

The study tested a single session of each treatment, and the authors said one session may not capture what repeated use does.

Recent findings suggest that the team didn’t track what the volunteers ate, and only asked them to state their first day’s meals. Soreness ratings were filled in online, and only 29 of the 40 people completed every one.

In a recent development, with nine women among 40 people, the team couldn’t ask whether men and women respond differently to heat and cold. And the blood marker for muscle damage is an indirect one, since no muscle tissue was sampled.

It has been revealed that bayne’s team wants larger groups, repeated exposures, and tests of timing and dose. “Further research is needed to understand whether repeated exposure to heat-based recovery strategies might produce longer-term benefits,” she remarked.

Until that work is done, heat changes the blood in ways cold does not, and the strength tests show no difference in recovery between them.

The full study was published in the journal Journal of Thermal Biology.
