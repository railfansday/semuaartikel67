Latest Update: Quorums in Times of Crisis

🌐 Source: Reason • 📅 25 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 8 min read (1545 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Last week the Fifth Circuit ruled that the House's proxy-quorum policy violated the Quorum Clause. I've yet to read any actual substantive disagreements with the merits. Rather, there have been a smattering of warnings about the potential consequences of the decision, attacks on the Fifth Circuit's track record in past cases, and predictive judgments about what the Supreme Court will do. I assure you. If you actually read the Fifth Circuit's decision and the dissent, there is not even a close call. Unless consequentialism matters more than originalism.

It has been revealed that much of the doomsaying is out of proportion to reality. In order for anyone to challenge a federal statute passed during this period, they would need to have both a reason and standing. Litigation is expensive and time-consuming. Parties do not simply file lawsuits for principle. Again, any remedy would be targeted to a particular law, so it is not possible to wipe out all legislation from this period. The courts do not have a magic eraser for the U.S. Code. Moreover, spending bills and related laws generally do not inflict injuries, so there is no obvious way to challenge many of the bills. Remember that Judge Hendrix found that Texas lacked standing to challenge the DHS Pilot Program, another proxy-passed-statute.

Perhaps the only major outlier is the Electoral Count Reform Act. The validity of this statute would only arise in the case of a contested election. There is good reason to have the validity of this law settled. But there is an easy path forward. This was a bipartisan statute that could be re-enacted to eliminate any doubts.

You might say it is unfair to make Congress re-enact a bill it already passed. I have zero sympathy for the House, which passed a landmark piece of legislation in the face of serious constitutional doubts. There was no actual health emergency that required the rule. The House could have easily followed social distancing protocols to allow members to spread out. The gallery upstairs would serve that purpose well.

The House also could have opened a Zoom link for those who are unable to attend. Had these members been able to watch and participate in the proceedings and cast their vote at the time of the roll call, the legal matter would have been much closer. In February 2021, I participated as a virtual witness for Merrick Garland's confirmation hearing. It could be done. Senators who were in the chamber posed questions that I was able to answer.

The purpose of a deliberative body is to allow members to vote after open deliberation. Designating a vote in advance of the proceeding eliminates any chance for deliberation. Even in my own world, I often walk into a faculty meeting thinking I'll vote one way on a matter, but after hearing other views, I change my position.

I suspect others can relate. The Supreme Court satisfied its statutory quorum requirement through remote oral argument. No one would have thought that Chief Justice Roberts could have sat alone in the Chamber, posing the questions that his colleagues sent in advance. Absent Justices could not submit signed proxy votes in advance of the oral argument that would be read aloud at conference. The same rules ought to apply to Congress.

In a recent development, the truth is the proxy rule was more about Speaker Pelosi's power than actual pandemic relief. As I said, I have no sympathy when Congress takes shortcuts. Courts with jurisdiction have an unflagging duty to exercise it. And all of the usual presumptions in favor of democracy do not apply when the legislative body ignores the bright-line procedural rules in the constitution. If INS v. Chadha was right--even though the heavens fell--then the quorum case is even righter.

According to recent reports, what matters to me are originalist principles. And the good judges of the Fifth Circuit followed the law where it leads. They were not cowed by potential consequences in other cases and the predictable hackles from commentators.

In a recent development, I've started to give the issue a bit more thought, and researched past instances in American history where Congress somehow managed to legislate during times of actual crisis. Here are four highlights.

Industry observers note that in August 1793, a yellow fever epidemic began to spread through Philadelphia—then the capital of the United States. At the time, Congress was in recess, and it was believed that holding meetings in the capital, including meetings of the legislature, would be unsafe. For that reason, President George Washington asked both James Madison and Thomas Jefferson whether he could summon—in effect, order—Congress to meet in a different location. They both advised that the President lacked the power to change the location of the meeting, but they proposed alternatives. Madison advised that Washington could suggest that the members meet him in nearby Lancaster or Reading, Pennsylvania. Jefferson insisted that Congress "must meet in Philadelphia, "even if it be in the open fields, to adjourn themselves to some other place." I am not aware of any evidence that either Jefferson or Madison or any of their peers ever considered the possibility that the Constitution's quorum rule could be relaxed in order to allow members to establish their presence or otherwise participate by proxy.

On August 8, 1814, about two weeks before the British would invade Washington, the national capital, and then burn the Capitol, President James Madison called on Congress to convene on September 19. Congress, unwilling to be defeated, found temporary accommodations in the Patent and Post Office Building (now the site of the National Portrait Gallery). Despite the risks of public safety, nearly 90% of the representatives were present. Again, there were no efforts to relax the Constitution's quorum rule, and there were no attempts to facilitate proxies as a means of carrying on legislative business.

Sources indicate that during the American Civil War, debates about the quorum proved particularly vexing by 1864. Should Representatives and Senators from States that took action to join the (purported) Confederate States of America be counted for purposes of a quorum? Ultimately, Senators from the Union States adopted a rule such that quorum was defined as "a majority of the Senators duly chosen." Because the southern state legislatures had not chosen fresh Senators for the United States Congress, vacancies for authorized senate seats were not filled, and so no one was "duly chosen." Thus, such authorized seats did not count for purposes of the quorum. I do not believe that this rule was an innovation, much less a relaxation of the quorum rule. Rather, the Senate's rule merely clarified the constitutional text and was entirely consistent with prior practice going back to 1790, when the Second Session of the Senate of the First Congress met. Moreover, the Civil War era congresses did not introduce any form of proxy voting.

According to recent reports, in 1918, the Spanish influenza raged through the United States. It is understood that more than one-half million people in the United States would die from this epidemic. The Senate had passed a resolution to authorize the President to appoint certain medical professionals to the Public Health Service. The House, however, lacked a quorum to act, and the Speaker adjourned that day's meeting. The following day, the members worked out a one-time "gentleman's agreement" where no one would object to the lack of a quorum; absent a timely objection by a member in attendance the chair would presume the presence of a quorum. The bill passed under these somewhat unusual parliamentary circumstances, which the House would not repeat during the remainder of the epidemic. There was no attempt to allow absent members to designate a proxy to vote for them. Moreover, the constitutionality of this practice was never tested in court. I agree with Judge Hendrix's take on the Spanish Influenza incident:.

Industry observers note that this occurrence does not support an argument that the Quorum Clause does not require physical presence. Quite the opposite, the House was aware it lacked a quorum at the time, with members urging those who considered raising points of no quorum to withdraw their points because they knew a quorum call would reveal the obvious—the House lacked the physical presence of a quorum and therefore could not proceed to business. And the members sought to pass the bill by unanimous consent because a roll-call vote would reveal the absence of a quorum. The House's decision to proceed in knowing violation of a constitutional requirement does not alter the proper interpretation of that Clause—"[p]ast practice does not, by itself, create power." See Medellín v. Texas, 552 U.S. 491, 532 (2008) (alteration in original) (quoting Dames & Moore v. Regan, 453 U.S. 654, 686 (1981)). Instead, it may merely represent action beyond constitutional authority. But given the House's own acknowledgement at the time and the history previously discussed, the historical practice of the House—even when acting without a quorum—demonstrates an understanding that members must be physically present in order to count towards the quorum.
