German Bundestag Lets Customs Freeze Crypto Over €100,000 Without a Criminal Case as the Bill Goes to the Bundesrat

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The German Bundestag on Thursday approved a government bill that would let customs freeze cryptocurrency and other assets worth more than €100,000 when their lawful origin appears implausible, without first opening a criminal case. Permanent confiscation would still take a finance-court judgment. The measure still needs the Bundesrat’s consent before a planned Jan. 1, 2027 start, and the timing of that upper-house vote remains unclear.

The Bundestag passed the Customs Financial Justice Act, a Finance Ministry overhaul to strengthen the customs administration and fight financial crime, on Nov. 12. The federal government introduced the bill on Sept. 30. The Finance Committee recommended passage on Nov. 11, with the Christian Democrats, Social Democrats and Alternative for Germany in favor, The Left opposed and the Greens abstaining.

Sources indicate that crypto is covered expressly, using the European Union’s Markets in Crypto-Assets definition, but it is not singled out. The same administrative procedure applies to cash, cars, real estate and other property. It reaches only “significant” assets: generally those worth more than €100,000, or more than €50,000 if the asset must be entered in a public register, such as vehicles or land. Because crypto is not registered that way, the €100,000 threshold applies. Customs may estimate the value, and closely related items can be treated as one asset.

Customs offices — not Germany’s Financial Intelligence Unit, which the same bill strengthens separately — may freeze those assets for up to 30 days without a criminal case if there are factual indications the origin is not lawful, meaning lawful acquisition “does not appear plausible.” A finance court may extend that hold once by 150 days. That extension is decided by a single judge, without a hearing, in an order that cannot be appealed. Filing a confiscation application stops the clock until the court rules, so the often-cited 180-day period is not a hard cap.

In a recent development, permanent confiscation still requires a finance-court panel to be convinced the origin is unlawful. The committee shifted that decision away from a single judge. Owners must be told they are not required to produce records, and anything they do provide may be used in tax and criminal cases. The official justification says mere silence, or a denial of knowledge, will as a rule not be enough to prevent confiscation.

Finance Minister Lars Klingbeil said in August that if suspects cannot show assets were legally acquired, “the Porsche or the Rolex are gone for now.” In Thursday’s debate, Social Democrat Ingo Vogel told the Bundestag that criminally acquired assets do not enjoy constitutional property protection, and that the bill is “no automatic confiscation” and “no blanket reversal of the burden of proof.”.

The Bundesrat, in an earlier comment, had asked for a true burden-of-proof reversal and for the court extension to run up to 12 months, citing, among other things, “crypto concealment structures.” The government refused the fuller reversal and did not adopt the longer hold in the bill the Bundestag approved.

Germany’s Federal Bar Association opposes the bill, warning that vague terms and missing paperwork, including for inherited assets, should not be treated as proof of illegality. The German Bar Association also rejected the government draft. The Blockchain Bundesverband, commenting on the ministry draft, said the law needed to distinguish custodial, self-custodied and decentralized arrangements, because with a self-custodied wallet control “regularly lies with the holder of the private key,” and state access then exists only through measures against the person, not through third parties. The bill itself is silent on how, or whether, customs could take self-custodied keys. A freeze of crypto would be carried out by attachment under the Tax Code.

The government expects about €308 million a year in extra revenue from 2028 from the procedure as a whole, not from crypto alone. The Greens argued that figure is a sliver of Germany’s estimated €100 billion annual money-laundering volume.

In a recent development, the newly introduced power is separate from an earlier criminal case. In 2024, Saxony’s prosecutors, with the Federal Criminal Police Office, sold about 49,858 bitcoin from the Movie2k case between June 19 and July 12, for €2.64 billion. That sale was an emergency liquidation in a criminal proceeding, not an exercise of the administrative tool the Bundestag has now approved.
