Physicists defy expectations by controlling the magnetic heart of a single atom

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that researchers at Delft University of Technology (The Netherlands) have accomplished something that physicists previously thought should not be possible. Using radio waves, they managed to directly control the magnetic spin of the nucleus inside a single atom. The unexpected result challenges existing assumptions about how atomic magnetism behaves and could eventually lead to new ways of storing and retrieving quantum information.

The findings, published in Nature Communications, also present scientists with a latest puzzle. Although the experiment demonstrated that this remarkable level of control is possible, researchers do not yet fully understand the mechanism that makes it work.

The Hidden Magnetism Inside Every Atom.

Atoms in magnetic metals such as iron and nickel behave like tiny magnets. However, the magnetism within an atom comes from different sources. Both electrons and, in some cases, the atomic nucleus possess a quantum property called spin, which can be thought of as a miniature magnetic compass needle.

Sources indicate that "By far the strongest magnetism comes from the electrons," states lead researcher Sander Otte. "But in some atoms, the nucleus also carries a spin. That spin is much weaker, but it is also much better shielded from the outside world.".

It has been revealed that that protection makes nuclear spin especially interesting to quantum researchers. Because it is less exposed to disturbances from its surroundings, it could offer a promising way to preserve delicate quantum information.

According to recent reports, to investigate these tiny magnetic properties, Otte's team uses a specially modified scanning tunneling microscope. This powerful instrument allows the researchers to deliver electrical signals to individual atoms with extraordinary precision.

Industry observers note that until recently, their experiments concentrated primarily on electron spin because it is easier to detect and measure. The nucleus's magnetic spin could only be observed indirectly through a phenomenon known as the hyperfine interaction, which creates a subtle quantum connection between the spins of the electron and nucleus. This connection can cause the two spins to become slightly entangled, meaning their quantum states are linked.

An Atomic Experiment Produces an Unexpected Result.

Recent findings suggest that in the latest study, lead author Hester Vennema and her colleagues took a different approach. They simultaneously applied two separate signals to a single atom, with one directed at the electron and the other at the nucleus.

In a recent development, by using these signals together, the researchers could investigate their effects independently. What happened next caught them by surprise.

Even when the electron and nuclear spins were largely disentangled, the team was still able to flip the orientation of the nucleus's magnetic spin. Based on their previous understanding, the nucleus should not have responded in this way.

In a recent development, "According to our simple understanding, this should not really happen," indicates Otte. "The nuclear spin should not be sensitive at all to the electrical signals from a scanning tunneling microscope.".

The result suggests that the interaction between electrical signals and nuclear spin involves a mechanism that scientists have yet to fully explain.

Controlling Quantum Spins Like Guitar Strings.

Recent findings suggest that achieving this level of precision required the researchers to adapt a technique called double magnetic resonance so it could operate on a single atom. The method allowed them to manipulate the magnetic behavior of the atomic nucleus separately from that of an electron.

It has been revealed that magnetic resonance works by applying a wave signal at a carefully selected frequency to change the orientation of a magnetic spin. However, quantum mechanics restricts these changes to specific allowed transitions, meaning the frequency must be precisely matched to the spin being targeted.

Vennema compares the process to playing a musical instrument.

In a recent development, "You can compare it to the strings of a guitar," Vennema explains. "Each possible spin rotation is like a string with its own note. You have to play precisely the right note to make a particular string vibrate. The "notes" of a nuclear spin are a hundred to a thousand times lower than those of an electron spin.".

According to recent reports, in other words, electrons and atomic nuclei respond to very different frequencies, much as different guitar strings produce different musical notes. By tuning their signals to the appropriate frequencies, the scientists could address the two types of spin separately.

A Quantum Breakthrough With a Mystery Still to Solve.

The discovery could have key implications for the future of quantum information technology. Unlike conventional computers, which store information as bits representing either 0 or 1, quantum systems can use quantum states to encode information in more complex ways.

It has been revealed that one major challenge is keeping those states stable long enough for information to be stored and retrieved reliably. Nuclear spins are attractive candidates for quantum memory because they are relatively well isolated from environmental interference.

The ability to control these spins directly could therefore open upcoming possibilities for writing, storing, and reading quantum information. However, the findings represent an early experimental advance rather than a ready-to-use quantum memory technology.

For now, one of the most key outcomes is the new scientific mystery the experiment has revealed. The researchers have demonstrated an unexpected way to manipulate nuclear spin, but the physical mechanism responsible remains unclear.

Industry observers note that "Back to the drawing board, then," claims Otte. "That is really how science works. There is always something latest left to figure out.".
