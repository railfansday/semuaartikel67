Sources indicate that roughly 11 percent of American adults are on a GLP-1 for weight loss, according to a 2026 Gallup survey; so chances are, you likely know someone who is using one for diabetes management, obesity treatment, or heart health—but the benefits might not stop there. Now, semaglutide, the popular GLP-1 medication found in Ozempic and Wegovy, is drawing serious attention from longevity experts.

A recent study published in Nature found that semaglutide administered to female mice late in life significantly extended their life span. The results pose a legitimate question: Could GLP-1 drugs lengthen human lives as well?

To understand whether GLP-1s have anti-aging benefits, researchers put mice that were 20 months old—or roughly 60 human years—on a semaglutide regimen for the rest of their lives. There was also a control group of female mice the same age that received saline injections instead of the semaglutide. The team targeted older, non-obese, non-diabetic female mice to minimize the effects of male aggression and injury, consistent with previous long-term aging research, according to study author Danica Chen, PhD, professor of metabolic biology and nutrition at the University of California, Berkeley.The results? The median life span of the mice that received the GLP-1 drug increased from 742 days to 834 days, roughly a 12 percent extension. The researchers also saw reduced inflammation and preserved cognitive and muscle function in the GLP-1 group.

In a recent development, but there’s a rub here: This study was done in mice, which are a far cry from human adults, meaning we don’t yet know the effects of GLP-1s on people—at least not yet. However, there has been research that suggests lower-calorie diets lead to longer life spans in humans. “The general cause of aging is accumulation of cellular damage,” Chen says. She explains that calorie restriction can reduce cell damage and improve the function of tissues (a group of similar cells working together to perform a specific task), both of which contribute to a longer life span.

Sources indicate that michael Corley, PhD, associate professor of medicine at the University of California San Diego, thinks the Nature study is “important and exciting” because it looks beyond the established metabolic benefits of GLP-1 drugs and tests whether they can influence the biological aging process. (Biological age denotes how well the body is functioning on cellular and molecular levels, while chronological age refers to the number of years a person has been alive.).

Industry observers note that “The finding that semaglutide extended life span by approximately 12 percent in older female mice, while improving several measures of physical and cognitive function, adds notable evidence to the emerging field of GLP-1 therapies as potential gerotherapeutics,” or treatments that target the root of aging, Corley says. In fact, Corley was involved in a separate study, published earlier this year in Nature Communications, that found that 32 weeks of semaglutide treatment improved multiple epigenetic aging clocks, including measures of biological age and the pace of aging.

Findings presented at the 2026 Aging Research and Drug Discovery Meeting have also strengthened this emerging picture, Corley explains. Novo Nordisk, a Danish pharmaceutical manufacturer with locations around the world, reported that semaglutide improved measures of biological aging across multiple organ systems, including the heart and kidneys.

Likewise, Eli Lilly, an American pharmaceutical company headquartered in Indiana, presented findings that tirzepatide—another GLP-1 medication, which is found in prescriptions like Mounjaro and Zepbound—positively influenced several epigenetic aging clocks, including organ-specific markers that measure the biological age of the heart, kidney, liver, and immune system.

“Together, these independent observations suggest that GLP-1-based therapies may affect biological aging through multiple molecular pathways,” Corley states. “The new mouse study is particularly interesting because it extends these molecular observations to actual life span, while also demonstrating improvements in several hallmarks of aging.”.

Industry observers note that so, could GLP-1 drugs actually extend the life span of humans? Corley thinks the more compelling opportunity isn’t necessarily extending life span, but extending health span—or the number of years people live in good health, free from chronic disease and disability.

It has been revealed that “Human life span has biological constraints, and it is unlikely that GLP-1 drugs alone will dramatically extend the maximum limits of human longevity,” he states. “However, by reducing the burden of cardiovascular disease, metabolic dysfunction, and other age-related conditions, these medications could help more people remain healthy and independent for longer, potentially increasing average life span without fundamentally changing the upper limits of human longevity.”.

However, improvements in biological aging don’t necessarily translate into longer lives, Corley cautions.

According to recent reports, “The critical next step is determining whether these molecular changes predict meaningful improvements in physical function, cognition, and resilience,” Corley says. “I think the greatest potential of GLP-1 therapies is not to push humans beyond the limits of longevity, but to help more people reach older ages while maintaining their health, function, and independence.”.

It has been revealed that to find out whether or not GLP-1 drugs can eventually be used as a legitimate longevity tactic in humans, Chen notes clinical trials in humans are an notable next step. For now, GLP-1s remain a titan of diabetes and weight management—but their role in human longevity remains to be seen.
