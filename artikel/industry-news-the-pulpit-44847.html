It has been revealed that it’s a good day for my soul. I score some candy, some great trail food, and get to offer some intergenerational guidance.

I accept Venmo, Cashapp, Zelle, Visa Gift Cards, and Guava candy read my last text to Pebbles. Greasetrap was adamant that he come down to my van in the morning to collect the seating pad. I was glad I left it out for him when I heard some rustling outside my van 6:30AM as I was still a ways away from mobile. I could hear the windshield wiper move, and then what sounded like acorns being deposited nearby. I knew it must be my payment – guava candies!

Recent findings suggest that the spot wasn’t the quietest so I stayed in bed a little longer before propelling myself into the usual gamut of chores. I made my way out of the van and onto the trail around 8:00AM with my sights set on returning to The Pinnacle with an option to return to the Windsor Furnace Shelter if I had time.

The Appalachian Trail follows more high land between routes 183 and 61 for a long stretch all the way to the small town of Port Clinton. The south end the land drops quickly, it would look like a ridge from below, but there is a wide flat area, unlike the narrow ridges that the trail has followed for much of the northern Pennsylvania section. A gravel road flanks the AT a short distance away, but I am saving that shortcut for tomorrow.

According to recent reports, there weren’t any vistas along the trail, but there were some open areas along the way that provided some interest. The trail down to Port Clinton was a little challenging. It was aggressively steep and had the shape of a water slide, and it had about as much friction with a layer of leaves several inches deep. I had a few moments where it looked like I might slide all the way to the Schuykill River. Fortunately trekking poles are helpful to catch such slides.

Its hard to tell, but this is heading downhill in a hurry.

After doing my best to no recreate the scene in The Goonies where everyone slides down and ends up falling into a cave, I found that I had plunged straight into an active rail yard. The Reading Blue Mountain & Northern Railroad operates the rails here, and at least one locomotive was actively moving along the tracks. It took me a moment to figure out where to go, but soon I was making my way across the Schuykill River and into Port Clinton. The trail is only in town for a block before following the shore of the river then crossing under route 61. People seem to enjoy writing essays paragraphs long on the beams under the bridge. I didn’t have the kind of time to sit and read, but I did find one piece of artwork that I liked.

Sources indicate that north of route 61 the trail climbs onto the next ridge which it follows a few miles before dropping in near the Windsor Furnace Shelter. I was figuring out my logistics for the rest of the day. I knew the spring at the shelter was dry, so I collected water from the creek fed by the reservoir up the hill. I hadn’t had any water since breakfast, so I planned to filter a liter at the shelter, then collect 2 more and carry them up to my camping spot at the Pinnacle. With my liter of water in tow, I approached the shelter to find a group of 5 (and a husky mix) having a picnic at the shelter. They were quite friendly and claimed to have “a lot of food” – music to my ears. Before I knew it a plate with a chicken wing and a cinnamon roll had been delivered to me, and a glass of white wine soon followed. Naturally I told the group about my hike and answered their questions while their husky constantly pursued whichever person of the group actively had a piece of food in their hand. I thanked the group for the early dinner, especially the fried chicken which really hit the spot. I found my way back to the creek, filled my water pouch up with 2 liters of water and waddled up the trail.

It has been revealed that the AT winds up for about two miles before arriving at Pulpit Rock, a vista that seemed like a smaller version of The Pinnacle. The extra time (well) spent at the Windsor Furnace Shelter meant that I probably was going to camp at The Pinnacle, I wouldn’t have time to go all the way there and get back before sundown. I figured it would be worth risking a wet tent with the chance of overnight rain to have a chance at another sunrise. I was closing in on The Pinnacle when I saw a trio of day hikers ahead. They looked considerably cleaner than me, but they were the kind of day-hikers that are eager to interact with us long distance hiker beasts. They asked if I was heading to The Pinnacle, and were using google maps to navigate. I think they felt that they would get there, but they were less confident in getting back to their car. The trail was much rockier and narrower than the easy dirt road north of the vista. One of them asked me if there was another trail to The Pinnacle. I confirmed that yes, the trail we were on continues. While I thought about whether that would be an easier way back to their car, I couldn’t help but add; “in fact, if you keep walking on this trail, you’ll end up in Maine. And if you turn around and follow it back the other direction, you’ll walk all the way to Georgia.” “What?! Did you guys hear that?” She called to her two companions, who were chatted a short distance behind us.

I was quite pleased to have a few people to introduce to the Appalachian Trail. “Has anyone ever walked the whole thing?” was the first follow-up question. Yes! Thousands of people every year walk the entire trail. I replied. Many more questions followed, the group was happy to hear that the viewpoint was getting close, and very worth the walk through the rocks. I took over as navigator as we all scrambled across the rocks to get down to the best view.

Its rocky on this trail to The Pinnacle.

See You Tomorrow at The Pinnacle.

In a recent development, the three all seemed to be in their early 20s and anointed me their “trail dad” since I’d guided the group and shared my knowledge of the trail, including recommendations for their next visit. By now I knew that they would not be getting back to their car before dark. I had already confirmed that all 3 had fully charged phones so they could use the light of their flashlights to find their way back. I recommended that they take the road past the Lehigh Valley Amateur Astronomical Society (LVAAS) observatory instead of the AT, it was shorter and safer to hike at night. After that they would be on easy state park trails the rest of the way. Everyone was pleased to have met me on the trail and eager to visit again. Maybe some day they will consider thru-hiking the whole trail.

According to recent reports, it was now past sunset. I had hoped to spend more time finding a viewpoint at some angle I had missed the other day, but wasn’t successful. The rain forecast was looking rough for tomorrow, I had some regret about not joining the group on the way back and staying at the shelter. A little night hiking wouldn’t have hurt, I would miss any chance at a sunrise but I would definitely have a dry tent. I mulled all this over while eating my dinner. I considered a short night trek over to the rocky ledges before bed, but wound up falling asleep. I only made it to 8:00PM before waking up for the first time. I felt compelled to check out the night view, I was fairly certain there wouldn’t be much to see at sunrise and seeing the lights flickering off toward the horizon would make up for it a bit. I enjoyed checking out the night scene more than I thought I would, its something I’ll need to consider more often on future hikes – though that may need to wait until next year to come to realization.

Not a great picture, but I liked the night vibes looking far across Pennsylvania from The Pinnacle.

Total: 24.4 miles, 47,127 steps.
