Thanksgiving food waste can start before the turkey even goes in the oven

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Americans wasted about 166,000 metric tons of food during Thanksgiving week in 2025, roughly 39% more than during a comparison week.

When Thanksgiving food waste comes up, leftovers usually get the blame.

Sources indicate that the stuffing went unfinished, or half a pie stayed on the counter until Sunday. In some homes, though, good food is discarded before the meal is even cooked. It is thrown out so a shelf can be cleared for the turkey.

Researchers documented this habit in surveys of U.S. households around Thanksgiving 2025. More edible food was discarded by those households in the holiday week than in a later, ordinary week.

Recent findings suggest that extra waste was reported by cooks who emptied the fridge to make room. Cooks who had to guess their guests’ appetites reported more too, compared with cooks who made just enough.

The researchers therefore see the days before the meal as a chance to cut holiday waste.

Ran Li of Penn State University and Brian Roe of Ohio State University carried out the surveys. Roe is a professor of agricultural, environmental and development economics.

According to recent reports, all of the figures are based on people’s own reports about their kitchens. Nobody’s trash was actually weighed.

“There’s not a lot of great data out there about which holidays cause the most waste,” Roe remarked. “My money would be on Thanksgiving because it is so focused around the meal – it is the massive tradition.”.

Household waste surveys normally skip holiday weeks, since researchers are after a typical week. Yearly totals based on such surveys therefore miss the holidays.

According to recent reports, for this study, 879 adults were surveyed before and after Thanksgiving 2025. Every one of them handled at least half of the cooking at home.

About a week after the first survey, they reported how much food their households had not eaten. That included anything thrown out, composted or fed to pets.

Bones, peels and pits were left out of the count. Only once-edible food was included.

Later, in December, 437 of the same households repeated the exercise for a non-holiday week.

Households that reported in both weeks discarded about 1.1 pounds (500 grams) of edible food per person during Thanksgiving week.

About 0.8 pounds (358 grams) was discarded in the December week. The Thanksgiving total was therefore 39% higher.

People who skipped the holiday meal discarded about the same amount in both weeks.

The results were then scaled up to the whole country. By the authors’ estimate, Americans discarded about 367 million pounds (166,000 metric tons) of edible food during Thanksgiving week.

That amounts to roughly a pound for every person in the United States.

Based on respondents’ estimates, Americans wasted about $3 billion worth of edible food during Thanksgiving week, roughly 40% more than during the December comparison week.

“That’s a pretty healthy chunk of change, particularly as people are concerned about high food prices right now,” Roe noted.

Four out of five respondents took part in a Thanksgiving meal as a host, a guest or both.

About 0.8 pounds (356 grams) per person was discarded that week by people who neither hosted nor attended a meal.

Guests who did not host averaged 1.2 pounds (545 grams). Hosts who did not attend another meal averaged 1.1 pounds (493 grams).

Industry observers note that the highest average, 1.8 pounds (818 grams), was reported by respondents who hosted one meal and attended another. That is more than double the amount for people who skipped the holiday.

These gaps remained after age, income, household size and shopping habits were taken into account.

According to recent reports, the meal was not blamed for all of that week’s waste. About a quarter of the discarded food was tied by respondents to hosting or attending a Thanksgiving meal.

Different foods were also thrown out by hosts and guests. More meat, turkey included, was discarded by hosts than by guests. Half of the people who only attended a meal had cooked a dish at home to bring.

About 500 respondents cooked for a Thanksgiving meal, either as hosts or as guests bringing a dish. Of those cooks, 74% said they had deliberately made more food than was needed.

Sources indicate that the cooks gave three main reasons. Leftovers were the goal for the largest group, 37% of the cooks. Another 25% did not know how much their guests would eat. A further 12% said a big spread was the tradition.

Each of these groups reported more waste than cooks who tried to make just enough. Leftover planners reported about 0.4 pounds (189 grams) more per person for the week.

Cooks who were unsure reported about 0.8 pounds (363 grams) more.

A plan for the extra food was thus associated with less waste than a guess was.

In a recent development, for cooks who guess, tools such as the Guest-Imator were suggested by Li and Roe. It is a calculator from the Natural Resources Defense Council.

A guest count and the amount of leftovers wanted are entered, and the calculator returns quantities to buy.

A use for the extra food was found by many cooks. Among hosts, 37% sent guests home with leftovers. About one in six respondents with leftovers froze some.

Sources indicate that the cooks were also asked about the days before the meal. Had food been thrown away to make room for the turkey and the other holiday groceries?

About one in five cooks said yes.

Nearly a pound (438 grams) more waste per person was reported for the week by cooks who cleared space. The comparison was with meal participants who neither cleared space nor cooked too much.

That extra amount alone exceeds the 0.8 pounds reported for the whole week by a person who skipped Thanksgiving.

“They would clear out a bunch of stuff,” Roe mentioned.

Li and Roe wrote that campaigns before the holiday could help. One example they gave was recipes that use up fridge items the night before the turkey is bought.

“Think about what’s in the fridge that can be used up before the turkey’s coming in so that you can have that place for all your fixings for the holiday meal,” he said.

Respondents also reported where the food went: the trash, the compost or the sink.

During Thanksgiving week, 98% more food per person went down the sink or garbage disposal than in December.

Residents of some cities are warned around the holidays about fatbergs. These are buildups of fat, oil and grease that can damage sewer pipes.

According to recent reports, the kind of food that went down the drain was not recorded. However, 85% more meat and fish was discarded during Thanksgiving week by households that reported in both weeks.

Li and Roe suspect that holiday cooking adds fat, oil and grease to what goes down the sink.

Sources indicate that because the study is a survey, it shows which habits were associated with more waste. It cannot show that any single habit caused the waste.

According to recent reports, the respondents were drawn from an online research panel. Their answers were adjusted to match U.S. households on age, income and household size. Even so, the respondents were not a random sample.

It has been revealed that in earlier research, surveys of this kind found 32% to 52% less waste than physical measurement of household waste did. The national totals may therefore be underestimates.

According to recent reports, each household’s waste was also divided by the number of people who normally live there. The number of people at the table was not used. A host’s figure therefore looks bigger, and a guest’s figure looks smaller.

Only about half of the Thanksgiving respondents reported again in December.

For some of them, the December week ended just before Christmas. That week may not have been an ordinary one either.

The survey also did not test any solution. It remains unknown if a portion calculator or a night of use-it-up cooking reduces what is thrown out.

According to Li and Roe, household waste should be weighed over a holiday in future studies.

They also recommended that similar surveys be run for other food-centered holidays.

The full study was published in the journal Waste.
