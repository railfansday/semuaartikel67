7 Details That Make GTA 6 A Flawed Game

GTA 6 has become the highest-rated video game of all time according to Metacritic, sparking debate about whether it truly represents the closest thing to perfection in our history.

As someone who has enjoyed the series since the century's beginning, and who considers himself a huge fan of the Red Dead Redemption franchise, I have mixed feelings about this assertion, as the game's shortcomings are not hidden at any point.

Sources indicate that do these weaknesses diminish its revolutionary and utterly awe-inspiring nature? Not in the slightest, as it remains an extraordinary adventure that we will admire for decades, even with its imperfections.

Therefore, in order to discuss the game honestly and respectfully while also questioning the merits of its unparalleled achievements, here are seven details that make GTA 6 a flawed game.

Considering its draw distance, countless interactive elements, and dynamic entities that come alive in Leonida every second you walk, it's completely understandable that GTA 6 doesn't have a consistent frame rate.

Although this might say more about the platforms it was released on than the product itself, it certainly impacts the overall experience, especially in the most spectacular sequences where so many factors converge that the game seems on the verge of crashing.

As someone who grew up with such limited hardware that I had to play Hollow Knight at 15 FPS, I'd be lying if I said it was a negative aspect that bothered me too much, yet its easy and regularly noticeable nature is equally undeniable.

When the next-gen and PC versions arrive, I'm sure I'll have to upgrade the article and remove this entry, but for now, it's the first drawback you notice, and it's impossible to ignore.

Striking the right pace for an open-world game's main story is among the most difficult tasks in the entire genre, as the chances of the player getting stuck, overwhelmed, or impatient are extremely high.

For better or worse, GTA 6 exposes itself to these pitfalls by presenting a campaign whose problem isn't necessarily its length, but rather the way many sections feel artificially stretched to reach such numbers.

The pacing is inconsistent, with entire hours of gameplay where the plot barely advances, only to have the entire storyline suddenly shift in specific moments, feeling like a vehicle accelerating and braking so hard it makes your back ache.

While Red Dead Redemption 2's story is roughly the same duration, Arthur Morgan's tale flows smoothly (let's forget about Guarma), and Jason and Lucia's, perhaps intentionally, suffer numerous ups and downs that disconnect you at times.

Given GTA 6's size and complexity, the existence of bugs was entirely predictable, and it's rather surprising that, given the hardware, it doesn't include even more technical issues that hinder the player experience.

However, their presence occasionally transcends the anecdotal, with absurdly inconsistent autosaves that cause you to lose hours of progress, permanent weather conditions that don't change, NPCs that appear and disappear, and crashes that shut down the game without warning.

These are just what I've experienced firsthand, but reading anecdotes and watching footage shared online, it's clear that the plethora of errors is much greater, ranging from merely annoying to downright game-breaking.

It's light-years away from being like Cyberpunk 2077's launch, so the comparison is grossly unfair, but the fact that it's been a launch with irregularities is obvious and immediately apparent as soon as you start the game.

I admire the calm with which Rockstar chose to introduce us to the most anticipated video game in history, offering a prologue that, beyond mere formalities, actually extends to the end of Chapter 1.

The entire campaign, both narratively and in terms of gameplay, doesn't truly unfold until after 10 hours, during which you spend more time performing mundane tasks than interacting with GTA 6's rich systems.

Until then, the activities and objectives are quite limited, with few shootouts, no interactions with the police, minimal need for customization, and moderate interactions that only experience occasional bursts of intensity.

While I find this approach daring by AAA standards and think it's commendable, I believe that Colter in Red Dead Redemption 2 also has a slow start, yet with a far superior pace and impact, showing there's room for improvement.

As a result, since GTA 6 inherits its entire exoskeleton from Rockstar's latest release, I have no choice but to compare them directly, and it becomes clear which of the two does a better job of immersing the player in their first hours of gameplay.

Since the studio hasn't lost its knack, understandable but erratic, for including dialogue in every commute, GTA 6 suffers from the same weakness that has plagued all the manufacturer's games for years.

I've lost count of how many times conversations between characters are interrupted because, since they're tied to the distance between two points, it's common to arrive rapidly at your destination only to have the exchange end prematurely.

I know they introduced the option to read the conversation history from the pause menu, but for Rockstar to offer this as their solution to such a long-standing and annoying problem seems truly poor.

It might sound nitpicky, though it breaks the immersion and makes you miss parts of the plot and the relationships between characters, so it's not appropriate to underestimate the concern's magnitude.

Speaking of perpetuating past transgressions, it's surprising how the morality system is an idea replicated ad nauseam in the video game industry, yet virtually no one can implement it successfully.

In this case, you again have a spectrum that oscillates between being a saint or a devil based on specific actions that feel terribly gamified rather than immersive, as they're more of a checklist of behaviors and actions than a genuine human scale.

That petting a cat has a positive impact, just as robbing a drug caravan by only shooting dozens of thugs in the knees is an interesting way to approach the ethical behavior of our protagonists, who will still canonically steal and murder no matter how hard you try to be good.

It's an arbitrary and artificial metric, and while it's partially redeemed by being a measure of criminal professionalism rather than civic morality, it's difficult to reconcile such a realistic and captivating world with a system of emojis that determine your worth as a criminal.

Even just having the system work behind the scenes in accordance with our natural inclinations, like Dishonored, without forcing any kind of action but allowing us to discover the consequences of our moves organically, would be much better, so it feels like a missed opportunity.

Video games find in technology the key to their existence, as it's the mechanism that allows interaction and, therefore, gives meaning to these magical digital worlds in which we can completely blend ourselves.

Consequently, the fact that GTA 6 is the most impressive technical feat we've seen in the industry is enough to consider it revolutionary, yet it deeply troubles me that this capability is used to enhance realism rather than gameplay.

When I'm allowed to perform an action in a title that only tells me how incredibly well-programmed it is, instead of said action serving a purpose within the thematic and gameplay loop its title proposes, it's just empty boasting.

Exercising, grocery shopping, refueling, or eating food are impressive in how they're animated and integrated, though none of them truly contribute to the overall gameplay experience that GTA aims to offer; they could disappear, and it would be completely unnoticeable.

Industry observers note that certainly, there's no such thing as necessary or unnecessary in art, so realism for its own sake also has its place. Regardless, I can't help but think that it slows everything down, and as the hours pass, it distracts you, even if only for a few seconds, from the truly captivating things hidden within the state of Leonida.
