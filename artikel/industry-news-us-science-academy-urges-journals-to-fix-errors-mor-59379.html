According to recent reports, science publishers take too long to retract or correct research papers, a report by the US National Academies of Sciences, Engineering and Medicine (NASEM) finds. Standards for fixing errors are also inconsistently applied across research journals, it claims.

There are no data on how long retraction or correction presently takes, as journals do not generally disclose when they were alerted to a problem in a paper; the NASEM report states they should. On average, according to the Retraction Watch database, retractions occur around 2.4 years after a paper is published. The report recommends that editorial review processes be completed within 12 weeks for most cases and should rarely stretch beyond 6 months — at least when editors are looking at concerns over a single paper.

Recent findings suggest that “The big picture is, the process is too slow,” indicates Randy Schekman, a cell biologist at the University of California, Berkeley, who chaired the 14-person committee that produced the NASEM report. A prompter and more transparent process would strengthen the reliability of the scientific record, he says.

The report was due to be published online on 19 November, but publication was delayed after journalists at Science noted that it contained some references with fabricated first names for authors, a possible sign that large language models had been used to write or edit the material and had been insufficiently checked.

“It's unfortunate that there were these flawed references in a report about fixing the literature,” says Jennifer Byrne, a cancer researcher and research integrity sleuth at the University of Sydney who first flagged the concern to Science when reviewing the report, which was shared ahead of publication with media outlets.

According to recent reports, the report had two main aims: to speed up the process of retraction or correction and to lower the “tremendous emotional barriers” for authors to correct honest mistakes in the record, Schekman says. “Part of the problem is that it’s too rare for a paper to be retracted,” he adds.

Industry observers note that the report also suggests that journals shouldn’t just wait for complaints to reach them, but instead “take proactive steps” to identify errors and integrity concerns. That might include journal-wide audits, or regularly monitoring social media and online platforms where post-publication issues are raised, it says.

Existing guidelines for publishers, such as those from the Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE) in London, only urge a “timely” response to concerns without setting out expected schedules, and don’t suggest journals scan online platforms for complaints.

According to recent reports, “A major theme running through the report is that we have to distinguish between retraction or correction, and research misconduct,” notes Dorothy Bishop, a research-integrity sleuth in Oxford, UK, who was on the NASEM committee. Authors shouldn’t associate retracting a paper with the stigma of misconduct, she notes. And although journal editors sometimes wait for a university to investigate whether there is research misconduct before correcting a paper, they shouldn’t, she adds.

Calls for changing these attitudes aren’t fresh: journals have long emphasized that retractions can cover honest mistakes. When it comes to journal processes, Holden Thorpe, the editor in chief of Science, urged that issuing corrections or retractions and making a determination of misconduct be separated in an editorial in 2022, and COPE guidelines already emphasize the point. But “there are still plenty of people who see a correction or retraction as an allegation of fraud,” indicates Thorpe, who was also on the NASEM committee. “Hopefully, this report will accelerate the recognition of the distinction,” he says.

“Publishers are already taking the proactive approach the report calls for, with growing investment in specialist teams, screening technologies and checks designed to identify integrity challenges before publication,” states Caroline Sutton, chief executive at the International Association of Scientific, Technical and Medical Publishers.

Chris Graf, director of research integrity at Springer Nature, which publishes Nature, notes, “We welcome the report’s acknowledgement that correcting the scientific record promptly is notable, but also that cases can vary considerably in their complexity.” He adds: “We are continually working to make these processes more efficient whilst maintaining our rigorous approach, including by increasing the resources dedicated to this work.” (Nature’s news team is editorially independent of its publisher.).

The NASEM committee, which included research-integrity researchers, publishers, lawyers and sleuths, spent over a year consulting on how to improve the reliability of the research record, and took evidence from external specialists during four sessions.

A lot of the discussion focused on identifying why barriers to fixing the literature exist, indicates Bishop, and the committee found that problems can’t be blamed on any one stakeholder, given that authors, research institutions and others are involved in the publishing and corrections process. The report also has recommendations for research institutions and authors, including that institutions establish a public e-mail address to which concerns can be submitted and hold researchers accountable if they fail to honour journal policies such as those that require authors to share raw data with those who request it.

Renee Hoch, another committee member who is head of publication ethics at the publisher PLOS, indicates that she expects COPE to examine NASEM’s recommendations and decide whether it’s suitable to revision guidelines.

The report’s tangible recommendations for journals to follow specific timelines and to be more proactive in seeking out concerns are “smart and useful”, indicates C. K. Gunsalus, a research-ethics specialist at the University of Illinois in Urbana–Champaign who was not involved in the NASEM process.

But she cautions that some of its exhortations — such as that researchers should uphold high standards and bear continuing responsibility for work after its publication — merely reiterate oft-expressed cultural norms that are hard in practice to enforce.
