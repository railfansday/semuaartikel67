📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The S&P 500’s largest drop around a recession has averaged about 30% since 1948, but the index has usually hit bottom before the recession ended. So don’t sell because one is coming or has just been declared. Keep money you’ll need within five years out of stocks beforehand, and keep investing on schedule through the downturn.

In a recent development, selling when a recession is declared often means selling after the worst is over. The National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER), which dates U.S. recessions, declared three of the last six recessions only after the S&P 500 had hit bottom.

That 30% average is our count across the 12 recessions since 1948, from Yardeni Research’s record of S&P 500 declines and the NBER’s recession dates. In four of them, the drop stayed under 20%. The worst, 57% from October 2007 to March 2009, came with a financial crisis. Economists Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff found that severe financial crises bring deep, long slumps in stock prices, output, and employment.

The S&P 500 usually peaked before a recession began. It hit bottom before the recession ended in 11 of the 12, most often three to six months before. The exception was 2001, when stocks didn’t hit bottom until October 2002, almost a year after that recession ended. In 2009, the low came on March 9, three months before the recession ended in June. An investor who sold and waited for the recession to end would usually have bought back after stocks had already started to rise.

Once a recession starts, keep investing on schedule, and leave your 401(k) contributions and any automatic investments running even after it’s declared. The S&P 500 rose in the year after 11 of the 12 recessions ended, dividends included, by First Trust’s count.

At the bottom of a 30% drop, each $500 you invest buys about 43% more shares of an S&P 500 index fund than it did at the peak.
