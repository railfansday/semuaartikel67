Book of Birkenstock: Inside Bureau Borsche's Editorial Design

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Bureau Borsche turns 250 years of Birkenstock into the Book of Birkenstock, a 130-metre timeline across 608 pages of archive and image.

In a recent development, the book reads as one long strip of history. More than 1,000 photographs, illustrations, and documents sit on a continuous timeline that runs from 1774 to 2024, and images cross the gutter from one page to the next so the story never breaks. A hundred historical events sit alongside the timeline, with notes on the social conditions of each era, and a 60-page index at the back sorts everything by model, country, and document type. What holds it together is restraint: the pages stay pale, the captions stay small, and the archive does the talking.

The layout was not placed by hand. Bureau Borsche built the book from a dedicated online database of 2,500 archived objects, each tagged with year, type, category, location, and shoe model, and pulled the material through an API. Column widths, cropping, and DPI were decided by custom code and rendered in the browser with JavaScript, so the grid flexes around the material instead of forcing the material into a fixed frame.

In a recent development, page numbers were placed automatically, and the index pages generated themselves from the same data, with references pointing back to the pages they came from. The final step exported every page as a single-page PDF. It is design systems thinking applied to a book: the rules are written once, and 608 pages follow them.

Three paper stocks divide the book into sections, and each one changes how the images sit. The archival section prints on yellow-tinted, semi-transparent paper, so the documents feel thin and handled. The middle section, photographs and advertisements, sits on glossy greyish stock. The final section moves to bright white glossy paper, the kind used for fashion magazines and art catalogs.

The cover keeps the same quiet register. It is blind-stamped on extra rough cardboard, carrying the first recorded mention of a Birkenstock shoemaker, from a church file dated 1774, with only the year printed in small black type at the centre. The spine does the loud work instead: the wordmark runs down it in heavy black capitals, with Steidl at the foot.
