Too much exercise could leave your brain stuck in overdrive

It has been revealed that exercising too intensely without enough recovery time may affect more than muscles and physical endurance. Latest research suggests that excessive training can also disrupt brain chemistry, potentially interfering with the processes that help the brain return to its normal resting state.

In a fresh study published in the journal Neuroscience, researchers in Brazil found that prolonged, exhausting exercise altered dopamine regulation in the hippocampus, a brain region key for memory and emotional regulation.

It has been revealed that the scientists observed that mice exposed to demanding exercise schedules continued to have elevated dopamine levels during periods when they should have been recovering. Dopamine plays an vital role in motivation and alertness, and the researchers believe that similar disruptions in humans could help explain why excessive training is sometimes associated with insomnia, anxiety, and persistent fatigue. If confirmed, this connection would provide further evidence that sufficient rest is essential for both physical fitness and mental well-being.

"Our findings show that excessive physical exercise affects not only skeletal muscle but also has a pronounced impact on the brain," mentioned Alisson Luiz da Rocha, the study's first author.

Rocha carried out the research during his doctoral studies at the Ribeirão Preto School of Physical Education and Sports at the University of São Paulo (EEFERP-USP) in Brazil, supported by a scholarship from FAPESP. He explained that the findings demonstrate how exercise influences the central nervous system, with effects extending well beyond muscle development.

How Scientists Simulated Overtraining.

To investigate what happens in the brain when exercise becomes excessive, the team studied two main groups of mice exposed to different levels of physical activity.

One group completed a single strenuous treadmill session, running until they reached exhaustion. The other followed an extended training program designed to reproduce the demanding conditions associated with overtraining syndrome in elite athletes.

In a recent development, overtraining syndrome occurs when the physical demands of training exceed the body's ability to recover. It can cause declining athletic performance, reduced appetite, ongoing fatigue, muscle pain, and a greater risk of injuries. Changes in immune function and metabolism are also common.

It has been revealed that for the extended training experiment, the mice exercised over eight weeks as the researchers gradually increased the workload. By the final week, they were completing two sessions each day, with only four hours of recovery between workouts.

According to recent reports, after the exercise programs, the scientists examined the animals' hippocampi. They measured dopamine and serotonin, along with proteins involved in producing and managing these chemical messengers. These included tyrosine hydroxylase (TyrH), an enzyme needed for dopamine production, and vesicular monoamine transporter 2 (VMAT2), which helps package neurotransmitters for release.

Dopamine Remained Elevated for 48 Hours.

In mice that completed just one exhausting treadmill session, dopamine levels increased shortly after exercise. This showed that even a single bout of intense activity could produce a measurable chemical response in the brain.

The more striking results came from the animals that underwent weeks of demanding training. In this group, both dopamine and TyrH remained elevated for 48 hours after the performance test, even though the mice were in their recovery period.

Recent findings suggest that this prolonged response suggests that repeated strenuous exercise without sufficient rest may interfere with the brain's normal regulation of dopamine. Rather than promptly returning to its usual chemical state, the brain continued to show signs of altered neurotransmitter activity long after the exercise had ended.

Adelino Sanchez Ramos da Silva, a professor at EEFERP-USP who coordinated the study, explained that finding comparable changes in humans could help researchers better understand the possible connections between overtraining, brain chemistry, and sleep disturbances.

It has been revealed that however, the researchers caution that the study did not directly measure sleep, anxiety, or alertness. Those possible consequences remain to be investigated, and additional research is needed to determine whether the same dopamine changes occur in people.

According to recent reports, dopamine is often associated with pleasure and motivation, but it also helps regulate wakefulness and other essential brain functions. Its presence is not harmful on its own. What matters is whether its activity remains appropriately balanced.

According to Rocha, difficulties could arise if dopamine remains unusually elevated when the brain should be preparing for sleep. Such changes could also disrupt the balance between dopamine and other neurotransmitters, potentially affecting how the nervous system regulates activity and rest.

Another notable finding involved serotonin, a neurotransmitter that has long been investigated for its possible role in fatigue. Despite the substantial changes in dopamine, the researchers detected no significant differences in serotonin levels between the animals.

Industry observers note that this result challenges the long-standing idea that serotonin alone is primarily responsible for excessive fatigue. Instead, the findings suggest that changes in dopamine regulation may also deserve attention when scientists investigate the biological effects of overtraining.

Why Recovery Is Essential for the Brain and Body.

Although the connection between these chemical changes and symptoms such as insomnia or anxiety has not yet been established in humans, the findings reinforce the importance of allowing sufficient time for recovery.

Industry observers note that excessive training without adequate rest can undermine the benefits of exercise, contributing to reduced performance, persistent muscle pain, and problems with immune function. The fresh results suggest that changes in brain chemistry may be another vital part of this process.

According to recent reports, for recreational exercisers and professional athletes alike, the researchers emphasize that training should be balanced with appropriate recovery and expert guidance. Increasing workout intensity does not necessarily produce better results if the body is not given enough time to adapt.

It has been revealed that rocha also conducted research during a postdoctoral fellowship at the School of Applied Sciences (FCA) of the State University of Campinas (UNICAMP) in Limeira. This work included an internship at the University of Michigan in the United States, where he currently works as a researcher.

It has been revealed that additional funding came from a Thematic Project led by Silva, a postdoctoral fellowship awarded to Ana Paula Pinto, and a direct doctorate scholarship supporting João Pedro Floriano. Both Pinto and Floriano are affiliated with EEFERP-USP.

The team is now participating in a latest international research project aimed at identifying which biological systems first begin to show signs of strain when exercise exceeds the level at which it normally produces beneficial adaptations. Understanding these early warning signals could help scientists determine when productive training begins to become harmful.
