Volvo's big batteries: We drive its updated 2028 plug-in hybrids

In a recent development, COPENHAGEN, Denmark—If we lived in the world that Volvo’s product planners expected back when the current XC90 debuted in 2015, both it and the smaller but related XC60 (which arrived in 2017) might have been the last of their kind as everything went electric. As we know, the future hasn’t turned out quite like that, especially in the critically notable US market. At the same time, Europe and China are electrifying, which seems like an argument against spending resources on developing all-upcoming replacements for models that might not have an eight-year lifespan in most markets.

Sources indicate that so instead, Volvo has given the XC60 and XC90 another lifecycle refresh. The exterior styling tweaks are minimal; the bigger three-row XC90 had already been given a latest look last year, and the XC60 now gains a similar grille and the same matrix headlights, for instance. And that’s no bad thing—both were, and remain, handsome shapes that are popular with Volvo’s customer base.

In a recent development, instead, the Swedish automaker’s attention has been on bringing the technology up to date for model year 2028 standards. Which means a much bigger battery for the PHEVs, with sufficient capacity for a couple of days of daily driving for most of us before needing to be recharged. There’s also new, modern infotainment and interiors that are now even nicer to spend time in than before.

We first saw the revamped 2028 XC60 (and learned about its technical details and those of the XC90) in September, but with the cars going into production now and due to reach the first US customers in the first quarter of next year, we got the opportunity to spend some seat time with the updated cars.

Sources indicate that both the midsize XC60 and three-row XC90 use the same PHEV powertrain. Under the hood, there’s a 2.0 L four-cylinder direct-injection gasoline engine that generates 247 hp (184 kW) and 226 lb-ft (306 Nm) together with a 70 hp (50 kW), 122 ft-lb (165 Nm) electric motor, both of which drive the front wheels. At the rear axle there’s a second electric motor, with an output of 174 hp (130 kW) and 228 lb-ft (309 Nm). Combined output for the hybrid system is 455 hp (339 kW) and 560 lb-ft (759 Nm).

Recent findings suggest that there’s also a much bigger battery, with a net capacity of 37.9 kWh (41.2 kWh gross). If you set the car’s mode to Pure, which relies solely on the rear electric motor for propulsion, that translates to an EPA range estimate of 78 miles (126 km) for the XC60 and 73 miles (116 km) for the larger, draggier XC90. Which is significant—when the average driver drives fewer than 30 miles a day, it means these PHEVs carry enough electrons for a couple of days of tailpipe-emissions-free driving before it needs to be plugged in again or the engine to fire up.

In a recent development, when it is time to plug it back in, expect it to take about four hours with a level 2 charger of at least 16 A, or 27 hours if you’re limited to 120 V. In Hybrid mode, where you get the benefit of both electric motors and the gas engine, Volvo expects a combined efficiency of 67 mpge in the XC60, and 61 mpge for the XC90.

Even if the gas engine is running and you’re in Hybrid or Power mode (which keeps the engine on all the time), you can drive these Volvos as if they were EVs with one-pedal driving: just move the transmission from D to B. The Volvos are always using their electric motors to slow the car before the friction brakes intervene, and in B you get the same amount of lift-off regen regardless of mode. For city traffic, it’s very effective. (In Pure, although only the rear motor deploys energy, both front and rear motors regen brake.).

Industry observers note that in power mode, with all 455 hp unleashed, you can chirp the wheels on a wet day, but these model year 2028 cars are about one large male human heavier than the outgoing model year, both of which were already pretty heavy. So don’t expect performance-car performance. The XC60 is the sweeter to drive on a winding road, but both are entirely at home cruising on a highway or creeping along in a city, which is where cars like these spend the vast majority of their time. There’s more road intrusion into the cabin of the XC60—the larger Volvo rides on air suspension that does a much better job of filtering that out.

A first-drive event like this isn’t the best place to get a good read on real-world efficiency. We usually have to share cars with other journalists, and there’s limited time to drive on roads that may not be representative—too sinuous, or not enough traffic, for example. We need to try all the different drive modes and see how cars respond to different inputs. And it’s rare you’ll spend the entire day with one car, finishing at the same point you started.

Industry observers note that that said, I can’t give you a definite read on range and efficiency for these two plug-ins. But my unscientific estimate is that my drive partner and I, in an XC60, drove about 60 km in a mix of city driving and higher speeds on the way to Malmo in Sweden, which is on the other side of a gigantic bridge, and we used about half the battery pack to do so. (Volvo asked us to put the PHEVs in Hold or Charge mode at 50 percent to leave some state of charge for whoever drove the cars after lunch.).

Sources indicate that thus, there was little charge left in the XC90 when it was my turn to drive the final stint back into Copenhagen. Since I wasn’t worried about combined efficiency, I ran the car in Charge mode for about 20 min, regenerating the battery from an indicated 10 km to an indicated 21 km, then switched to Pure once we reached the city, with about 16 km to go.

Volvo already had a reputation for great automotive interiors, and with this refresh, it has pushed things forward a little. There’s a upcoming wood finish, new textiles, and new backlighting. The resemblance between the two interiors from the front seat is now quite strong, particularly since the test cars Volvo brought to Denmark wore identical color schemes inside.

Sources indicate that our final 1.6 km to the hotel took more than 30 minutes to negotiate thanks to a reception for very important people that apparently took place next to the hotel. Most of this jam was on one street, with our destination in sight, but the frustration was in part ameliorated by Volvo’s secret weapon: its seat massage function.

Lots of other OEMs offer massaging front seats, which inflate and deflate the internal air bladder to massage your shoulders, back, and sometimes butt. But none massage you with quite the firmness of a Volvo seat. And they have the power to distract you from terrible in-car situations, as I discovered when stuck in a V90 Cross Country with Nigel the Cat, whose way of remonstrating with me about his trip to the feline ophthalmologist was to scream at high volume until he ran out of breath, at which point he’d pant for a few minutes, then start again. The seat in that car is the only reason we both made it home safely. And so, in Copenhagen, I set the seat to pulse and did some box breathing.

As well as new interior materials, you’ll also notice a new infotainment screen in both cars. Gone are the old 9-inch displays, replaced by a new 11.2-inch touchscreen that stands a little proud of the dash rather than recessed into it. Just this one upgrade alone really lifts both interiors; the old, small screen was showing its age. Most Ars readers will recoil at the news that Gemini has replaced the previous Google Assistant, but at least the voice-recognition capabilities are improved. Gemini will still struggle with some commands—I asked it to tell me my current efficiency, which it failed at, but it’s rather good at looking stuff up on the Internet while I drive.

Industry observers note that there are also some updated advanced driver-assistance systems, like a warning if cyclists approach your opening door, and an updated 360-degree parking view. But there’s no point-to-point assist like Mercedes is introducing, or a hands-free “level 2+” system like Super Cruise; Volvo’s experience with the EX90 may have left it a little gun-shy.

Industry observers note that with a battery big enough for daily driving and a more modern interior experience, there’s a lot to like about both the 2029 XC60 and XC90. However, we still don’t know how much they’ll cost when they go on sale in Q1 next year. With twice the battery capacity, it’s reasonable to expect a slight price rise, but without knowing how much, it’s hard to say whether it’s a good deal or not.
