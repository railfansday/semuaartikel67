Tech Report: F1 in Las Vegas: You've never seen an F1 race like this before

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Las Vegas welcomed Formula 1 to its streets this past weekend. And for the first time since the event’s inception in 2023, the Grand Prix delivered on a long-promised spectacle. The racing was closer to an IndyCar oval race than anything F1 fans will have seen since slipstreaming was the order of the day at places like Monza and Hockenheim, back when humans were still landing on the Moon and downforce had yet to fundamentally change racing.

Recent findings suggest that the Vegas street circuit—the one that looks like an upside-down pig—is an extreme outlier on the calendar. It has plenty of long straights, so this year’s cars still suffer from energy starvation throughout the lap, as the inadequate size of their lithium-ion hybrid batteries means they become depleted very rapidly, leaving the cars on just over half power with their V6 engines operating without the benefit of the hybrid’s electric motor.

But the 17 turns are mostly all low speed, so there’s no risk of seeing the cars driving demoralizingly slowly through corners that are supposed to test one’s mettle.

It has been revealed that and because it takes place at night, in the desert, in November, it’s cold—much, much colder than anywhere else the sport will visit all year. This coming weekend in the Middle East, track temperatures may well exceed 122° F (50° C ); on Saturday night in Nevada the track was a mere 59° F (15° C). Combined with the concrete streets, that spells low grip, but there is an added benefit—virtually no tire wear or overheating.

In a recent development, and finally, there’s the slipstream. This was far more powerful than in years past, thanks to the new aerodynamic regulations. We saw this play out in qualifying, which took place on Friday night, when Mercedes’ Kimi Antonelli snatched pole position by being the last man on track and benefiting as the cars ahead cleared the air out of his way. Conversely, the Ferrari duo of Lewis Hamilton and Charles Leclerc had a torrid time by not working together—a preview for the following night, as we’ll see….

Antonelli’s pole was quickly forgotten, though, as the latest World Champion would only start sixth due to a grid penalty for using an engine component outside his allowance.

Recent findings suggest that because of the penalty, Red Bull’s Max Verstappen was on pole, with Mercedes’ George Russell next to him. To say there is little love lost between these two drivers would be to undersell the depths of animosity in that relationship. This was evident in FP3, when the pair clashed on track in what looked like a repeat of the Verstappen/Liam Lawson incident in Mexico. This time, no penalties were handed out.

“He does this week in, week out with many drivers, and everybody accepts it, and I’m not just going to accept somebody to screw me over like this and walk around like they’re bigger and better than everyone else. Somebody’s got to stand up to that guy,” Russell stated on Friday. That set up the prospect of a lap 1, turn 1 coming together, but instead the start of the race was remarkably clean. But not boring.

Sources indicate that within a lap, Russell had passed Verstappen for the lead on a straight, before the Red Bull reclaimed the position going into turn 17 on the last corner. This set the tone for things to come, with the Mercedes repeatedly getting past the Red Bull at turn 14, only for Verstappen to regain the lead through sheer commitment, taking back his lead in the corners.

Don’t be tempted to call this yo-yo racing; it was nothing like the races we saw early in the season, when everyone was still trying to understand their hybrid deployment and regeneration strategies. Like an oval race, knowing when to try to move up through the pack was vital; choosing it wrong meant losing multiple positions, not just one. But like an oval, it was also possible to work one’s way up through the pack.

Recent findings suggest that the second Red Bull of Isack Hadjar looked more competitive than at any other time this year, and, had the two teammates worked together, they might have been able to leapfrog each other to break free of the pack—a tactic we see used to good effect on oval races. Whether Verstappen’s temperament would allow him to let his teammate past even temporarily remains unknown (I have my doubts), but Red Bull never tried it, instead ordering Hadjar to hold position, leaving him vulnerable to attack from behind. His day was ruined when his front wing exploded on lap 25, dropping him from third to dead last.

So Verstappen never broke free of the chasing pack, with five or six cars covered by as little as 2.5 seconds for much of the time.

Again and again Russell took the lead briefly but lost it again to Verstappen, who worried over the radio that the Mercedes team had figured out his strategy and was going to be able to defeat it on the last lap.

“I think in the beginning we had the deployment immediately correct, but of course, people are learning and watching what you’re doing. At one point, I couldn’t get back into the lead before the start-finish line, so I just—that’s why I asked the question,” Verstappen noted after the race. “But I think we had it set up well, and it was just a crazy amount of fighting. The slipstream was so massive that you—it was just impossible to drive off, and it was just crazy how many people were just following you.”.

Sources indicate that but Mercedes fumbled. George Russell crashed into a barrier on lap 47—probably as he ran over a piece of Hadjar’s broken wing, preventing him from braking in time. And Antonelli blew his own chance when, instead of charging his battery for that last-lap, last-corner pass, he accidentally drained it entirely. The team that claimed the 2026 Constructors’ championship last time out in Mexico had to settle for another weekend off the podium.

Verstappen claimed his 75th win, one he noted was more like Mario Kart than any of the previous 74. McLaren’s Lando Norris was just 0.64 seconds behind him, with the other McLaren of Oscar Piastri a similar gap behind his teammate. The orange pair worked well together and took home a significant bounty of points in their fight with Ferrari for second place in the constructor’s championship as a result.

Ferrari’s Friday was already being called a disaster, as the two red cars couldn’t make it into the third and final qualifying session. Had they worked together to tow each other, this might have been possible. But things are far from harmonious at Ferrari between their two very closely matched drivers, with plenty of fireworks recently in Mexico City.

In a recent development, during the race, things got worse. The cars stratified themselves according to performance, which meant teammates running together often. Early on, Hamilton and Leclerc kept it mostly clean, although Hamilton earned a 10-second penalty when he went off track to avoid Norris and Leclerc, dropping him to 14th at the time. With safety cars, strategy, and that strong tow, Hamilton clawed his way back up the running order and was again in front of Leclerc by lap 34.

In a recent development, four laps later, Leclerc tried to pass Hamilton—despite being specifically told by his team five corners earlier to hold position. Leclerc misjudged it, like he did in Monza and Mexico, and the cars made contact, dropping Hamilton to 10th. The race stewards clearly apportioned blame to Leclerc, handing him a 10-second penalty. Hamilton eventually finished seventh, Leclerc 10th.

Leclerc appears desperate to show the team he’s better than the seven-time champion it hired to race alongside him, but time and again his efforts end badly, costing the team points instead. Team boss Fred Vasseur had evidently read Leclerc part one of the riot act before the media pen—the pallid, tear-stained face of the Monegasque driver was that of a man who looked like he knew he really screwed up this time. Having signed a massive multiyear contract, his position with the team is presumably safe. But were I Vasseur, one of Ferrari’s reserve drivers would be getting a call to get ready to replace him for the weekend in Qatar.

Like Leclerc and Hamilton, Russell has been writing checks about Verstappen that his racing hasn’t cashed, and in the post-race interview, Verstappen had simply epic levels of disrespect for the other driver.

“The thing is, he speaks a lot next to the track, and honestly, you know, for me, it doesn’t really bother me because he’s so far below my level that it doesn’t really make any sense for me anyway to try and challenge that, you know? So it doesn’t bother me,” Verstappen said. “And today, karma is a bitch. It was honestly fantastic when I looked in my mirror and I saw what was happening, so I was like, ‘Fantastic, my day is only getting better.’ And of course, when I then crossed the line in first, my day was complete.”.

So many unique circumstances went into making this particular Las Vegas Grand Prix as exciting as it was, and there’s little chance the next two rounds, which both take place in the Middle East, will see similar conditions. But F1 can take something away from this race to make future seasons better, and it’s to do with the tires.

It all goes back to the 2010 Canadian Grand Prix. Like this year in Vegas, Canada 2010 was chaotic. The weather was cold, the traction zones were covered in a low-grip surface to combat the tarmac disintegration seen in the years before, and the action was unpredictable as each team tried to figure out on the fly how best to adapt.

In a recent development, F1’s powers that be saw this, and when they awarded Pirelli a sole supplier contract for tires the following year, they instructed the tech giant to make race tires that would cause the same effect. Of course, it hasn’t ever worked out like that. Canada 2010 was a one-off and caught the teams off guard. But running the same Pirelli tires race after race, everyone quickly figured out the optimal strategy, leaving little room for chaos.

Sources indicate that and the Pirelli tires, while better than they once were, are still difficult to drive on. Pirelli mandates high minimum pressures out of fear of tire failures, and next week in Qatar has told teams they cannot run a set for more than 27 laps. As we frequently see in races, tires that stay within their temperature window while running in clean air can promptly overheat when following another car. In the wake of a car ahead, the following car has less downforce and slides more in the corners, which puts more heat into the tires, and the trailing car falls behind. Pirelli makes the tires that F1 asks for, but after this weekend’s race, it’s time for the sport to ask for something different.
