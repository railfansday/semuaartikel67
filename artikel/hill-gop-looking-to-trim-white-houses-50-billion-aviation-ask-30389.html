Hill GOP looking to trim White House's $50 billion aviation ask

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Senate Republicans poured cold water Tuesday on the administration’s upcoming ask for a $50.1 billion emergency air traffic control and airport infrastructure package, arguing much of it has little to do with the immediate problems that led to a nationwide ground stop on Friday.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy and Office of Management and Budget Director Russ Vought last week asked for $30.1 billion before Duffy voiced support for the rest of the funds at a Friday night press conference on the ground stop.

The original ask contained roughly $12 billion that was tied directly to air traffic control upgrades, Senate Majority Leader John Thune, R-S.D., remarked, with the remainder going toward long-sought airport terminal and other upgrades to aging aviation facilities.

Duffy and Vought laid out in a letter to House and Senate appropriators on Tuesday what the extra $20 billion would be for — half for construction of five fresh air route traffic control centers, and half for construction, replacement and improvement of up to 140 air traffic control towers.

Sources indicate that they justified the increased demand because of the “urgent need” that became evident after the ground stop. Duffy and Federal Aviation Administrator Bryan Bedford fanned out across the Capitol to try to sell the request, including a stop at Senate Republicans’ weekly policy lunch.

But it swiftly became evident that their own allies on Capitol Hill want more and better justification for why so much extra money is needed.

Not only are there concerns that much of the request would go to terminal reconstruction and other longer-term projects, but lawmakers keep noting the FAA hasn’t yet spent about $8 billion out of the initial $12.5 billion “down payment” on air traffic control modernization provided in last year’s budget reconciliation law.

According to recent reports, “I don’t think the full $50 billion will be funded,” Senate Commerce, Science and Transportation Chairman Ted Cruz, R-Texas, said Tuesday. “What we just discussed there, and what I expressed, is that the request needs to be focused on safety modernization.”.

Still, Cruz said the administration had done a “good job” with the initial $12.5 billion modernization blueprint so far and he expected to see continued bipartisan support for the effort.

According to recent reports, senate Republicans are considering adding additional aviation funds through a filibuster-proof reconciliation bill, a process that could begin as soon as this week with a floor “vote-a-rama” on a resolution to unlock that process. Cruz remarked that while reconciliation could be the vehicle used, additional aviation funds need to be “safety-focused.”.

Sen. Thom Tillis, R-N.C., said after the lunch with Duffy and Bedford that he expects Congress will look at a package more in the $10 billion to $30 billion range.

According to recent reports, for his part, Sen. Rick Scott, R-Fla., said the airline industry itself should foot at least a portion of the bill, considering how much federal support the carriers have received over the years.

Sources indicate that “I don't understand why the airline industry doesn't pay for it. We give them unbelievable amounts of money,” Scott remarked, adding that he doesn’t like adding to the deficit and is “not significant on government running things.”.

Duffy explained the need for the high price tag as the result of decades of pent-up demand that are now threatening major disruptions and safety risks to the traveling public.

“This has never been done. To be clear, we are keeping planes safe in the sky, and we are switching out equipment that’s been a problem for decades,” he stated. “I and the president have decided we are going to lean in and fix it.”.

In a recent development, he called the effort a “historic investment” reflecting the ways air travel has changed since much of the nation’s infrastructure was built — noting that the newest tower was constructed in 1969 and much of the software being used is from the 1990s. Still, Duffy said, on the current course of funding, it would take 300 years to replace every U.S. tower.

Sources indicate that “I'm not going to tell [Congress] how to do their work, but I'm telling everybody what we need,” Duffy stated. “And whether it's a party-line vote or if it's a bipartisan vote, I do think this is one moment in a very charged political environment — one moment where almost all Americans and bipartisan members of both bodies are kind of in agreement that we should lean in to do this.”.

Even as Duffy petitioned for additional funds Tuesday, he said that the “root cause” of the equipment instability that made the ground stop necessary is still unclear as investigators analyze the router that caused the problem.

A “routine switchover” from old copper wires to digital fiber in the telecommunications system “started to create concerns in our network,” Duffy explained Tuesday.

In a recent development, the telecommunications transition includes the conversion of thousands of routers to what are known as Juniper devices — a process being undertaken by contractor L3Harris Technologies. Duffy and Bedford explained that 68 other routers had been converted without incident prior to the problematic switchover Friday.

Bedford said Tuesday that use of the router that officials have identified as the source of the system instability has been suspended “and we've disabled the ones that are out there.”.

According to recent reports, the decision to suspend use of the router followed an inquiry sent to the agency by Senate Commerce Committee Democrats a day prior. “With thousands of conversions remaining, FAA must address outstanding safety and reliability concerns before expanding installation,” they wrote.

Recent findings suggest that bedford noted he was “highly confident” that similar problems wouldn’t erupt again. “Every element of this is going to be analyzed to see what we learn here and make sure that it doesn't get repeated,” he said.

David Jordan, Lia Chien and Jacob Fulton contributed to this report.
