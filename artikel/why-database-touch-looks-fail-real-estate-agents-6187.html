Why database touch looks fail real estate agents

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that agents are consistently trained to do their touches. “Have you done all your touches?” “Have you done your outreach?” “Are you touching people?” “Are you sending things of value?” That antiquated philosophy needs to stop and almost nobody in this industry cares to address it or examine why it fails. It is wildly ineffective, and I would argue that most of the time it hurts the agent’s brand rather than helping it.

If I walked up to your front door 32 times a year, rang the bell, and remarked, “Just a reminder that I’m in real estate,” how long would it take you to look into a restraining order? Nobody would call that a relationship. Yet the same schedule applies when it arrives as an email one week and a postcard the next.

It has been revealed that agents are constantly told they need to remind people that they are in real estate. That is an interaction about what the agent cares about, not what the other person cares about. Trust isn’t built by constantly telling someone how amazing I am, why they need me, or why I’m relevant or crucial. That is blatant narcissism, and yet somehow we call it great top-of-mind marketing.

The National Association of Realtors’ 2025 Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers shows the problem. Ninety-one percent of buyers mentioned they would use their agent again or recommend them. But among repeat buyers, only 18% went back to an agent they had worked with before, and among sellers the figure was 29%. The touches are supposed to close that gap.

Sources indicate that the industry explains that clients forget, so its answer has always been more reminders—the rest of the survey points somewhere else. 80% of sellers contacted only one agent before hiring, and 66% hired someone referred to them or someone they had used before. Sellers are not comparing the agents who emailed or mailed them the most. They call the one person they trust. Seeing your name in their inbox every month does not make you that person.

One of the agents I coach has phenomenal email automation, with reminders and alerts set up for everyone they have ever worked with. By their own count, 90% of their business comes from less than 5% of the people in their database. If 90% of your business comes from less than 5% of those people, why are you digitally harassing the other 95%?

In 2003, researchers Russell Hill and Robin Dunbar measured how many people we keep up with by using the most ordinary list there is, the one for Christmas cards. Participants logged every card they sent and everyone in the household it went to. Networks averaged about 150 people, and participants stayed in touch weekly with the people they felt closest to.

Sources indicate that A later study by Sam Roberts and Dunbar followed people for 18 months. Friendships lost closeness when contact dropped off, while family relationships were far less affected. Contact matters, and the touch prepares are right about that much. They go wrong by treating a Christmas card to the whole database as the same act as staying close to 150. A mailing treats everyone alike, so the people who matter most get the same thing as the strangers, and the relationships that produce business fade.

Sources indicate that pick your 150. Write down the people you would be glad to call and stop when you reach that number. They will be past clients and friends whose lives you know something about. Dunbar’s later research found an inner circle of about 15 inside the 150. Give those 15 their own rhythm, a conversation with each of them every month, because in my experience that inner circle is where most referrals come from. A database you can’t keep up with starts to feel like a weight. A list of 150 is small enough that you notice when one of them is about to move.

Industry observers note that reconnect at four months. A conversation creates what I call mind share: the trust that comes from talking with someone. Name recognition never builds it, and without the next conversation it fades. In my coaching, I see it run low about three to four months after the last conversation. Once a week, scroll back about four months in your text messages, pick three people, and send something like “Hey, was just thinking about you. Hope all is well; would love to catch up.” Leave real estate out of it. Anyone in your text messages already has a relationship with you that a mailing list never will, so the message reaches someone who trusts you at the point where that trust is lowest. Keep it to three. Send 30, have 10 people answer and your afternoon is gone, which is the fastest way to make sure you never do it again.

If you lead agents, ask how many conversations they had with their 150 this month, not how many touches they made. That number tells you whose relationships are fading before the referrals stop. We keep overcomplicating this business by pushing agents to do more and more, which only creates overwhelm and makes them more likely to do less. We owe them better.
