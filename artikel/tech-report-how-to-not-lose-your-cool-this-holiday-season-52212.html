Industry observers note that while the holidays can bring a great deal of joy, they can also trigger anger in a lot of people. Think having to face difficult people during annual social gatherings, being stuck in traffic or stranded at an airport because of delays, or feeling underappreciated for all the effort you’ve put into a holiday that others may not have acknowledged.

It has been revealed that anger is a powerful emotion. Chronically angry people are more likely to suffer from heart disease, diabetes, and impaired immune functioning. They're also more likely to have unhappy marriages, be lonelier, and struggle at work.

Anger itself is not the problem. It's a normal and healthy emotion like any other. But when it goes unchecked, it can make us do and say things that cause irreparable damage or act in ways we deeply regret.

To help you keep your cool this holiday season, here are five anger-management techniques you can start using—today.

According to recent reports, look around your surrounding environment and try to find five things that are blue. Alternatively, you can seek out five things that begin with the letter A. Either approach helps shift your attention from the source of anger to a neutral stimulus. This brief break helps you de-escalate through cognitive shifting by allowing your emotions to cool down. The more time we spend ruminating and focusing on what’s upsetting us, the more hotheaded we can become. Distraction techniques like these interrupt that anger-rumination-explosion cycle, reducing activity in the amygdala and allowing our heart rates and blood pressure to come back to baseline.

Inhale all the way. Hold your breath for three to four seconds. Exhale all the way (try to make your exhale longer than your inhale). Hold your breath again at the bottom of that exhale for another three to four seconds. Repeat several times until you feel a reduction in distress. This breathwork technique and others like it help regulate the nervous system by increasing activity within the parasympathetic nervous system—the branch of the autonomic nervous system undergirding resting and digesting functions—and downshifting activity within the sympathetic (fight/flight) nervous system.

It has been revealed that when you feel yourself getting heated, try to imagine the anger you’re feeling as an object in your body. First, where is it? Then, what does it look like? Feel like? What’s its temperature and texture; its weight? Be as descriptive as you can. Then, take a deep breath in and, with that breath, imagine creating space around the object in your body. On the exhale, imagine breathing out its intensity—almost like you’re cooling off the object of anger in your body.

Industry observers note that many studies have found that mindfulness techniques such as this help to ease anger, in part by creating some distance between you and the anger itself, so that anger doesn’t take you on as great a roller coaster ride. Taking time to visualize and work with the emotion in the body also helps slow you down, so you don’t knee-jerked-ly blurt out something nasty or otherwise act impulsively in a way that may haunt you (and possibly ruin Thanksgiving dinner).

Recent findings suggest that if you feel yourself starting to get worked up—you're gritting your teeth, your face is flushed, your breathing is shallow, your fists are clenched—see if you can excuse yourself to splash some very cold water on your face or hold some ice cubes to your chest or your temples. If you’re in a part of the world where it’s cold during the holiday season, go take a walk outside in the icy air. Exposure to very cold temperatures helps to trigger the diving reflex, which promptly lowers heart rate and can essentially shock your body back into a less elevated state.

It has been revealed that this one is especially helpful for dealing with difficult people over the holidays. If you’re surrounded by folks who aren’t aligned with you politically, can be quite rude, or make unnecessary comments about your life, body, or any other personal aspect, imagine a force field around you. Really go deep with the visualization here. What color is the force field? What is it made of—ice? Fire? Laser beams? Does it make a noise? Then, imagine the difficult person’s (or persons’) statements bouncing off of the force field (better yet, if they make a funny noise when they do so), leaving you safe and protected. You can go the extra mile and imagine the statements bouncing back and tripping the other person, spilling all over them, or whatever other amusing visual will bring you more of a smile than a grimace.

All of these techniques can be useful in moments of high frustration leading to intense anger. (I use them with my clients, and also with myself.) If you find yourself becoming angry quite often or if you find it particularly challenging to de-escalate and not do things you later feel badly about, it may be worth connecting with a therapist who can help you practice skills like these as well as do some deeper work around the origins of any chronic anger you may be struggling with. Don’t hesitate to get help if this is the case. Your heart health will thank you if you do.
