Recent findings suggest that A skier’s fall ritual: taking inventory of the gear closet. What stays, what goes? The jacket survived another season, the boots are fine as long as nobody asks questions, and the goggles might be saved by a upcoming lens. Eventually, something has to be replaced, and everything on the ski shop racks has a marketing pitch. The 2027 Winter Gear Guide is our case for what’s actually worth the money.

Our men’s ski pants testers alone logged 430 ski days last season, which is a lot of time to find out whether a pant seat is truly waterproof. Spread that scrutiny across skis, boots, outerwear, and accessories and you get the 2027 Winter Gear Guide: more than 400 products, a SKI Test at Deer Valley, and a ski boot test on Mount Hood (50 pairs, 10 testers, two weeks).

It has been revealed that of those more than 400 products, 20 earned highest honors. Best in Test is reserved for the winners of SKI Test, our annual ski shootout at Deer Valley, where each ski goes head to head with a full category field and comes out on top. Editors’ Choice goes to gear that brought newly introduced tech that changes how well the product works, proved themselves season after season, or surprised us in a category where nobody saw it coming. Best Overall is the simplest: the product that tested better than anything else in its category.

If you’re on the hunt for latest gear this season and only the best will do, start here. Each pick links to its full roundup, where you can see how it stacks up against the competition and read the cons—we always have cons.

Sources indicate that few skis have defined a category like the Enforcer. At 99 millimeters underfoot, it’s built for skiers who attack the whole mountain, from refrozen groomers to chopped-up afternoon snow. Its heft keeps it calm where lighter skis get bounced around, and edge grip on firm snow is a standout. The tradeoff is effort: it rewards sharp technique, floats less well than its width suggests, and can feel like work in bumps. Expect a driver’s ski. Read the full review.

Women who spend more time off the groomers than on them will find a willing partner in the M-Pro W 98. At 98 millimeters, it moves between corduroy and off-piste chunder without hesitation, and its stability builds confidence when speed climbs. It’s a directional, significant-mountain-leaning ski, which means it asks for strength and a committed stance. Lighter or less aggressive skiers may find it beefy. For rippers who want one ski to charge anywhere, it delivers. Read the full women’s all-mountain ski guide.

Sources indicate that the M-Free 108 won Best in Test without winning a single performance category, a reminder that consistency beats flash. Testers found it versatile enough to cover a wide range of terrain, and it floats like a ski much wider than its 108-millimeter waist. That makes it a smart pick for freeride-minded skiers who want one pair for storm days and everything in between, rather than a specialist that shines in only one condition. Read the full review.

Where some wide skis chase fun, the Santa Ana 102 chases speed. It stays calm through chopped-up snow and meets every snow type with energy, as long as the skier can match it. That’s the catch: lesser-skilled skiers may find it unforgiving, it’s stiff in bumps, and driving it takes real leg strength. For strong skiers who want a 102-millimeter ski that rewards commitment and holds its line when the going gets tough, it’s the benchmark. Read the full review.

Industry observers note that the Mantra name has long meant power, and the 88 shrinks that formula into a frontside ski. Testers praised how smoothly it rolls from one edge to the other, with a responsiveness closer to a race ski than most frontside designs. It’s stiff and demanding, and the tip can deflect when snow gets cut up, so it’s happiest on groomed and firm terrain. Pick it if you ski fast, ski hard, and want a precise tool. Read the full review.

If you want a frontside ski that takes the guesswork out of starting a turn, the Arcade W 88 is it. A substantial tip pulls you into an arc intuitively, and it handles groomers, bumps, and mixed snow with equal composure. It wants to stay on edge, so it’s not the choice for sideways, slarvy fun. Skiers who prefer a grippy, predictable ride across changing conditions will find a trustworthy everyday partner here. Read the full review.

Sources indicate that carving skis have drifted wider and friendlier, but the Montero AX still feels like race ski’s cousin. With an 80-millimeter waist, it tips onto edge with little effort and stays planted at speed, rewarding skiers who drive it with intent. The price is steep, and less assertive skiers may find it unforgiving. If you live for corduroy and want a ski you can trust when the speedometer climbs, this is the one. Read the full review.

Sources indicate that powder skis can be lively or powerful, rarely both. The Antimatter 114 manages it. Generous rocker at the tip and tail plus a 114-millimeter waist keep it on top of deep snow, while the ski still feels energetic when you hit it hard. It’s a lot of ski for intermediates and comes only in long lengths, and a few testers noticed some wobble in a straight line at full speed. Expert-leaning powder hounds will love it. Read the full review.

Armada’s Locator 112 is a touring ski with a soft-snow personality. At 1,575 grams per ski, testers found it exceptionally playful in powder. It gives up some reliability on firm snow and isn’t the most nimble in tight terrain, so it’s best matched to substantial, open lines and storm-day objectives rather than icy couloirs. Choose it when your blueprint is untracked snow and a lively ride. Read the full review.

Industry observers note that A liner refresh, not a redesign, keeps the Shadow MV on top. It suits skiers who equate comfort with a precise, snug fit rather than extra room, with a 100-millimeter last and a progressive flex that transfers power straight to the ski. Testers liked the shin fit and flex feel, and the liner’s design helps avoid pressure points. It runs slightly short, so size carefully. Read the full review.

Tecnica’s Mach 1 is a perennial favorite in SKI’s boot tests, and the women’s TD2 version earns Editors’ Choice by being easier to live with. It’s simpler to get on than past generations, fits a broad range of foot and leg shapes, and delivers a quick, snappy flex that translates to rapid edge response. On fit: Ankle pockets may bite at first. Read the full review.

The Radical Pro is a midweight touring boot that doesn’t feel like a compromise. At 1,400 grams, it offers a remarkably free 60-degree range of motion for climbing, then stiffens progressively as you flex forward for the descent. Fit is generous through a wide last, which suits many feet but may feel cavernous on narrow ones. For backcountry skiers who need one boot to handle long approaches and confident skiing, it makes a strong case. Read the full review.

Raide’s TourTech is a shell built for people who earn their turns. It breathes well when you’re working hard on the skin track, packs down small enough to stash when the sun comes out, and carries thoughtful backcountry touches. The tradeoff of a shell is warmth: on the coldest storm days you’ll need to layer underneath. It isn’t cheap, but for women who tour more than they lap lifts, it earns its Editors’ Choice. Read the full review.

Industry observers note that fit is the whole argument for the Bigline Bib. The cut has plenty of room for puffy long underwear, but three adjustment points (waist cinches, long suspenders, hem buttons) snug it down so nothing drags or sags. A soft three-layer Pertex Shield outer reliably deflected soggy Pacific Northwest storms. Thigh pockets swallow a skin each, so slackcountry laps are covered. These are uninsulated, so be sure to layer up for cold days. Read the full review.

Sources indicate that testers had a hard time finding fault with the Chariot Primo, which is a rarity among women’s ski pants. Its hybrid fit is relaxed enough to layer and move in without getting baggy, and short, regular, and tall inseams let you skip the tailor. Three-layer PNW Primo fabric offers 20,000 millimeters of waterproofing, and TechSteel kick patches guard the cuffs. With no insulation, you’ll need to layer for the weather, but we love these bibs’ versatility. Read the full review.

According to recent reports, A former Editors’ Choice winner, the Defiant Shell takes the best all-around honor among men’s jackets on comfort, weather protection, and smart details. Venting is excellent, the pocket layout is generous, and the double front zipper is a thoughtful touch. In stormier conditions, its proprietary membrane kept wet, heavy flakes from soaking through. It’s uninsulated, so you control warmth with layers. Read the full review.

Salomon’s Skarn Prime earns the overall nod in the goggles category by doing a lot well. A toric lens gives an expansive field of view, the face foam is layered three deep, and the magnetic lens swap is quick and intuitive. It also pairs with a wide range of helmets. The catches are fit and price: it’s sized large, so smaller faces may swim in it, and $295 is steep. If it fits your face, it’s the one to beat. Read the full review.

In a recent development, sweet Protection’s Switcher 2Vi MIPS took the overall helmet title on comfort, goggle compatibility, and airflow. The Occigrip turn-dial makes fit adjustments quick, and the 2Vi platform and MIPS layer handle impact protection. Venting is excellent, which helps when the temperature climbs. The only real complaint was bulky ear pads. At 560 grams and $300, it’s not the lightest or cheapest lid, but it covers a lot of ground with few compromises. Read the full review.

According to recent reports, rab’s Guide GTX Gauntlets give you protection without bulk. A Gore-Tex Active shell keeps weather out, 100-gsm PrimaLoft Gold and a high-pile lining supply the warmth, and the low-profile cuff tucks over or under a jacket sleeve cleanly. They’re built to last through a hard season, and Rab also offers an undercuff glove and a mitten version if the gauntlet isn’t your style. At $170, they’re a smart, do-it-all pick. Read the full review.

It has been revealed that swany’s Nexus leans on a upcoming heating platform called ewool and 22-watt-hour batteries, and the result is heat that lasts: up to eight hours on low, four on normal, three on high. Testers liked the haptic feedback when changing settings, the high-quality leather, and that you can charge without pulling the batteries out. A mitt version is also offered for the coldest hands. At $480 it’s an investment, but it’s the heated glove to beat. Read the full review.
