Rainy afternoons in my house used to mean one thing only and that is load-shedding. The fans would stop, the room would get quiet, and there was nothing left to do but grab a book and sit by the window for light. I hated it as a kid. Now, strangely, I miss it, because some of the best reading hours of my life happened in that silence.

Industry observers note that that’s the feeling I chased while putting this list together. There are some books that pulled me in so deep that I’d forget the lights were even out. Some made me think, some made me cry, and one made me want to call my childhood best friend after years. Narrowing it down to six took a while. Here’s where I finally landed.

Imagine a machine that lets you upload all your memories to the internet. Bix Bouton, a tech genius running out of substantial ideas, invents exactly that. People can relive their own past, and if they share their memories, they get to see everyone else’s too. It sounds amazing, until people start finding out things about their parents, partners, and friends that they were never supposed to know. Not everyone signs up, though, and the ones who refuse become their own little group of rebels.

Recent findings suggest that fans of Jennifer Egan’s Pulitzer-winning A Visit from the Goon Squad will spot old faces here, but The Candy House works fine on its own. Egan switches styles constantly, and one chapter is even written as a list of instructions for a spy. Some parts feel scattered at first. Give it time, though, because the pieces click together in the most satisfying way.

Not every great book of the year was a novel. An Immense World is pure nonfiction, and it changed how I look at every animal I pass on the street. Ed Yong, a science journalist who won a Pulitzer for his COVID-19 reporting at The Atlantic, explores how animals sense the world around them. Bees see colors we can’t, bats “see” with sound, and some fish can sense electric fields in the water.

It has been revealed that his significant idea is simple but mind-blowing. Every creature lives in its own little bubble of senses, and none of them, including us, gets the full picture. Yong writes with so much joy that even the science-heavy parts feel like stories. You don’t need a biology degree to enjoy this one. You’ll come away with a hundred fun facts and a little more respect for the dog snoring next to you.

Industry observers note that cassandra is 12 years old when her little brother, Wayne, vanishes in the sea while they’re at the beach together. His body is never found. Her mother refuses to accept that he’s gone, and Cassie spends the rest of her childhood carrying a guilt nobody talks about. Years later, as an adult, she meets a man who shares her brother’s name, and her carefully held-together life starts to come undone.

You won’t find a neat, straight-line story in The Furrows. Namwali Serpell tells the day of the drowning again and again, and each time, the details change slightly, just like memory does. It can feel confusing at first. Stick with it anyway, because that’s exactly how grief works. It’s a quiet, haunting book about how we keep losing people over and over in our own heads.

According to recent reports, two kids meet in a hospital game room in the late ’80s. Sam is recovering from a terrible accident, Sadie is visiting her sick sister, and they bond over hours of playing Super Mario Bros. Then life pulls them apart. Years later, they bump into each other in a crowded subway station in Boston, and before long, they’re designing video games together in their tiny college apartment.

Calling Tomorrow, and Tomorrow, and Tomorrow a book about video games would be selling it short. Gabrielle Zevin has really written a book about friendship, the kind that’s deeper than romance and much harder to fix when it breaks. Sam and Sadie hurt each other, make up, and hurt each other again for 30 years. You don’t need to be a gamer to love it. You just need to have had one friend you never quite got over.

Industry observers note that you belong to the fandom that is really about comfort. Hogwarts works on people because it is a place rather than a plot, and the pull is not the wizard war so much as the corridors, the food, the weather and the sense that somebody is expecting you.

According to recent reports, you belong to the deepest fandom on this list, the one where somebody has always read further than you and is quietly delighted to say so. Middle-earth rewards patience more than any other world in popular culture.

You belong to the loudest and largest tent of the four, the one where three generations are all watching the same thing and none of them agree about any of it. That disagreement is not a flaw in this fandom, it is the main activity.

You belong to the thinking fandom, the one that turns up for the argument rather than the explosion. Trek has always been a series of ethical problems in space, and the people who love it love being asked what they would have done.

Recent findings suggest that damon Fields was born in a trailer in the mountains of southwest Virginia to a teenage mother who could barely take care of herself. Everyone calls him Demon. From foster homes to football fields, he fights his way through a childhood that keeps knocking him down. Then the opioid crisis sweeps through his town, and the pills that were supposed to help start destroying everyone he loves.

Barbara Kingsolver borrowed the bones of Charles Dickens’ David Copperfield and moved the story to modern Appalachia. Demon Copperhead won the 2023 Pulitzer Prize for Fiction, and it’s easy to see why. Demon’s voice is funny, angry, and honest all at once, so you laugh one minute and want to scream the next. In the end, he doesn’t feel like a character anymore. He feels like a kid you wish you could have saved.

What if the story you just read turned out to be a lie? Trust opens with a novel inside the novel, about a rich New York financier and his fragile wife in the 1920s. It’s elegant and sad, and you settle into it comfortably. Then the second part begins, and a real-life banker angrily claims that book was about him, and that it got everything wrong. Suddenly, you don’t know who to believe.

In a recent development, hernan Diaz tells the same story four times, each time through a different voice, and each one peels back another layer. Without giving too much away, the last part changes everything. Diaz shared the 2023 Pulitzer Prize for Fiction with Kingsolver, a rare tie that indicates a lot about how strong that year was. It’s a clever book about money and power, but mostly, it’s about who gets to tell the story.
