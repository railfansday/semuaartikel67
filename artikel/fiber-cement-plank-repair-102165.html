I need to remove 12 courses of blind-nailed 5-1/4-in.-wide fiber-cement lap siding with a 4-in. exposure to repair a 5-ft. by 5-ft. section of wall behind it. I’d like to reinstall the old siding without it looking like a repair. What’s the best way to remove fiber-cement lap siding without damaging the planks? How can I make removal and reinstallation less noticeable?

It’s challenging to remove blind-nailed fiber-cement lap siding, but patience and an extra set of hands make removing and reinstalling the planks easier.

Industry observers note that first, mark out the damaged area with painter’s tape. You’ll have to remove full pieces of siding for the repair, so look for the nearest butt joints between individual planks to the left and right of the taped area. Mark the joints in every course of the taped repair area. Plank joints are generally staggered by two studs in successive courses—32 in. or 48 in., depending on the stud spacing. If you’re lucky, the repair area will fit within one 12-ft. plank, so you’ll need to remove just one plank per course between the nearest joints. More likely, you’ll need to remove two. This takes more time, but the removal process is the same.

The first plank is the hardest to remove. Start with the uppermost course in the area you marked out and gently pry up the overlying plank far enough to locate the nails. Then mark the nail locations with small pieces of tape on the overlying course. The butt joints to the left and right of the plank may have been caulked. If so, carefully cut through the sealant with a utility knife.

It has been revealed that when fiber-cement planks are 8 in. or wider, you can lift the bottom edge far enough to pry out the nails from the plank below by slipping a thin flat bar under that plank and up to the shank of the nail. Gently pry the plank up slightly and then remove the flat bar. Press down on the top edge of the plank so the nail head stands proud of the surface, then set the flat bar under the nail head and pry it out.

Sources indicate that prying up narrower planks to pull 2-in. or longer nails often breaks the plank, so in these situations I use a thin cutting tool to cut the nails holding the plank—the Malco Hidden Nail Cutter is made for this job, though you can also use a reciprocating saw with a long metal-cutting blade. Set a 1-ft.-sq. piece of PVC or coil stock under the flat bar to avoid scratching the finish, gently lift the plank up about ¼ in. at each blind nail location, and use the Malco tool or recip saw to cut the nails. Don’t pry too much or you’ll break the top edge of the overlying plank. As you cut the nails, have a helper hold one end of the plank so you can control its removal and be careful sliding the plank downward so the cut-off nails don’t scratch the plank beneath.

With the first plank out of the way, most of the nails holding the next plank down are exposed. Pull the exposed nails using the flat bar method: insert the flat bar between the plank and the wall sheathing at the nail location and pry against the wall; once the nail is broken free and raised 1/4 in., push the top of the plank back against the wall so the nail head is proud, and finish pulling the nail with the flat bar. Cut any blind nails that remain before removing the plank.

Continue working down, removing planks left and right as close to the taped-out area as possible. Remove the cut nails from the planks so they don’t scratch the siding during handling, and label the planks so you can reinstall them in the same locations.

After you make your wall repair, install a new piece of water-resistive barrier (WRB) taped along its perimeter to the existing WRB and tuck its top under the existing WRB to shed water. Then reinstall the planks in reverse sequence. I install new nails in the old holes, using ring-shank nails with a slightly larger diameter than the originals for a secure grip. At the ends of planks where they slide beneath an overlying course, you’ll have to face-nail. Make sure the nail penetrates through the overlap at the underlying and overlying planks near where the blind nail used to be.

Sources indicate that work your way up the wall until you reach the last plank that will slip beneath the existing plank at the top of the repaired area, then face-nail the last reinstalled plank. Face nails can be left exposed and touched up with color-matching paint, but they will be noticeable compared to the blind-nailed siding on the rest of the wall. You can somewhat hide the heads by countersinking them: Make recesses with a drill-mounted countersink and then putty and paint the heads so they’re less obvious. Don’t try hammering the nail heads below the siding surface because you’ll break the fiber cement and the nails won’t hold well.
