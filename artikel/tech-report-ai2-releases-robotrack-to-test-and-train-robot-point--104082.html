Tech Report: Ai2 Releases RoboTrack to Test and Train Robot Point Trackers

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The Allen Institute for AI (Ai2) released RoboTrack on November 23, 2026, pairing a robotic point-tracking benchmark and training suite with two models. Ai2 reported that RoboTracker completed 17 of 20 wet-lab pipetting trials, compared with eight using object masks and five using optical flow.

Point tracking follows the same physical point through video, estimates when it is occluded and identifies it when it reappears. Those trajectories can help robots learn from demonstrations and guide manipulation, but grippers, hands, clutter and changing viewpoints can break that correspondence. RoboTrack addresses the problem with RoboTrack-Bench, a human-verified evaluation set, and RoboTrack-15M, synthetic training data generated from robot simulations. A technical report and model weights accompany the release.

RoboTrack-Bench contains 307 clips and 2,522 human-verified tracks drawn from DROID, Ego-Exo4D, MolmoAct 2 and EgoVerse. The clips cover four robot embodiments and human manipulation in cluttered scenes, filmed from first- and third-person views. Thirty-three synchronized two-camera pairs identify the same physical points across views.

Recent findings suggest that annotators prioritized moving or occluded points over static backgrounds, and a separate verification pass checked every track frame by frame. Fifty-seven percent of points are occluded at least once; each track provides position and visibility labels for every frame.

Recent findings suggest that average Jaccard (AJ), a higher-is-better measure combining tracking accuracy and visibility prediction, is the main metric. Ai2’s evaluation of the final CoTracker3, BootsTAPNext, AllTracker and CoWTracker models found scores 3.0 to 6.6 AJ lower on RoboTrack-Bench than on RoboTAP. CoWTracker, the strongest off-the-shelf baseline, fell from 70.5 AJ on RoboTAP to 63.9, indicating that controlled tabletop results did not transfer intact to the benchmark’s broader interactions and viewpoints.

Recent findings suggest that bootsTAPNext adds training on 15 million real videos to TAPNext. Ai2 reported that this raised its RoboTrack-Bench score from 55.1 to 60.2 AJ but lowered its synthetic RGB-Stacking result from 75.0 to 65.5, so the extra training helped on real manipulation while hurting on simulated block stacking.

Sources indicate that roboTrack-15M targets robot-object interaction rather than general scene motion. Its 3,000 videos capture 1,500 picking episodes across 300 procedurally generated homes, recorded by wrist and base-mounted cameras. A simulated Franka arm handles everyday objects, while MolmoSpaces and MuJoCo supply geometry, object poses, depth and camera information to calculate point positions and visibility without human annotation. Each video contains 5,000 tracks.

Point selection proved as important as dataset size. Ai2 reported that its first synthetic-data attempt produced no RoboTrack-Bench gain because many points lay on static backgrounds. Restricting supervision to the robot and movable objects, and excluding the robot’s fixed base, produced a 63.0 AJ ablation score, 1.5 points above the Kubric-only control. That ties the improvement to supervision resembling manipulation motion rather than to synthetic-data volume alone.

RoboTracker fine-tunes the 17-million-parameter AllTracker, while RoboTracker-Warp fine-tunes CoWTracker, which has about 1 billion parameters. Both use RoboTrack-15M alongside the original training data.

Ai2 reports that RoboTracker improved from 62.6 to 63.4 AJ on RoboTrack-Bench and RoboTracker-Warp from 63.9 to 66.0, with both models remaining close to their base models on general video. Warp had the highest AJ among the evaluated models on four of five benchmarks. Both checkpoints are listed in Ai2’s public artifact collection, but RoboTracker-Warp’s weights are restricted to noncommercial use.

The wet-lab system used one arm of a bimanual I2RT YAM robot to pick up an electronic pipette, draw liquid from one vial, dispense it into another and return the pipette to its holder. It learned the sequence from one teleoperated demonstration divided into steps. At each key frame, RoboTracker estimated the alignment between demonstration and live-camera points; the robot then replayed the demonstrated motion without further feedback, making alignment accuracy central to success.

The test split layouts evenly between clear views and partial occlusion. Ai2’s results show RoboTracker succeeding in all 10 clear-view trials and seven of 10 occluded trials. SAM3 mask-centroid alignment completed seven clear-view transfers but only one occluded transfer, while RAFT optical flow completed three and two, respectively.

Ai2 attributes optical-flow failures to large displacements and mask failures to similar-looking objects or occlusion. Point tracking instead carries the same vial’s correspondence through the video, although RoboTracker still failed under extended or complete occlusion. The experiment connects better point correspondence to physical execution in a task where the pipette tip must align with a vial opening within millimeters.

Those results do not resolve the trackers’ remaining weaknesses. Ai2 says they still struggle with long occlusions, re-identifying points after reappearance and small objects against busy backgrounds. The benchmark is modest in scale, deformable-object and tool-use tasks are underrepresented, and only two tracker families were fine-tuned. RoboTrack-15M separately omits human hands and industrial, outdoor and laboratory scenes, and its simulated rendering and motion differ from real video.

Ai2’s next step is to simulate precise actions such as pipette handling more closely and test whether their trajectories improve tracking during deployment. The open question is whether training data built around the exact manipulation a robot must perform can extend the gains beyond simulated object picking.
