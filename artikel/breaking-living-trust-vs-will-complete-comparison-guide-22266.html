In a recent development, A will (technically a "last will and testament") is a legal document that specifies how your assets should be distributed after your death. It must go through probate — a court-supervised process that validates the will, pays debts and taxes, and distributes assets to beneficiaries. A living trust (specifically a revocable living trust) is a legal entity you create during your lifetime that holds your assets. When you die, the successor trustee distributes those assets according to the trust terms — without court involvement.

The critical distinction is probate. In states with expensive or slow probate processes — California, New York, Florida, and many others — a living trust pays for itself many times over. In states with simplified probate procedures (like Texas or Wisconsin for small estates), the cost advantage of a trust is smaller but the privacy and incapacity benefits still apply.

Industry observers note that you need both: A trust does not name guardians for minor children (only a will can do that), and a trust only controls assets that have been formally transferred into it. A pour-over will acts as a safety net — it directs any assets not in the trust at your death to pour into the trust, where they are distributed according to the trust terms. The pour-over will still goes through probate, but it ensures nothing falls through the cracks.

Recent findings suggest that the term "living trust" almost always refers to a revocable living trust — one you can change, amend, or dissolve at any time during your lifetime. You remain the trustee, manage the assets as you always have, and pay taxes on trust income using your personal Social Security number. For income tax purposes, a revocable trust is invisible — the IRS treats it as if it does not exist.

Estate planning is governed primarily by state law, and the value of a living trust versus a will varies significantly depending on where you live.

It has been revealed that nine states are community property states: Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, Upcoming Mexico, Texas, Washington, and Wisconsin. In these states, most assets acquired during marriage are owned 50/50 by both spouses regardless of whose name is on the title. At death, only the deceased spouse's half passes through their estate proposal.

Sources indicate that the remaining 41 states follow common law (also called "equitable distribution"). Ownership depends on whose name is on the title. This distinction affects how assets pass at death and whether a trust or will provides better spousal protection.

Without a trust, each state where you own real property requires a separate probate proceeding (called ancillary probate), multiplying costs and complexity. Transferring out-of-state property into a living trust eliminates ancillary probate entirely — the successor trustee can transfer or sell the property without court involvement in any state.

The living trust vs will decision is not about which is better — it is about which is better for your specific situation. For estates with more than $100,000 in probatable assets, especially in high-cost probate states, a revocable living trust saves thousands of dollars and months of delay while providing incapacity protection and privacy that a will cannot match. For smaller estates in simplified-probate states, a well-drafted will with proper beneficiary designations may be all you need. Either way, the worst estate plan is no estate plan. More than 60% of American adults have neither a will nor a trust, leaving their families to navigate probate without guidance, pay avoidable costs, and endure unnecessary delays during the most difficult time of their lives.
