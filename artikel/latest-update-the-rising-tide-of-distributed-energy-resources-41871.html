Latest Update: The Rising Tide Of Distributed Energy Resources

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Centralized power plants can trip widespread brownouts or blackouts when under stress. They make fat targets for enemy weapons, too. In contrast, distributed energy resources (DERs for short) can improve overall resiliency in the face of both natural and human-made disasters, while also enabling ratepayers to take advantage of utility discounts, and even earn revenue from their personal energy assets. The DERs field includes — but is not limited to — rooftop solar, EV charging stations, and home batteries. And, we ain’t seen nothing yet. DERs stakeholders have also begun moving into the distributed computing field as well.

DERs first surfaced on the CleanTechnica radar in the early 2000’s during the Obama administration, when the US Department of Energy began promoting decentralized, home-and community-based energy resources as the leading characteristic of a modernized and more resilient electricity grid. Made possible by the falling cost of solar, wind, and energy storage, the agency’s embrace of small-scale, localized electricity generation was a sharp contrast with the traditional emphasis on large centralized power plants for energy security (see more DERs background here).

Nowadays the DERs field embraces practically any non-centralized power generation resource, regardless of size or type. The DERs field can include, for example, a fresh gas power plant commissioned to run an adjacent AI data center.

To get some perspective on the journey of DERs since the early 2000’s, consider the case of the leading rooftop solar installer Sunrun. After launching in 2007, Sunrun segued into energy storage and virtual power plants, with “virtual” referring to smart grid technology that coordinates thousands of far-flung energy producing (and consuming) devices to balance supply and demand at peak periods of consumption.

For the record, Sunrun now presents itself as a storage-first company. The company’s focus remains on renewable energy but the storage field is source-agnostic, embracing practically every energy resource on the planet.

Sources indicate that sunrun CEO Mary Powell also prefers to use “distributed” instead of “virtual” in reference to decentralized power systems, to emphasize that hardware is the foundational element. As of this month, Sunrun ranks as the biggest distributed power plant in the US, with solar and storage assets spread among 164,000 enrolled households.

Back in 2021, Sunrun entered into an installation partnership with the home energy management startup SPAN, known for its next-generation home electrical panels among other products, and that layed the ground work for a partnership on distributed computing systems. Earlier this year, SPAN introduced XFRA, a newly introduced product consisting of computer nodes to be staged at homes and smaller commercial spaces to form a distributed data center. The model is similar in concept to the familiar Internet-enabled citizen scientist platform, except that XFRA deploys its own nodes instead of using a participant’s personal computer.

Recent findings suggest that this past September, Sunrun declared its selection as an authorized installation partner for XFRA with a focus on new home developments, aiming for gigawatt-scale distributed computing networks beginning in Texas and other selected markets. “Sunrun’s battery-plus-solar systems will maintain reliable electricity for both the homes and the XFRA compute nodes, while preventing the overloading of local distribution infrastructure—allowing SPAN to maximize node deployment.,” Sunrun explains.

“By pairing home energy generation and storage with XFRA compute nodes, we are unlocking new economic value for our customers and for Sunrun, while also solving certain speed-to-power constraints that would otherwise limit AI deployment,” Sunrun CEO Mary Powell elaborated in a press statement.

Arch Rao, founder and CEO of SPAN, also chipped in his two cents. “We can now drastically increase our penetration of XFRA compute nodes by pairing them with Sunrun energy systems to deliver cost-effective AI compute capacity at unprecedented speed while also reducing compute cost for certain AI applications,” Rao said.

Back when the Energy Department initially began promoting DERs as a function of the energy transition, the agency used the term “prosumer” to describe ratepayers who are empowered to engage with their local power grid as a renewable energy producer, not simply as a passive consumer. The distributed data center adds another layer of engagement to the prosumer mix.

At a personal appearance during Climate Week in Upcoming York City in September, Powell referenced distributed computing as the latest demonstration of the organization’s ongoing dialog with its customers and other stakeholders, leading to innovative new products that put more power management tools in the hands of ratepayers.

Powell reiterated the point during an appearance at the 2026 “DERVOS” summit at the Brooklyn Navy Yard on November 5, and the DERVOS gathering itself provides further insights into the widening scope of DERs activity.

“DERVOS” is a play on Davos, which is shorthand for the annual meeting of the World Economic Forum. Like the World Economic Forum, DERs stakeholders have emerged in force upon the global economy as well as here in the US, stretching far from its do-it-yourself roots in the off-gridder movement of the 1970’s.

The event itself is the brainchild of the collaborative trade organization DER Task Force. The group formally launched in 2019 but it claims roots much earlier, in the 1970’s-era New York City activist organization Energy Task Force, whose activities were highlighted by the installation of a wind turbine in the city — illegally, of course.

By the time DERVOS 2026 rolled around, the DER Task Force had become an organization with a reach far beyond Fresh York, and it has also embraced the size-doesn’t-matter ethos  under the slogan, “Everything is DER.”.

“That’s what we mean by Everything is DER: not that everything is small, or even literally ‘distributed,’ but that the center of gravity is shifting toward energy resources that are closer to load and controlled by those who need the power,” the Task Force explains (emphasis theirs).

Recent findings suggest that “Sometimes that shift is bottoms-up, sometimes it’s centrally planned, but either way, DERs aren’t the sideshow anymore. They’re the main event,” they add.

According to recent reports, I’ll have much more to report from DERVOS 2026 in the coming days and weeks, but for the time being let’s go back to the foundational idea of improving grid resiliency and security. Climate change aside, in recent years Russia and the US have both provided textbook cases for transitioning out of energy systems that involve building fat, juicy infrastructure targets for attack, alongside precariously long energy transportation systems.
