According to recent reports, for years, marketers were told that better data would lead to better decisions. But Joe Marchese thinks that approach missed the more basic question: did you earn anyone’s attention in the first place?

Now that AI has raised the stakes again through agents that buy on the behalf of consumers, marketers may be entering a world where machines influence the buying decision before the brand ever gets a direct chance to speak.

This episode of The Speed of Culture podcast welcomes Joe Marchese, CEO of Human Ventures Holdings, to explore what that shift means for trust, brand, and competitive advantage.

Their conversation moves from the failures of impression-driven advertising to the fiduciary problem of AI agents, why strategy becomes more valuable as optimization gets automated, and why shared cultural experiences may matter more precisely because machines cannot fully understand them.

Joe Marchese is CEO of Human Ventures Holdings, and co-author of Human Dot AI: How the Fight for Your Attention Became the World’s Biggest Business. His career has spanned entrepreneurship, advertising technology, media and investing.

Recent findings suggest that joe founded true[X], an advertising manufacturer built around the idea that engaged attention was more valuable than sheer impression volume, before the company was acquired by Fox. He later worked across television advertising at Fox and now invests in companies through Human Ventures, with a particular focus on how technology and AI intersect with media, culture, hospitality, and real-world human connection.

[03:50] Data Is Useless Without Attention — Joe’s original true[X] thesis was simple: not all impressions are equal. Marketers could have enormous amounts of data about someone, but without their attention, none of it mattered. By contrast, attention alone still creates the opportunity to tell a story. His criticism of programmatic advertising is that quality attention was undervalued largely because it was harder to measure than impression volume.

[06:42] AI Agents Need a Trust Test — Joe compares AI agents with human agents in real estate, insurance, and entertainment. If an agent is supposed to act on someone’s behalf, the user needs confidence that its incentives are aligned with theirs. The problem comes when advertising enters the equation. If an AI recommends the product that pays rather than the product that is best, the relationship starts to look less like trusted advice and more like hidden persuasion.

In a recent development, [09:04] Invest in What Machines Can’t Understand — Joe offers CMOs a sharp filter for AI-era strategy: anything machines can understand, they will eventually optimize. That pushes competitive advantage toward areas that remain difficult to automate, including strategy, culture, experiential marketing and real-world human connection. For marketers, the question becomes less about how to outperform the machine at optimization and more about where human judgment still creates differentiated value.

[13:00] Shared Experiences Still Carry Outsized Value — Joe points to live events, festivals, hospitality and media tied to real-world experiences as areas with enduring value. His investment thesis reflects the belief that technology can help humans connect, but that physical spaces and shared cultural moments create something difficult to replicate digitally. For brands, that makes experiences and sponsorships more than awareness plays; they become part of the cultural infrastructure around the audience.

[16:45] Brand Is Shared Cultural Currency — Joe rejects the performance marketing idea that every impression outside a narrow target audience is wasted. A brand needs to matter not only to the buyer, but also to the people around them – friends, family, colleagues, and wider culture. That shared narrative is part of what makes brand valuable, even when the effect cannot be captured neatly by last-click attribution.

Industry observers note that [18:33] AI Changes the Incentive Problem, Not Just the Interface — Joe’s book Human Dot AI examines how the attention economy shaped the last 25 years through algorithms designed to decide what deserved people’s time. He believes the next phase is more consequential because conversational AI can build deeply personal relationships with users. His warning is that if those relationships are tied to performance or advertising incentives, marketers and consumers need to think carefully about who the AI is actually serving.
