Use AI Last, Not First

Posted November 17, 2026 | Reviewed by Margaret Foley.

Most of us today do the same thing the moment a problem lands on our lap. Almost automatically, we open the chat window and pull up our favorite AI tool. Then we type in our prompt. Seconds later, a rather reasonable-appearing answer appears on the screen.

It feels like a win. Yet it’s not.

Industry observers note that the problem isn’t that AI gives bad answers. It’s that AI seems like the easy way out. But it's deceptive, depriving us of the opportunity to use our human creative abilities to solve problems in fresh and novel ways.

According to recent reports, AI cannot live in the now; it exists only as a snapshot of history and a crawl of data and the internet. That history cannot come up with ideas right now. And it turns out that right now is when you need the fresh ideas the most, to solve challenges that are plaguing you. AI can only take a very narrow view of the past and try deceptively to apply it to the future.

Industry observers note that so before you reach for AI first—because after all, AI is making everyone’s ideas the same anyhow—reach for it last. Here are some ideas for pushing back on the use of AI and instead relying on your own abilities to solve problems.

In a recent development, instead of reaching for AI first, you go first. Write down three ideas that you have for the problem that just fell into your lap.

Sources indicate that write down the three worst ideas you can possibly come up with. Instead of pressing yourself to get it right from the get-go, stretch your solution mindset to think of issues creatively without the pressure to get it right.

According to recent reports, then take a stab at three ideas that will actually solve the problem. You may see some cross-pollination from the worst ideas list too.

Sources indicate that in a recent MIT Media Lab experiment1, people who wrote down ideas first and then later switched to AI indicated higher recall and activation. People who started with AI and then wrote on their own? They showed under-engagement.

Write down your ideas with a pen and paper first—old school. It turns out that doing it the old way may be more likely to make creativity and more lucid thinking2 happen for you.

According to recent reports, I have friends in various industries who literally brag to me about the time they spend on their AI prompts. They say things like, “Nir—if you spend an extra five minutes on each prompt you put in, the AI answer is so much better.” I even have a friend who dedicates 15 minutes or more to each prompt. Every day.

Yet no matter how long you spend prompting the AI, you still get AI answers that sound much the same as the answers everyone else is getting. Instead, turn prompt time into being bored time.

Monitor the time you spend on AI each week asking it to do things for you. At the end of the week, see how much time you were on AI, and instead shift that time to being bored. So if it’s 20 minutes a week or 40 minutes a week, use that time differently. Plot down a 40-minute block of time during the week and call it “me time.” And then, instead of prompting an AI, be bored. Creativity and innovation will flow.

According to recent reports, the research backs this. Sandi Mann and Rebekah Cadman3 found that experiencing a state of boredom increases creative potential, idea generation, and problem-solving performance.

So take the time you would waste on AI and invest it in yourself instead – the ideas you make while bored can be life-changing.

According to recent reports, chances are the problem that fell into your lap is rife with assumptions. Some of the assumptions are ones that you had made, some may be from a coworker, some may be from your ignorance of nuance, and some may just be stubborn histories.

Instead of getting bogged down with complexity, write the central problem on a piece of paper and then cross out all the assumptions made.

This process will bring clarity to an otherwise emotional or difficult problem by eliminating the assumptions that are preloaded into the problem. It also reframes the original problem in a simpler way, allowing you to solve it without all the extra fat and weight.

Recent findings suggest that technion professors Ronen, Goldschmidt, and Erez4 studied how people interpret a problem while solving it, which they call problem structuring. They found that extra clarity helped mainly because people used it to rethink the problem itself. How you frame the problem matters, not just the time you put in.

In a recent development, overall, our urge to go to AI first for all our challenges is a fallacy. We may think it's faster, cheaper, and better, but like all things that seem too good to be true, it is. If instead we try the three ideas listed above and then turn to AI as a last resort, we can begin to strengthen our creative problem-solving core to allow for new and novel ideas that no AI can ever generate.
