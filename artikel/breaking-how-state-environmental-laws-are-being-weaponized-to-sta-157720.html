📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Congress is fixated on cutting red tape that drives up the cost of housing—but state policies have a role to play, too, a study finds.

The Pacific Legal Foundation studied state-level policies and found that certain environmental review laws are being "weaponized" to stall developments, driving up the costs and reducing the affordability of housing. This comes as state lawmakers have proposed many new laws this year to curtail policies that stall building, with mixed success.

The study looks at lawsuits that use state environmental policy acts, as well as environmental rights amendments written into state laws. Both of these kinds of disputes are expensive and time-consuming. Developers have to examine and reexamine the environmental impact of developments they want to build, and courts must then determine what cities are required to do to ensure a clean environment for residents.

"A real cost is borne on the people who are renting and buying those homes and apartments," Kyle Sweetland, the study's author, tells Realtor.com®.

About a dozen states have pitched laws that would make it easier to build homes, many of them by reducing zoning or lot size requirements. But some proposals have also met with failure, like California's Proposition 45, which aimed to expedite project reviews through the California Environmental Quality Act.

It has been revealed that the Realtor.com Housing Supply Gap research shows the U.S. faces a shortage of 4.03 million homes. Our Let America Build campaign has tracked how states have responded to this shortage, and which are tackling the problem.

It has been revealed that california has a long history of slow development on account of environmental review. In one recent example, a 49-unit housing development was stalled for 4.5 years in environmental litigation, which the developer mentioned had raised the cost of the project by $9 million. In another, a 34-home subdivision was stalled for nearly 40 years, in part because of neighbors' environmental concerns.

According to recent reports, it's not just a California problem. Sweetland looked at 16 states and the District of Columbia that have environmental policy acts that affect developers, or amendments that affect municipalities. Wyoming, Minnesota, New York, Hawaii, and Massachusetts have both.

But other protections are also in place in California, Nevada, Washington, North Dakota, Illinois, Wyoming, Michigan, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Connecticut, Rhode Island, and the District of Columbia.

It's hard to track precisely how much this kind of litigation drives up the costs of housing. Realtor.com senior economist Joel Berner also notes that it's hard to tell how many housing projects didn't start in the first place because developers expected a lawsuit.

It has been revealed that "The bigger costs come from what the lawsuit does to the project’s financing and schedule," Berner says. "Appeals typically add years, and in the meantime, most projects can’t secure construction or grant funding. Loans, taxes, and insurance keep accruing, and construction costs can rise while the project waits.".

In a recent development, change could come at the federal level. Last week, the Senate passed a controversial bill that would reduce some federal environmental regulations, like the National Environmental Policy Act, to speed up construction of big-energy projects. Environmental groups opposed the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, which next heads to the House.

It has been revealed that in some instances, the environmental lawsuits don't actually produce better outcomes for the environment. Seattle found that just 16% of cases filed using environmental laws over the 2010s resulted in decisions requiring environmental remediation. In New York, about 22% of cases over 15 years resulted in more environmental reviews, an outside researcher found.

It has been revealed that berner says the balance is that environmental laws are sometimes the easiest avenue residents have to oppose a residential project. It's just a matter of not abusing the process.

"While challenging a project is a relatively cheap thing to do, being on the receiving end of a legal battle can derail a project even without going all the way through the litigation process," Berner indicates. "This uncertainty adds to the cost of capital for builders and squeezes out lower-margin projects like affordable and infill housing.".

In a recent development, pacific Legal Foundation says one solution for these challenges is to narrow the scope of some environmental laws. For instance, Sweetland says legal challenges from outside environmental groups could be limited. That way, the people challenging housing developments have a real vested interest in their outcomes.

Sources indicate that he also suggests requirements that groups that lose cases have to pay the legal fees. That isn't typical everywhere, even if developers pay the legal fees when they lose. That provides some incentive that those launching lawsuits have a legitimate claim, as opposed to using the courts as a stalling tactic.

"The law shouldn't just give anybody the ability to have a license to stop a building from moving forward," Sweetland states.
