Latest Update: How Bitcoin still competes with a 2.92% Treasury yield after inflation

🌐 Source: Cryptoslate • 📅 17 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 3 min read (557 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways

  • Industry observers note that treasury’s nominal yield data showed a decline to 5.12% from 5.15% on Friday, Nov.
  • The real yield had stood at 2.63% on Nov.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The 10-year Treasury yield after inflation rose to 2.92% on Monday, Nov. 16, up 29 basis points since Nov. 6. The move left Bitcoin competing with that yield even as the headline 10-year Treasury rate fell.

Industry observers note that treasury’s nominal yield data showed a decline to 5.12% from 5.15% on Friday, Nov. 13, a three-basis-point move. Its real-yield series moved the other way, edging up one basis point from 2.91%.

The real yield had stood at 2.63% on Nov. 6. The 29-basis-point rise therefore spans multiple sessions, with Monday adding only one basis point.

In a recent development, bitcoin provides no contractual interest income. A higher yield on inflation-protected government debt can therefore raise the opportunity cost of holding it: investors have a stronger income alternative against which to weigh Bitcoin’s potential returns and volatility.

The 2.92% figure is also a long-term Treasury curve benchmark, not an overnight borrowing rate or a promised return for every investor. Treasury estimates its constant-maturity nominal and real par yields from indicative market quotations. An investor’s realized return on a particular bond can differ.

Sources indicate that the November 16 bond rally lowered the nominal benchmark while the 10-year real yield reached 2.92%. Compare Monday’s small move with the rise since November 6.

A simple subtraction of Treasury’s real par yield from its nominal par yield gives a spread of 2.20 percentage points on Monday, down from 2.24 on Friday. The four-basis-point narrowing explains how the nominal benchmark could fall while the real benchmark rose.

Industry observers note that the spread is often described as inflation compensation. But the Federal Reserve’s explanation shows why it cannot be read as a pure inflation forecast: it also reflects compensation for inflation risk and differences in liquidity between nominal Treasuries and inflation-protected securities. The subtraction alone cannot show which component drove Monday’s change.

Historical research supports caution about the transmission to crypto. The authors of a 2023 IMF working paper found that U.S. monetary tightening reduced a broad crypto price factor through investor risk-taking. Their study does not estimate how Monday’s one-basis-point real-yield move affected Bitcoin.

Watch the inflation-adjusted Treasury yield for signs that competition from bonds is easing. The headline rate can fall while that yield stays elevated.
