Canada's top court has largely upheld a Quebec law banning public sector workers from wearing religious symbols, including the hijab and the cross.

The law, known as Bill 21, faced legal challenges from a range of groups, including female Muslim, Sikh and Catholic teachers, who argued it violated their fundamental rights.

Quebec passed the law in 2019 under a unique fixture of Canada's constitution called the "notwithstanding clause" - a legal loophole that allows governments to override certain rights, like freedom of religion.

The Supreme Court's decision is one of the most closely watched in Canadian history, with an impact that will be felt both in Quebec and across the country.

Reacting to the ruling on CBC, Prime Minister Mark Carney said his government would study its implications, adding it was important for all legislatures "to respect the rights and liberties of all Canadians, including those in the minority.".

The Supreme Court was asked to weigh in on a number of questions, including on the constitutionality of Bill 21, whether Quebec's use of the notwithstanding clause was appropriate, and whether there are limits to its use.

On Friday, the top court confirmed the validity of Bill 21, but ruled that the law infringes on democratic rights by banning Quebec's elected officials from wearing a face covering for religious reasons.

The court also ruled that a Canadian court has the power to declare whether a law is consistent with the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms even if the notwithstanding clause is invoked, but that they would not do so in this case.

While the majority of the seven justices found that Quebec's law is largely valid, some dissented to parts of the decision.

Justices Andromache Karakatsanis, Michelle O'Bonsawin and Nicholas Kasirer wrote that the notwithstanding clause "cannot be used to authorize laws that are incompatible with the fundamental character of Canada as a free and democratic country".

Specifically, they said they believe the law infringes on the charter's guarantee to freedom of religion, freedom of expression and the right to equality.

What to know about the Supreme Court decision on Quebec's secularism bill.

Civil liberty groups in Quebec have criticised Bill 21, saying it disproportionately affects Muslim women who wear the hijab, arguing that it has excluded them from key areas of public life in the province.

Stephen Brown, CEO of the National Council of Canadian Muslims, called the law "discriminatory" and criticised the court's decision.

"Entire categories of people are now barred from entire sectors of employment, not because of what they have done, but because of who they are," he mentioned.

The chair of the English Montreal School Board, one of the main groups challenging the law, said the board was "obviously very disappointed" with the ruling.
