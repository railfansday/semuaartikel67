Okay, don’t freak out too much. The title of this whole article might look a bit incendiary, and sure, talking about these “aging badly” can be exhausting, especially when it’s not done with appropriate nuance. But the person tackling this topic does love the work of Stephen King and wants to emphasize that while many of his older books have elements that date them, most are still pretty great. There are little things here and there that date them, honestly, rather than the bunch of them being outright broken when read today, versus how they might've been experienced some decades ago.

Recent findings suggest that like, some of the Stephen King books that aren’t worth tackling are his newer ones without much that could be deemed problematic. You should read all the books below before tackling the likes of Lisey’s Story, The Colorado Kid, and Cell, for some examples. And these books were all written more than 30 years ago, and some are closer to 50 years old. Times changing and things getting dated after that long? All that’s inevitable, and interesting to think about and address, all the while still stressing that these Stephen King books are still worth tackling, and that the smaller dated elements don’t really get in the way of appreciating the good stuff. See? Nuance! You're in good hands here, don’t worry.

It’s not exactly niche in the overall scheme of things, but as far as Stephen King books go, The Talisman might qualify as a deep cut. And it’s one where King was the co-author, since Peter Straub was also credited with writing The Talisman, and then its sequel, Black House. He was credited with Other Worlds Than These (2026), which concluded the trilogy, but that was more of a solo effort on King’s part, since Straub passed away in 2022.

It villainizes some types of people a bit casually, and then the character of Speedy Parker would be written differently nowadays.

Anyway, Black House and Other Worlds Than These have technically aged “better,” and they’ve also not aged as much objectively, but The Talisman is the strongest novel of the three. It villainizes some types of people a bit casually, and then the character of Speedy Parker would be written differently nowadays (and he kind of was, when he plays a smaller role in Other Worlds Than These), but 1984 was a while ago. You can find those smaller moments a little awkward, but they're not too extreme, and they’re also a very small part of what’s ultimately a very large – and sprawling – novel.

Industry observers note that it’s always tricky when a coming-of-age story tries to accurately portray the way kids talk. Like, once kids reach a certain age, they're potty-mouthed. You don’t always hear kids talking that way, because some avoid doing so around adults, but also, if you're an adult, obviously, you were once a kid. You were once one of those young people who refrained from swearing too much around parents and teachers, but let rip quite ridiculously around friends.

So, with The Body (a novella included in the collection Different Seasons), the kids swear a lot. That’s okay, even today, but maybe some of the slurs they use wouldn’t be included today, or if they were, the story would be more outwardly critical of such language. But you can argue it’s okay, in this instance, because these are boys who are rebelling (and doing something as daring as searching for a dead body), and further, the story’s set in the late 1950s, when things were less politically correct even compared to the early 1980s, when The Body was published. So, it’s a more minor example, but still worth noting for present purposes.

In a recent development, of all the books here, Christine might be the one that feels the least essential, or the least classic, but look, it was still written early on during Stephen King’s career, at a time when he was mostly putting out bangers, and Christine definitely has its fans. It being the weakest book of the first 10 or so years of his writing career isn't really all that bad a thing when it’s got so many classics it’s up against, and in the overall scheme of things, maybe it’s still a classic.

It’s got one of those typically gonzo high-concept Stephen King premises, since it’s about a possessed car that drives two friends apart. And some other things. Christine goes off in all sorts of strange directions, and all the while, you get some stuff that hasn’t really aged all that beautifully. But it’s the same sort of “problem” you get with The Body, because it’s about young characters who aren’t really supposed to be the most considerate of people, so it more or less works (it’s more the complications with quality, regarding Christine, that make it only sort of worthy of qualifying for inclusion in this particular ranking, with the word “Classic” being in the headline and all).

Industry observers note that there are only elements of Carrie that date it. That dating is okay when you consider the fact that it’s the oldest of King’s published novels, being his debut and all. And that sense of those small things being dated is further mitigated by the fact that the core of Carrie holds up and feels timeless, since it looks at bullying – both at school and a slightly different/more abusive sort at home – in an uncompromising way, and difficult experiences at home and school as a teenager? That’s always relevant, obviously.

It’s more the details in some smaller moments that are addressed either a bit fast or somewhat casually. There’s corporal punishment, some discomfort with Carrie casually wanting to act out violently before she actually snaps, and then even smaller things like smoking on school property. Like, yeah, you can tell it was written in the 1970s. It’s acceptable, though, and all the stuff that holds up and continues to be powerful is impressive. Some of those dated elements were why Mike Flanagan gave the world yet another Carrie adaptation in 2026; that one is decidedly more modern, and sure, intentionally doesn’t feel dated in the minor ways the original book (and the first adaptation, directed by Brian De Palma) might.

Like Carrie, Rage was a very early Stephen King book, being the first he wrote/published under his Richard Bachman pseudonym. And the books have another similarity, being set in high school and following a troubled individual who acts out in some kind of violent way, though Rage is a lot more realistic, and without supernatural elements, since that character’s a teenage boy who brings a gun to school and….

…Well, you do the math. He doesn’t go on a full-on rampage, but he commits some violence and then threatens to do more, which is where much of the tension and intentional discomfort of Rage comes in. This one’s undeniably controversial, though, since it was forward-thinking on one hand, being written before such violence in schools was as big a problem, but then a story about such a topic at all, and handled in this way, is uncomfortable in some potentially unintended ways. King himself seems to be of the opinion that Rage has aged poorly, since it’s the only Stephen King book that’s deliberately out of print (and therefore a little harder than usual to find a physical copy of).

The content is probably the biggest factor towards The Stand being a somewhat controversial Stephen King book, but then again, it is such a huge book (especially the uncut version from 1990). Also, this epic was written a while ago, so with more pages, there are going to be more opportunities for offense to be caused, and then with it being nearly half a century old, things might well be expected to feel dated.

It’s a book with a huge build-up to a conflict that involves the fate of what’s left of the world, and there’s also a lot of world-building, including some iffy stuff in terms of race and gender roles (to keep things vague, and not get bogged down in details). There’s also an extremity to some of the content of The Stand which is admittedly alarming, but at least some of it’s understandable. If you're looking at controversy more broadly, there is also the elephant in the room that is the ending to consider, but before then, and in terms of stuff that is technically dated, sure, you get a little of it throughout The Stand.

On the topic of absolutely incredible Stephen King books that are also incredibly long and quite controversial in parts, here’s IT, which has that infamous element involved in the ending, and it can’t be detailed here, but if you know, you know. As far as the basics of this one go, it’s hard to summarize the whole thing broadly, but essentially, IT is about a terrifying entity that takes on the form of all sorts of creatures and beings, including Pennywise the Clown.

It has been revealed that and that being terrorizes people in that unbelievably cursed town of Derry every 27 years, with the main characters fighting the titular villain as kids and then as adults 27 years later, with the timelines flipped between throughout the entirety of the novel. It’s thrilling stuff, but you do get a lot here that’s very eyebrow-raising, some of it intentional and some perhaps not so much (including that aspect of the ending, which is hard to overlook and even harder to describe while keeping things “safe for work,” so to speak).
