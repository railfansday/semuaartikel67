Boston City Council slams Thanksgiving Day narrative taught in American schools.

The Boston City Council recognized Thanksgiving as a "National Day of Mourning" ahead of the holiday, continuing a recurring acknowledgment from city leaders as nearby Plymouth, home of the "first Thanksgiving," battles activists in court over their Indigenous peoples observances.

It has been revealed that last week, the Boston City Council passed a resolution designating Thursday, Nov. 26, as a "National Day of Mourning" in Boston after it was introduced by Councilor Benjamin Weber and co-sponsored by 11 other councilors. While the resolution carries no legal weight or changes to city operations, it reflects an annual tradition for the Boston City Council, which has passed similar ceremonial recognitions ahead of Thanksgiving in previous years.

"The story of Thanksgiving that so many of us grew up learning in school fails to acknowledge the devastating consequences that colonialization had and continues to have on Indigenous peoples, " Boston City Councilor At-Large Julia Mejia said in favor of the resolution during a city council meeting on Nov. 18.

"I think it's crucial that we recognize that these are not simply injustices of the past," Mejia continued. "And as a city, we have a responsibility to ensure that our commitment to equity and justice includes the Indigenous peoples whose lands we live on.".

Meanwhile, in nearby Plymouth — the famed site of the 1621 "first Thanksgiving" feast between the Pilgrims and the Wampanoag people, where the United American Indians of Newly introduced England (UAINE) has held the annual National Day of Mourning every year since 1970 — town officials and organizers appeared in court Monday over a dispute about this year's event logistics.

The legal battle centers on a 1998 settlement, signed after a clash between police and demonstrators the year prior, that requires Plymouth to make a mobile stage available for the event.

According to CBS Boston, town officials now say they will not set up a stage on state property without proof of insurance or an outside vendor, citing liability concerns, while UAINE argues the demand violates the decades-old agreement.

Recent findings suggest that the Plymouth Independent reported that Plymouth Superior Court Judge Brian Glenny took the arguments under advisement Monday, leaving a ruling pending just days before Thursday's 57th annual gathering on Cole's Hill. According to the outlet, Glenny did not express when a ruling might come, though lawyers involved indicated he understands "time is of the essence.".

Industry observers note that however, regardless, a UAINE leader told the Plymouth Independent that the event would continue as planned. "I can assure you we will be there Thursday," Mahtowin Munro told the local outlet.
