Industry News: Waymo halts highway operation nationwide after CA DMV pause

Waymo has pulled its vehicles from highways nationwide after the California Department of Motor Vehicles ordered the company to pause highway operation in the state, following a viral video showing a Waymo trying to pass a police blockade.

Recent findings suggest that waymo is Google’s robotaxi subsidiary, which has been operating autonomously on public roads in some form since 2017. It has now expanded to 15 areas, recently adding another 250 square miles just last month.

Industry observers note that the vehicles can be hailed through Waymo’s app, and operate with no driver in the driver’s seat, 24/7 in the covered areas. Since it can operate at any time but only within geofenced areas, this counts as “level 4” autonomy, as compared to the level 2 autonomy available on most consumer vehicles.

In a recent development, until recently, Waymo only operated on city streets. These are more complex than highways, but lower speed. Accidents are more common on city streets than on highways, but can be more spectacular on highways given the higher speeds involved.

Most driver assist systems have been rolled out gradually, starting with highways, due to this lower complexity. But Waymo started with city streets first, bucking that trend.

The firm started offering rides on highways late last year, but only in limited markets. It had been testing highway operation for several years prior, typically with a driver and with no public ridership.

Everything was going pretty well, with Waymo declaring its autonomous vehicles have stopped 841 injuries over the course of their 271 million miles of operation. And in terms of competition, Waymo is far ahead of Tesla’s Robotaxi effort.

But then, the viral internet stepped in. Last week, a video surfaced showing a Waymo trying to pass two police cars who were trying to slow down traffic on I-280 in Northern California. This is one of the main routes through Silicon Valley, and the opening of highway operations drastically reduced ride times in the area.

The video indicates two police cars behind a construction vehicle, lights on and spread out across the freeway. Then it pans to show a Waymo vehicle moving quickly in the far left lane. One of the officers accelerates and swerves in front of the Waymo vehicle, at which point the Waymo swerves and slows down. (One media citation states that the video seems to have been sped up by the original poster, but we’ve embedded the short local news report on it above).

Sources indicate that in response to this, the California DMV contacted Waymo and ordered the firm to pause highway operations while it examines the incident. The order was made on November 11, but wasn’t reported until today.

It has been revealed that the DMV cited multiple instances of Waymo vehicles incurring into construction zones, not simply the one viral clip above. It mentioned that previous incidents had occurred and been noted, and corrective actions were implemented, but that the incidents keep happening.

As a career computer programmer, I struggle to understand the current level of programming testing. Granted, today’s programming is far more complex than in years gone by and the languages are vastly different (I wonder how many coders today can read a memory dump of their programs), but it sure seems like practices like desk checking, and using carefully constructed test data have disappeared. It seems like the mindset is if the compiler produces error free code , the coding must be good. I recall a COBOL programmer where I worked wrote a procedure with 72 nested IF statements. The compiler generated object code with no errors or warnings, but the program would produce invalid output. Turned out the compiler got to a certain point in the procedure and just generated garbage code.

DMV’s pause will remain until Waymo can demonstrate “effective corrective actions” have been taken.

While the DMV’s order only applies to California highways, Waymo also paused highway operations in other states. It remains operating on city streets in all coverage areas.

Update: Waymo emailed us this statement after it saw this article.

“Safety is at the foundation of everything we do. Following recent freeway construction zone encounters on U.S. 101 in California that did not involve any injuries or collisions, the California DMV has implemented an operating restriction on freeway operations in the state. In alignment with our safety-first principles, we have also temporarily restricted freeway operations across our other operating markets to evaluate our performance. Our ride-hailing service continues to operate safely on surface streets, serving over half a million trips each week. We are working with the California DMV and other state officials diligently and responsibly to resume freeway operations.”.
