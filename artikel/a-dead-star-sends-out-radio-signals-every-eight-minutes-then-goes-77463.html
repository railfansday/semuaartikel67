A dead star sends out radio signals every eight minutes, then goes quiet for a day

A white dwarf beside a Sun-like star sends out radio pulses every 7.9 minutes, the first of its kind found with such a companion.

When a star like the Sun dies, it leaves behind a dense core called a white dwarf. Astronomers found one that sends out a burst of radio waves every 7.9 minutes during a roughly six-hour window.

It’s the first known radio-pulsing white dwarf with a Sun-like companion.

Radio-pulsing white dwarfs have been seen before, but every one with a known partner had a small, dim red dwarf beside it.

According to recent reports, small shifts in when the pulses arrive revealed the radio source moving with the white dwarf. That places the source on or near the star itself.

Sam McSweeney, now a commissioning scientist at the SKA Observatory, led the work.

The research was conducted at the Curtin University node of the International Centre for Radio Astronomy Research in Western Australia, with six colleagues in Australia, Spain, the United States, and Britain.

In an interview with Earth.com, co-author Natasha Hurley-Walker was asked what surprised her most when the team saw the white dwarf moving.

“We were extremely excited!” she said. “We knew immediately that this would be an independent test of our hypothesis that it was the orbital geometry that was causing the radio waves to appear and disappear every 32.5 hours.”.

The source turned up in a survey of the Milky Way with the Murchison Widefield Array, a field of radio antennas in the Western Australian outback.

On December 4, 2024, it produced a run of bright pulses, each under four seconds long and spaced 7.9 minutes apart.

It has been revealed that over the next nine months, the array caught 214 of them. The MeerKAT telescope in South Africa added 33 and fixed the position precisely enough to match it to a star in the Gaia catalog.

Industry observers note that spectra from the W. M. Keck Observatory in Hawaii highlighted that star to be a G-type star. Its surface is near 9,400°F (5,200°C), and it has about 94% of the Sun’s mass.

The system, cataloged as GPM J071507−114153, is roughly 11,000 light-years away, with a wide margin of error.

A pulse every 7.9 minutes is too fast for an ordinary star to spin without flying apart. So the pulsing object has to be something compact: a white dwarf or a neutron star.

Many follow-up observations caught nothing at the predicted times.

When the team sorted the hits and misses, a second rhythm appeared. The pulses are only on for about six hours, and that window comes around every 32.5 hours.

The team interprets the 32.5-hour cycle as the orbital period and the 7.9-minute spacing as the white dwarf’s spin period.

The researchers think the stars’ positions in their orbit determine when the pulses reach Earth.

In a recent development, the timing gave them more. If the radio source orbits with the white dwarf, its distance to Earth changes as it goes around. Its pulses should then arrive a few seconds early on one side of the orbit and late on the other. They did.

Sources indicate that hurley-Walker noted that delay is what let the team say where in the orbit the waves are made. Without it, she said, they could have been coming from the pair’s common center of mass, which doesn’t move.

According to recent reports, the companion’s spectrum demonstrates it swinging at no more than about 110,000 miles per hour (50 kilometers per second). Combined with the delay, that caps the mass of the compact object.

The cap is too low for a neutron star, so the team settled on a white dwarf.

Astronomers call these objects long-period transients, radio sources that switch on and off over minutes to hours. The first one was found in 2021. The paper counts a dozen, four of them confirmed as a white dwarf paired with a red dwarf.

Theorists built their models for how the pulses arise on that pairing, with the red dwarf’s wind and magnetic field feeding the white dwarf.

A G-type companion in a 1.3-day orbit is a counterexample, and the authors wrote that the models will need to stretch to include it.

It has been revealed that hurley-Walker said the find also opens a different door. A Sun-like star outshines a white dwarf in visible light, so pairs like this one are hard for optical telescopes to pick out.

“Radio telescopes can now find these binaries that optical telescopes would have struggled to detect, which gives astronomers a latest window on understanding binary systems,” she told Earth.com.

Industry observers note that the MeerKAT pulses carried another clue. The orientation of the radio waves, known as their polarization, rotated the most when the white dwarf passed behind its companion.

That can happen when radio waves pass through magnetized gas. The pattern resembles what spacecraft have measured near the Sun’s corona.

“What we think is happening is that the radio waves from the white dwarf are passing through the magnetic field of its stellar companion,” Hurley-Walker noted.

Recent findings suggest that the authors left a second explanation open. A mass ejection from the companion could have crossed the line of sight by chance, since only 14 pulses had a usable twist measurement.

How the white dwarf makes the pulses is still unknown. The team has almost no handle on its magnetic field, and the distance to the system is uncertain by thousands of light-years.

The two arrays that found it are both forerunners of the Square Kilometre Array, now being built in Australia and South Africa, and McSweeney is helping commission the Australian half.

Hurley-Walker stated she is designing surveys for it to find more of these systems and to test where they are, how fast they pulse, and how bright they get.

The detection software that found this one, she added, was written by Curtin doctoral student Csanád Horváth.

Until those surveys run, this eight-minute pulse beside a Sun-like star is a single system, and the modelers have to explain it.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Astronomy.
