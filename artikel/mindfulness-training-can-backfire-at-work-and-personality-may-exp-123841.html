Mindfulness training can backfire at work, and personality may explain why

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Mindfulness training may increase negative feelings and rude behavior among employees who already tend to distrust and manipulate others.

Industry observers note that many companies offer mindfulness training in the hope that it will help employees manage stress, get along better and treat each other with kindness. But it doesn’t seem to work that way for everyone.

In a month-long experiment, some workers who practiced mindfulness reported more anger, disgust, and contempt toward coworkers than people with similar personalities in the other groups.

Workers who felt more of this hostility also reported more rudeness at work.

The pattern emerged among employees who scored high on a personality trait called Machiavellianism.

Recent findings suggest that people who score high on this trait tend to distrust others and manipulate them for personal gain. Psychologists group it with two other personality traits under the name dark triad.

In a recent development, previous research has linked mindfulness to greater empathy and helping coworkers. So why did these employees respond so differently?

The study was led by Adam Kay, an assistant professor in the School of Management at the University of South Florida‘s Muma College of Business.

“Mindfulness training makes us more aware of our thoughts and feelings, but that doesn’t necessarily make us better people,” Kay said.

Kay carried out the experiment at a large North American healthcare organization with 12 hospitals, 90 community health sites and 33 other workplaces.

Six hundred employees volunteered, roughly two-thirds of whom were healthcare professionals. Kay randomly divided them into three groups.

The first group followed a 30-day online mindfulness program. It involved five minutes of daily instruction, along with emails and text messages reminding employees to take five mindful breaths, five times a day.

Recent findings suggest that the idea was simple. Notice the colors around you, listen to nearby sounds or feel your feet touching the floor. Then focus on your breathing and notice your feelings without judging them.

According to recent reports, the second group followed a similar breathing exercise without being asked to focus on the present moment. The third group received training later.

After some employees dropped out and others failed attention checks, 473 remained in the study. Before the experiment, they had completed a personality survey containing statements such as “It’s not wise to tell your secrets.”.

Three weeks into the experiment, Kay asked employees how often they had felt anger, disgust and contempt toward their coworkers over the previous two weeks.

For people who scored low on Machiavellianism, it barely mattered which group they belonged to.

It has been revealed that things were different for those with high scores. Employees who practiced mindfulness reported stronger negative feelings than people with similar personalities in the other two groups. The difference was moderate.

According to recent reports, interestingly, people in both training groups felt more mindful afterward. Yet the same rise in negative feelings did not occur among those who simply practiced breathing.

Paying closer attention to their inner experiences may have made the difference.

Sources indicate that after the program ended, employees answered another survey. This time, Kay wanted to know how they had treated their colleagues during the training.

One statement on the survey read, “I ignored or excluded a colleague from professional camaraderie.”.

Psychologists call these small acts of disrespect workplace incivility. They’re often subtle enough that it’s hard to tell whether someone intended to be hurtful.

The more anger, disgust and contempt employees felt, the more rude behavior they reported. This helped explain the link between mindfulness and incivility among workers with Machiavellian traits.

Industry observers note that “Workplace incivility isn’t always obvious,” Kay said. “It usually involves subtle acts of disrespect that are easy to dismiss or deny – but that doesn’t make it harmless.”.

More obvious forms of mistreatment, such as embarrassing someone in front of other coworkers, did not follow the same pattern.

“That workplace incivility can be so damaging yet so difficult to pin on perpetrators may be precisely why it appeals to Machiavellians, who tend to be calculating and reputation-conscious,” Kay said.

Imagine working alongside someone you secretly dislike. Most days, you’re too busy answering emails, attending meetings or finishing your work to give those feelings much thought.

Recent findings suggest that now you spend part of every day paying attention to your thoughts and emotions. You may begin to notice just how much you dislike that person.

According to recent reports, kay suspects something similar happened during the experiment. Mindfulness may not have created those negative feelings. Instead, it may have made existing ones harder to ignore.

The paper even opens with a reference to philosopher Friedrich Nietzsche’s famous observation about staring into an abyss that stares back at you.

Kay considered several other possibilities. Employees were not spending more time dwelling on angry thoughts, hiding their emotions or viewing their colleagues as less than human.

A bad mood didn’t explain the findings either. Neither did the other two dark triad traits, narcissism and psychopathy.

This isn’t the first time researchers have discovered unexpected effects of mindfulness. Earlier research found that it can reduce people’s motivation to repair damaged relationships.

Sources indicate that machiavellianism is a personality trait that exists to varying degrees in the general population. Companies could encounter similar problems in almost any workplace.

Recent findings suggest that kay isn’t suggesting that employers abandon mindfulness training or start testing their staff for certain personality traits. Doing so could raise serious concerns about privacy, fairness and stigma.

Instead, companies could pay closer attention to how employees treat one another after completing a program. Lower stress and better productivity aren’t the only outcomes worth measuring.

In a recent development, programs that teach compassion and ethical behavior alongside mindfulness might help. However, the people who could benefit most from such lessons may also be the least interested in taking them.

There’s an key limitation to this research. Everything Kay learned about the employees’ feelings and behavior came from the employees themselves.

Perhaps mindfulness simply made some workers more willing to admit that they had been rude. Although Kay protected their anonymity, he cannot completely dismiss that possibility.

He considers it less likely because the pattern appeared only among workers with high Machiavellian scores who practiced mindfulness, and only for subtle acts of disrespect.

The experiment also took place at a single healthcare organization and used a commercial mindfulness program that hasn’t been independently tested.

Hospitals have strict rules about professional behavior, which may discourage more obvious forms of hostility.

Future studies could ask coworkers whether they noticed changes in behavior. Researchers could also observe employees directly or test different mindfulness programs.

Recent findings suggest that “Mindfulness training can bring our darker feelings into sharper focus,” Kay said. “And sometimes, it’s our coworkers who pay the price.”.

According to recent reports, learning to recognize our feelings and learning to treat others better are two different things. Mindfulness may help with the first without necessarily improving the second.

The study is published in the Journal of Business Ethics.
