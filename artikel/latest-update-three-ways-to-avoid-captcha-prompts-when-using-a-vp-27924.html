Sources indicate that VPNs make it harder to track your online activity across websites, but it also makes them suspicious about your origins. When you borrow an IP address that hundreds of other people are also using for any number of reasons, you’re much more likely to get repeat verification requests from online firewalls.

That said, you don’t have to keep clicking on those traffic lights over and over. You can simply switch to a more reputable VPN server. Moreover, browsers now offer persistent access tokens to eliminate CAPTCHA requests, while VPNs offer dedicated IPs to keep sites from getting suspicious. Here are three easy fixes for repeat CAPTCHA prompts when using a VPN, so you don’t have to trade convenience for online privacy.

Industry observers note that CAPTCHAs are simple puzzles that websites use to filter out bots and spam. They’re supposed to be easy for people but hard for robots, such as simple object recognition tasks or audio-visual challenges. However, they can still disrupt your browsing experience if they happen too frequently.

Recent findings suggest that most modern CAPTCHA providers, like Google's reCAPTCHA v3 or Cloudflare Turnstile, start working long before they give you a puzzle to solve. It usually starts with a risk score that studies your IP address, browser and device signals, or recent online activity.

If the CAPTCHA provider can’t confidently identify your request as human based on those signals, it requests that you complete a puzzle before deciding if the request should be blocked. That means when you get repeat CAPTCHA requests, there’s something affecting your browser’s risk score. For example, popular VPN servers spread the same IP address across any number of customers, which can easily make a website mistake you for a bot farm. If you change your IP address frequently by switching servers often, that might look suspicious too.

Industry observers note that luckily, there are ways to deal with frequent CAPTCHA requests using tools and services available with your browser and VPN. Cloudflare has already cut its CAPTCHA requests by 91% using its Managed Challenge platform. Apple has also been shipping token-based verification on devices since iOS 16 and macOS Ventura. In June 2026, Cloudflare even confirmed a joint effort called the Private Access Control Tokens (PACT) standard with Chrome, Edge, Firefox, and Shopify.

Industry observers note that while there’s no direct off switch for CAPTCHAs, you can use different fixes to improve your browser’s trust signals when using the internet. That way, you’re less likely to be hounded by verification requests.

The easiest fix for repeat CAPTCHAs is to stop connecting to whichever server your VPN app picks automatically. Proton recommends choosing servers with lower traffic load. In fact, its apps display load as a percentage to help you avoid the most crowded options. I'd also skip massive traffic hubs like Upcoming York or London, since that's where most traffic originates from. Servers in smaller cities located nearby are often just as fast but never as populated.

You should also avoid changing IP addresses too frequently, which can look suspicious on its own. Pick a server that seems to behave well, then continue using it for at least one session. Also remember you’re more likely to get premium server options if you’re on a paid VPN strategy. Major platforms like ExpressVPN, Surfshark, Proton, and Nord are particularly likely to have a larger server collection to pick from.

Private Access Tokens (PAT) let you verify the authenticity of your request using your device rather than an IP address. Apple calls it Automatic Verification, where an Apple server validates your device and Apple Account to assign you an anonymous token.

Sources indicate that to enable Automatic Verification on an iPhone or iPad, go to Settings > [your name] > Sign-In & Security > Automatic Verification. On a Mac, open System Settings > [your name] > Sign-In & Security > Automatic Verification.

Industry observers note that unfortunately, there’s no equivalent feature for Windows, Android, and Linux consumers. That said, Cloudflare offers the Silk extension for Chrome, Firefox, and Edge that serves a similar function. A quick look at the ratings will tell you it’s not as effective, but Cloudflare’s upcoming cross-browser standard called PACT seems promising once it is released.

If you pay a bit extra for a dedicated IP add-on, VPN providers can assign you a unique IP address that isn’t shared with anyone else. For example, NordVPN offers dedicated IPs in 30 countries for $2-5 a month and Surfshark charges $3.75 a month for it.

There are also dedicated providers like Private Internet Access, which charges $3.99 per month for a zero-trust setup so not even their employees know which IP address is assigned to you. On the other hand, Proton VPN offers dedicated IPs only to business plan users, so you’ll need to be on the Workspace Standard, Premium, or Enterprise intends to get one.

That said, there is a trade-off here too. NordVPN itself admits that a dedicated IP makes it easier to link online activity back to a single user. Also, many websites can still tell that the IP address originated from a VPN data center rather than a consumer’s device.
