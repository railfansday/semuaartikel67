Why am I’m Hiking?

So, the question I usually get from people when I tell them I’m hiking the Appalachian Trail is, “Why?”.

There’s a lot of emotion behind that question, and it can lead to very different answers.

I could talk about the people who ask with frustration, disbelief, sadness, or even fear. Maybe they’re worried that the things I normally handle will now fall into their laps. Then there are the people who sound surprised or curious. I may have to explain exactly what backpacking is and what thru-hiking entails. Those conversations lead to a whole different brand of questions. These are the best discussions, and I can go down all kinds of rabbit holes about gear, food, concerns about animals, and where I’m going to sleep.

Sources indicate that the question, though, is still this: Why am I going to hike for six months, leaving a perfectly good couch, bed, wife, career, and a couple of affectionate cats behind? All to be cold, wet, and worn out most days from doing nothing but walking? Miles and miles of walking.

I have to go way back, maybe 14 years ago, when my wife and I were exposing the kids to all kinds of different vacations, ideally the kind that didn’t cost much. For several years, we had been camping at National Parks, enjoying the sights and activities. One of those years, when we were in the Rockies, we saw a young couple leaving camp with their tiny kids, each carrying an even tinier backpack. We remembered a couple of backpacking trips we’d taken after we got married but before we had kids. The gear had been sitting on a shelf for years, but at least we still had some.

“Let’s take the kids next year!”.

The next year, we suited up. Our kids now had their own tiny packs. We had food, fuel, and ponchos in case it rained.

Did I mention we were on a small section of the Appalachian Trail in the Smoky Mountains?

It rained for three days straight before we even started our hike, and then it rained for the three days we were on the trail. Thankfully, we stayed in shelters, but there were a lot of lessons learned. Lessons about food, clothing, footwear, water treatment and yes, raingear. Still, I came off the trail with a huge smile. Can’t say that about the kids, but for me, I had a blast! Spoiler – both my kids are now young adults and camp, sometimes willingly but I’m getting distracted.

Industry observers note that the biggest lesson for me came when we got off the trail. My daughter pointed to the large sign behind me that mentioned “Appalachian Trail” which told me about its more than 2,150 miles of hiking that people tackle every year.

Pretty sure my response was, “That’s ridiculous. No one can do that.” The food, if nothing else, would weigh hundreds of pounds, not to mention all the fuel we’d need to boil water. It would take an Ironman-caliber person just to carry all of that.

Recent findings suggest that somewhere around there, I started going down the rabbit hole of research. Resupply every few days? Okay. That’s feasible. Water filters? That would save a lot of time compared to hours of boiling water (which, yes, we had been doing). Lighter-weight gear? I could do that.

I fell in love with the concept of it.

Industry observers note that so why? Why would I spent 6 months in the woods hiking?

It has been revealed that it wasn’t the romantic feel of trail life (though a lot of trail journals do talk very positively about it). I could talk about my love of nature and wanting to be out in the woods, watching the slow change of the seasons. That isn’t completely it. I can do that from my bedroom window. I could mention making newly introduced friends or reducing the clutter in my life. I could learn that last lesson by just going to my parents’ house and looking at the hoarding nightmare in their basement that’s slowly becoming my problem. All key things and outcomes I’ve read about from other hikers.

Sources indicate that no, I think for me, I fell in love with the idea of an adventure. One that would be within my grasp as a middle-aged, desk-jockey engineer who really needed some outdoor time to recharge his batteries before returning to the world of engineering work. Not quite ready for retirement, but not wanting to spend all my years letting my glutes go to crap while I sit in a chair and pound on a keyboard, arguing over engineering details with team members spread out across the globe while we work toward a common, fresh design.

In a recent development, I don’t picture it as some grand adventure of heading off at sunrise, marching to Mordor with a ring, or even trekking through the wilderness to find myself. But I do want to have an adventure that, as I get older, I can fondly stare off into space remembering… remembering the tough days, the worn-out feelings, the beautiful views, the green tunnel, latest friendships, and new experiences.

Industry observers note that that’s why I’m heading out to the woods for six months. It’s not to run away from legal trouble (I don’t even have any overdue parking tickets). It’s not to flee from my job (I actually do like what I’m doing, but after thirty-plus years, a break would be nice). It’s not to escape from my family (I happen to like them, too. Well, most of the time, but I’m sure that feeling is mutual).

Recent findings suggest that so, if I had to state the biggest, single reason, it’s because I’m looking to have an adventure, one where the journey is the destination. And if I can make it to Katahdin with a bunch of new friends and great stories, all the better.

About me? Sure. I’m a native Detroiter and have lived my whole life in Michigan where I live with my wife – more than 25 years – and a couple of college-aged children. Well, technically the kids live off at college but they do come home to make the house look a natural disaster struck while we weren’t looking. I’m an engineer, working for one of the Substantial Three automakers and have well over 30 years of experience doing everything from breaking transmissions to quality improvements and even designing cooling systems for both trucks and cars. Outside of work, I love exploring and spending time outdoors. I’ve learned a lot about camping over the last few decades, including relearning wonderful lessons like not leaving an unlocked cooler in camp and proper flow directions on my water filter. Somewhere in there, I fell in love with backpacking and found a whole latest world of mistakes I can learn from. Eventually, I discovered long-distance thru-hiking and convinced both my job and my family that taking a vacation from me for six months would be beneficial to them (suckers!). Catch me on a good day, and I’d be happy to talk about camping, backpacking, video games, books, music or just about anything else fun. Just don’t ask me about spreadsheets, checklists, FMEAs or change control meetings.
