📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a recent development, prostate cancer is the second leading cause of cancer deaths in men, so you’d think doctors would be adamant about screening guys for it. Yet for years, prostate cancer screening via the PSA (prostate-specific antigen) test has been plagued by vague recommendations, and some back-and-forth messaging that flipped between “get it” and “the benefits might not be worth it.” Until recently, organizations including the American Cancer Society (ACS) used to give a recommendation that at age 50, guys should talk with their doctors about screening, then engage in a process of “shared decision-making” about whether to get the test.

Many guys didn’t know whether they should get it or not. Without a clear recommendation, it was easy for some to think this PSA blood test wasn’t very important to do at all. The landscape has changed.

It has been revealed that the American Cancer Society just came out with upcoming guidelines that for the first time, unequivocally say that men should get the screening test. Guys at average risk should start at age 50. Guys at higher risk (more on that later) should start at 45.

Why that’s a significant deal: “The new guideline not only gets rid of shared decision-making, which is really difficult to execute, is subject to bias, and is really time-intensive,” says Yaw Nyame, MD, of the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center at the University of Washington Medical Center. “But it couples that with a better understanding of what present-day prostate cancer screening is.”.

Sources indicate that today, the PSA test is “just the first step, not the final answer,” claims William Dahut, MD, chief scientific officer at ACS, and a contributing author to the guidelines. “It is a simple blood test, and an elevated result does not automatically mean cancer of the need for a biopsy.” Many more tools including MRIs and biomarkers help physicians drill down on who needs additional testing. “Our goal is to find prostate cancer earlier, while avoiding unnecessary procedures and treatment,” Dr. Dahut adds.

What changed that prompted the revised recommendations? “It’s key to understand that guidance evolves as the evidence does,” says Dr. Dahut. Research has piled up to indicate that PSA screening reduces prostate cancer deaths.

Industry observers note that “We do not need to make a disclaimer that maybe you should, maybe you shouldn’t do it. Everyone should do it and you should start early, especially if you have a higher risk of cancer,” notes Dr. Nyame, who was on the expert panel that reviewed the new ACS screening guidelines.

Sources indicate that while all men should get the test, according to the fresh guidelines, it’s not a “one size fits all” screening recommendation, Dr. Nyame explains. Recommendations vary according to risk. Find your category and screen according to that.

Sources indicate that other prostate screening guidelines came out earlier in 2026 from the American Urological Association (AUA) and the Society for Urologic Oncology (SUO) that had slightly different recommendations for the age at which you should start screening. What gives? The difference in recommended age and frequency of screening “has to do with how the guideline creators are viewing the different studies that they’re using to support their decision making,” Dr. Nyame claims.

Sources indicate that it doesn’t mean guys should throw up their hands and do nothing in frustration. “The AUA/SUO and ACS guidelines recognize the benefit of knowing a PSA test result earlier in life. A PSA test in the 40- to 50-year age range is a good predictor of the likelihood someone has or will develop a clinically significant prostate cancer,” states James A. Eastham, MD, chief of the urology service, Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center. A higher level early in life is a strong signal that you need to be really diligent about checking your PSA.

“We’ve watched prostate cancer incidence increase—especially metastatic cancer, for over a decade now,” claims Dr. Nyame. “And to know that we have a guideline that is so action-oriented toward reversing that course is really exciting.”.
