Physicists just sent an unblockable message through a brick wall using gravity

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Researchers at the University of Queensland have demonstrated an unusual fresh way to communicate, using gravity to send a message directly through a brick wall. Instead of relying on radio waves or other electromagnetic signals, the team used an old elevator, a massive counterweight, and an extremely sensitive instrument capable of detecting tiny changes in gravitational pull.

Recent findings suggest that the experiment took place inside UQ's Parnell Building. Physicists repeatedly moved an elevator between two floors, shifting its 1.8-metric-ton counterweight between two positions. Each position represented a binary digit, either 0 or 1, allowing the researchers to encode information through the movement of the heavy mass.

Recent findings suggest that on the other side of a brick wall, a device called a gravimeter was positioned in a nearby bathroom. The instrument measured the extremely small changes in gravity produced by the moving counterweight. By decoding those measurements, the team successfully recovered a one-word message: 'Gravity'.

Why Gravity Could Succeed Where Wi-Fi Fails.

The experiment highlights a potentially crucial advantage of using gravity for communication. Unlike conventional wireless signals, which can be weakened or blocked by physical obstacles, gravitational fields pass through ordinary materials without being screened out.

Sources indicate that dr. Charles Woffinden from UQ's School of Mathematics and Physics explained why this property makes gravity an intriguing alternative to existing communication technologies.

"Unlike the electromagnetic signals used in modern wireless communication, a gravitational signal cannot simply be screened out by placing ordinary material in its path," Dr. Woffinden remarked.

"Anyone who has lost phone reception in a tunnel or struggled with Wi-Fi through a solid wall will understand the appeal.

"While the present system is far from practical, it demonstrates the possibility of communicating using a method that ordinary physical barriers cannot block.".

Sources indicate that the basic principle is surprisingly simple. Any object with mass produces a gravitational field, and moving that object changes the field around it. Although these changes are generally far too small for people to notice, sufficiently sensitive instruments can detect them. By controlling how a large mass moves, researchers can create a sequence of gravitational changes that represents digital information.

From Weighing Trucks to Sending Messages.

The idea for the experiment emerged from a separate research effort that had little to do with communication.

According to recent reports, dr. Andrew Groszek noted the team had originally been developing a contactless weighbridge, a system designed to measure the weight of vehicles without requiring them to drive onto a conventional scale.

"We were aiming to determine the mass of trucks and other large vehicles by measuring their tiny gravitational pull," Dr. Groszek mentioned.

"Once we had shown that the idea worked, we realized the same physics could be used to encode and transmit information.".

Recent findings suggest that that realization opened the door to a different application. If a sensitive detector could measure the gravitational influence of a large vehicle, it could also detect deliberately controlled changes in the position of a heavy object. Those changes could then be used to represent the ones and zeros that form the foundation of digital communication.

Despite its success, the demonstration revealed a major obstacle to practical gravity-based communication: transmitting information this way is extraordinarily slow.

Professor Andrew White explained that the elevator's age and limited speed placed a severe restriction on how rapidly the researchers could send their message.

"Our lift was snail-paced - it's from the 1950s - so our experiment transmitted just 1 bit per minute, meaning it took an hour to send a single word," Professor White said.

According to recent reports, for comparison, a bit is the smallest unit of digital information, representing either a 0 or a 1. Modern communication systems transmit enormous numbers of bits every second. The gravity experiment, by contrast, required an entire minute to communicate just one bit.

According to White, improving the system would require finding ways to produce detectable gravitational changes more quickly.

"The speed of gravity communication would come down to how substantial the mass is, how fast you can move it, and the sensitivity of the detector.

"It took more than 100 years to go from the first telegraph system to the mobile phone, so while gravity communication is not practical today, it could be in the future.".

Could Quantum Sensors Make Gravity Communication Practical?

Although the experiment relied on a conventional gravity sensor, the researchers believe more advanced technology could eventually make the approach faster and capable of operating over greater distances.

Professor Matthew Davis emphasized that the team's most notable achievement was demonstrating that gravity could be used to transmit recoverable information.

"It's not often that you get to demonstrate an entirely fresh way of communicating," Professor Davis remarked.

"We have shown experimentally that information can be encoded in the gravitational field and recovered at the other end.

"Our experiment used a classical gravity sensor, but our modeling indicates that quantum sensors could, with rather ambitious assumptions, support ranges of up to about 500 meters and data rates of around 15 bits per minute.".

Quantum sensors exploit properties of quantum physics to make exceptionally precise measurements. In principle, their greater sensitivity could allow researchers to detect much smaller gravitational changes, potentially improving both the range and speed of gravity-based communication.

However, the team's projections depend on ambitious assumptions, and the suggested performance has not yet been demonstrated experimentally. Even at 15 bits per minute, the system would remain dramatically slower than modern wireless technologies.

It has been revealed that still, the potential applications are intriguing. A sufficiently advanced gravity-based communication system could someday transmit information in environments where radio waves, light, and other conventional signals cannot penetrate.

For now, an aging elevator and a gravimeter in a university bathroom have demonstrated something remarkable: information can be carried by changes in gravity and recovered on the other side of a solid wall.

The research was published in Science Advances.
