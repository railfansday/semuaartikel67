Industry News: Oil Prices Are Making Fashion’s Polyester Addiction Even Uglier

🌐 Source: Vogue • 📅 19 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 6 min read (1274 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

  • According to recent reports, for more than half a century, virgin polyester has been the bedrock of clothing production.
  • The effects are already being felt further down the supply chain.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Fashion’s reliance on cheap synthetic fibers is becoming an increasingly expensive gamble as oil prices soar.

According to recent reports, for more than half a century, virgin polyester has been the bedrock of clothing production. But conflict in the Middle East is exposing the vulnerability of this business model that previously took advantage of relatively stable polyester prices. Now, ongoing disruption in the global energy market has shaken the industry. Brent crude averaged $124 a barrel in October, its highest level since July 2022, when prices averaged $127 a barrel in inflation-adjusted terms, according to the US Energy Information Administration (EIA). Meanwhile, shipping disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz have also hit supplies of naphtha, an upstream feedstock for polyester production, and monoethylene glycol (MEG), an essential ingredient for the fiber. Imports of MEG into major Asian markets (where the vast majority of polyester is produced) fell by roughly 50% in the five months following the disruption, compared with the same period a year earlier, according to oil market analysts OPIS.

The effects are already being felt further down the supply chain. Polyester staple fiber prices in China rose by 22% in the third quarter of 2026 and by 34% since the start of the year, according to Lenzing, the Austrian fiber manufacturer behind Tencel and Ecovero. In April, the Japanese textile manufacturer Teijin Frontier, a supplier to Uniqlo parent Fast Retailing, stated that it would be raising prices for polyester fibers and spun yarns by 20% or more in an emergency price revision.

According to Textile Exchange, polyester accounts for 59% of global fiber production, and despite incremental gains in the total amount of recycled polyester produced (from 9.3 million tonnes in 2024 to 9.7 million tonnes in 2025), its overall market share is shrinking compared to virgin. “Polyester has fueled the exponential rise in the amount of textiles that has happened over the last few decades, especially in hyper-fast fashion,” indicates Katelyn Toth-Fejel, senior lecturer in fashion and sustainability at London College of Fashion. “Any business built on polyester is going to [feel] every oil shock.”.

Nike is one of several major retailers to flag the risk in its 2026 annual report, warning that volatility in commodity and raw-material prices, including petroleum derivatives, “has had and could have a material adverse effect on our costs, gross margins and profitability”. Earlier this year, Next also warned that persistent fuel and fabric cost spikes could hike retail prices by up to 10%, depending on the extent of permanent damage to global energy infrastructure.

In a recent development, rising polyester prices and instability come as many customers have turned against the fabric, becoming wary of synthetics replacing natural fibers throughout their wardrobes. Toth-Fejel indicates shoppers are more aware of the harms of microplastics. Global volatility is now adding to that consumer pressure, she says, pushing fashion to reduce its overreliance on polyester, whether virgin or recycled.

For brands and manufacturers operating on tight margins, rising prices present a difficult choice: absorb higher production costs, negotiate with (already squeezed) suppliers, or pass increases on to customers. Those that have already invested in recycled materials may be better placed to weather the shock, turning an earlier sustainability decision into a commercial advantage.

According to recent reports, previously, recycled alternatives have struggled to compete with virgin materials on price. According to commodity price reporting agency Argus, in north-west Europe, recycled PET flakes (plastic fragments from shredded containers) were €280 a tonne more expensive than virgin PET resin on average in 2025. By April 2026, however, they were €300 a tonne cheaper, as disruption to Middle Eastern supplies pushed up virgin PET prices. These figures relate to PET feedstock rather than finished textile fibers, but illustrate how promptly geopolitical shocks can change the economics of recycled materials.

In a recent development, british brand Superdry started integrating recycled polyester into its outerwear in 2020, and it now features across all padded outerwear, from shells and interlinings to fillings. Head of sustainability Martyn Jennings claims the brand initially paid a premium for recycled polyester, but has worked with suppliers to achieve cost parity. “The volatility of virgin polyester has literally zero impact on our sourcing strategy [now],” he says.

Superdry’s FW26 Everest Bomber Jacket is made from recycled polyester.

According to recent reports, likewise, around 90% of the polyester outdoor brand The North Face uses in apparel is made with recycled content, according to parent company VF Corporation. VF states recycled polyester costs have historically been more stable than virgin alternatives.

Sources indicate that speaking at Textile Exchange’s annual conference in Vancouver last month, VF’s vice president of global sustainability, responsible trade and government affairs, Sean Cady, explained the thinking behind the strategy. “We made that choice for carbon reduction and sustainability reasons, and to build price and supply resilience into our supply chain. This has enabled us to continue sourcing recycled polyester at very profitable prices, without having to deal with the volatility of virgin polyester.”.

According to recent reports, not all brands see switching to recycled polyester as a price play. For Montreal-based manufacturer Gildan, diversifying fiber inputs is about maintaining flexibility as supply conditions change, rather than necessarily securing cheaper materials. Greater use of recycled fibers offers “greater sourcing optionality” as “markets evolve”, claims executive vice-president and chief commercial officer Chuck Ward. “Recycled polyester can still command a premium over virgin polyester and be susceptible to energy costs that are inherent to the recycling process. We do not necessarily see this [investment] through a price stability lens.”.

The production line for wholesale apparel manufacturer Gildan.

Sources indicate that adidas chief executive Bjørn Gulden made a similar point on the company’s first-quarter 2026 earnings call. “When oil prices, and I would say fresh polyester, are going up in price, the market for recycling coincidentally goes up the same amount,” he mentioned.

Recycled polyester may offer some protection from oil-price volatility, but it does little to remove fashion’s broader reliance on fossil fuels.

In a recent development, toth-Fejel cautions that recycled polyester is not immune to rising production costs. It accounted for just 11.8% of global polyester production in 2025, according to Textile Exchange’s 2026 Materials Market Report. Around 97% of recycled polyester is still made from PET plastic bottles, while textile-to-textile recycling accounts for just 3%. Its feedstock might be less exposed to oil-price fluctuations, but processing, energy and transport still carry significant costs. “It is still quite expensive because it is a very energy-intensive process,” she states, and most of the industry has been slow to decarbonize.

In a recent development, there are also questions about whether plastic bottles are best used to make clothing. Once turned into textiles, the material can be harder to recycle again. Plus, much of today’s recycled polyester comes from bottles that could otherwise enter bottle-to-bottle recycling systems. European requirements for greater recycled content in plastic bottles could put further pressure on supplies, making competition for feedstock another constraint as demand grows, she adds. Textile-to-textile recycling could offer a more direct route to circularity, but this is still several years away from commercial scale.

Beyond recycling, retailers are also exploring other alternatives to diversify their raw material base, from bio-based synthetics made using renewable feedstocks to regenerated cellulosic fibers such as lyocell.

For Toth-Fejel, the problem runs deeper than brands’ choice of fiber. Polyester has helped underpin a fashion business model built on producing ever-greater volumes of clothing at ever-lower costs. Replacing virgin polyester with recycled alternatives does little to challenge those underlying economics, she argues. “There is no easy solution if that’s what your business is built on.”.
