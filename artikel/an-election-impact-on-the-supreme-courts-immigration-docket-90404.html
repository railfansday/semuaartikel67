An election impact on the Supreme Court’s immigration docket

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The effect of this month’s elections is likely to impact immigration policy over the coming years as politicians and political analysts assess how reactions to the Trump administration’s immigration law enforcement tactics affected voters’ preferences. But the significant shift towards Democrats in states previously controlled by Republicans is likely to impact immigration law even sooner. The Supreme Court is at the moment considering whether to add to its calendar a case about the constitutionality of an immigration law enacted by Iowa in 2024. The justices had previously signaled their openness to weighing in, but the state’s Republican attorney general, who asked the court to hear the case, lost her reelection bid to a Democrat, casting doubt on the case’s future.

Industry observers note that iowa’s Senate File 2340 criminalizes certain immigration conduct. For example, anyone who has previously been excluded or deported from the United States by federal immigration officials but “is at any time found” in Iowa can be convicted of an aggravated misdemeanor and imprisoned for up to two years. As an alternative to potential conviction, the law allows a state judge overseeing a criminal prosecution to order the defendant to leave the United States. Anyone who fails to leave as required can be convicted of a felony and imprisoned for as much as 10 years.

The state’s law, enacted in April 2024, was designed to closely track federal immigration law. The Immigration and Nationality Act permits criminal prosecution of migrants who are found in the United States after having previously been removed. It also allows immigration judges to order a migrant to depart the United States. By resembling federal law, Senate File 2340’s proponents hoped to assist the federal government’s efforts to penalize and remove from the United States people who had violated federal immigration law.

It has been revealed that in two separate lawsuits, challengers questioned the law’s constitutionality. An advocacy group based in Des Moines, the Iowa Migrant Movement for Justice, and two Iowa residents who had previously been removed from the United States filed one lawsuit, while the Justice Department, during President Joe Biden’s tenure, filed the other. In June 2024, the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of Iowa sided with the challengers in both cases. Since Senate File 2340 regulates immigration, it treads on an area of law that is the responsibility of the federal government, U.S. District Judge Stephen Locher concluded. As the Supreme Court explained in a 2012 decision, Arizona v. United States, involving Arizona’s attempt to enact its own immigration laws, “The federal power to determine immigration policy is well settled.”.

In a recent development, in addition, any time that Congress enacts legislation in an area in which it is authorized to do so, including immigration, the supremacy clause of the U.S. Constitution prioritizes federal law over state law. “Under this principle, Congress has the power to preempt state law,” the Supreme Court explained in Arizona. By enacting a detailed, complicated set of immigration laws, including criminal offenses related to migration, “Congress ‘left no room’ for state regulation” of migration, Locher explained. Since Senate File 2340 attempts to regulate migration, it is likely preempted. Locher thus blocked Iowa from enforcing it.

Recent findings suggest that within days, Iowa’s attorney general, Republican Brenna Bird, appealed the district court’s decision to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 8th Circuit. While the case was pending before the 8th Circuit, President Trump returned to the White House, and the Justice Department asked the district court to dismiss its lawsuit. Despite the federal government’s shifting position, Senate File 2340 did not fare well before the 8th Circuit. Deciding Bird’s appeal of the case brought by the Iowa Migrant Movement for Justice and the two Iowa residents, the circuit court concluded that each section of Senate File 2340 conflicts with federal immigration law and is therefore likely preempted.

It has been revealed that having lost at the 8th Circuit, Bird asked the Supreme Court to weigh in. Iowa’s law should be allowed to go into effect because it merely “mirrors federal law,” Bird told the justices in her request for review last July. Unsurprisingly, Iowa Migrant Movement for Justice and the two residents who challenged the law urged the justices not to add this case to their calendar. They argued that the 8th Circuit applied the law in line with other federal courts that have grappled with similar disputes, so the court’s intervention is unnecessary.

Last month, the justices signaled that they were more interested in this case, Bird v. Iowa Migrant Movement for Justice, than most others vying for their attention. On Oct. 19, the court invited the solicitor general – the federal government’s top lawyer before the Supreme Court – to explain whether the Trump administration thinks the court should become involved. Calling for the solicitor general’s view is a key sign that the justices are closely considering whether to add the case to their calendar. Of the thousands of cases that the court is asked to review every year, the justices request the solicitor general’s view in a small handful – “around 10 or 11 times every term,” according to SCOTUSblog contributor John Elwood. For that reason, invitations to the solicitor general “serve as powerful signals that a petition has attracted the court’s serious attention,” Elwood explained.

The solicitor general has not yet responded to the court’s invitation, but there is reason to believe it will ask the court to grant Iowa’s request – or would have absent this month’s electoral swing. By voluntarily dismissing its lawsuit last year, the Trump administration removed the federal government from stopping Iowa’s enforcement of Senate File 2340. Since then, it has left no doubt that it stands with Iowa. After the 8th Circuit concluded that Iowa could not enforce the law, the Justice Department urged the full court to rehear the case and side with Iowa. “[T]he panel opinion misapplied preemption principles to affirm the district court’s preliminary injunction,” the Justice Department told the 8th Circuit in an amicus brief that it filed in the Iowa Migrant Movement for Justice’s case.

But the results of this month’s elections may have dimmed the prospects of Supreme Court review. Bird lost her reelection bid to Democrat Nate Willems. Though Willems has not publicly taken a position on Bird’s defense of Senate File 2340, in an interview he accused her of having “filed too many of what I think of as political lawsuits.” He instead said he would focus on “times when there is a real threat to Iowans or a real harm,” and promised to boost the attorney general’s work on behalf of consumers, workers, and the environment. Willems’ Democratic colleague Rob Sand won the state’s gubernatorial election. Sand also has not publicly explained his position on Senate File 2340, but with Democrats holding two of the highest offices in state government, they are likely to align with each other.

Sources indicate that once sworn in as Iowa’s next attorney general, Willems will be able to move forward with or drop Bird’s defense of Senate File 2340. Under the court’s rules, a case will be dismissed when both parties agree. Since Iowa Migrant Movement for Justice and the state’s two residents who challenged the law have indicated that they do not want the court to intervene, all that is left is for Willems to make his view known. Having already lost in two courts, Senate File 2340 remains alive only thanks to Bird’s insistence that those judges misunderstood the state’s role in punishing migrants. But if he wants, Willems can turn the attorney general’s office’s attention to other matters, providing yet another reason why elections matter.

César Cuauhtémoc García Hernández is the author of three books about immigration law and policy.
