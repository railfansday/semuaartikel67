Tech Report: Saudi pop music is thriving — but for the right reasons?

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that today, rapper Asayel Bishi is one of Saudi Arabia's rising hip-hop stars, signed to the country's biggest record label for latest music, MDLBEAST. But not so long ago, she was making international headlines for a totally different reason.

In a recent development, in 2020, after Bishi released a music video for a song, "Bint Mecca," or "girl from Mecca," featuring people dancing in a local cafe, Mecca's governor called for the arrest of Bishi's whole production team. He said the video violated the holy city's local customs and traditions. In the end, the situation was resolved with a discussion about filming permits.

And by early 2023, Bishi was signed to MDLBEAST and has since racked up hundreds of thousands of streams on music platforms like Spotify.

In the past, other Saudi rappers have ended up in prison for similar, alleged transgressions. The fact that Bishi's story went a different way is probably partially because the 31-year-old's career path is on a similar trajectory to the Saudi pop music industry as a whole.

As part of Saudi ruler Mohammed bin Salman's Vision 2030 blueprint, unveiled in 2016 and which aims to move the country away from economic dependence on oil, the country has poured billions into the local music industry.

Industry observers note that the pace of development increased in 2019 after the country held its first major festival, Soundstorm, an annual event that now draws major international acts and over 400,000 guests. MDLBEAST was created then, primarily as the festival organizer. Since then, it has also become a record label, radio station, distributor, promoter and talent incubator.

A recent report for the Saudi Music Commission suggests the music sector could be worth $500 million (€455 million) by 2034. But it's difficult to figure out how well Saudi pop is really doing now because independent monitoring of the sector only began relatively recently.

Streaming platform Spotify reports that people listening to Saudi-made music inside Saudi Arabia increased by 195% between 2020 and 2024. However, the most-streamed artists inside the country are still international.

Although there was a mostly-hidden Saudi music scene before this recent boom, observers say the growth of the Saudi music sector is not organic.

It's all about building an industry from the top down, Amr Abdelrahim, a researcher at Sciences Po in Paris, reported in a 2024 study. "After 2016, young middle-class musicians and creative professionals, who had been careful to avoid drawing official attention to their countercultural activities suddenly became central to the ambitions of the state," he wrote.

The careers of young Saudi acts — like Bishi, for example — "became tied to the destiny of the crown prince," the researcher explained.

The Saudi royal family has long dispersed money from Saudi oil sales to their subjects, in order to ensure economic dependence and loyalty. But, Abdelrahim argued, "what is new about Saudi investments in culture under MBS is an unprecedented degree of centralization and the emergence of new clientelist networks around the country's de-facto ruler," using a common abbreviation for Crown Prince Mohammed.

Those involved "are inadvertently participating in the consolidation of the Saudi regime," he argued.

Observers say a lot of the upcoming music businesses are vague about their ties to the state. But it's clear they couldn't work without the state's financial support and approval.

In a recent development, "Almost all major events are organized by the state or funded by closely linked entities," a spokesperson from Saudi-focused organization, ALQST for Human Rights, told DW. "This affects the type of music produced, as musicians have to be mindful of what is acceptable. This is not just about the music they produce, but also how they act and what they say to their fans.".

Sources indicate that ALQST believes Saudi musicians have even less artistic freedom under the fresh musical system than before, when they would have played or performed privately. They can lose their contracts and livelihoods, be prevented from performing or even arrested, ALQST's spokesperson said.

In a recent development, "In the past, the music scene was not regulated," explained the guitarist and bassist for Saudi black metal band, Al-Namrood. But now that it is, the state can more closely monitor musicians who once stayed hidden and only played privately, the musician, who goes by the name of Mephisto, told DW.

According to recent reports, al-Namrood are one of the rare examples of a Saudi act that isn't working with state support. The group first formed in 2008 but have never played at home and have kept their identities secret for almost two decades.

"The exchange is clear: You want to perform, it has to be under our rules," Mephisto continued. "And I totally understand the perspective [of some musicians]. But typically the artist must refrain from any criticism of the religious or authoritarian. [The state] doesn't necessarily buy an artist's loyalty, it just creates incentives that make criticism increasingly costly.".

Industry observers note that for Al-Namrood, who have often condemned Saudi conservatism, religion and politics in their music, that's not an option. "We don't want anything to do with them," Mephisto remarked.

In her 2020 paper for the journal, Arabian Humanities, Nora Derbal, a professor at the University of Hamburg, noted Saudi artists told her that if they got a call from authorities asking them to participate in a government project, it would be an offer they couldn't turn down.

The artists she spoke with were debating "their cooptation and loss of autonomy, and about the consequences for not joining in state projects," she wrote.

On the other hand, in various interviews, Saudi acts who have partaken in their country's pop music renaissance have also been positive: They don't have to hide anymore, they can play for larger audiences and they are paid. In an interview, one Saudi DJ described the first Soundstorm festival as like "Berlin after the fall of the wall" while others said they felt they were making history.

Recent findings suggest that "So that's the interesting paradox," said Fannie Agerschou-Madsen, a postdoctoral researcher at the University of Roskilde in Denmark, who's written several papers on the topic. "We have all these social liberalizations and challenges to conservative norms but at the same time that's not being followed by political liberalization.".

It has been revealed that but that doesn't mean that some room isn't being created for meaningful change, Agerschou-Madsen told DW. Locals told her they enjoyed the newfound freedom of going to a dance party although, she noted, the subversiveness seemed to be more directed at the social conservatism than Saudi authorities.

Young Saudis are well aware of the red lines and state control of the music scene and are continually negotiating boundaries, the researcher argued. In that way, "their everyday practices create space for political and social change, little by little, although perhaps not in what you'd consider a typically revolutionary way.".

So is the Saudi state co-opting local musicians, including those who might have said something controversial before they ever get a chance to?

Industry observers note that A DW enquiry to MDLBEAST was not answered. And the answer likely lies somewhere in the middle.

According to recent reports, for some young Saudis, the newly introduced music scene has been about fun, self-expression, belonging and creativity, Agerschou-Madsen wrote in a new, as-yet-unpublished paper shared with DW. "Others, however, view it as a top-down project that commodifies fun while sidestepping deeper concerns of political freedom and social equity.".
