Is It Rude to Ask Guests to Bring Food for Thanksgiving Dinner?

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, at its best, Thanksgiving is an opportunity to gather around and share our appreciation for one another through contribution and collaboration. But let’s be honest, any holiday can be a minefield, and there’s always something fresh to navigate—even beyond a dry turkey. One particular concern is preparing a meal that will suit everyone and one large enough to make sure no one leaves hungry. And so, a host may be inclined to ask guests to contribute to the buffet.

In a recent development, however, there is a right way and a wrong way to broach the matter, and luckily, it’s a topic that the best hosts and advice columnists have adeptly tackled time and time again. This begins, perhaps, with the most vaunted of all: Emily Post. Well, her descendants, anyway. The Post Institute suggests that it is acceptable, given the “potluck” nature of the day, to ask guests to contribute, but hosts must “Have a flexible menu strategy...be prepared to be ‘coordination central.’”.

It has been revealed that the Post Institute’s advice also highlights something important: The onus is very much on the guest to offer to contribute. Here’s how they should do it: “Offer to contribute to the meal—but don't dictate the menu. Your best bet is to make your offer open-ended and follow your host's direction.” Plus, it never hurts to take responsibility for your own dietary needs, though good hosts will always do their best to accommodate.

Martha Stewart and Ina Garten share similar protocol. In an interview with the Los Angeles Times, Stewart suggests that during potlucks more broadly, “The host can provide the main course,” and the guests can bring the sides. Using her “duck breast with fig sauce menu” (a multi-course dinner party plan starring the namesake dish) as an example, she says, “One person can bring the braised red cabbage and someone else can prepare the potato pancake or the hazelnut brittle for the ice cream.”.

Garten is enthusiastic about guest contributions, telling Bon Appétit, “I actually love to ask people for help.” She also offers specific marching orders: “I might put somebody in charge of wine if they like wine.” Using a guest’s talents and interests to get to the best outcome is a smart idea, and can even go beyond sweet potatoes and pumpkin pie to napkins or place cards.

Industry observers note that myka Meier, the Beaumont etiquette founder, has an entire treatise on the topic, “A guest who accepts a dinner invitation should not discover the day before that they are responsible for the main course.” Instead, she suggests offering a choice, telling guests: “We are having a potluck supper on Saturday at six. I will make the main dish. Would you enjoy bringing a vegetable side, bread, or dessert?” She also reminds hosts to tell their guests how many are joining.

The most crucial aspect, as with all facets of Thanksgiving, is to have fun and celebrate with loved ones—be lenient, forgiving, and courteous. With a bit of preparation, everything will turn out well.
