In a recent development, one way to read the tea leaves on the recent U.S. midterms is that, at long last, Washington gets serious about regulating artificial intelligence; the unchecked build-out of those wildly unpopular data centers is reconsidered; Massive Tech is held to account; and the United States becomes not just the most advanced AI power but also the more trustworthy AI power. But if you were expecting that the electoral “techlash” would lead to coherent AI governance, policy, and statecraft, then prepare to be disappointed. The more likely outcome is that we shall live with two more years of muddied U.S. technology policy at home and abroad, with one clear winner: Beijing.

For while Democrats did benefit from the promise of checking AI growth, and they now own the legislative machinery, the party must balance three competing impulses: to rein in AI, to ride the AI-led economic momentum and avoid economy-wide slowdowns before the 2028 elections, and to avoid ceding the AI leadership to China.

Sources indicate that to make gnarly matters more tangled, U.S. AI’s central player, Nvidia, has lost its co-founder and CEO over an alleged Chinese intelligence operation, scrambling all three impulses. And we should also remember that over at the White House, a bruised but belligerent Trump retains the executive levers and the veto—as well as an impulse to stymie whatever the Democrats wish to demonstrate as a “win” to the American public.

Before we get to Trump, though, let’s first consider what drives each of the camps in the Democratic coalition.

Industry observers note that the first camp can be thought of as the Restrainers. They want to check the proliferation of data centers while holding AI executives to account for independent testing of advanced models—and holding the companies liable for harm.

According to recent reports, midterm voters rewarded candidates in this camp, who pushed back on a sense that AI development was proceeding unchecked; Sherrod Brown won back an Ohio Senate seat with ads calling his opponent “the face of AI data centers,” while Jon Ossoff in Georgia and Abdul El-Sayed in Michigan also won races with opposition to data centers as a key element of their campaign platforms. In nine states, governors and governors-elect, too, have promised or imposed a pause on new data centers, stalling an estimated 41 percent of the country’s data center pipeline. Meanwhile, right after the elections, veteran Sen. Bernie Sanders called for a national data center moratorium.

While slowing down AI would be the most readily available and popular move by lawmakers, the Restrainers still have to get their house in order. They will need to prioritize the many risks and concerns around AI and focus their limited time on the most significant ones. Weighing these will not be easy. A household worried about its power bill, for instance, needs a different intervention compared with a parent who doesn’t want their child left alone with a chatbot.

A second camp is made up of the Riders. They have their eye on the economy and want to ensure that AI investments keep carrying U.S. growth, the stock market, and household retirement savings. AI has propped up the American economy in recent years; letting the air out of that balloon could produce a bust, a recession, and shrinking retirement accounts, which could be bad news come 2028 if voters lay the blame on Congress.

The sharp Nov. 5 reminder about the connection between AI growth and stock market growth is hard to ignore—right after the midterms results had sunk in, the S&P 500 stock index fell 4.4 percent, its worst day since the April 2025 tariff shock, led by stocks tied to the data center build-out, while the Nasdaq also had its deepest one-day drop in 19 months, with investors worried about tougher AI rules from a Democratic Congress.

The Riders will urge caution: hold the hearings, pass disclosure rules, but don’t touch the capital spending contributing to the economy and the stock market. Several moderate Democrats, such as Pennsylvania Gov. Josh Shapiro or Kentucky Gov. Andy Beshear, who are also presumed to be business-friendly, are considering presidential runs. They are likely to be the influential voices in the Riders camp.

Sources indicate that as for the third camp, let’s call them the Racers. These are Democrats who prioritize national security concerns and want to keep sensitive technology and advanced chips away from adversaries. Specifically, they are eager to ensure that China not win the AI race. The Racers have support from the likes of influential senators such as Elizabeth Warren and Democratic leader Chuck Schumer as well as post-midterms in-the-spotlight senators including James Talarico.

According to recent reports, while the AI race against China was not the concern that galvanized voters in the midterms and swept Democrats into Congress, a startling new development has the potential to make this a more pivotal political lever: the scandal involving Jensen Huang, who admitted to an affair with a woman alleged to be a Chinese spy. This puts a spotlight on the extent to which China is keen to compete and centers the geopolitical stakes of the AI race in voters’ minds.

Huang’s abrupt exit from the organization that he co-founded puts wind at the backs of the Racers, especially with curbing chip sales to China becoming a rare cause with bipartisan support. But it also pushes the other camps to revise their agendas.

The Restrainers’ scrutiny of Big Tech’s leadership has national security implications. They will by now have realized that Nvidia did not have so much as a board chair until the day that Huang left—hardly a case for letting the industry police itself. For the Riders, with Nvidia as the load-bearer for the AI edifice, instability at the top could produce instability across the economy. They can point to half a trillion dollars of Nvidia’s market value being lost within a few days of the scandal breaking and the board investigation beginning.

A Democratic coalition made up of these camps necessarily has a mix of divergent priorities. But while the legislative agenda will be set by political calculation, Congress can only guess at what a lame-duck president who controls the agencies, the veto, the export licenses, and his own stake in the AI trade will do in response.

Sources indicate that we have just had a preview: Even as a bipartisan chip bill is in process on Capitol Hill, the White House moved first, issuing its own rule to choke off China’s access to U.S. chips through data centers abroad.

Recent findings suggest that the public will vote again in two years, too short for politicians and too long for technologists. With all these conflicting forces in motion, I would argue that Congress needs to allocate its limited bandwidth to a prioritized short list of AI risks, such as those recorded in my own register of 103 documented cases.

Sources indicate that in the absence of a reconciliation across the divergent camps, the result could be a U.S. AI build-out that advances in fits and starts as deals get negotiated, an export regime that can swing with each hearing, a standoff between legislators on Capitol Hill and the White House, and allies who hedge because they cannot predict U.S. policy.

The Chinese AI industry would be handed the perfect case for promoting itself to the world as the comparable, trustworthy, and cheaper alternative. And it has managed to get to this point even without access to the most advanced chips that Huang himself had wanted to sell to them.

Meanwhile, to win, Beijing will not need a newly ascendant Democratic Party to pick the wrong camp. It only needs Washington to pick none.
