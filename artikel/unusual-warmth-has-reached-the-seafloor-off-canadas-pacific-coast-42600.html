Unusual warmth has reached the seafloor off Canada’s Pacific coast – and scientists may know why

A seafloor sensor off Vancouver Island logged its highest temperature since monitoring began in 2009, and researchers say El Niño may be behind the spike.

El Niño is usually described as a warming of the surface waters in the tropical Pacific.

This fall, sensors off Canada’s west coast recorded unusually warm water hundreds of feet below the surface.

Industry observers note that one seafloor sensor logged its highest temperature since monitoring began there in 2009. The scientists who operate the sensor noted the warm pulse is consistent with a strong El Niño.

The sensors are operated by Ocean Networks Canada, an ocean-monitoring initiative of the University of Victoria.

The readings were declared by the organization itself this week. They have not yet been published as a study.

Industry observers note that the record was set at a site called Barkley Canyon Upper Slope, off Vancouver Island. A sensor rests on the seafloor there, about 1,300 feet (400 meters) down. That depth is roughly a quarter of a mile.

On November 8, the water at this site reached 46.8°F (8.21°C).

Jay Cullen is the interim president and chief executive of Ocean Networks Canada.

“We’re seeing significant warming not just at the surface, but hundreds of meters below it,” Cullen noted.

According to recent reports, the November 8 reading was 4.5°F (2.49°C) above the long-term average for the site. It was also 1.6°F (0.89°C) warmer than the previous record, which was set in 2019.

Industry observers note that the water has cooled since then, but normal temperatures have not returned. In mid-November, it was still about 2.9°F (1.6°C) warmer than average.

Warm water has been found at other sites too. The Barkley Canyon sensor is one part of a much larger network. Roughly 530 miles (850 kilometers) of seafloor cable connect its five main research sites.

One of those sites is Clayoquot Slope, which lies more than 3,900 feet (1,200 meters) down. Temperatures well above average are being recorded there as well.

Since late summer, sites between 1,300 and 4,100 feet (400 and 1,250 meters) deep have logged record seasonal temperatures.

Sources indicate that the deepest site is the exception. At Cascadia Basin, about 8,700 feet (2,650 meters) down, temperatures remain within their usual range.

Sources indicate that el Niño was declared by the U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration in June.

On November 12, the agency’s Climate Prediction Center said El Niño was still strengthening.

It has been revealed that A strong or very strong event was judged likely to continue into early 2027. The chance of that was put at more than 79%.

How can El Niño affect waters off Canada’s Pacific coast?

Steve Mihaly is a senior scientist at Ocean Networks Canada. According to Mihaly, the energy of a strong El Niño travels north from the equatorial Pacific.

It moves as waves that follow the coastline, and oceanographers call them coastal trapped waves.

Recent findings suggest that sea level has also been above normal since the warm water arrived. The rise was recorded by tide gauges at Bamfield and Tofino, two communities on Vancouver Island.

Pressure recorders on the seafloor picked it up as well. By mid-November, sea level was about 10 inches (25 centimeters) above normal. Local wind and atmospheric effects were accounted for first.

The scientists then attributed about 6 inches (15 centimeters) of the rise to the waves.

At Barkley Canyon Upper Slope, the water temperature began climbing sharply in late October and reached a record on November 8.

Mihaly mentioned northwest winds persisted into the fall, pushing warmer surface water offshore and drawing colder water upward.

This process, known as upwelling, helped mask the warming at depth.

A second network, called VENUS, is run by Ocean Networks Canada in the Salish Sea.

According to recent reports, these sheltered waters are shared by British Columbia and Washington State. The VENUS sensors reach depths of about 980 feet (300 meters).

Temperatures in the Strait of Georgia, which is part of that sea, have been above average throughout 2026.

Jordan Watson is the science director of Ocean Networks Canada.

Industry observers note that “Since the start of 2026, the VENUS observatory has shown temperatures consistently higher than we’ve previously observed,” Watson noted. “And this was before any El Niño effects started to appear.”.

Watson described the area as known for migrating salmon, spawning herring, and marine mammals.

Recent findings suggest that warming from El Niño could add to the already high temperatures there. That would put more stress on marine ecosystems, the organization stated.

In a recent development, so far, only temperatures and sea levels have been reported by the scientists. No effects on marine life have been reported yet.

The record is also a short one. Monitoring at Barkley Canyon began in 2009, so it covers less than two decades.

The scientists were not certain about the cause either. They noted the warming likely marks the arrival of El Niño’s effects on Canada’s Pacific coast.

Cullen noted that conditions will be monitored closely over the coming months.

A fresh tool is also being planned by Ocean Networks Canada and CIOOS Pacific, a regional ocean-data group. It will track ocean conditions across the region.

The seafloor data will be combined with measurements from other groups around the Salish Sea.

Fisheries and Oceans Canada and the University of Washington are among them. More details will be shared later this year, the partners said.

Live and long-term readings can be viewed on Ocean Networks Canada’s data dashboard.
