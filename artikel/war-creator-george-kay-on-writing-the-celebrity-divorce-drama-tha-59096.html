SPOILER ALERT: This article contains major spoilers for “War,” which had its season finale on Nov. 19 on HBO and HBO Max in the U.S., and drops on Nov. 20 on Sky and NOW in the U.K.

Industry observers note that over eight episodes of “War,” actress Carla Duval (Sienna Miller) and tech bro Morgan Henderson (Dominic West) have been squabbling over the couple’s multibillion-dollar fortune as they prepare to part ways for good. The winners, of course, have been the lawyers.

The series centers around two law firms: the modern and fast-paced Taylor & Byrne, founded by Nicholas Taylor (James McArdle) and Serena Byrne (Phoebe Fox), and the stuffy, old-school Cathcarts & Sons, led by St. John Smallwood (Pip Torrens) and Beatrice Ubosi (Nina Sosanya).

Recent findings suggest that if there’s bad blood between Duval and Henderson, relations between the lawyers are worse and bloodier still. Which is perhaps why, in a season finale twist, the divorcing couple decide to negotiate their own settlement amicably, and away from their scheming lawyers.

With Season 1 wrapped up, creator and showrunner George Kay sat down with Variety to talk about some of the real-life inspirations for the show, why he decided on that surprising ending and what we can expect from Season 2, which will see Taylor & Byrne and Cathcarts return with a newly introduced case and new clients.

According to recent reports, A little bit. Twenty years ago, I had a company that made a documentary series about divorce, and it was quite grim viewing, unsurprisingly. Especially because it was real people, and there was no charisma going on — [“War” director] Ben Taylor wasn’t directing it, basically, and it wasn’t Sienna and Dominic. So I remember having that at the back of my mind, thinking the thing we don’t want here is to make this grim or bleak or anything. And in fact, one of the phrases that we came up with in the writers’ room was that this divorce would be the world’s most expensive couples’ therapy. Even though you don’t physically stay married, you end up closer together.

You want to leave people feeling: “That was good. I liked hanging out with those people.” They were awful a lot of the time, but everyone’s redeemable. And Carla and Morgan don’t despise each other. They just drifted and drifted and drifted.

Sources indicate that I wanted to end that story in Season 1, because I wanted him to find his home. And so that was always intended as a season-long story, and really wrapped around that is that thing of “Is it possible to be a good lawyer and a good person?” And I firmly think it is.

We’re not going to extend the espionage thing. But with Johnny, it’s a question of can you ever trust someone who’s done that? Which I think is a relatable thing, whether that’s your relationship or your workplace. If someone’s done something really untrustworthy, can you ever trust them again? So that will linger, and become a massive part of Johnny’s story. But the other thing is, we wanted to find a similar character who’s not at the upper echelons of a law firm to really get behind in Season 2. That is Emma Laird, who plays Molly [a junior lawyer at Taylor & Byrne]. We cast Emma, and I remember saying to her, “You’re amazing. Just so you know, please come and do Molly in Season 1, because there’s loads more coming in Season 2.”.

Every season, I think it’s worth having someone slightly more junior through which to see the whole thing through a prism of them.

In a recent development, we don’t see her in Season 2. We have guest stars for each season. But we do have a world. So people [in “War”] don’t use X; they use Zephyr. The whole thing keeps kind of bleeding into itself.

That was written with a writer called Toby Finlay, who’s brilliant, and we talked about that a lot in the writers’ room. If you can’t arbitrate on a divorce, you’ll go to litigation, and that’s when it will go public, because it’s not protected anymore. And what’s fun about that is when you’ve got a kind of Morgan Henderson and a Carla Duval, and they are very much in the public eye, that scene triggers the going public of the whole thing. So we were always thinking we want this whole thing to blow up in public on chat shows around “The Milliner,” which is Sienna’s film.

In a recent development, we wanted to put the camera behind the chat show host and the guest, because it’s only when you go on stage, you suddenly get the on-stage eye-view of what it’s like to be on a chat show. We’re so familiar with Graham Norton or Fallon or whatever, seeing it from the front. We felt it was such a private thing to see it from the other side, and then you can hear the lights kind of cracking in the heat, you see the light obfuscating the audience and the pressure of that. She’s such a talented actress, Sienna. She can do this thing of having a public smile, but with private worry in the eyes.

According to recent reports, she was really great in terms of dealing with the script and talking to me about, “I don’t think she’d quite say it like that,” and we would sit there together on set and tweak stuff. But never did we talk about the meta thing of Sienna Miller in the public glare. She was treating it as a proper part.

According to recent reports, “Notting Hill” plays that much more for laughs with Rhys Ifans in his pants. But actually, I felt that [showed] the press physically bearing down on you.

Recent findings suggest that I really wanted to use it as a way to pressurize Sienna, like that door might physically collapse on her. It was a quicker way into just seeing that the weight of the press attention was right there, so it’s a little nod to “Notting Hill,” just in the sheer kind of [gasp] of opening the door. But it’s not used for laughs.

Who was the guy that chased that woman down the corridor? It was awful.

I think it’s more that sort of stuff happens in hotels, and there’s CCTV in hotels. You’re always looking in thrillers to find ways you could kind of revisit. So it wasn’t trying to plug into any one particular incident.

According to recent reports, I wanted to make it a bit different because — full disclosure — I didn’t want Olivia Wilde to think we were just aping her experience. But also, I thought by distancing ourselves from it, we could make it even worse for Carla by putting it right in her biggest night, her biggest moment. This really is the culmination. She’s on a career comeback at that point. She’s been in some less good movies than “The Milliner,” if you can imagine, and so this was a really special night for her. So using that for dramatic effect felt good.

But the thing that I was really inspired by the Olivia Wilde thing was just how professional and upset she was at the same time. Like we’re talking about Sienna’s ability to [show] the pain it’s caused, and then you see how she’s painting on a smile swiftly to get through it. And I really spotted that in Olivia Wilde’s way of dealing with that in that moment, which I thought that just reflected very well on Olivia Wilde. So I was happy to be inspired by that because it’s probably an awful moment in someone’s life, and you’ve got to be choose the right way to kind of be inspired.

In a recent development, there were lots of fun names. The script editor came up with a brilliant list of films that are sort of mid-level Euro, kind of awards worthy, like “The Red Violinist of Marseille” and things like that, or you know, “The Harpist of Madrid.” But in the end, it was just “The Milliner.” We had this story that she was undercover in occupied Europe with a hat.

It has been revealed that the thing we look for in the divorce [case] is to analyze the relationships of all the lawyers. And with the libel case, it’s all about reputation. How our reputations are affecting us at work and what our work reputation means to us in life is a really cool thing to have exposed by a libel trial. So it’s about playing those two things off against each other, and bringing in a few upcoming characters and a few new bits of casting to make it believable that what we see as predominantly family lawyers can also have other people in their departments who have libel experience.

Sources indicate that nick Mohammed really comes through in Season 2, which was again part of our pledge to him. And there’s a newly introduced hiring at Cathcarts, who starts to — not cause problems, but definitely shakes up the dynamic.

According to recent reports, tonally, it’s adjacent but it’s not exactly the same. And we want to have different aspects. It’s a different album.

I’m actually looking the other way. I’m looking for the thing that affects people and then looking for the legal equivalent. It’s the relationship stories that we’ve seen in movies or in our own lives that we really relate to on an emotional level, and then promptly scouring law to say, “Well, where is that felt?” So it’s less with Walliams or Russell Brand. Like, I’m interested in how the justice system will treat those people, but I’m not using that for inspiration for drama because I don’t know how it feels for anyone involved because I don’t know it. So we’re trying to start with the feeling.
