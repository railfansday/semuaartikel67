What We Are Bound To Lose in an Optimized Future

December invites a peculiar ritual. We look back at the year, count our achievements, contemplate our disappointments, and begin constructing a better version of ourselves for January. In 2026, artificial intelligence became an increasingly available accomplice in this exercise. It can summarize our accomplishments, identify inefficiencies, suggest resolutions, and construct an impressively optimized roadmap for 2027. Yet imagine reviewing your year differently. Instead of asking what went according to blueprint, consider what happened because it didn't. The conversation you never intended to have. The problem that refused to disappear. The project that demanded more than you thought you could give. Perhaps the moments that changed you most were precisely those that resisted optimization.

This possibility deserves attention as we enter another year of accelerating algorithmic assistance. We have become extraordinarily good at eliminating inconvenience, yet psychological flourishing may depend partly on experiences that cannot be anticipated, accelerated, or outsourced. The interplay between friction and flow offers a way to understand why.

We generally prefer ease, but our relationship with effort is more complicated than this preference suggests. A 2024 meta-analysis in Psychological Bulletin confirmed that mental effort is commonly experienced as unpleasant. Yet research published in Cognition in 2025, drawing on six studies involving 2,883 participants, found that exerting effort can increase an activity's perceived meaningfulness. One experiment compared writing with and without ChatGPT assistance. Greater effort contributed to meaning only up to a point. Still, the findings illuminate something that efficiency calculations routinely overlook. We may dislike the difficulty of an experience while valuing what that difficulty allows us to become. This paradox helps explain why flow, the state of intense absorption described by Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, cannot simply be manufactured by making everything easier.

According to recent reports, A meta-analysis examining the relationship between challenge and skill found that their balance contributes significantly to flow, alongside clear goals and a sense of control. More recent research in Communications Psychology emphasizes autonomy and the personal significance of an activity. Flow emerges through engagement with something sufficiently demanding to absorb us, yet manageable enough to sustain participation. The pianist needs a piece that stretches her abilities; the writer needs an idea that refuses to settle into easy sentences. Friction provides the resistance through which skill becomes experience, and experience becomes satisfaction.

Now consider an algorithmic environment increasingly designed to anticipate our desires and remove obstacles before we encounter them. Recommendations narrow our search; predictive systems streamline our choices, and generative AI can produce results without requiring us to navigate the uncertainties of creation. Such assistance can be enormously beneficial, particularly when it relieves unnecessary burdens. Yet when friction disappears indiscriminately, we risk losing opportunities for the very engagement that makes flow possible. A life engineered for effortless outcomes might become strangely impoverished in the experiences through which we develop competence, confidence, and curiosity. This remains a psychological risk rather than an established destiny, but it raises an essential question: Are we optimizing the quality of our experiences, or merely the speed with which we move through them?

In a recent development, research suggests that our conventional understanding of a good life may itself be incomplete. Some consider psychological richness a dimension of well-being alongside happiness and meaning. A related 2025 review in Trends in Cognitive Sciences describes psychologically rich lives as those containing diverse, interesting, and perspective-changing experiences. To be clear, such experiences are not uniformly pleasant. An unfamiliar place, a challenging encounter, or an unexpected reversal may enlarge our understanding precisely because it disrupts familiar assumptions. This opens an interesting perspective on December's customary stocktaking.

In a recent development, the experiences that made 2026 psychologically rich may be poorly represented by our conventional measures of success. A friendship deepened through disagreement, an unexpected professional change, or an abandoned plan that opened an unforeseen possibility might have contributed more to personal development than another efficiently completed task. Of course, hardship is not inherently beneficial. Financial insecurity, discrimination, illness, and exhausting administrative obstacles can diminish well-being and restrict agency. The distinction worth preserving is between friction that erodes our ability to flourish and friction that invites us to exercise it.

It has been revealed that the approaching latest year makes this distinction painfully relevant. Research on the fresh start effect shows that temporal landmarks, including the beginning of a new year, can motivate humans to pursue aspirations. Perhaps we could use this psychological opening differently in December 2026? Rather than designing another year around the elimination of effort, we might ask which forms of friction deserve a place in our lives. Which difficulties strengthen our abilities? Which uncertainties invite discovery? Which activities would lose their meaning if someone—or something—completed them for us?

In a recent development, the challenge is to choose meaningful friction deliberately, without turning everyday life into an obstacle course. BLISS offers five practical ways to cultivate that judgment.

According to recent reports, B — Begin before borrowing. Give your own imagination an opportunity to work before requesting an algorithmic solution.

According to recent reports, L — Let learning take time. Preserve activities in which gradual improvement brings satisfaction, even when shortcuts are readily available.

According to recent reports, I — Invite the unfamiliar. Explore beyond personalized recommendations; seek perspectives and experiences that might challenge established preferences.

S — Stay with the struggle. When a difficulty is productive, resist the urge to eliminate it immediately. Allow yourself the satisfaction of finding a way through.

According to recent reports, S — Save space for surprise. Leave some time unplanned, some questions unanswered, and some possibilities unexplored until they present themselves.

We can welcome intelligent technologies that reduce unnecessary suffering while protecting the experiences that develop natural intelligence. An increasingly capable algorithmic future need not become a frictionless one, provided we retain the freedom and capacity to choose where human effort matters. As 2026 ends, perhaps the most meaningful resolution for 2027 is not to become more efficient, organized, or productive. It is to become more attentive to what makes life worth living. The winding path through unfamiliar territory may take longer, demand more, and occasionally lead somewhere unexpected. Yet it may also reveal capacities and possibilities that a perfectly optimized route would never allow us to discover.

Industry observers note that we need friction to find flow. And sometimes, the detour is the destination.
