Late last week, the Trump administration released a proposed rule that includes a slew of technical changes in Medicare Advantage for 2028, citing enhanced competition as a key goal.

Analysts dove into the beefy regulation and projected that changes to MA's star ratings program would likely cull more than $100 billion in federal spending over the next decade.

Per a note from Leerink Partners, estimates from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services suggest that the changes would cut federal spending by $11.5 billion in 2031 and $107 billion through 2037.

The Leerink analysts estimate that about 58% of Medicare Advantage contracts would see lower scores using the newly proposed methodology, with about 20% of contracts losing quality bonus payments, which are tied to star ratings performance.

Sources indicate that in the rule, CMS proposed moving to a set of 15 measures to determine the star ratings scores, which are focused on member experience and outcomes. The proposed rule would also eliminate the Reward Factor, and in tandem, these changes "would remove a cushion for contracts sitting at the critical QBP threshold, while concentrating performance across fewer measures," according to the Leerink report.

The analysts said that the potential impact of these proposals "reminds us just how many aims have been living at 4 stars for years with the hugely vital Reward Factor.".

In a recent development, the report estimates that the changes to the star ratings, if finalized, would have an uneven impact on insurers. For example, Humana would see the number of contracts earning four or more stars decline from 95% to 82.9%. Clover Health, meanwhile, would likely maintain 100% of its contracts, earning at least four stars.

According to recent reports, unitedHealthcare, by comparison, could see a drop from having 67.9% of its contracts in intends with at least four stars in 2027 to just 8.8% under the new methodology. For Aetna, the Leerink analysts project a decrease from 70% to 0%.

It has been revealed that given the scale of the changes, the Leerink analysts write that the rule sets the stage for a significant overhaul of the policies and rules that govern Medicare Advantage. The proposal is "almost overwhelming in size, scope and the impact on the industry.".

Molly Turco, a policy analyst at TD Cowen, wrote in a note Monday that the proposed rule "is more significant than expected, in both revenue impact and scope and touches most core aspects of MA.".

"It appears CMS aimed to reform long-standing concerns, especially Stars, in a way that takes money out of the program, while also loosening regulation on things like agents/brokers, seamless enrollment, network adequacy, etc. to help the program grow," Turco wrote. "However, we think these growth/deregulation policies are unlikely to offset the headwinds for MA.".

She noted that while most of the most painful changes wouldn't impact plans until 2031 at the earliest; the delay does suggest CMS may be comfortable including more significant short-term changes to risk adjustment in the forthcoming 2028 Advance Notice, which is expected in January.

Ryan Langston, an equity research analyst at TD Cowen, wrote in the note that the proposed rule would likely be net negative for insurers with large, multi-state contracts and more people enrolled with intends earning at least four stars.

The delayed timeline of at least four years does grant looks time to adapt, Langston wrote, but the financial impacts would likely force benefit changes. The proposed rule could also spook investors concerned about what's included in the 2028 rate notice, he said.

"The combination of a smaller measure set, elimination of reward factors and reduced improvement measure protection likely lower Star ratings across the industry and force plans to mitigate lost revenue via benefit revisions," Langston wrote.

According to recent reports, the Leerink analysts wrote that health prepares are almost certain to fight the regulation. Lawsuits have become commonplace in the stars space, with multiple payers suing the feds over the most recent round of scores.

It has been revealed that "We believe a lot of what has been proposed will change and/or not be implemented at all," they wrote. "Expect industry litigation.".
