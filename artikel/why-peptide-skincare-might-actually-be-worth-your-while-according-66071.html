Why Peptide Skincare Might Actually Be Worth Your While, According to Experts

According to recent reports, the recent buzz around peptides came to a head last month when U.S. Secretary of Health and Human Services (HHS) Robert F. Kennedy Jr. announced that six unregulated injectables, including the highly controversial “Wolverine stack” BPC-157 and TB-500, will now be available for broad use throughout the United States. Meanwhile, unsurprising to derms and estheticians everywhere, the rising tide of interest in peptides has spilled over into skincare, where the molecules have long served as an active ingredient.

“I’ve had a lot of patients come in since the HHS ruling,” says Erik Peterson, MD, a board-certified dermatologist in Fresh York City, who likens the recent policy change to “opening the floodgates,” resulting in a surge of interest—particularly from patients who’ve come across claims of peptide-based skincare being like ‘botox in a bottle.’.

Sources indicate that “It’s interesting how they are suddenly so popular, because peptides have been around for like 30 years in skincare,” notes Malika Sloan, medical esthetician and founder of Sloan Studio in Newly introduced York City. “They’ve been incorporated into cosmetic formulations for decades, with ingredients like matrixyl helping to popularize them in the early 2000s. What’s exciting now is how much more sophisticated the formulations have become.”.

If you’re curious about peptide-based skincare products—what they potentially can do, what they definitely cannot—and which products experts recommend for the best results, here’s everything you need to know.

Peptides, regardless of whether they’re injected via syringe or applied topically with a serum or cream, are short chains of amino acids that instruct your body to execute specific functions. Insulin, for example, is a peptide that helps the body regulate blood sugar, and it’s been in use since the 1920s. More recently, GLP-1 drugs (short for glucagon-like peptide-1) have been found to curb food cravings and slow stomach emptying, making them highly effective for weight loss. The list of uses for peptides is virtually endless, ranging from muscle growth and wound healing to balancing hormones and managing inflammation.

In skincare, topical peptides work much the same way as their injectable counterparts, but on a more localized level, directing your skin to carry out specific functions, “the first of which tends to be increasing production of collagen and elastin, which are two of the major determinants of skin quality,” says Dr. Peterson. They’ve also been shown to help even out skin tone, repair the skin barrier, and reduce the appearance of fine lines and wrinkles.

Sources indicate that the world of peptides is still a bit of a Wild West. A quick Google search for peptide skincare will yield dozens of results for unresearched compounds promising you flawless skin. And then, of course, there are the influencers. “You see all these kids on TikTok who are talking about their beauty routines, and they’re like 19 years old,” states Peterson. “It’s like, ‘Amazing, your skin obviously looks great, because you’re a child.’” In reality, the list of peptides that you can truly depend on to move the needle (without an actual needle) is surprisingly short, at least until more research is done.

According to recent reports, “The two peptides that are out right now that have any sort of viable data on them are palmitoyl pentapeptide-4 and GHK-Cu,” states Peterson. Signaling peptides, as they’re called—such as palmitoyl pentapeptide-4, better known as matrixyl—have some of the strongest clinical and anecdotal evidence for stimulating collagen and reducing the appearance of wrinkles. Although, as Peterson points out, much of the scientific data has been industry funded, “so we always have to be a bit skeptical.”.

Carrier peptides—like GHK-Cu, a popular copper peptide—on the other hand, “have some good lab data, but very minimal human data,” Peterson says. Nonetheless, products containing GHK-Cu are widely heralded for their ability to repair damaged tissue, reduce inflammation, and stimulate production of collagen and elastin. “I find copper peptides particularly interesting because of the research surrounding tissue repair and collagen-related remodeling pathways,” says Sloan.

As for acetyl hexapeptide-8, or argireline, as it’s more commonly known—aka “botox in a bottle”—don’t waste your money. “From a physiological perspective, it can’t possibly work,” notes Peterson, who explains that the molecules of this particular peptide are simply too large to pass through the surface of the skin. He compares it to skincare products that contain collagen rather than spur the body to produce its own. “Collagen is a massive molecule,” he says. “It would be like putting cow’s milk on your face—it’s not going to do anything.” Sure enough, a 2023 study found the effects of topical argireline to be “not statistically significant.”.

Instead, for those looking for the most noticeable results, Sloan recommends seeking out products that combine proven peptides with complementary, similarly validated ingredients—like SkinCeuticals’ P-TIOX Anti-Wrinkle Peptide Cream. “I like that the cream also incorporates ceramides, niacinamide, and moisturizing ingredients, making it an interesting option for someone looking to address both hydration and visible signs of aging,” she notes. Peterson’s pick, The Rich Cream from Augustinus Bader, flanks signaling peptides with hyaluronic acid and vitamin E to improve moisture retention and bolster the skin’s protective barrier.

Peptides are a relatively low-maintenance addition to most skin regimens. “One of the reasons I love incorporating peptides into at-home skincare is that they’re generally well tolerated and relatively easy to combine with other ingredients,” claims Sloan, who notes that peptides can typically be used alongside other popular actives like vitamin C, niacinamide, hyaluronic acid, and retinoids. “I typically recommend incorporating a peptide serum after cleansing and before moisturizer,” she says.

In a recent development, just keep in mind that peptides alone won’t take the place of a well-rounded skincare regimen. “The best, most robust data still comes from the tried-and-true favorites of all dermatologists—protecting your collagen with consistent sunscreen use, and the best signaling molecule of them all: topical retinoids,” Peterson states.

And, of course, don’t expect overnight results—despite what social media might have you believe. “Peptides are just signals for your skin, not magic in a bottle,” Peterson says. “The few studies that there are show at least three months of consistent usage before results are seen, so stick it out if you want to give it a real try.”.
