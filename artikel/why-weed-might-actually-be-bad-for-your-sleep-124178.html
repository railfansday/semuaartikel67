Why Weed Might Actually Be Bad for Your Sleep

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that everyone’s done it before. A pre-game gummy. A nighttime toke. Weed is, at this point, a widely used sleep aid. It usually works pretty well, too. But if you’ve made it a habit, you might just be sabotaging your shut-eye in the long run. According to a new October 2026 study, consuming pot in any form before bed causes people to have poorer quality of sleep—without them even realizing it. In other words: It has exactly the opposite of its intended effect. Here’s what you should know before popping an edible before you doze off.

Industry observers note that the study, which was performed by Northwestern University researchers and published in JAMA Network, looked at 1,266 adults who participated in the Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults (CARDIA) study, which followed participants for 35 years to determine how behavioral, biological, and environmental factors affected their risk of heart disease later in life. Of that sample size, 891 wore wrist-based activity monitors for at least a week so that researchers could examine their sleep patterns. After analyzing the data, the researchers found that those who were long-term cannabis owners had “poorer objective sleep, including lower sleep efficiency, greater fragmentation, and more time awake during the night,” Dr. Thanh-Huyen Vu, an author of the study and a research associate professor of preventive medicine at Northwestern University’s Feinberg School of Medicine, tells GQ.

Here’s what all of that means: Sleep efficiency is essentially how much of the time you spend in bed you’re actually asleep. So, lower sleep efficiency means spending more time lying awake instead of sleeping while sleep fragmentation refers to how often your sleep is interrupted, like when you’re unknowingly tossing and turning to get comfortable. The researchers also looked at how much total time participants spent awake during the night. It all boils down to the fact that long-term cannabis users weren’t sleeping as soundly or as continuously throughout the night—sometimes without them even knowing it.

According to recent reports, the researchers can’t say for certain. Because the study was observational, it only established an association between long-term cannabis exposure and poorer sleep quality.

One possibility, Vu suggests, is that cannabis may “alter normal sleep architecture,” potentially changing the way the body cycles through different sleep stages.

According to recent reports, over time, it’s those changes that can contribute to more nighttime awakenings, which result in less restorative sleep. She says more research is needed to understand the underlying biological mechanisms.

In a recent development, deep sleep is one of the most important stages of sleep because it’s the phase when your body recovers physically, processes memory, and regulates the immune system. Interruptions in sleep result in less time during the deep sleep cycle, which inhibits your body from doing all the important things that help you function properly when you’re awake. The less deep sleep you get, the worse you feel in the morning. The reason why participants don’t pick up on these mid-sleep wake-ups is that they’re easy to forget. “Sleep trackers can capture some of these patterns, even when people don’t perceive their sleep as being particularly poor,” Vu says. It’s the same way in which you think you slept eight hours one night but woke up feeling groggy regardless.

Not necessarily. Given the sample population—middle-aged adults—the results may not apply to people in other age ranges or even those who use cannabis for medical reasons. Still, Vu states it’s key for people to understand that cannabis’s relationship with sleep may be more complicated than simply helping you drift off faster.

Recent findings suggest that if you regularly use cannabis before bed, paying attention to your sleep patterns may help you better understand how it’s affecting you. Wearable sleep trackers can offer some insight into nighttime awakenings and sleep efficiency, but you won’t get the same sleep analysis if you were part of a study like this. It might be worth trying out a period where you don’t use weed to fall asleep to see if it makes a difference in whether you feel more well-rested, or your mind feels sharper. Though the study still leaves a lot of questions to be answered, its data is clear enough to make anyone with a nightly gummy habit reconsider.
