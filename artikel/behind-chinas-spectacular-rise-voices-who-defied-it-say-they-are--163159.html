Behind China's spectacular rise, voices who defied it say they are being drowned out

China's economic might is drowning out our voices, say activists and dissidents.

For Uyghur activist Dolkun Isa, who has lived in exile in Germany for nearly three decades, the thinning of the crowds is the clearest measure of how much his cause is slipping off the world's radar.

Industry observers note that "Back in 2018, we could easily get thousands of people out in the streets to protest against China's human rights violations. Nowadays, we have trouble finding even 500.".

It was in 2018 that reports emerged that China had detained up to a million Uyghurs and other Muslims in mass internment camps in its north-western province of Xinjiang. Beijing, which mentioned the camps were for "re-education" to counter separatist violence, denied the allegations detailing alarming rights abuses.

But an international outcry, including protests, followed. Western governments rolled out trade bans and sanctions against senior Chinese officials, and froze their assets, and at least nine countries issued parliamentary resolutions condemning the detentions.

It was a time "when we felt the world was finally listening", claims Dolkun, 59, who served as president of the Munich-based World Uyghur Congress from 2017-2024 .

Sources indicate that but today, "we find ourselves competing against economic interests and geopolitical calculations to capture the world's attention. For me, this is one of the most painful aspects of our struggle".

Uyghur activist Dolkun Isa has lived in exile in Germany for nearly three decades.

Few corners of the world remain untouched by China's rise. Its electric vehicles pack highways from Brazil to Belgium, made-in-China batteries, solar panels, AI models and microdramas fuel daily consumption in so many places, and Labubu dolls, Pop Mart's furry gap-toothed plush, dangle from backpacks almost everywhere.

China's immense economic clout now also has the flex of soft power, drowning out voices that say their fight against the Communist Party cannot compete with the country's gleaming skylines, advanced exports or futuristic ambitions.

Buoyed by its successes, Beijing is also swapping the "stick" for the "carrot", claims Uyghur journalist Arslan Hidayat, who was born and raised in Australia, where he runs Kashgar Times.

Activists tell the BBC Beijing has been offering family reunions, money and the promise of safety - in return, they are asked for information about their networks and to promote pro-party narratives. The result, activists say, is a movement with fewer voices and less trust.

The Chinese embassy in the UK dismissed the activists' claims that they would be asked to spy on or disclose information about fellow exiles.

Sheznhen, a rich, buzzing city on the soth-eastern coast, has emerged as one of China's star attractions for tech and tourism.

China's "economic muscle" has earned it respect abroad and reshaped foreign policy, says Ian Johnson, a Pulitzer Prize-winning journalist who has long reported on Chinese civil society.

"At the same time, Western governments have become much more inwardly focused and populist, pushing human rights down their lists of priorities.".

China's human rights record has not earned a mention in any of the three summits Xi Jinping and US President Donald Trump held this year, including the one in Shenzhen last week. As Beijing's leverage on the global stage has grown, so has the cost of confrontation or condemnation.

This shift in Beijing's image and tactics is deeply worrying, dissidents say, because it is weakening both their networks and the international pressure they hope to build.

Industry observers note that in July Tibetan activist Lobga Rangzen died after he set himself on fire outside the UN headquarters in Newly introduced York. He was protesting against China's sweeping new "ethnic unity law" which, among other things, requires minorities, including Tibetans, to learn Mandarin. The UN called it an "absolutely tragic" incident, but attention promptly faded.

His death was "an appeal to the international community", says Tenzin Dorjee, a researcher at the Tibet Action Institute, but he thinks much of the world is reluctant to criticise China because of the power it wields.

That same month Dolkun tried to rally supporters against the same law but he met with a muted response.

"Many people have dialled down on their activism over the years because they are thinking about their loved ones back in China," Dolkun claims.

Donald Trump and Xi Jinping have held three summits so far this year.

Arslan says he has interviewed at least a dozen Uyghur exiles who were "invited to go back to China on the condition that they would provide information to Chinese agents from time to time".

The questions seem "fairly innocuous", he adds: "Who lives around you? Which Uyghur restaurants do you frequent? "We believe these are used to build out the profiles of dissidents overseas.".

Beijing's embassy in the UK said in its response to the BBC that "facilitating visits to China by overseas Chinese to reunite with their families" is a "normal part" of consular services.

"China respects and protects the lawful rights and interests of people of all ethnic groups, including the Uyghurs, and their freedom to maintain contact with family members overseas," it added.

These family visits often happen in large groups and are well documented on China's official platforms.

In Turkey, which hosts tens of thousands of Muslim minorities from Xinjiang, Chinese Consul-General Wei Xiaodong was photographed sending off some 30 participants from Istanbul in January.

Consul-General Wei Xiaodong (in the red scarf) pictured sending people off in January.

According to recent reports, "He expressed hope that the diaspora returning to Xinjiang would experience the region's development... and would actively promote these developments upon their return to Turkey," read a post on the consulate's WeChat page.

Beijing has repackaged Xinjiang into a traveller's haven. The region saw more than 300 million domestic and international tourist visits last year, more than double the number in 2018, Chinese authorities say.

The post from China's Turkish consulate concluded with a clip showing loved ones hugging and presenting bouquets as they reunite with family at the airport in Urumqi, the capital of Xinjiang.

"People are happy to stay quiet and lay low for the little communication they've been allowed to establish with their loved ones," Arslan says.

It has been revealed that though the trips are publicised, it is unclear what makes someone eligible to participate in them. Some suggest that even dating histories come under the microscope.

Alim*, a Uyghur exiled in Turkey, says a friend who made an appeal to the consulate to consider her for a trip was rejected, with staffers alluding to her past relationship with a Kazakh man who at one point was "quite active" in lobbying for his parents who were detained in China.

Sources indicate that "She was very eager to go on one of these 'family visits' because she hasn't seen her loved ones back home in so many years... She was crushed that she was rejected," notes Alim.

"The way I understand it, the 'ideal participant' is someone influential enough to amplify China's messages, yet not associated with anything or anyone too political.".

Xinjiang has been sold as an exotic destination, bringing in huge numbers of tourists.

When Mehmut* was invited on a family visit in October 2024, he jumped at the opportunity.

In a recent development, the Uyghur businessman hoped a trip back to China would help him re-establish the links needed to "keep our business afloat". Far from the warm reunions Mehmut had expected, he says his engagement with friends and family "felt superficial".

Industry observers note that "It was not like officials were actively monitoring me or restricting my movements. But there was a great dose of fear and self-censorship in my conversations with people," he says.

"Some were caught off guard to see me return, others appeared very cautious with what they told me. The experience just made me feel very uncomfortable.".

These visits do not just serve Beijing's narrative; they also drive a wedge within communities, activists say.

Abduweli Ayup, a prominent Uyghur linguist based in Norway, has observed former activists going off the grid after returning from these trips.

"Some have deleted their social media accounts and cut off contact with us," he indicates. "Others appear to be suspicious of us or concerned that what we were doing would compromise theirs or their families' safety.".

The fact that only a few get selected to visit the homeland "creates confusion within the diaspora," says Tibetan activist Karma Rinchen.

"They get sidelined when they return... Very sadly, our suspicions that some of them are feeding information back to the Chinese authorities have turned out to be true.".

Karma Rinchen's parents were among thousands who fled Tibet alongside the Dalai Lama in 1959.

Rinchen serves as information secretary in the Tibetan government-in-exile in the northern Indian town of Dharamshala. His parents were among thousands who followed their spiritual leader Dalai Lama into exile after China annexed Tibet.

Over the decades, waves of others have fled Chinese rule seeking greater freedom or asylum from Beijing's crackdowns - dissidents and student activists in the wake of the Tiannanmen Square massacre; lawyers, journalists, purged officials and critics in the years after, and, more recently, Hong Kong saw a steady exodus build as the pro-democracy movement was crushed.

Many have kept on fighting. But now, they say, Beijing's might is chipping away at their struggle, making it ever harder to stand up to the Chinese Communist Party.

"Many among us have been separated from our families for generations and are eager to set foot on Tibet soil," Rinchen notes. "This is exactly the kind of desperation the Communist Party seeks to target.".

But he adds this only means communities have to "work harder to make sure their voices are heard".

A memorial for Tibetan activist Lobga Rangzen who set himself on fire outside the UN headquarters in Latest York.

In a recent development, "For Tibetans, this is not something we can simply walk away from. Our struggle is about our identity, our culture, our religion and the basic rights of our people. We have been doing this work for many decades, and we have learned to remain patient and persistent," Rinchen adds.

For many, that persistence continues to be tested on a personal level.

Uyghur musician Mirkamel Tourghoun has lived as a refugee in Paris since 2008, spending most of his time writing music and making films about Uyghur culture.

The 42-year-old has chosen the painful path of cutting off contact with his family who remain in Urumqi.

In January, he received a call from someone claiming to be an official from the city.

According to recent reports, "What if we get rid of all this [political] pressure, and allow you to return and see your parents? Do you have such prepares?" the caller asked.

Sources indicate that "What's happened in the past is in the past. You should turn a fresh page. The pen is in your own hand.".

The official claimed his father was gravely ill and that time was running out to see him. "It's emotional blackmail," Mirkamel indicates.

Industry observers note that he declined the official's invitation: "Tell my family to forget they ever had a child like me. There is no other solution.".
