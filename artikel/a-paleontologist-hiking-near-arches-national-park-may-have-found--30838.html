A paleontologist hiking near Arches National Park may have found a new armored dinosaur

A new dinosaur bonebed discovered near Moab, Utah, may contain an unknown armored dinosaur species dating back 138 to 141 million years.

Recent findings suggest that in mid-October 2026, the Utah Geological Survey (UGS) opened a upcoming dinosaur excavation near Moab, in eastern Utah. The team calls the site the Ironstone Bonebed, and it lies north of Arches National Park.

The bonebed appears to contain the remains of a new species of armored dinosaur, the agency noted. State Paleontologist Jim Kirkland is describing the animal and naming it in honor of Don DeBlieux, the Utah assistant state paleontologist.

In a recent development, the site is estimated at 138 million to 141 million years old. The UGS called it “currently tied as potentially the oldest Cretaceous dinosaur bonebed in North America.”.

DeBlieux has said he spotted the first fossils while exploring the area on foot. They were a shoulder blade and an upper arm bone from an armored dinosaur.

Industry observers note that he oversees the UGS field paleontology program and its fossil preparation lab. In 2002, he found a skull weathering out of sandstone near the middle of the Wahweap Formation, in southern Utah’s Grand Staircase-Escalante National Monument.

According to recent reports, that skull became the horned dinosaur Diabloceratops eatoni. DeBlieux spent more than 800 hours over two years removing the rock from around the bone.

Kirkland has said the crew has identified about four kinds of dinosaurs at the newly introduced site so far.

Kirkland, who has held his post since 1999, is known for naming numerous dinosaur species, most notably Utahraptor. After 25 years of working side by side with him, DeBlieux will retire at the end of 2026.

Geologically, the new locality is nearly equivalent to Doelling’s Bowl, a UGS site some six miles (10 kilometers) to the east.

DeBlieux also found Doelling’s Bowl, in 2005, when he came across the vertebrae and a hip bone of a dromaeosaurid, a raptor-like predator.

A 2012 study of Utah raptors named it Yurgovuchia doellingi and reported a bone bed covering tens of acres (at least several hectares).

Plant-eating iguanodonts, including one compared to Iguanacolossus, dominate it, with at least ten individuals. At least three polacanthine ankylosaurs, a group of armored dinosaurs, were also found there.

Sources indicate that the bonebed lies in the lower Yellow Cat Member of the Cedar Mountain Formation, below a marker layer of calcrete. It yielded Mierasaurus bobyoungi, a long-necked sauropod descended from Jurassic turiasaurs of the Iberian Peninsula.

Recent findings suggest that kirkland and DeBlieux co-authored its 2017 description. By then, Doelling’s Bowl had also produced teeth from a large allosauroid predator and a fresh, still unnamed species of polacanthid ankylosaur.

“Ankylosaurs were so well protected that even their eyelids were armored,” Kirkland and Mark Loewen, then of the Utah Museum of Natural History, wrote in 2011.

It has been revealed that at the time, the Cedar Mountain Formation had yielded more ankylosaur species than any other geologic formation in the world. Several more from it had been found but not yet described.

It has been revealed that gastonia burgei came from the basal Yellow Cat Member. It was closely related to Polacanthus foxi, from nearly age-equivalent rocks on England’s Isle of Wight.

Younger layers have produced other Cedar Mountain dinosaurs, including the ankylosaur Peloroplites cedrimontanus from the Ruby Ranch Member.

Industry observers note that utah’s first known nodosaurid, Animantarx ramaljonesi, was the first dinosaur discovered solely by technology. A radiological technician found it in 1994 by mapping low-level radiation across a quarry near the top of the formation.

Cedarpelta bilbyhallorum was then North America’s only known ankylosaurid from the Lower Cretaceous. Ankylosaurids are the branch of armored dinosaurs known for tail clubs and boxy skulls.

It has been revealed that the UGS expects the latest site to shed light on the transition from the Jurassic to the Cretaceous. A catastrophic event at the end of the Jurassic led to the near-complete extinction of Utah’s Morrison Formation dinosaurs, including Stegosaurus.

Across most of the western North American mountain belt, the gap between Jurassic and Cretaceous rocks spans 20 million to 25 million years.

In eastern Utah, microfossils, chemical signatures and uranium-lead dating of ancient soils cut it to five million to seven million years.

Kirkland, DeBlieux and two colleagues reported that revised dating in a 2025 conference abstract. In those rocks, an increase in larger coelurosaurs, a group of meat-eating dinosaurs, documents the extinction.

Sources indicate that the ancient soils also point to a short wet interval after the drier Late Jurassic. Where Early Cretaceous wetlands formed in eastern Utah appears to have been controlled by movement of ancient salt deposits.

Kirkland has argued that the same salt tectonics explain why Utah’s oldest Cretaceous dinosaurs are restricted to northern Grand County.

As buried salt beds were squeezed into ridges, nearby ground subsided, and Lower Cretaceous sediments collected in the low areas.

Across much of the rest of North America, erosion was removing rock at the time. Salt movement peaked in the early Mesozoic, as reflected by the Salt Valley anticline at Arches National Park and the Moab fault.

The information was obtained from the Utah Geological Survey.
