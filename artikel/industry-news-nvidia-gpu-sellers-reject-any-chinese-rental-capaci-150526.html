CNBC reported on November 26, 2026 that Nvidia chip sellers have told neocloud operators, which rent out accelerated computing capacity, they will not supply GPUs if any of the resulting computing capacity will be rented to Chinese companies, citing two people familiar with the private communications.

According to recent reports, the condition is broader than Nvidia’s earlier position, according to the report: the company had said it would not sell to customers renting more than 50% of their GPUs to Chinese companies, whereas the seller messages reject any Chinese rental capacity. That moves tenant screening directly into hardware procurement across a supply chain running from chip sellers to neoclouds.

The disclosure follows a related development on November 25, 2026, when some Nvidia GPU sellers were seeking replacement buyers for chips apparently intended for data centers where Chinese companies would rent access. The earlier development concerned resale; the new one is an explicit condition on purchases.

According to recent reports, the seller messages followed the Bureau of Industry and Security’s November 16, 2026 announcement of an interim final rule that took effect November 19, 2026. BIS said the measure is designed to prevent remote-computing arrangements at third-country data centers from circumventing existing restrictions on sensitive U.S. technology, bringing remote-access controls into closer alignment with controls on physical shipments.

The rule requires a license when there is knowledge that covered advanced-computing items being exported, reexported or transferred within a country are destined for data centers worldwide that provide, or will provide, access to more than 12 million aggregate TPP to companies headquartered in Macau or a Country Group D:5 destination, including China, or to companies whose ultimate parent is headquartered in those locations.

In a recent development, aggregate TPP is the sum of the covered integrated circuits’ or commodities’ total processing performance. Digitally or physically linked data centers can be combined toward that threshold when they share resources as a unified whole. Applications are reviewed case by case.

In a recent development, exporters and foreign data centers are expected to check customers for direct or indirect ownership or control by persons located or headquartered in those destinations, including through an ultimate parent. A data center’s refusal or inability to provide sufficient compliance information is a red flag that must be resolved before proceeding.

Sources indicate that A later change from a permitted end use to a prohibited one can require BIS authorization. Data centers already providing covered compute before publication must obtain a license within 90 days of the effective date to continue without facing future export restrictions.

Computing used exclusively for commercial recommender systems is excepted when required information is submitted; systems generating novel text, images, audio, video, software or other content do not qualify. Separate destination-based license requirements apply to covered items sent to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand or Brazil, with an exclusion for end users that are headquartered in the United States or have a U.S.-headquartered ultimate parent, provided reporting requirements are met.

U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer told CNBC’s Squawk Box on November 25, 2026 that the Commerce rules were intended to close loopholes involving indirect access. He described both smuggling through places such as Southeast Asia and remote access from data centers in third countries.

Industry observers note that the reported condition and the rule move compliance in the same direction: tenant screening at the point of GPU purchase, backed by ownership checks and authorization obligations that continue after deployment. Under the reported condition, a neocloud’s hardware access depends on the customers its capacity will serve, wherever the data center is located.
