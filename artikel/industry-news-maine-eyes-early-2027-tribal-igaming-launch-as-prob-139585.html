Industry News: Maine Eyes Early 2027 Tribal iGaming Launch as Problem-Gambling Funding Draws Scrutiny

Maine's Gambling Control Unit is heading toward an early 2027 launch of tribal-run online casinos after a single public hearing. A former lawmaker warns that the state's behavioral health system is too thin for a possible rise in gambling addiction, while a federal lawsuit still targets the tribes' exclusivity.

Maine’s regulator is moving toward an early 2027 launch of tribal-run online casinos. Only one public hearing has been held so far. WGME’s I-Team reported on Nov. 16 that the Gambling Control Unit began iGaming rulemaking in June. Residents in the report raised a separate concern. They worry the state lacks the mental health and problem-gambling resources to cope.

Maine will become the eighth state with legal online casinos. Its four Wabanaki tribes hold exclusive licenses under LD 1164, the Wabanaki internet gaming act. The Legislature lists the measure as enacted as Public Law 538.

Recent findings suggest that the money set aside for addiction services is small next to the state’s cut. The legislative fiscal note sends 2% of adjusted gross iGaming receipts to the Gambling Addiction Prevention and Treatment Fund. The state’s own 18% share is projected at $1.8 million in fiscal 2025-26 and $3.6 million in fiscal 2026-27.

Critics doubt the system can absorb the demand. Patty Locuratolo Hymanson, a neurologist and former state lawmaker, told WGME that Maine lacks the behavioral health resources to handle a possible rise in gambling addiction from online casinos.

Lawmakers reshaped the revenue split before passage. A committee amendment raised the state’s share from 16% to 18%. The same amendment cut the opioid treatment fund’s allocation from 3% to 1.5%. The fiscal note also carries ongoing General Fund appropriations of $645,354 for six Gambling Control Unit positions. Those include two criminal intelligence analysts and two auditors.

The regulator itself was skeptical at first. Before the bill became law, Executive Director Milt Champion opposed it in written testimony. He argued iGaming wasn’t warranted while sports wagering was only in its second year.

It has been revealed that rulemaking is still underway. The unit issued its third version of the draft rules on Sept. 30, 2026, and took comments until Oct. 30. Champion has warned that major revisions would force a fourth publication. The third draft keeps the tax rate on adjusted gross receipts unchanged. It also retains the license fee and bond requirements. The regulator is hiring six staff members dedicated to iGaming.

The launch also faces a court fight over tribal exclusivity. Churchill Downs and Oxford Casino Hotel brought their suit against the Gambling Control Unit and Champion in the U.S. District Court for the District of Maine. They are asking the court to declare the law unconstitutional. The challenge goes to the heart of the market’s design, which reserves online casino licenses for the tribes.

The next deadlines are procedural. Under Maine’s rulemaking law, the unit has up to 120 days after comments close to adopt the rules. The Attorney General’s office has up to 150 days from that same date to approve them as legal. The unit’s rulemaking page also lists a public hearing notice marked Nov. 30, 2026, which could change that timetable.
