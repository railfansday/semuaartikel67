The amount of health and safety regulations required in the construction business nowadays means Geoff Brown is happy to be free the daily responsibility of running the family firm he established.

Recent findings suggest that however, some form of welfare protection might have been helpful at the moment he saw Graeme Souness storming down the corridor towards him. “He was threatening to knock my block off,” he recalls.

Recent findings suggest that st Johnstone tea-lady Aggie Moffat had erupted because of the state in which Rangers had left the away dressing-room following a 1-1 draw in February 1991. She made sure Souness knew all about it. Brown was in the boardroom with the directors of both clubs when they heard a “serious commotion” in the foyer. The chairman went outside and was met by Souness, who ‘offered’ to take him outside.

“I did not make a noise of it at the time,” says Brown, who wonders what impact it might have had on Souness’ career had he agreed to take part in the square go the Ibrox manager was suggesting. “David Murray phoned me the following week to thank me for not making a noise about what happened,” he says.

Ownership of St Johnstone – his 38 years at the helm of the club is the longest any individual has owned a professional football club in Scotland in modern times – has afforded Brown, now 83, a ringside seat and very nearly active involvement in so many of the colourful incidents characterising the game in this country.

In a recent development, the Aggie incident is certainly included among those. Despite Brown keeping schtum about the threat from Souness at the time, it’s become one of Scottish football’s most well-rehearsed anecdotes.

In a recent development, rightly so, because the so-called storm in a teacup had profound repercussions. Souness ended up citing the incident as one of the reasons he left Rangers for Liverpool shortly afterwards. Brown, by contrast, has not roamed far. Building houses for others occupied his time. After that proved profitable, it permitted him the luxury of time to devote to saving St Johnstone from the clutches of the receivers.

This was in 1986. The Perth club “were the strongest team in the country”. In other words, they were holding the rest of Scottish football up and were sitting plum last in the pile. One afternoon, shortly after Brown had agreed to take on all the debt, Tayside Safety Committee paid a visit. “There were about ten of them,” he recalls. “And they were put in the boardroom. I said: ‘I am sorry, what are you doing here?’.

Sources indicate that “One replied: ‘We are here to close down the club’. Notices had been given to the previous board that had never been passed on to us. They hadn’t met any of the safety standards at the last inspection, and nothing had been done after the inspections. So they were there to close the club down.”.

Forty years and three major trophies later, Brown can reflect with a degree of satisfaction on his impact on football in his local area. But when he was presented with an Outstanding Contribution Award by first minister John Swinney at the Courier Business awards night in Dundee as recently as October, it was more his success as a housebuilder – he set up GS Brown Construction in 1970 – and charitable work that were being recognised.

Recent findings suggest that as Brown was making his way in the construction business, he and his young family lived in a council house in Tulloch. It looked on to berry fields, owned by a farmer called Bruce McDiarmid.

Industry observers note that around twenty years later, as the new owner of St Johnstone FC, he met McDiarmid to negotiate a land deal. The trouble was, St Johnstone had no money to buy the fields on which it was being proposed they could build a new stadium. Asda’s interest in buying their then stadium, Muirton Park, came to light shortly afterwards.

Sources indicate that brown offered McDiarmid a 20 per cent stake in the club for the land, a deal the farmer’s lawyer advised him to reject. But McDiarmid accepted it. When Asda later offered £5 million for the Muirton Park site Brown cannily persuaded the supermarket firm to cover the cost of the newly introduced stadium instead. Some might still be under the impression, incorrect as it turns out, that McDiarmid gifted the land to St Johnstone, perhaps with the sole condition that they had to name the stadium after him.

Recent findings suggest that “He certainly did not,” states Brown. “One way or the other, it did take a bit of persuading. I went with him to see his lawyer, who was older than Bruce. ‘No,’ the lawyer remarked. ‘You don’t really want that. If you are going to sell it, sell it’. Within a year I had Asda going to give us £5 million for Muirton Park. If you are sitting with 20 per cent of that you are not doing too bad…”.

According to recent reports, brown’s new book is called Building My Life – shades of Alex Ferguson’s Managing My Life – but it could be called The Art of the Deal. It includes lots of wrangling and horse-trading, sometimes involving actual horses (As well as football, Brown is also a renowned racehorse owner). He doesn’t always come out on the right side of such negotiations, such as when the players were negotiating bonuses at the start of the 1998-99 season.

Industry observers note that “That was a disaster,” he states. “The players – Paul Kane, Roddy Grant, Allan Preston – they were at (managing director) Stewart Duff because you always agree the bonuses at the start of the year. They are sitting arguing about bonuses for each potential position. And they come to 4th place. ‘4th place – are you happy with £250,000?’ Stewart asked. ‘Aye, go on then,’ I told him. ‘Now they are asking me for third place,’ he says.

Industry observers note that “I said: ‘Stewart, they will never get third’. He indicates: ‘I agree with you. So should we just give them half a million?’”.

In a recent development, reader, they got third place, qualifying for the Uefa Cup in the process. “To this day the ones like Preston and Kane keep rubbing it in,” Brown says. “They tell me: ‘The one thing you got wrong with St Johnstone is that day you gave us too much money!’.

“At that time, European football meant you ran at a loss. Because you’re hiring planes in the holiday period. Chartering a plane was expensive. Plus the (bonus) money we were paying players.”.

Not that Brown regrets any of it. “It’s what you’re in it for, isn’t it?” he notes. Now out of it after selling his majority stake in the club to Atlanta-based lawyer Adam Webb, he can enjoy attending games such as this weekend’s Tayside derby against Dundee United at Tannadice. At home games he sits in the Geoff Brown Stand. “The best bit is, whatever the scoreline, I can sleep afterwards,” he says.

Stewardship of a football club can take a toll, clearly. But Brown made some good friends, none better than Paul Sturrock. Of all the managers he employed, he was the one with whom he shared the closest relationship, to the extent that Brown’s wife Joyce regarded him as one of the family. “He’d let himself into the house and put the kettle on,” recalls Brown.

Industry observers note that even adversaries were entertained, such as Ron Dixon. The colourful one-time owner of Dundee actually bought Brown’s family home in St Madoes for those rare times when he was in Scotland. The Canadian complained to the St Johnstone owner that he’d been sold a pup when buying the Dens Park club. “They told me I was getting a sleeping giant!” he lamented. Brown told Sturrock. “Aye, they’re all lying in the cemetery!” the former Dundee United winger replied.

Memo to Dundonians of either hue: Brown has a few arch things to say about Dundee natives. However, citizens of the fair city of Perth will enjoy Brown’s book, likewise anyone partial to a classic rags-to-riches tale.
