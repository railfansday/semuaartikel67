What Your Coneflowers Need in November – 5 Tips to Get Them Winter-Ready and Help Backyard Birds

Coneflowers (Echinacea spp.) are tough, low-maintenance perennials that are staples in many gardens. Their tall blooms bring long-lasting color and attract pollinators like bees and butterflies.

Though considered low-care perennials, these flowers are about as close to no-care as a flower can be. By the time November rolls around, these beautiful plants don’t need much. In fact, when you are growing coneflowers, it’s more important to know what to avoid than to complete a task list.

I’ve pulled together a list of the top five things to do – or not do – to your coneflowers in November while you're busy winterizing the garden. The primary goals are wildlife support, disease control, and frost protection.

Recent findings suggest that coneflowers bloom with cheerful, daisy-like flowers from early summer through fall. By November, the plants have faded, and cutting them back may seem like the obvious next step.

But don’t be tempted to prune coneflowers in the fall unless you are a total garden neatnik. It’s far better to wait to prune until spring. If you leave the dried seed heads standing, you give songbirds a valuable food source to help them make it through the winter. Goldfinches, chickadees, and cardinals will thank you. In addition, the hollow stems of the coneflowers provide habitat for overwintering beneficial insects. Initiative to prune them in early spring.

In a recent development, every rule has an exception, and it’s best not to prune coneflowers in November unless the foliage is diseased. Whenever you see sick foliage – in this case, coneflowers with powdery mildew, leaf spots, or aster yellows – it’s high time for a haircut.

Recent findings suggest that cut back any diseased or pest-infested coneflower stalks to a few inches above the soil. Toss all the trimmings in the trash, not the compost heap. Don’t forget to sanitize your pruners between plants and after you are done. Equate 91% Isopropyl Alcohol from Walmart is a handy option – simply dilute it to around 70% before using it to disinfect the blades.

Recent findings suggest that if powdery mildew is a recurring problem, consider keeping Bonide Neem Oil Spray on hand for next growing season. Applied at the first signs of infection, it can help control the disease and prevent it from spreading, provided the product label covers the intended use.

Sources indicate that coneflowers are hardly prima donnas, but they do insist on well-draining soil. Waterlogged soil causes root and crown rot in coneflowers, sad endings for such beautiful perennials.

According to recent reports, the easiest way to check your flowers’ drainage is to wait for a late autumn rain. Let the rain do its thing, then – after an hour or two – head out to the coneflower bed and take a look. If you see standing water or water pooling on the soil for hours after the rain stops, your drainage is poor.

Industry observers note that poor drainage is not a death sentence. You can remedy this by layering a mixture of compost and, where appropriate, horticultural grit, such as this 1–2 mm coarse horticultural grit from Home Depot. This will redirect the flow of water away from the core roots.

You can consider transplanting the perennial in spring to a location with better drainage.

Organic mulch helps keep your coneflowers’ roots protected through the winter. Spread a loose layer – 2 to 3 inches (5–7.5 cm) deep – around the base of the plants to shield the root zone from extreme temperatures and keep weeds away.

A natural shredded mulch, such as Miracle-Gro Organic All Natural Mulch from Home Depot, is a good choice. But take care: don’t let the mulch touch the main crown, or crown rot may develop.

Industry observers note that perennials with their roots in the ground are less vulnerable to winter temperatures than potted plants. While coneflowers are tall plants with stems that can reach 4 feet (1.2 m), dwarf varieties grow well in containers.

These smaller perennials will come back every year if you provide them adequate winter protection. First, move the containers close to a structure in a sheltered spot. The Bamworld rolling plant caddies from Amazon make moving heavy pots much easier.

If your hardiness zone is USDA 5–9, you may be able to overwinter them outside with adequate protection. Mound mulch around the pots for insulation, or try the leaf insulation trick. For extra protection, wrap the containers in a layer of Valibe frost-protection fabric.

In cooler zones, move your potted coneflowers into a cold, unheated garage or shed where their roots will be protected from extreme freezing temperatures. Keep the soil barely moist through dormancy, but never waterlogged, and empty any saucers after watering.

Industry observers note that think of these four tasks as the biggest things NOT to do with coneflowers in November. Avoiding them will almost guarantee your coneflowers greet spring happy and healthy.
