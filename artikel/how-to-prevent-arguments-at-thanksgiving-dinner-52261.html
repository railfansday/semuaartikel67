Sources indicate that the days leading up to Thanksgiving can feel like a whirlwind. Between figuring out who’s bringing what and dodging holiday traffic, nerves can wear thin before the first guest arrives. We wrestle the extra leaf out of the closet to stretch the table a little longer, and someone has been up since dawn coaxing the turkey toward golden. By the time everyone sits down, the coats are piled high on a bed down the hall and someone is sneaking a roll before grace. Then, somewhere between the first helping and the pie, a loaded remark cuts across the table and the tension becomes palpable. We spend weeks preparing the meal, yet the conversations shared over it rarely receive the same forethought.

Recent findings suggest that over the years, journalists have asked me how to keep Thanksgiving conversations from boiling over, and I always say we can’t control what others say or do, but we can choose how we respond. The key is remembering the person across the table matters more than proving a point.

It has been revealed that before the gathering, it helps to take an inventory of ourselves. I think of it like packing for a trip somewhere new. We check the forecast and pack for the weather we expect, knowing the skies can still shift once we land. In the same way, we can reflect on which topics might feel sensitive this year.

If some relatives tend to bring up challenging subjects, a quick heads-up can make those conversations easier to navigate. We might say, “I’ve been looking forward to being together, and it would mean a lot if we could keep things lighter this year.” Sharing this means everyone can arrive on the same page and enjoy one another’s company.

Thanksgiving conversations have a way of wandering into topics no one meant to open. A comment lands the wrong way and suddenly we’re deciding whether to let it go or respond. A racing heart can be a cue to take a breath and pause. In that pause, we can ask ourselves three questions:.

Does this need to be remarked right now?

Does this need to be said by me?

According to recent reports, these questions can help us decide how, if at all, to engage. If the answer to any of them is no, we can steer the conversation toward common ground. We don’t have to follow every conversation into the weeds. Sometimes the kindest thing we can do for a conversation is to decide not to have it.

Industry observers note that at the same time, steering clear of hard topics isn’t always the answer. A difficult conversation doesn’t have to strain our relationships. Research on Thanksgiving discussions suggests that college students whose families encouraged open conversation reported more positive effects on family closeness and less stress afterward.

A disagreement does not have to become an argument. Much depends on whether we are trying to prove a point or to understand someone. Scholarship on serial arguments indicates that people often care more about working things out together than gaining the upper hand. Arguments tend to take hold once difference feels like a threat, pulling us into an either/or mindset where one view must prevail over the other. To stay on the side of understanding, we can ask ourselves, “How can I express my views while still showing respect and valuing this person?” If a conversation feels uncomfortable, we might say, “I hear you, and I hold a different perspective.” Sentiments like these help others feel heard, even when we see things differently.

Sources indicate that of course, it wouldn’t be Thanksgiving without someone bringing up that topic—the one half the family has been hoping to avoid. We try to change the subject, and for a few minutes the conversation moves on to football and Black Friday deals. Then, just as everyone relaxes, that same relative finds a way back to the topic. It’s understandable to feel frustrated, yet shutting down the conversation can create more distance. After all, this is someone who matters to us, and there may be something more going on beneath the surface.

Difficult conversations can be complex webs of unspoken needs and hidden emotions. Maybe our relative is trying to make sense of something they’re going through, or looking for connection without knowing how to express that need. They may feel anxious or unheard. Or, there may be reasons we haven’t considered at all.

Recent findings suggest that without fully knowing the reason, some thoughtfulness can go a long way. It’s worth considering the experiences that may be influencing their viewpoint. Stepping into another’s shoes, even for a moment, can foster understanding and empathy. Offered sincerely, an affirmation such as “I can tell how much you care about this” conveys they are seen and heard, which can change the tone of the whole conversation.

Industry observers note that other times, we watch from the sidelines as two relatives barrel toward an argument we all saw coming. Family arguments often gain momentum through the quick volley of replies, each one arriving a little faster and a little louder than the last. Before long, the momentum takes over in ways the original comment never would have on its own.

It has been revealed that in situations like these, we can interrupt the pace, not the people. A calm invitation such as “This feels a little tense. Maybe we can pause this for now” can slow things down, and families tend to respond more openly to understanding than to judgment. Asking a relative to help clear dishes or check on the pie breaks the rhythm without putting anyone on the spot, giving each person a graceful way to step back, dignity intact. The goal isn’t to referee or get everyone to agree, but to give the whole table a chance to reset.

Keeping the peace at Thanksgiving doesn’t require everyone to share the same point of view, and understanding someone else’s doesn’t mean giving up our own. We can, however, choose how we treat one another, even when we disagree.
