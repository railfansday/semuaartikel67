The old story about stress is that you wanted to avoid it wherever possible, and you especially wanted to avoid responding to stressors if you could. You wanted to be calm, untroubled, and chill most of the time.

This old story has been found to be absurdly, wildly wrong, but still it persists like a bad dream, while those of us who respond powerfully to life's ups and downs have been taught to question ourselves constantly (and then stress out about our stress responses).

The new story may surprise you, because it focuses on the strengthening power of stressors, the health benefits of a strong stress response, and the cognitive interventions that can help you access the healing heart of stress.

In a recent development, first things first. Stress is not a thing that happens to you; it's a deeply individual response to something that matters to you.

An alternative definition for the word stress is to underscore, highlight, or focus on something because it's important to you. Your stress responses are connected to how much importance you place on a potential stressor. The higher your stress response, the more importance the stressor has for you.

If your stress response is high, you're not failing at being chill, and the stressor didn't make you react; you're marking something as deeply crucial and worthy of your focus and attention. So the first intervention is to focus on what's important to you.

In her book The Upside of Stress: Why Stress is Good for You and How to Get Good at It, researcher Kelly McGonigal focuses on a research-backed group of simple mindset interventions that help people reframe their relationship with their stress responses.

Recent findings suggest that instead of trying to get you to calm down, McGonigal helps you identify and then write about the values that are most crucial to you. This might seem to be a strange approach, but the connection between importance and your stress response is very clear. As such, connecting to your values can help you identify and then learn about the things that mean the most to you.

With these interventions, your stress responses can help you develop deep knowledge about who you are and what's important to you. And perhaps, instead of seeing yourself as a stressed-out mess, you can reframe yourself as a person who feels deeply and cares deeply.

If you've been exposed to any form of stress management, mindfulness, or meditation that pathologizes your stress responses, you need to read The Upside of Stress and support yourself with healthier ideas.

In part 2 of this series, I focused on a surprising and heartening aspect of our newer knowledge about stress: The strength of your stress response is not a problem at all, because it's directly connected to your resilience and your skills.

In a recent development, you may have heard the saying, "Teach your butterflies to fly in formation." This comes from people like actors, athletes, skydivers, and other professionals who choose to be in stressful situations as a part of their livelihoods. Research has shown that high-performing people don't develop lower stress responses over time; they stay high. But what changes over time is their relationship to their stress responses.

Instead of fretting or trying to manage, suppress, or downregulate their stress responses, high-performing people learn to reframe their heightened activation as necessary for preparation and mastery. Many even learn to see their robust stress responses as zest for life.

This is true even for people like skydivers, whose work (or play) is literally life-endangering. Their stress responses are valuable forms of activation that prepare them for uncertainty (and fun).

Learning to reframe your stress responses means learning how to support your necessary activation as you face uncertainty. Stress is good for you.

Sources indicate that in part 1 of this series, I focused on the "weasel word" of stress, which hides the true, emotional nature of our stress responses from us. Unmasking our emotions and learning how to identify and work with the energy and focus they bring to us means that we can develop robust skills for times when the chips are down, and our deepest values are engaged.

It has been revealed that researcher Lisa Feldman Barrett (and colleagues) have shown that simply identifying and naming our emotions with precision can help us develop strong emotion regulation skills—with no other intervention. So, if we persist in misidentifying our emotions with the weasel word of stress, we can't develop those skills.

Recent findings suggest that in part 1, I identified the three powerful emotions that are usually a part of our stress response (anxiety, panic, and anger), and I offered some ideas for how to work with them intentionally. My book, The Language of Emotions, focuses on how we can learn to access the astonishing genius in all of our emotions.

Another old story about stress is that our brains react to the world: A stressor "happens," and our brains and bodies react without thought or choice. But researchers have understood for decades that our brains are predicting the future instead of reacting like hapless jack-in-the-boxes.

This predictive nature means that our brains are using our stores of past knowledge to help us make predictions and decisions about what may happen next.

If we believe outdated and damaging information about our stress responses, that bad information will become a self-perpetuating part of our predictions, and we'll misread our activation as a problem instead of a necessary part of preparation, resilience, and mastery.

When we have better information about stress, the values we hold, the importance of our strong stress responses, and the emotional truth hidden inside the weasel word of stress, we can reframe stress as necessary. And we can set ourselves up for better predictions in the future.
