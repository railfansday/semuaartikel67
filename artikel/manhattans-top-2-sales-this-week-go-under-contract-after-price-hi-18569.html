The top-two sales in Manhattan last week were in signature luxury buildings that have seen very strong sales and were able to raise their prices from initial launch.

Recent findings suggest that the No. 1 contract, a 17th floor unit at 1122 Madison Ave., is pending with an ask of $38.75 million, a bit over its original ask of $38 million in January.

Sources indicate that the next highest contact, Penthouse B at The Henry on 211 W. 84th St., went under contract with an ask of $28.75 million, a hefty bump over its August 2024 price tag of $24.95 million.

Both units were sponsor sales in upcoming construction, and are full-service luxury buildings with renowned architects and designers. The buildings essentially look at each other from across Central Park, one on the upper east side, one on the upper west.

"This is a sponsor strategy," says luxury broker Michael Reisor, of the Compass-affiliated Reisor.Team in Manhattan, who is familiar with both locations. "It's escalating pricing based on absorption.".

Escalating pricing can create a sense of exigency in the buyer, offering a strong incentive not to dilly-dally with an offer, as the price will likely increase over time.

It has been revealed that "The initial release pricing will always go up as the market absorbs apartments," he states. "This gives people the urgency of, 'If we don't act now and secure the apartment, then it's just going to get more expensive.'".

Sources indicate that however, the early release strategy "doesn't always work unless buildings are this in demand," the broker indicates of the two top sales. The strategy is obviously successful, as the first building has only one unit left, and the second has only four.

Both complexes have highly desired locations as well being architecturally significant with bold names attached: Studio Sofield for 1122 Madison Ave. and Robert A.M. Stern Architects (RAMSA) for The Henry.

Recent findings suggest that nor did either development spare expense on finishes, materials, appliances, or amenities. (There is one key difference, however: The Madison Ave. building doesn't have a parking garage.).

It has been revealed that additionally, both are boutique buildings with a controlled number of residences: 26 for the first, and 45 for the second. With buildings like this, states Reisor, there usually is no downward negotiation on pricing.

Recent findings suggest that "If you're selling really well, especially on a certain line, developers are always looking to maximize what they can get," agrees Manhattan broker Lisa K. Lippman of Brown Harris Stevens, who is familiar with both buildings.

She states that once The Henry sold its penthouses rapidly, it was off to the races in terms of price.

In February, the 1122 Madison building's penthouse duplex went under contract for $89.5 million, setting a record for a condo on the Upper East Side, and also giving leeway for the building to raise prices.

Lippman, who heads up sales at another Stern building, 200 W. 88th St., has just raised prices on its A line due to its popularity, thanks to it being a corner line with four-bedroom layouts.

"We sold so many of them that we felt we could raise prices there," she states.

"When a building debuts and signs a significant number of contracts in a relatively short period, the sponsor may recognize that the remaining inventory is worth more than originally anticipated," agrees Michelle Griffith of Douglas Elliman, who has shown clients in both buildings.

She notes that there is a strong pricing difference between new development and resale, saying, "On the resale side, it's generally less common for sellers to raise an asking price once a property is already listed.".

The 1122 Madison Avenue full-floor condo comes with five bedrooms, five bathrooms, two balconies, a powder room, a library, a home office, and a laundry room. Then there's the sweeping Central Park and Metropolitan Museum of Art views from the great room, library, eat-in kitchen, and primary bedroom suite.

Despite being built in 2026, the building has a convincing prewar look. The unit boasts European white oak Parquet de Versailles-inspired flooring in the entrance hall and entertaining spaces, and quarter sawn European white oak plank flooring in all living areas, as well as soaring 11-foot high ceilings.

The impressive amenity suite contains two floors of leisure and wellness spaces, with a squash court with a retractable basketball hoop, a fitness center programmed by The Wright Fit, a private training studio with Pilates reformer, a spa with a steam room, sauna, and cold plunge, and a sports simulator.

On the lower level is a drawing room, a residents' lounge with bar, private dining room, billiard room, media room, game room, and children's playroom.

All but one unit is sold out, yet the complex won't be completed until fall of 2027.

Griffith says the building is doing particularly well because it's a boutique, brand-new condo in a neighborhood that is "largely defined by older co-op buildings.".

The sales agent, Cathy Franklin of Corcoran, told Realtor.com she not wish to comment.

Across the park at The Henry, Penthouse B offers a full-floor with six bedrooms, 6.5 bathrooms, a living room, library, and formal dining room, chef's kitchen with an eat-in area and a large pantry, and terrace with the ultimate perk of Central Park views.

Industry observers note that designed by Robert A.M. Stern Architects, the building showcases the high quality work the atelier is known for, including "a classic facade of Indiana limestone and hand set brick," along with prewar-style touches such as "terrace setbacks, bay windows and decorative detailing—emblematic of the best Stern buildings.".

The week ending Nov. 15 saw 34 luxury (over $4 million) contracts signed, 11 fewer than the week before, according to Olshan Realty. As usual, condos out sold co-ops, but at 17 condos versus 12 co-ops, the gap had slimmed considerably since the week before.

Experts say that co-op sales, which often come with an arduous board-approval process, are increasing to due to a lack of inventory.

In a recent development, however, there will always be those who prefer an older building with a classic New York feel. The two top buildings this week managed to convincingly create that prewar (pre-1939) feel while being newly constructed.
