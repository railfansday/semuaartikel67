Bradley Barcola should take Thierry Henry’s advice over inability to shoot

When Bradley Barcola was being created at the football factory, shooting was clearly a Friday afternoon job.

There is a lot to like about Liverpool’s third most expensive signing of all time. For a start, he’s absolutely rapid. Only Harvey Barnes has hit a higher top speed this season than Barcola’s 35.9 km/h.

Industry observers note that that kind of pace should make Barcola one of the most feared wingers in the Premier League, the kind of player that full-backs dread playing against. The kind of player that it used to be mentioned only Kyle Walker could deal with.

And yet, Barcola is all bark but no bite.

He has shown on numerous occasions this season that yes, he may get past you, but he’s not going to do a lot with it.

It has been revealed that barcola has an xG of 2.1 in total for this season, putting him 49th in the league and just 0.1 behind his teammate Florian Wirtz. But unlike the 48 players above him, Barcola has failed to score a goal and even that stat is a little generous.

The thing about Barcola is not only he is bad at shooting, he is particularly bad at getting shots away. The Frenchman has had 56 touches in the opposition box this season, the sixth most of any player in the Premier League, and yet has managed 15 shots in that time. Alexander Isak, who has just one touch more, has got a shot away 31 times.

Sources indicate that this is not a fresh trend either. Barcola had the fourth most touches in the box in Ligue 1 last year and got 11 goals, making him the joint eighth top scorer in the division.

According to recent reports, that hesitancy to shoot was best displayed in the 4-0 thumping of United. In the 68th minute, when a depleted United back line was caught too high up, Barcola broke clear and got into the box but the ball got caught under his legs, allowing the United defenders to recover and block a shot that in the end had an xG of 0.39. Had he got it away much quicker, that xG would have been a lot higher.

In a recent development, the point isn’t missed on Barcola either. His only contribution from a G/A point of view on Sunday was an assist for Rio Ngumoha after the former PSG man had broken clear.

“I am unsatisfied with my match” he told French broadcaster Canal Plus. “I know that I can do a lot better and I know my abilities. I come out of it with an assist, [but] I could have scored three or four goals.”.

After a 4-0 thumping of your biggest rivals, no one is going to cry too much about the wasted chances but in the tougher matches, either in the latter stages of the Premier League or the Champions League, you cannot afford to have a player be so blunt up front.

Barcola reminds you of another winger who used to play for Liverpool. Change his running style and you would find yourself looking at a copy of Raheem Sterling.

123 goals in the Premier League does not scream someone who is poor in front of goal and while that would be a little harsh, Sterling didn’t half miss some golden opportunities.

He shared that ability to turn a golden chance into one that was stuck under his feet. That wasteful nature is best summed up by Sterling being 19th in the all-time list for Premier League goalscorers but having played more games than 10 players ahead of him.

The hope for Liverpool is that Iraola can coach Barcola into becoming a better finisher but Sterling worked under Pep Guardiola and was never able to master that ability. Barcola has also come from working under Luis Enrique, hardly a Sunday League manager.

Iraola has noticed Barcola’s wastefulness too, letting slip an answer that was meant as a compliment.

“I think it’s an assist that is a big merit assist because he was coming from very good movements, arriving against the ‘keeper and not scoring a couple of quite clear situations,” Iraola said.

There is a debate over whether finishing is something you are born with, some arguing that it is a skill in football that can never really be learned, but perhaps Barcola can take solace in the words of France’s greatest ever goalscorer, Thierry Henry.

Industry observers note that “I was quick,” Henry told the Blizzard back in 2014. “I had to have 10 chances to convert one into a goal – but at the same time, I kept creating these chances. Then I told myself: ‘You won’t have these chances all the time. You must stick them into the net.’.

What Henry said next is what Barcola should most listen to for it could be the key to becoming an elite finisher.

It has been revealed that “Then, to avoid over-thinking in front of the keeper you work on your finishing, so that it all becomes automatic, so that you don’t think anymore. The hardest thing for an attacking player? When he has time to think.”.

According to recent reports, barcola is one of the quickest players in the league and maybe it is that very pace that is also making him a problem in the box. Barcola has too much time to think and having not scored in the league this season, the voice inside his head reminding him of that fact in crucial moments will only get louder.

Sources indicate that for £123m, you can’t really be described as “not a finisher” and Barcola clearly has to improve in that area. He is playing in the position of Mo Salah, one of the best finishers in Premier League history, and if he can bring even half that effectiveness, it will be a substantial step from where he is today.
