Sources indicate that from Croteau v. Family 5, Inc., decided Wednesday by Florida Court of Appeal Chief Judge Jonathan Gerber, joined by Judges Marlon Weiss and Johnathan Lott:.

It has been revealed that in this personal injury case, the plaintiff appeals from the circuit court's final summary judgment which found … that the plaintiff could not prove her negligence action against a bar which had served her alcoholic beverages before she was injured later that night. The summary judgment rested on the circuit court's application of "the dram shop statute"—section 768.125, Florida Statutes (2020):.

In a recent development, liability for injury or damage resulting from intoxication. A person who sells or furnishes alcoholic beverages to a person of lawful drinking age shall not thereby become liable for injury or damage caused by or resulting from the intoxication of such person, except that a person who willfully and unlawfully sells or furnishes alcoholic beverages to a person who is not of lawful drinking age or who knowingly serves a person habitually addicted to the use of any or all alcoholic beverages may become liable for injury or damage caused by or resulting from the intoxication of such minor or person.

The underlined second exception is at glitch here…. In addressing "what constitutes 'knowledge' in order for a vendor's conduct to be found negligent," the [Florida Supreme Court has] found:.

Sources indicate that [T]he cause of action [under section 768.125's second exception] only requires evidence that the vendor had knowledge that the individual the vendor served was a habitual drunkard. Serving an individual multiple drinks on one occasion would be insufficient, in and of itself, to establish that the vendor knowingly served a habitual drunkard alcoholic beverages. On the other hand, serving an individual a substantial number of drinks on multiple occasions would be circumstantial evidence to be considered by the jury in determining whether the vendor knew that the person was a habitual drunkard….

According to recent reports, [In another case,] we affirmed a summary judgment for the defendant bar, where the plaintiff patron was unknown to the bar's staff, was not a bar regular, and had purchased up to four drinks over two to three hours. We held that we could not impute notice of the patron's alcohol addiction to the bar when the patron had not shown any signs of intoxication, despite his alcohol consumption ….

Here, consistent with [those precedents], the bar's serving the alcoholic beverages to the plaintiff for the first time on the incident date, without more, should not be considered sufficient evidence to impute knowledge to the bar that the plaintiff was "habitually addicted to the use of any or all alcoholic beverages." ….

According to recent reports, roster v. Moulton (Fla. App. 1992)—the primary case upon which the plaintiff relies—is distinguishable….

Industry observers note that [In Roster,] [i]t is undisputed that [the patron] consumed 8–10, 12–ounce cans of beer and two straight shots of tequila over the period of an hour and a half. After a short absence from [the bar], during which [the patron] drank yet another beer served elsewhere, [the patron] returned to [the bar's] walk-up window, where he ordered and was served another beer. The [bar manager] admitted that it would be unusual for a person to consume that many beers and two shots of tequila without showing signs of intoxication. Despite [the patron's] considerable alcohol consumption, [the patron] did not stagger and did not slur his speech. The manager twice asked [the patron] to leave the bar only because [the patron] became loud and argumentative. [The patron] pled guilty to the charge of driving while under the influence of alcohol and admitted that he had a drinking problem from the age of sixteen, which he still had at the time of the accident. At that time, [the patron] had never received treatment for his alcohol addiction….

By affidavit in Roster, "two experts[] … essentially stated that the large amount of alcohol that [the patron had] consumed over a relatively short time period, together with the lack of impairment [which the patron had] demonstrated, would have put a reasonable server of alcohol on notice that [the patron] was habitually addicted to alcohol. [The bar] submitted no affidavits to oppose these viewpoints." ….

However, unlike in Roster, as the bar's answer brief submits:.

… [W]hatever alcohol [the plaintiff] may have consumed at [the bar], she claims she drank over a five-hour period—much longer than the 90 minutes during which the Roster [patron] consumed 10–12 drinks. Furthermore, [the plaintiff] claims she sat, ordered drink(s), and watched television for five hours, which is much different than the Roster [patron,] who left the bar …, drank another alcoholic beverage elsewhere, and then came back to the bar where he was served yet another drink. Also, unlike … in Roster, there is no evidence that [the plaintiff] demonstrated a level of intoxication consistent with someone who had either a high or low tolerance for alcohol. Finally, unlike the [patron] in Roster, [the plaintiff here] did not become loud or argumentative at the [bar]. By her own admission, she was quiet, not boisterous, not loud, not abusive, and she simply kept to herself….

Unlike the Roster patron, the plaintiff here was an anonymous patron, whose behavior during her visit to the bar was, by her own statements, unremarkable.

Judge Lott agreed, but added that he thought Roster was wrongly decided, including in its reliance on expert evidence:.

Section 768.125, Florida Statutes (2020), indicates that a bar owner is not liable for damage caused by the sale of alcoholic beverages, except that (as relevant here) a person "who knowingly serves a person habitually addicted to the use of any or all alcoholic beverages may become liable." The language does not require expert testimony, and does not ask how the latest version of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) defines pathologies related to alcohol use….

Recent findings suggest that the statutory language asks whether the server has actual knowledge that the person is, as a matter of habit, addicted to alcohol…. You know such a thing when you see it—and it's hard to imagine being able to see it given only a single occasion of indulgence…. [A]nyone can have too many drinks. What separates a "habitual drunkard" [which is how the concurrence elsewhere interprets "habitually addicted"] is that the habitual drunkard feels compelled to do so on account of their addiction, and does so regularly as a matter of habit….

According to recent reports, turning back to Roster—where was the evidence that the defendant actually knew that the plaintiff was habitually addicted to alcohol? The defendant observed the plaintiff drink heavily on one occasion wherein he handled his liquor well, until he didn't. No evidence of "addiction" was presented, much less of a "habit" of it. The expert affidavits about his apparent alcohol tolerance added nothing to that inquiry.

Recent findings suggest that in sum, Roster passed the buck from the statutory language to expert opinion, and reached the wrong result in turn. In an appropriate case, we should consider whether to recede from it….
