MoviePass Explores a Sale, Pitching Streamers on a Theatrical Bundle

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

MoviePass is exploring a sale and has held talks with several potential buyers, including a number of major streaming companies, according to a person familiar with the sale process.

The talks have been underway for roughly six weeks and have reached the executive level, but no formal bid has been made, according to the person.

At the center of the pitch is a hypothetical product: a “movie theater tier” sold as an add-on to a streaming subscription and facilitated by MoviePass.

The talks come as theatrical attendance is recovering, particularly among Gen Z moviegoers, and as media companies race to build direct relationships with audiences.

Film-adjacent consumer brands have also become hot properties, as evidenced by The Upcoming York Times’ recent $330 million bid for Letterboxd, a valuation of more than 20 times its projected 2026 earnings.

No target price has been set, but a valuation based on revenue, rather than earnings, could trade at a multiple of between eight to ten times revenue, the person remarked.

A sale would also mark another turn for a brand best known for burning through cash.

MoviePass did not provide a comment on the record.

The brand still carries outsize recognition, but acquiring customers at scale as an independent tech giant has proven difficult without raising capital, a reality that prompted the sales process, the person remarked.

The deck positions MoviePass as the missing piece of a streamer’s subscription, according to the document.

“Every major streamer owns the home screen,” it reads. “MoviePass completes the picture: the theater, brought into the same subscription—one bundle for the film at home and the film in the cinema.”.

A studio-owned streamer would pay full price for each ticket, but as the film’s distributor, it would recoup roughly half of that price through its share of the box office. As a result, each redemption of the buyer’s own film would cost it roughly $5, according to the document.

If 1 million members convert, the model would produce between $60 million and $78 million in profit per year; at 20 million members, it would yield between $1.2 billion and nearly $1.6 billion annually, according to the document.

Recent findings suggest that critically, the $10 price is simply a stand-in. The final figure, as well as the number of ticket visits it covers, could vary, according to the person.

The appeal of the product is strongest for companies that own both a film studio and a streaming service, according to the person.

Streaming companies have shown the most interest, but other types of buyers, including a data-focused firm, are also in the mix, according to the person.

Industry observers note that the theoretical moat lies in a series of patents that MoviePass holds, including one that lets members pay at any theater that accepts Mastercard without a direct integration with each chain, according to the person. In 2018, MoviePass sued rival Sinemia for patent infringement, and Sinemia shut down its U.S. operations the following year.

The infrastructure could also become a revenue source of its own, licensed to other studio-streamers at $1 per member per month, according to the document.

Any deal remains far from certain. A transaction would require sign-off from many decision-makers at large companies that have not yet decided how to proceed, according to the person.

Recent findings suggest that alice Ma is the co-founder of Mad Realities, the social video studio behind, among other programs, the viral series Shop Cats, which profiles New York City bodegas and the shop cats that prowl them. The organization makes what it describes as television for social media, and in June it joined the roster of Fox Creator Studios, which is now backing new formats like the dating show Love Pitch and the culinary competition Picky Eaters.

Recent findings suggest that as creators and legacy media converge, studios like Mad Realities are carving out a latest tier of programming: pricier than a creator with a phone, but cheaper than traditional TV. In our conversation at Web Summit in Lisbon, Ma and I discussed the origins of the manufacturer, how it develops shows, and the future of social television.

This interview has been edited.

Mark Stenberg: Where did the idea for Mad Realities come from?

Alice Ma: In 2020, at the peak of Clubhouse, I built a Clubhouse competitor for teenage girls in my dad’s garage. It was a really good app, but no one cared because software alone doesn’t have a moat anymore. So I found my co-founder, who had hosted the No. 1 show on Clubhouse, and we asked: “If people want the show, where do you go?” You either wait years for a TV deal or build an entire media business. So we started the network we would want.

Mark: How does a show go from an idea to a series?

Sources indicate that alice: Development looks similar to TV: We develop ideas internally, and we take outside pitches, often from producers. Sometimes a host comes to us with a topic we really want to work on. The difference is that in TV, development means you aren’t making anything, and a pilot is very expensive. For us, we make the pilot by making the show. If we have conviction in it, we order more episodes, and our deals are structured with options.

Mark: How does the business work?

According to recent reports, alice: We’re 10 people, plus contractors, and we raised a seed round. We worked with 25 brands in 2025, and so far, the demand has all been inbound, with 60% to 70% coming directly from brands.

Mark: Is the goal to move toward higher production value?

It has been revealed that alice: More resources against a show doesn’t always have to mean higher production value. I think of production value as unlocking a story that couldn’t have been told before. Picky Eaters, the first show we launched with Fox Creator Studios, is a four-camera cooking competition—nobody could make this in their bedroom with a camera. That being said, if another manufacturer notes it needs five times our budget for the same kind of show, it will lose, because its economics will never be as good.

In a recent development, mark: Creators and marketers often say that YouTube is more appealing because it is more predictable. Do you agree?

Alice: YouTube’s algorithm is more stable and therefore predictable for large creators. But TikTok and other short-form algorithms enable overnight virality and discovery, which means we can test new formats more quickly.
