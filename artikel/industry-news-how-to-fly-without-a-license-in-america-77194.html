Recent findings suggest that gaining a pilot's license can be costly and prohibitive for many, including for those who just want to fly in a hang glider or ultralight aircraft. The FAA created an exemption on the premise that a vehicle light and slow enough poses minimal kinetic risk to people and property on the ground. It ruled that pilots of these aircraft, subject to strict parameters, can be mostly free of its regulations. This allows some people to enjoy the thrill of flying without having to worry about regulatory red tape.

It has been revealed that when the FAA drafted Part 103 in 1982, it drew a hard line. It set tight restrictions on the weight and speed of the aircraft and then permitted the pilot to not be regulated at all. Other countries have gone in a different direction, requiring basic pilot training and registration and relaxing the limitations on the aircraft. Here is what to know about legally flying in the United States without a pilot's license or medical certificate.

Sources indicate that flying is extremely regulated, and pilots are required to have licenses to fly aircraft. But as with many things in life, it's not quite that simple, and there are allowances on the margins. The FAA’s Part 103 rules let you fly an “ultralight vehicle” with no pilot certificate, medical, registration, or airworthiness certificate.

The FAA explains, "Pilots of ultralight vehicles subject to Part 103 are not required to have training or previous experience prior to the operation of these vehicles. You should consider receiving adequate training prior to participation." How the aircraft is advertised does not override FAA regulations. The vehicle may be advertised as "powered ultralight," "hang glider," or "hang balloon", but its weight and performance determine how it is classified under FAA rules.

Buyers are encouraged to be aware of aircraft advertised as meeting the requirements for use as an ultralight vehicle, but do not. Being within Part 103 means operators are not required to have a student, sport, or private pilot license, nor are they legally required to log flight hours or pass an FAA written or practical checkride. A checkride is the practical test a pilot takes to earn a new certificate, rating, or to maintain proficiency.

To qualify as an ultralight vehicle, the aircraft must be single-seat and, if it is powered, it must weigh under 254 lb (115 kg) empty with no more than five gallons (19 liters) of fuel. Its speed is capped at 55 knots at full power and a 24-knot stall speed. Unpowered ultralights, such as hang gliders, must weigh under 155 lb (70 kg). Balloons and gliders are considered unpowered vehicles.

Industry observers note that if the ultralight exceeds these limits, it is deemed to be an aircraft and requires a registration and a certified pilot. An ultralight also cannot be operated under Part 103 if it has been issued a current US or foreign airworthiness certificate. Flying within Part 103 limits operations to daylight only and prohibits operating within congested areas. This includes areas where people have assembled (e.g., a stadium).

In a recent development, pilots operating aircraft under Part 103 are still required to keep ultralights separated from commercial traffic and populated areas. Ultralights are permitted to fly freely in Class G (uncontrolled airspace), but are prohibited from Class B, C, D, or surface-level Class E airspace unless the pilot obtains prior authorization. They must also yield the right of way to all conventional aircraft. Weather conditions are required to be reasonably clear with visual reference to the surface.

In a recent development, due to the strict empty weight and stall speed limits, qualifying powered ultralights fall into four main design categories. These are Powered Paragliders (Paramotors), Powered Parachutes (PPCs), Weight-Shift Control Trikes, and Fixed-Wing Ultralights. Powered Paragliders are the lightest and most portable of the categories and generally consist of a backpack motor unit and a ram-air fabric wing. The motor typically weighs around 46–65 lb (21–29 kg).

Sources indicate that powered Parachutes are wheeled carts that carry a small two-stroke or four-stroke engine suspended under a large, square parachute canopy. These designs provide a very slow flight speed and high stability. Weight-Shift Control Trikes are a type of minimalist hang-glider-style wing mounted above a lightweight three-wheeled carriage. These are steered in pitch and roll by shifting the center of gravity rather than using traditional flight control surfaces.

Industry observers note that fixed-Wing Ultralights are the closest category to a conventional aircraft and resemble traditional miniature aircraft. They are lightweight, low-speed recreational airplanes with rigid wings and conventional three-axis flight controls (ailerons, elevator, and rudder). These aircraft are designed for affordable, accessible sport and leisure flying. U-Fly-It Aerolite 103 (discussed below) makes some aircraft that fit into this category.

In a recent development, the FAA stipulates that ultralight operations can only be conducted as a sporting or recreational activity. The operators of the vehicles are responsible for assessing risk and ensuring their own safety. The FAA also cautions, "the ultralight community is encouraged to adopt good operation practices and programs in order to avoid more extensive regulation by the Federal Aviation Administration.".

It has been revealed that in the US, ultralights can be purchased through specialized dealers, manufacturers, and classified marketplaces such as Barnstormers, Hangar67, and PilotMix. Examples of ultralights on the market include the Northwing ATF Trike, Northwing Maverick Ultralight, Aero-Tech 2000 Lockwood Drifter, and U-Fly-It Aerolite 103. The prices of ultralights vary considerably on the marketplace. Second-hand examples can cost a few thousand dollars; newly introduced craft may cost up to tens of thousands.

U-Fly-It Aerolite 103 says of its products, "The Aerolite 103 Quick-Build Kit has one of the lowest assembly times in the industry. Average for a first time builder is approximately 30-40 hours, and experienced builders can assemble the aircraft in less than 30 hours." It also advertises its Complete Aerolite 103 Quick-Build Airframe Kit at a price of $19,450. Customers have the choice of having the kit assembled through a dealer for an extra fee.

According to recent reports, it should be noted that the information above is regulated by the FAA, and the FAA only has jurisdiction in US airspace. None of this is binding in other countries, and other countries have different sets of rules and regulations. In some cases, other countries are more generous with their allowances than the US, and in other cases, they are more restrictive. That mentioned, all other peer countries are more restrictive in the sense that pilots must uniformly have some sort of basic license or registration.

Among countries like Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia, Newly introduced Zealand, and Europe, the United States appears to be the only country that does not require a pilot license/permit or ultralight registration. At the same time, the minimum ultralight category in some countries can be much heavier than in the US. In Canada, it's 1,200 lb (544 kg MTOW), while it's 661 lb (300 kg) in the United Kingdom.

Some countries (e.g., Australia, Newly introduced Zealand, Europe/EASA) may allow the aircraft to be two-seaters, although the conditions in which a passenger can be carried vary. New Zealand and Australia take a self-administration approach rather than direct government licensing. In Australia, it is controlled under Civil Aviation Order 95.10 and 95.55, and in New Zealand CAA Part 103, with New Zealand borrowing FAA numbering.

The next step up from a Part 103 ultralight in the United States is the FAA Sport Pilot Certificate (governed by 14 CFR Part 61, Subpart J). The FAA created two upcoming pilot certificates in 2004 to enable pilots to operate aircraft that meet specific criteria without the need for a medical certificate. Those certificates were Sport Pilot and Flight Instructor with a Sport Pilot Rating. The Sport Pilot rule was massively expanded by the FAA's MOSAIC final rule, which went into effect in October 2025.

Under these updated rules, light sport aircraft no longer have a 1,320 lb (598 kg) weight limit. The weight limit has been abolished. Under MOSAIC, a light-sport category airplane is permitted to have up to four seats and a VS0 stall of 61 knots or less (14 CFR 22.100). A sport pilot may fly any airplane with a VS1 of 59 knots or less and up to four seats (14 CFR 61.316).

Other regulations include that the cabin must be non-pressurized, it can't have multiple engines, and it must have fixed landing gear. However, sport pilots can now fly retractable landing gear and manually controllable-pitch propellers after receiving specific ground/flight training and an instructor endorsement. Obtaining a Sport Pilot Certificate requires 20 hours total, or half the minimum 40 hours for a Private Pilot.
