Offshore wind turbines may reshape the low clouds around them

🌐 Source: Earth • 📅 19 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 6 min read (1229 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Clouds can change how much electricity offshore wind farms produce, while turbines can also thin low clouds nearby.

Recent findings suggest that low clouds over the sea do more than just block sunlight. The tops of these clouds lose heat to space, which cools the air and keeps it moving.

This moving air also affects the wind turbines below.

In computer simulations, turbines in the last rows produced about 11% more power when the air above the clouds was drier than when it was moister.

Industry observers note that the wind turbines also affected the clouds. When the clouds were low enough to touch the turbine blades, the turbines made them thinner over several miles in the direction of the wind.

Clouds are left out of many simulations of offshore wind farms.

It has been revealed that researchers at the University of Twente in the Netherlands put them in. Their results suggest that planners of large offshore farms should take clouds into account.

All of this was tested in computer simulations, not at real wind farms.

According to recent reports, the clouds in question are stratocumulus. These low, lumpy sheets are the most common cloud type on Earth.

Sources indicate that in an average year, they cover about 25% to 30% of the North Sea. Wind energy projects are widespread there, and more are planned.

Recent findings suggest that the North Sea is cloudy so often that its wind farms’ energy output may be affected, mentioned Davide Selvatici. Selvatici is a postdoctoral researcher in the university’s Physics of Fluids group.

Modelers had often worked with idealized skies that held almost no cloud, stated Richard Stevens. Stevens is a professor of fluid mechanics at Twente.

Sources indicate that thijs van den Ham, a co-author, mentioned wind farm simulations need clouds in them. “They do play a role, and they do matter,” the author added.

Humidity by itself doesn’t do much to a turbine. Moist air and dry air differ in density by less than 1%. Power output changes by about the same amount.

So the team looked at how clouds change the air’s motion. An earlier study by Selvatici and Stevens had already examined this. In that work, the slowed wind behind a simulated farm recovered faster under low clouds.

The simulations were detailed enough to follow individual gusts and swirls of air.

The work was done in two stages. Cloud layers were first simulated without any turbines, and the temperature and moisture of the overlying air were varied. About 300 simulated hours passed before each of those layers reached a steady state.

A wind farm was then added beneath a selection of those cloud layers. It had 60 turbines in 10 staggered rows, and each was rated at 10 megawatts.

Rotors measured 585 feet (178 meters) across, which put the blade tips 682 feet (208 meters) above the water.

The Washington Monument is shorter than that. At such a height, the blades could reach into the lowest simulated clouds.

Recent findings suggest that A block of air 37 miles (60 kilometers) long surrounded the farm in the model. The block was divided into more than 560 million cells.

Two sets of runs were carried out so that the cloud’s effect could be separated from the effect of moisture.

Cooling at the cloud top was held fixed in the first set. In the second set, cooling was allowed to respond to the moisture overhead, which is how real clouds behave.

When cooling was held fixed, the drier and moister cases differed by about 3% in power from the last rows. A gap that small lies within the uncertainty of the simulations.

Once cooling was allowed to respond, the same comparison produced a difference of 11%.

The cloud itself was responsible for the larger difference, according to the team’s analysis.

Heat is emitted upward by a cloud top, while water vapor in the overlying air radiates heat back down. Less heat is returned by drier air, so the top of the cloud cools more promptly.

Industry observers note that that stronger cooling stirred the air underneath more vigorously and the cloud-topped layer deepened. As a result, more of the wind’s energy was transported downward into the farm.

Energy arriving from above is most key at the back of a wind farm.

More than half of the power in the front rows comes from wind that flows in. Each row, however, leaves slower air in its wake for the rows that follow.

In the final rows of the simulated farm, turbulent mixing supplied up to about 90% of a row’s power.

It has been revealed that A second effect appeared when the air above the cloud was moister. The cloud-topped layer was then shallower and the approaching wind was slowed more strongly ahead of the farm. The first row of turbines produced less power as a consequence.

Temperature was also found to matter. In the runs with fixed cooling, the researchers varied the temperature contrast between the sea surface and the air above.

The air temperature was taken about 2 miles (3 kilometers) up. The farm’s output then differed by about 20% from a reference case.

Satellite images had already suggested that wind farms affect clouds.

On two mornings, June 5, 2016, and November 14, 2022, North Sea wind farms were photographed by NASA’s Terra satellite. The images highlighted partly cleared cloud near clusters of wind farms. The team took them from NASA Worldview.

In a recent development, the simulated thinning was strongest under two conditions. The base of the cloud was near the blade tips, and the cloud held little liquid water.

Sources indicate that in the run with the lowest cloud, the cloud layer’s top was 587 feet (179 meters) above the sea. That is below the blade tips.

Behind the farm, that cloud held up to 85% less liquid water than in a matching run without turbines.

Warmer, drier air from above was mixed into the cloud by turbulence from the turbines. More of the cloud’s water evaporated.

Ahead of the farm, the cloud changed the opposite way.

Recent findings suggest that the wind slowed as it approached the first row. In that same run, the cloud there held up to about 43% more liquid water than the incoming cloud.

The authors compared that to the Horns Rev wind farm. There, slowing wind ahead of the turbines has been linked to fog forming upwind.

According to recent reports, every run was set in the daytime, with the sun at a fixed angle. Sunlight weakens a low cloud, and the authors wrote that daytime conditions may have amplified the thinning.

The simulations also had no rain, a single farm layout, and the same large-scale wind in every run.

The simulated clouds thinned in the same general way as the photographed ones. The amounts were not compared.

Large clusters of wind farms weren’t simulated either. The authors wrote that those need more observations and more modeling.

Next, the team looks to simulate more kinds of weather and larger farms spaced farther apart. Selvatici noted that simulating real weather conditions will take more computing power than is available now.

The full study was published in the Journal of Renewable and Sustainable Energy.
