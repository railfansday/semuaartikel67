A few days ago, I attended a talk at Yale’s Jackson School of Global Affairs at the annual Yale Assembly for alumni delegates. When I asked what the most pressing global problems are at the moment, the surprise that caught my ear was “the collapse of the recognition of facts.” When others cherry-pick data that serves their own agenda, it blocks connection. How well we respond to disconnection can have huge implications.

Industry observers note that sometimes it’s hard to know if people don’t want to connect, don’t know how, or both. At a class dinner out, the unexpected topic of parents came up. One parent seemed to find pleasure in music more than in her child. Another parent often noted to his young daughter, who could not possibly read his adult mind, “You know what I mean, right?” Someone else repeatedly said, “You don’t know what it was like,” and yet could not answer his daughter’s questions about what his traumas were actually like.

Distinguishing “won’t connect” from “can’t connect” would help us understand the context of the disconnect, and that clarity helps us move forward.

Sources indicate that music is about connection, but musicians are not all naturally gifted at it. However, to stay afloat in the unsupported, wild west of the music industry, we have to upgrade our communication skills like everyone else. We have to connect with our audiences, supporters, partners, and presenters, even when the other side doesn’t want to – or doesn’t know how to – connect back.

My work in music is often misunderstood to be the work of playing notes quickly and accurately. But it is the moment-to-moment listening and the consequent response that create the spirit of the piece, not the individual notes. This year I am taking my piano studio to perform in Rome, to practice performing as responding to a latest environment in the moment.

Most of my time is spent listening for what is still missing. Building connections between the different musical elements helps me to infer the composer’s intent. In conversation too, creating a tensegrity of multidimensional motivations helps me to tease out what the speaker is not saying, before I make any interpretations.

It has been revealed that author Iain McGilchrist posits that the search for truth requires four avenues of inquiry: science, reason, intuition, and imagination. We have to play with our awareness of not just the past and present, but the future. Only with whole-brain processing (both left and right sides) can we approach the complexity of truth.

Sources indicate that well-meaning people with a trust deficit may regress to hearing only their inner bubble of a few familiar themes and pre-formulated responses. We can challenge that response by labeling it, for example, “It seems like you are angry about this.” Even a wrong guess works, because they will have the urge to correct it, and that reveals something. If we are tolerant enough to wait and listen, we may draw out secrets that will change everything about our connection.

It has been revealed that when we anticipate that a conversation or an encounter will not be pleasant, we greatly reduce its chances of building a connection. Our counterpart, when expecting friction, does the same.

In a recent development, FBI negotiator Chris Voss shares a technique he calls “neutralizing negativity.” When we emotionally prepare the other side by saying “you are not going to like this,” you have taken the sting out of the interaction. I tried this with my Yale Alumni College class last week, where we studied two works by Mozart and Bartók. Before I introduced the difficult music of Bartók, I asked them to notice their posture towards their listening, as compared with how they had listened to the Mozart. While both works are intense, Mozart is associated with easy listening and Bartók with unpleasant dissonance. While listening to the unfamiliar dissonance of Bartók still took a lot of effort, the naming of this negative posture effectively helped people connect more than they would have otherwise.

Industry observers note that A colleague recently shared that when she volunteered to be a mentor, she discovered her mentees just wanted her contacts and did not want to connect. I don’t know whether to cry or laugh, because in my youth, I would have done anything to find mentors who wanted to connect with me and not just my success. I do still believe that with a persistent repetition of stating our values, we can build up both our integrity and their awareness for the potential of future connection. We have more power in calling out these moments than most of us realize or act on.

Meanwhile, the above strategies worked for my business negotiations this week. But even if the connection had failed, I am still learning so much from processing these tricky relational crossroads. As Stephen Nachmanovitch writes in his marvelous book Free Play, “Trusting someone else can involve gigantic risks, and it leads to the even more challenging task of learning to trust yourself.” In music and in life, through the process of reaching out, we connect more deeply with ourselves.
