“Hollywood Rewrites History” and Calls for Authentic Female Characters: Tallinn Debates Cinema Amid Global Crisis

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

What stories should we tell? Who should tell them? And how can they be told with authenticity? Those were some of the topics in focus in a panel discussion entitled “Women, War and Witness: Cinema in Times of Global Crisis” at Tallinn 2026 on Thursday.

Recent findings suggest that it brought together some big names in person and via video call, including Golshifteh Farahani (Alpha, Pirates of the Caribbean: Dead Men Tell No Tales), the France-based Iranian actress; Halkawt Mustafa, the director of Iraq’s international Oscar contender No Paradise If You Are Killed by a Woman, about a female Kurdish sniper who returns to Mosul to rescue her younger sister; and Natalia Libet, a Ukrainian film producer and co-founder of the Kyiv-based women-led production tech giant 2Brave Productions. Also speaking were Mariia Vynogradova, founder of Talented U, a non-profit supporting Ukrainian media professionals and filmmakers in the U.K., and her co-founder and London-based Ukrainian film and TV director Ksenia Bugrimova. Egyptian film critic and programmer Ahmed Shawky moderated the session that was part of the Industry@Tallinn & Baltic Event (ITBE), the market of the Tallinn Black Nights Film Festival (PÖFF) in Estonia, the 30th edition of which runs through this weekend.

Recent findings suggest that ”Hollywood rewrites history,” Farahani argued. “You are on one side, and then white supremacy is on the other side, deciding, giving money, rewriting history. So, what to do? They have the power. They have the studios. They decide.”.

She continued, “It’s just absolute bullshit what Hollywood demonstrates of our region.” She shared an example: “When I left Iran, it was the time when Bush was calling Iran [part of] the axis of evil,” Farahani stated. “I went to America, and all the parts that I was reading were either terrorists, involved in some terrorist action or some party scene in Tehran that was so underground. I was coming fresh from Iran, Tehran. It’s my country, and I knew what was going on there.”.

She couldn’t get herself to misrepresent the life she knew there. “I had to say no to many things, because it was actually [risking] my dignity. Even if that party story was right, and it was beautiful and about a lesbian couple being hammered by the society … I didn’t want to be involved in that because what if some hunters were standing up there saying, ‘Ah, you see what they’re doing!?’ Because of that, we’re going to go and bomb this country.”.

Farahani concluded that she feels both Hollywood and the regimes in various countries are trying to influence the world. “We are victims of propaganda from both sides,” she said. “It’s not only one side.”.

Mustafa also had a bad Hollywood experience when he wanted to make a narrative feature about the man who was hiding Saddam Hussein for many years. But Hollywood folks kept wanting to make it about the heroes who captured Saddam, he shared. He eventually ended up making it as a documentary, Hiding Saddam Hussein, in Norway.

Sources indicate that mustafa, who is an Iraqi Kurd who lives in Norway, noted he has been criticized for not showing certain flags in No Paradise, which he said isn’t part of his storytelling ethic. “They were asking me where is the Kurdish flag and where is the Iraqi flag and where is the Norwegian perspective?” he shared. “I just tried to portray a Kurdish woman and [show] how it is to live on the front line. … It’s about trying to tell something from a human perspective. I just try to find my moral position and find my creative way to do it, and I don’t care anymore. I try to be honest with myself and do my best.”.

The director shared that he spent much time with an actual female fighter “to try to understand not only the gun or the fight, but the character behind the fight, the woman behind the gun and where they came from, why they fight. The most important thing is to not only show them as a victim, but [show] them as a human being, and what they are capable of doing.”.

According to recent reports, bugrimova remarked women’s portrayals on screen have long been in need of an authentic makeover, saying “nothing has changed” in decades. “The men are usually the heroes, and the female characters usually the victims or the mothers or volunteers,” she highlighted. “Or if they are, for example, soldiers, we should be feeling pity for them because of that. I think it’s completely wrong.”.

She called on creatives and executives to give women “a chance to be portrayed in their ordinary lives,” explaining: “They can be exhausted. They can be heroes, or they can be just simple persons. And it’s very vital to write stories about that.”.

Ukrainian creatives face another challenge these days due to the invasion by Russia, she argued. “We are expected to explain the whole history of Ukraine or [all] Ukrainian narratives through a film,” Bugrimova mentioned. “But for example, in British culture, in the U.S. and also in Estonia, when you create films, you’re not expected to explain your entire country or the entire situation of what happened in just 90 minutes. You build the film around a personal story, the character.”.

Sources indicate that farahani echoed that. “Extraordinary comes from ordinary,” she argued. “When you’re coming from a country of war like Ukraine, you become the representative of the country, and you’re not allowed to be an individual. Your individuality is taken away from you, and you need to be a flag and represent the history and all that stuff.”.

She concluded: “When you are in a certain position, you have to be in that box, and you’re not allowed to exist [outside] that box [people put you in]. So not only are you imprisoned by bombs and war and oppressive regimes, you are [also] imprisoned by the world that is putting you in that box.”.
