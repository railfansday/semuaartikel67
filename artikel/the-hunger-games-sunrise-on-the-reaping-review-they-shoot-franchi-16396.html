📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that A grimmer, gloomier “Hunger Games” is on the horizon with “Sunrise on the Reaping,” adapted from Suzanne Collins’ prequel novel about the origin story of the hardened alcoholic Haymitch Abernathy. Here, the teenage version of Woody Harrelson’s character in the original films is played by Australian actor Joseph Zada, the blond, ringletted Adonis who also starred as the moody Cal Trask in the recent “East of Eden” series.

Franchise fans might be riveted by Francis Lawrence’s latest installment — points for consistency, as he’s directed all the films dating back to 2013’s “Catching Fire” — but those audiences not already invested in the dystopian material and tales of tributes will find themselves left out in the cold by an overlong (it’s 158 minutes, so get comfortable), visually soupy, and narratively morose reprisal. But no matter, as this fresh slice of bankable I.P. is likely to rake in a hysterical amount of money, and perhaps launch Zada’s career as a face of new Hollywood stardom. Industry observers note that the film is set during the 50th Hunger Games, otherwise known as the Second Quarter Quell. Only 16 years old, Haymitch is thrust into games that are rigged from the start — and whose broadcasting is ultimately edited and deepfaked to look like pro-Capitol propaganda. (The film nudges a little bit toward the ethics of state surveillance and how footage can be manipulated or even fabricated for political ends.) His pastoral, if impoverished, life in District 12 is small but happy, with girlfriend Lenore (Whitney Peak) and a mother and younger brother (Kara Tointon and Smylie Bradwell) with whom he shares close affinity, plus a best friend we’ll eventually find out will become the father of Katniss Everdeen (Lawrence) in the later films.

In a recent development, after a reaping (refresher: that’s when tributes are selected ceremonial-style, here as by a grotesquely pulled-tight-in-the-face Glenn Close as Drusilla Sickle) turns bloody, long-shot Haymitch ends up being selected at only 16. Violence follows him everywhere, as during the subsequent Tribute Parade, tragedy strikes Haymitch’s childhood friend (Molly McCann) during a horse-drawn Tribute chariot ride. Sequences meant to be thrilling often lack connective tissue to the rest of the material, or are devised in such a maelstrom of CGI (as goopy and cacophonous as a Marvel battle) that it’s difficult to feel engaged. It’s also unhelpful that most of the supporting cast is so wildly over the top, especially Capitol residents played by Close and a smarmy and mannered Kieran Culkin. Jesse Plemons as a Hunger Games camera operator brings a shade of moral dimension to the proceedings, but as with his “Digger” role, the actor feels woefully underused. Ralph Fiennes as Capitol president Snow, meanwhile, is given but scraps of material to work with.

In a recent development, the by-the-book script by Billy Ray and Michael Lesslie appears to stick faithfully to Collins’ 2025 novel, with plenty of potential for visual appeal that goes largely unrealized. The Hunger Games arena Haymitch is thrust into is rife with hidden dangers: poisonous streams, a deadly force field, and other trippy flora and fauna that vibe like a dark mirror version of “Charlie and the Chocolate Factory,” but again, “Sunrise on the Reaping” trips up on the CGI fakery.

Industry observers note that the movie’s stacked young cast of tributes and mentors (those victors of previous Hunger Games tournaments) includes Mckenna Grace as a District 12 tribute who’s not to be trusted, the endearingly wily Ben Wang (recently excellent in the SXSW film “Brian”), plus a gravely underused Maya Hawke as the winner of the 49th Hunger Games, Kelvin Harrison Jr. as victor of the 34th, and the great Lili Taylor as a legacy tribute played previously by Lynn Cohen (aka Magda on “Sex and the City”) in the second film. These characters mostly exist to introduce themselves to Haymitch, be established as good or evil or helpful or unhelpful, and then be shuffled out of the scene. Elle Fanning is rather amusing, however, as the Capitol stylist Effie Trinket, styled and nearly drowned out by extremely outré fashions. Glenn Close also (with a visage channeling Katherine Helmond as Ida Lowry in “Brazil”) could’ve been used to better effect.

It has been revealed that the specter of death looming over the film should not be confused for much in the way of actual depth, and while Zada’s performance is charming enough, his character lacks the gravitational charisma or arc that, say, Jennifer Lawrence brought to the original films. He feels like a cog or a function to get this prequel to line up with the prior films — and isn’t that what he is? Which makes the movie, and ergo probably the book, feel a little bit like a cold cash grab rather than a necessary deepening of the series’ lore. When Haymitch comes home from Second Quarter Quell and descends into depression and alcoholism, that’s when the movie gets most interesting. Not a great sign when your film’s tepid coda is also its best moment.

And equally worrying is a series of cameos tacked onto the film’s end that smack of depressing fan service. These movies seem to be mostly critic-proof and hugely appealing to their core audience, but “Sunrise on the Reaping” is a dreary slog, another death-march “They Shoot Horses, Don’t They?” riff with a pretty Gen Z cast, and one that mistakes new layers of darkness (and trauma!) for the sort of profundity that its filmmakers hope will burnish a creatively withering property.

“The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping” opens in theaters on Friday, November 20.
