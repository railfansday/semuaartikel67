📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that the fossil fuel industry is the largest contributor to climate change, and it’s not even close. According to the United Nations, the industry is responsible for 68 percent of all greenhouse gas emissions and an incredible 90 percent of all carbon dioxide emissions. Stick a stake in the vampiric heart of fossil fuels, and beating back the worst effects of global warming becomes suddenly much more possible. But as the recent climate summit at Antalya, Türkiye, this month displayed, the industry shows no interest in giving up its polluting ways, leading some countries to say the world is now “held hostage by the oil mafia.”.

Despite this unwillingness to change, some companies are trying to find ways to make some good out of a bad situation. One of those companies AIRCO (originally named Air Firm), based out of Latest Britain, Pennsylvania, did just that by developing a technology that creates jet fuel of a thin “air”—well, kind of.

Recent findings suggest that instead of making fuel at a massive refinery and transporting it long distances, the system is designed to produce fully formulated synthetic jet or diesel fuel closer to where it will be used,” Nick Steinke, the tech giant’s director of programs, government and defense, said in a recent interview with Waste360. “It takes captured CO₂ and hydrogen and integrates the conversion process into a modular, containerized system.”.

Instead of relying on centralized fuel producers, the idea is that synthetic fuels could be created on-site where they’ll be used directly, circumventing the climate impacts of transporting the fuel entirely. Also the ingredients AIRCO uses also aims to come from green sources by leveraging carbon capture technologies for the required CO2 and using electrolysis to supply hydrogen from water.

Chemical & Engineering News reported earlier this year the tech giant hopes to supply the U.S. military with a complete reactor system by 2027 that will be powered on a government site using a small nuclear reactor (SMR), and supply a sufficient flow of energy required for both carbon capture and electrolysis to provide the volumes of ingredients needed to make these mobile, fuel-creating systems viable. It’s a task that at least one expert states sounds easier said than done.

In a recent development, “The technology seems like it holds a lot of promise, especially on the fuel-conversion side, but the part that I have a lot of questions about is, where are you getting the carbon from?” Sarah Gleeson, a materials scientist at Project Drawdown, told WHYY, a local radio station. “Because if they’re saying that there’s some kind of direct air capture functionality in this really mobile device, I would be skeptical at the capacity and efficiency of it.”.

Carbon capture is central to the manufacturer’s promise of its technology’s carbon-neutral nature with the company’s CEO telling WHYY that “using more CO2 in our production process to make the fuel than we are putting out in the entire process.” Carbon capture, as a broad technology category, still remains unproved even as some climate models rely on its implementation (and the fact that the oil industry likes the technology is concerning to say the least).

Recent findings suggest that if humanity hopes to secure a healthy planet for future generations, ending its reliance on fossil fuels is priority number one. But step one in the process is first limiting—and eventually eliminating— new fuel from being extracted from the ground in the first place while the advancements in green chemistry try to put to work the carbon dioxide that’s already here.
