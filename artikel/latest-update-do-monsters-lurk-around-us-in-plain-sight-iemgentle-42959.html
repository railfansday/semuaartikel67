Latest Update: Do Monsters Lurk Around Us in Plain Sight? Gentle Monster’s Director Worries They Do

Sources indicate that A newspaper clipping about a ring of pedophiles captivated the attention of Austrian filmmaker Marie Kreutzer one morning six years ago. The article detailed the buildup and the arrest in explicit detail, but one description felt more haunting than the rest.

According to recent reports, “It was shocking because most of the guys in that ring of criminals were normal family dads,” Kreutzer tells Bazaar on a recent call. “It made me so much more aware of how close these perpetrators are, more than we want to think, and that I must almost certainly also know one.… When I read that article, and it described how the police officers were standing at the doorsteps of these guys, my first thought was, ‘How would that be for me if it were my husband?’ ”.

In a recent development, kreutzer decided to channel this shock, the all-consuming fear that we cannot trust those we love the most, into her latest film, Gentle Monster. Releasing on Netflix on November 18, the film tells the story of Lucy Weiss (Léa Seydoux), an experimental pianist who leaves her life behind to raise her young son with her doe-eyed husband, Philip (Laurence Rupp). The trio exists happily in the German countryside, complete with all the comforts of a cozy domestic life. Lucy and Philip love each other and their son, enjoying a sex-filled marriage that appears to have only the regular bumps. This illusion is disrupted when police knock on the door, arresting Philip for his alleged participation in a child pornography ring. Kruetzer designed the couple introduction to feel as if the rug were pulled out from under the viewer.

In a recent development, “It was obviously very notable to have all these scenes where they interact before everything happens and where we see that they have a very natural, loving relationship,” she says. “For me, [Rupp] has nothing secretive or mysterious about him. He’s very there. And that’s what I wanted for the role, because I thought it would be good to see him and never really be open to believing that that guy would really do something like that.” She remembered telling Rupp, “It’s not like you’re playing a petty criminal. You’re playing a liar.” Kruetzer wanted him to “make us want to come back to you.”.

In this way, Rupp embodies an uncanny confidence throughout the entire movie. He, of course, carries a sense of guilt, yet an audience can’t easily parse out the weight of his internal feelings. Kruetzer also refrains from giving too much direction about his innocence or guilt. Even when he is released from prison the first time, his nonchalance diffuses any skepticism; you might believe him when he indicates he has nothing to do with it. And when evidence mounts, including actual files, he twists the story again, telling Lucy he did it for the money. Rupp’s sympathetic portrayal is confusing—enough so that you can understand Lucy’s turmoil. Kruetzer remembers telling him to “play it as if you just messed up the taxes,” and later he told her, “I didn’t really play the monster; I played the gentle.”.

Industry observers note that this portrayal adds weight to Seydoux’s indelible performance as Lucy. The entire film orbits her reaction to this horrific revelation. Seydoux’s anger and forgiveness oscillate at a frenetic pace throughout the movie, pulling the viewer into Lucy’s point of view, as well as what Kreutzer first felt when she read the newspaper clipping.

Casting Seydoux, the director says, felt intuitive. The French actress dove headfirst into the script, expressing great interest in the character and the story. “The subject matter is tough, but the fact that it’s through the woman’s perspective—and not through the victim or perpetrator—it’s through her emotions that the story unfolds [is what interested me],” Seydoux says in Bazaar’s profile from last week. One bone-rattling scene in particular, where Lucy faces off with Philip in a light rain and directly confronts him about his arrest, underscores why she’s considered one of the best living French actresses. “As soon as we turned the camera towards her, it was really surprising what came out of her and what poured out of her,” Kreutzer remarked. “I remember that the crew was in shock.”.

Industry observers note that the main storyline provides enough anxiety and suspense, yet Kreutzer wrote a side plot for Gentle Monster to offer perspective. One of the cops who knocks on the Weiss family door, Elsa (Jella Haase), provides a removed point of view on Lucy’s internal dilemma. The character provides a broader understanding of the crime at the center of this story, emphasizing how widespread and hidden this problem is. “For Lucy, her life is falling apart because of this, whereas for Elsa, it’s just one of many cases, and it’s not even a bad one,” Kreutzer says. “It was notable for me to show the magnitude of the problem and to not let the audience lean back and think, ‘Okay, that’s very dramatic, but it has nothing to do with me.’ ”.
