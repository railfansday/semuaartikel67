GameStop boss Ryan Cohen (last seen attempting to buy eBay) has called for the return of physical game discs, lamenting the lack of control players have over digital purchases in a chat with Bloomberg held on the eve of Grand Theft Auto 6's release.

It has been revealed that in case you missed it, a media meteor impacted yesterday and blasted our landscape free of life. Grand Theft Auto 6 is here and—despite our Ted Litchfield's noble attempts to get you all thinking about Queen's Domain instead (you should play Queen's Domain)—it's all the world can talk about. It's also only available digitally.

"Unquestionably, the decision to move to all digital has hurt consumers, and it’s also hurt GameStop", mentioned Cohen, remarking that players ought to be able to do with their purchased games as they will: "When you buy a game, you should be able to sell it, trade it or lend it to a friend". Cohen did not namecheck GTA 6 specifically, but did say that he had been speaking about this issue with all "the obvious" publishers.

Recent findings suggest that gameStop, of course, makes a tidy chunk of change from resales of games traded in at its stores—a segment of its business that becomes impossible to maintain in a world without physical discs for players to bring in and sell. Publishers oppose trade-ins "because removing a used copy eliminates an alternative to buying latest", says Cohen, accusing them of "taking away competition" by restricting sales to digital-only channels.

GTA 6 is a portent of the world to come. Earlier this year, Sony announced that PlayStation would drop physical discs starting in January 2028, and has held firm to that course in the face of a vocal player uproar.

Issues around game ownership have reached the point that politicians are weighing in: last month US lawmaker Richie Torres announced the Power To The Players Act, that would prompt the US Federal Trade Commission to protect players' access to purchased games, even in the case of online-only games that get unplugged.

"When you pay for a game, you should own it", mentioned Torres. "Consumers must have the right to own, the right to play, and the right to transfer the games they buy. A game that can be taken away the moment a company pulls the plug is not a purchase. It is a rental disguised as a sale".
