📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

My mom called me greedy in jest this weekend because I had the gall to eat … more than five (but fewer than 10!) square slices of a delicious ube cake in her presence. Unlike each savored bite of that addictive purple pastry, I took her criticism on the chin because I believe that greed and indulgence function as a Venn diagram rather than as synonyms. And because there was still plenty of cake to go around.

Sources indicate that indulgence is in the air right now, and not just because we’re closing in on Thanksgiving weekend. Marquee auctions have rolled in like a thick fog on Monday, not long after the ADAA fair and an adjunctive torrential downpour rinsed the last of art fair season out of Latest York late last week. A press release from Sotheby’s informed me that $1.1 billion worth of art will go under the hammer this month, underpinned by today’s sale of a rarely seen van Gogh landscape expected to fetch a record-breaking $180 million. A brief shakeup ensued less than a week before the painting goes to auction, as the descendants of its former owner suddenly called on Sotheby’s to withdraw the work, alleging that it was sold under duress due to Nazi persecution.

Don’t worry, though — the auction house shared in a follow-up release that the descendants and the Blaquier-Arrieta family, who are selling the painting alongside 11 other multimillion-dollar masterpieces from their collection, have come to a “mutually satisfactory resolution” that allows the sale to proceed as scheduled.

Really glad they worked that out.

On the other side of the same coin, protesters gathered outside of the Museum of Modern Art during an annual benefit for the museum’s Debra and Leon Black Family Film Center last Thursday, calling on filmmaker and honored guest Steven Spielberg to reject the recognition and boycott the museum over Leon Black’s Epstein-related sexual abuse allegations. Cold, biting rain soaked the group of 20 artists and activists outside the gala, and Spielberg hasn’t acknowledged their demands.

According to recent reports, all things considered, Iraqi-British data journalist and illustrator Mona Chalabi’s first-ever book, which was released last week, comes at a most opportune time. The book, Ten Lives: How What We Have Shapes Who We Are (2026), comes from Chalabi five-year endeavor in examining class inequality in the United States by analyzing the incomes, expenses, tax deductions, systemic barriers, and livelihoods of 10 Americans across the nation. In a recent interview with Hyperallergic’s Lakshmi Rivera Amin, Chalabi noted that journalists themselves often don’t acknowledge or even recognize how their own class upbringing can color their reporting on wealth and poverty, whereas other markers like race and gender are positioned as lenses of understanding.

A newly introduced video exhibition on the High Line also tackles greed and indulgence through the lens of the British Empire’s storied trade monopolies and resource theft across South Asia and China. Works by Shahzia Sikander and the artist duo Hylozoic/Desires unpack the British stronghold on salt in India and the empire’s deliberate opium influx in China during the Qing dynasty, while also highlighting historic and speculative subversions that disrupt dominant colonial narratives.

In a recent development, I wasn’t super fond of the High Line’s most recent film installation, but this one sounds more up my alley on a subjective, personal history level. I’m looking forward to checking it out soon — before the weather really turns south — and visiting the Mighty Miniatures exhibition that’s opening this Saturday at the National Museum of the American Indian.
