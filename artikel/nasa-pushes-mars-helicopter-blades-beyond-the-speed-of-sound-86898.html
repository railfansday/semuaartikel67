NASA pushes Mars helicopter blades beyond the speed of sound

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

NASA is pushing the limits of helicopter flight on Mars, testing rotor blades at speeds once considered too risky. The faster these blades spin, the more lift they can generate, potentially allowing future aircraft to carry heavier scientific instruments and travel greater distances across the Red Planet.

During tests conducted in March at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) in Southern California, engineers successfully pushed the tips of experimental Mars helicopter blades beyond the speed of sound without causing them to break apart. The experiments took place inside a specialized chamber designed to reproduce the harsh atmospheric conditions on Mars. Results from 137 test runs could help engineers develop a new generation of more powerful aircraft capable of carrying scientific equipment.

According to recent reports, "NASA had a great run with the Ingenuity Mars Helicopter, but we are asking these next-generation aircraft to do even more at the Red Planet," said Al Chen, Mars Exploration Program manager at JPL. "That's not an easy ask. While everything about Mars is hard, flying there is just about the hardest thing you can do. That's because its atmosphere is so incredibly thin that it is hard to generate lift, and yet Mars has significant gravity.".

According to recent reports, ingenuity made history on April 19, 2021, when it became the first aircraft to achieve powered, controlled flight on another world. However, it was designed primarily to demonstrate that flight on Mars was possible, not to transport scientific instruments. NASA now hopes to go much further. Its recently confirmed SkyFall project, along with other potential future aircraft, could carry sensors and scientific instruments to investigate Mars from the air. Flying at low altitudes would provide valuable information to support future robotic and human missions.

Why Flying on Mars Requires Extreme Rotor Speeds.

Industry observers note that helicopters generate more thrust by spinning their rotors faster or using blades with a larger diameter. That basic principle applies on Earth and Mars, but the Red Planet presents a much greater engineering challenge.

Mars has an atmosphere only about 1% as dense as Earth's, leaving helicopter blades with far fewer air molecules to push against. To produce enough lift, engineers must operate rotors at exceptionally high speeds, bringing their tips close to the speed of sound. Small rotors on Earth can also spin thousands of times per minute, but the denser atmosphere makes it unnecessary to push their tips toward supersonic speeds to generate substantial lift.

According to recent reports, during Ingenuity's 72 flights on Mars, its engineers limited the helicopter's composite-skinned foam rotors to 2,700 revolutions per minute. This restriction served two key purposes. It kept the blades away from the complex and potentially unpredictable conditions near the sound barrier, and it provided a safety margin in case sudden winds (from a dust devil, for instance) caused the blade tips to move faster relative to the surrounding air.

According to recent reports, "If Chuck Yeager were here, he'd tell you things can get squirrely around Mach 1," remarked JPL's Jaakko Karras, the rotor test lead. "With that in mind, we planned Ingenuity's flights to keep the rotor blade tips at Mach 0.7 with no wind so that if we encountered a Martian headwind while in flight, the rotor tips wouldn't go supersonic. But we want more performance from our next-gen Mars aircraft. We needed to know that our rotors could go faster safely.".

Sources indicate that the speed of sound also differs considerably between the two planets. At sea level on Earth, Mach 1 is approximately 760 mph (1,223 kph). On Mars, sound travels at roughly 540 mph (869 kph), largely because the atmosphere is much colder and consists primarily of carbon dioxide.

NASA Tests Supersonic Rotors in a Mars Simulation Chamber.

Industry observers note that to determine whether the latest blades could safely reach these extreme speeds, Karras and his colleagues used JPL's historic 25-Foot Space Simulator. The rotor systems were developed and manufactured by AeroVironment in Simi Valley, California.

Industry observers note that the researchers began with a three-bladed rotor designed for potential future Mars helicopters. After installing it inside the chamber, they removed the surrounding air and introduced a carefully controlled amount of carbon dioxide to recreate the thin Martian atmosphere. They then spun the rotor at progressively higher speeds while exposing it to simulated winds.

Industry observers note that because the experiment involved the possibility of a rotor breaking apart at supersonic speeds, engineers reinforced part of the chamber with sheet metal. The team monitored the experiment from a control room located just a few yards away, using instruments and a camera to track the rotor's performance.

It has been revealed that the rotor eventually reached 3,750 revolutions per minute, bringing its blade tips to Mach 0.98, just below the speed of sound. Engineers then switched on a fan to produce headwinds against the spinning blades. With each successive test, they increased the wind speed to push the system further.

Breaking the Sound Barrier Boosts Lift by 30%.

Recent findings suggest that the experiments ultimately drove the rotor tips to Mach 1.08, surpassing the sound barrier and increasing the aircraft's lift capability by 30%. Crucially, the blades survived the supersonic conditions.

That additional lift could make a major difference for future missions. More powerful Mars helicopters could transport heavier scientific instruments, advanced sensors, and larger batteries, allowing them to conduct more ambitious investigations and remain airborne for longer periods.

Recent findings suggest that following the successful tests of the three-bladed design, the team evaluated a second rotor intended for SkyFall. This version has two blades that are slightly longer than those used in the first configuration. Because of their greater length, the blade tips could reach the same near-supersonic speed at a lower rotational rate of 3,570 revolutions per minute before simulated headwinds were introduced.

"The successful testing of these rotors was a major step toward proving the feasibility of flight in more demanding environments, which is key for next-gen vehicles," stated Shannah Withrow-Maser, an aerodynamicist from NASA's Ames Research Center in Silicon Valley and member of the test team. "We thought we'd be lucky to hit Mach 1.05, and we reached Mach 1.08 on our last runs. We're still digging into the data, and there may be even more thrust on the table. These next-gen helicopters are going to be amazing.".

Next-Generation Mars Helicopters Could Launch in 2028.

Engineers working on SkyFall have already incorporated the latest test results into the project's performance specifications. The findings provide an important foundation for designing helicopters that can operate under more demanding conditions while carrying substantially more equipment than Ingenuity.

Inspired by the historic success of Ingenuity, which remains the only rotorcraft ever to fly on another planet, SkyFall is designed to transport three next-generation helicopters to Mars in December 2028.

If successfully developed and deployed, these aircraft could expand the possibilities for exploring Mars, providing scientists with a new way to investigate the planet's surface and gather information from locations that may be difficult for traditional rovers to reach.
