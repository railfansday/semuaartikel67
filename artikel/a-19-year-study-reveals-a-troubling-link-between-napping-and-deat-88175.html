A 19-year study reveals a troubling link between napping and death

A daily nap may seem like a harmless way to recharge, but fresh research suggests that certain napping habits could provide notable clues about a person's health as they age. Scientists have found that taking longer or more frequent naps, particularly in the morning, is associated with an increased risk of death among older adults.

It has been revealed that researchers from Mass General Brigham and Rush University Medical Center examined the napping patterns of 1,338 older adults over a period of up to 19 years. Their findings, published in JAMA Network Open, suggest that monitoring daytime sleep could help identify underlying medical conditions or early signs of declining health.

In a recent development, "Excessive napping later in life has been linked to neurodegeneration, cardiovascular diseases and even greater morbidity, but many of those findings rely on self-reported napping habits and leave out metrics like when and how regular those naps are," remarked lead author Chenlu Gao, PhD, an investigator in the Department of Anesthesiology at Mass General Brigham and an affiliated research fellow in the Division of Sleep and Circadian Disorders in the Department of Medicine. "Our study is one of the first to show an association between objectively measured nap patterns and mortality and suggests there is immense clinical value in tracking napping patterns to catch health conditions early.".

Why Scientists Are Studying Daytime Napping.

Daytime naps are common among older adults, with estimates suggesting that 20% to 60% regularly nap. An occasional nap can help restore energy and improve alertness, but excessive daytime sleeping in later life has been associated with several health concerns.

Despite these connections, researchers still have an incomplete understanding of how specific napping behaviors relate to long-term health. Many earlier studies depended on participants remembering and reporting their own sleep habits, which may not provide an accurate picture. They also often overlooked key details, such as how long naps lasted, when they occurred, and how much napping patterns changed from one day to the next.

In a recent development, to obtain more reliable measurements, the research team analyzed information from the Rush Memory and Aging Project. Debuted in 1997, this long-running study was designed primarily to investigate memory, thinking abilities, and neurological decline among older adults in northern Illinois, most of whom were white.

Recent findings suggest that starting in 2005, participants wore wrist activity monitors for 10 days to record their daily periods of rest and movement. Researchers used these measurements to identify daytime sleep and assess nap duration, frequency, timing, and daily variation.

Longer and More Frequent Naps Linked to Higher Mortality Risk.

By 2025, researchers had assembled up to 19 years of follow-up information from 1,338 participants. They then examined whether napping habits measured during the initial assessment were associated with the likelihood of dying from any cause during the subsequent years.

Recent findings suggest that the results revealed several notable patterns. Older adults who slept longer during the day or took naps more frequently had higher mortality rates. The timing of naps also appeared to matter.

Each additional hour spent napping per day was associated with approximately a 13% higher mortality risk. Taking one additional nap per day was linked to about a 7% increase in risk.

It has been revealed that one of the most striking findings involved morning naps. Participants who napped in the morning had a 30% higher mortality risk compared with those who napped in the afternoon.

However, not every aspect of napping was associated with poorer outcomes. Researchers found no increased mortality risk linked to irregular napping schedules, suggesting that changes in nap timing or duration from day to day were not necessarily indicators of greater risk.

Could Napping Habits Reveal Hidden Health Problems?

According to recent reports, the researchers emphasize that their findings do not mean daytime naps directly cause health problems or shorten lives. Instead, excessive napping may reflect medical conditions or changes in the body that have not yet been recognized.

Sources indicate that "It is crucial to note that this is correlation, not causation. Excessive napping is likely indicating underlying disease, chronic conditions, sleep disturbances, or circadian dysregulation," noted Gao. "Now that we know there is a strong correlation between napping patterns and mortality rates, we can make the case to implement wearable daytime nap assessments to predict health conditions and prevent further decline.".

In a recent development, these findings raise the possibility that wearable activity trackers could eventually become useful tools for monitoring health in older adults. By identifying changes in daytime sleep habits, healthcare professionals might be able to recognize warning signs of underlying illness, disrupted sleep cycles, or neurological decline earlier.

Although further research is needed to determine how effectively napping patterns can predict specific medical conditions, the study suggests that something as ordinary as a daily nap could provide valuable information about a person's overall health.
