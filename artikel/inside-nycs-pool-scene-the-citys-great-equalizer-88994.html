Inside NYC’s Pool Scene, the City’s Great Equalizer

Just when we thought Newly introduced York had seen it all, I caught wind that the first-ever five-borough pool tournament—in the city’s history—was about to kick off.

According to recent reports, it was all thanks to Dan Tran, the brains behind the Five Boro Break. Tran has been shooting pool across the city for more than a decade and has played on more than 130 tables out of the 400 to 500 he estimates are in NYC (he even catalogs them out of his own goodwill for other New Yorkers, on Pool Tables NYC). He’s just that guy: an absolute force of nature with a heavy case of do-the-impossible syndrome. One day over summer, at a league night with fellow pool-playing regulars, he wondered if there was a way to get folks like these together for one significant game—to unite New York’s pool-playing dive bar families and play as one.

Dan Tran, the brains behind the Five Boro Break, has been shooting pool across the city for more than a decade.

It has been revealed that the idea was a tournament “built by pool players, run by pool players,” indicates Tran, who you’ll most often find playing at George & Jacks in Williamsburg. “No money changing hands, no ulterior motive.” Just bringing together this community, which Tran is the first to admit existed long before he stepped on the scene.

So this powerhouse did it: 256 players, 16 venues, all five boroughs, over two days in early November. When I caught up with Tran the week before the tournament, he still couldn’t believe he was bringing the idea to life. “I was like, 'What am I signing up to do once I post this?' But then I posted it, and it just took off—like completely took off. And everybody was like, 'Yeah, we can totally do this.'”.

I rode the wave of Tran’s infectious energy and spent an entire weekend hopping between dives watching the tournament’s pool players do what they do best.

The audience at NYC bar Sugar Mouse applauds Five Boro Break creator Dan Tran at the finals.

It has been revealed that on Saturday, November 7, you could feel it in the air. There was something electric about knowing the grittiest corners of Upcoming York City were locked in on the same game. That distinct four o’clock autumn sunlight was shining on Bushwick Ice House, one of the 16 venues, when I walked in with Traveler’s Julia Abbonizio and Skyli Alvarez, who were ready to photograph the scene. Gathered around a red-felt table were a dozen or so players who shot us “what’s up” nods as we entered; the bartender moseyed over with “what’s it gonna be?” eyes. Without a word exchanged, we were already part of the bar’s family.

It has been revealed that the crowd around us was dressed in their dive-bar best (jeans), and glowing under the neon-red Budweiser lights. The energy in the room matched the game unfolding. One moment, the place roared with Miller High Life clinks and friends catching up. The next it would go pin-drop silent as someone bent over and lined up a shot. More often than not, we’d hear a “nice” before the cue even struck, or a congratulatory clap before someone sank the 8-ball, because everyone knew the game well enough to see exactly what was coming.

Will Pay, a Bushwick Ice House regular, designed the tournament's felt-green shirts.

Satoe Onizuka plays Netta Rawner (left) at Bushwick Ice House.

According to recent reports, will Pay, a Bushwick resident and the creative mind behind the tournament’s T-shirt and site design—dark green, like a pool table’s felt, and featuring a solid orange 5-ball to nod to the boroughs—was running the show. He jokingly told me he’s something of a pool scout around the city, sniffing out the best shooters to join his league. Pay attested that, when Tran initially unveiled the tournament, interest at his local dive Bushwick Ice House was immediate. Within 10 minutes of the link being live, the waitlist to play at the venue had passed 100.

Recent findings suggest that while Satoe Onizuka, the stealthiest star of the regulars, shook another hand after winning yet another round, I snuck outside of Bushwick Ice House to chat with Pay, who was wearing the Five Boro Break shirt he designed. We got into the type of conversation you do at a dive bar or around a pool table. The love of the game. The importance of dives as sacred spaces. The alluring seediness of it all. Pay knows how we got here: “The game itself is called billiards, but it got casualized to ‘pool’ because people used to throw a ton of money on the table—pool their money—and gamble,” Pay mentioned. “Obviously, that’s gone out the window because everyone’s super chill these days, but what’s cool is that it still has that air of danger, of being something a bit forbidden.” Will’s right: it’s never corny to shoot pool. No matter who you are, it makes you feel like the slickest person in the room.

There’s also that sense that you’re part of something bigger. Back inside, Pay gestured around the room. “Look at this place,” he said in admiration. “There’s literally no real estate for this table. But it’s here. And without it, this place would simply not be what it is. These people would probably not be here in this bar. I don’t even think this bar would exist.”.

The enthusiasm at Bushwick Ice House reminded me of how this tournament could only have happened because NYC dive bars already operate as tight-knit communities of their own. They’re the ultimate third places, the spots that aren’t home or work—and the pool table is, in fact, their beating heart.

By 9 p.m. on Saturday, when we stumbled into LES’ Sadie’s Ward, '80s dance-pop shook the walls. Drinks were flowing, the room glowed disco-lit, and everyone was having such a good time you had to shout to be heard by the person next to you. A group of hodge-podge regulars stood two and three deep around the pool table, watching the small, curly-haired Amanda Andries, one of the bar’s most beloved shooters, sink her way toward Sunday’s finals.

Sources indicate that andries is one of the Ladies of Sadie’s, a magnetic group of female regulars at this bar, who were gassing each other up and out-shooting all the 6-foot-something men in flannels that dared to play them. For all the history that tables at bars like Sadie’s have absorbed, it’s the people gathered around them who make the pool scene so captivating—and at this bar, the social fabric is very tightly knit.

East Williamsburg's Anchored Inn was one of the 16 bars that hosted Five Boro Break games.

We sat in the small hideaway across from the bar as the games rolled on, reflecting on the players we’d met throughout the tournament so far: soft-spoken, sharp-shooting Audrey Jones at Anchored Inn over loaded Carmen fries; Allegra “Legz” Fierro outside the graffied, mirrorless bathroom at Blue Ruin Bar; and Elvin Estevez, a polite killer on the table, in the leather booths at Twins Lounge. I’d asked each to tell me their golden rule of playing pool in this city, but it all came back to one recurring theme: be a good person. There’s a camaraderie in wanting to keep NYC’s pool scene a sacred place.

In a recent development, it’s easy not to suck, though. Sam Shumway of Pool Table Archive stated to me by phone in the days following the event. Five years ago, he started posting photos of the pool tables he’d played on in cities across the US, but his hobby grew into a living record of pool culture.

“Pool tables are special because they’re a snapshot of a time and a place in a bar,” he told me. “A lot of these tables are older than you are. Like, that’s a 40-year-old table. Imagine how much life it’s seen around it.” You can picture a montage of scenes immediately: 4 a.m. closings, birthday toasts, newly introduced friendships, awkward first dates, breakups, and more than a few questionable decisions. Maybe a win in the first-ever cross-city tournament.

Allegra “Legz” Fierro in Hell's Kitchen's Blue Ruin Bar, just before a match.

Industry observers note that come Sunday, we walked into a jam-packed, purple-lit Sugar Mouse for the finals. The winners from each of the 16 bars would go head-to-head across two pool tables. A live dad rock band jammed in the corner, and all I saw were familiar faces from Saturday’s hijinks, smiling, watching respectfully, nodding when a friend nailed a shot, and hugging and shaking hands when one missed. As the field dwindled to four semifinalists, then just two, the crowd drew tighter around the remaining table. Two large projectors cast a live feed onto the wall, as former WPBA No. 1 Jennifer Barretta commented and Hall of Famer John Leyman refereed with a stern eye.

We were all there for the moment Brennan Brooks of the BCA Brooklyn Travel League took the inaugural win, cementing his right to take home that iconic sign. But as I took in the room around me, what stood out most was seeing people packed in like sardines to watch their fellow dive bar regulars take the stage. It was a kaleidoscope of communities, each representing their local with love. The Five Boro Break was never just about winning; it was about creating a space where everyone could be part of something.

A triumphant moment at Sadie's Ward on the Lower East Side.

The East Village's Sugar Mouse hosted the tournament final.

It was a fresh alternative to life on a screen—maybe that’s part of why so many people showed up. “People are craving human connection again; people are craving something to do with their hands,” Shumway had remarked. And though it might look too cool, or intimidating, everyone has a place if they want it. “Pool brings together people who would normally have nothing in common. And there are no pre-qualifications for putting your name on a chalkboard. You might get beat, and that’s okay.”.

Recent findings suggest that at the end of the Finals night, all of us riding a collective high, Tran stood next to the championship table to thank everyone who helped bring his vision to life. He couldn’t get a word out before the room burst into applause. Thirty seconds of it, and it demonstrated no signs of letting up. Humble as he is, he just smiled.

Tran rattled off the bars, businesses, and people who stepped up to make the tournament possible, shaking his head happily. “This is what pool does,” he said. “It brings people together rather than pushes people apart.”.

Or as Shumway likes to put it: “Pool is the great equalizer.”.
