The Eurovision Song Contest crowned its first Asia champion in Bangkok on Saturday, in an event that tested whether a high-camp singing competition with an ardent fan base could appeal to audiences in a new market.

It has been revealed that carmelle of the Philippines was declared the winner of the inaugural Eurovision Song Contest Asia, beating out artists and original songs from 10 other countries: Bangladesh, Bhutan, Cambodia, Laos, Malaysia, Mongolia, Nepal, South Korea, Thailand and Vietnam. The show was live-streamed on YouTube and broadcast by television stations across the region.

According to recent reports, eurovision has been a fixture of the European cultural calendar since it began in 1956. Past winners include Sweden’s ABBA and the Canadian chanteuse Celine Dion, who represented Switzerland in 1988.

Sources indicate that asian artists have readily embraced the kitschy outfits and spectacular sets that are part of Eurovision tradition, with costumes that include traditional dress, as well as feathered capes and sequined corsets. Malaysia’s performer plans to enter the stage in a giant teardrop.

But the Eurovision format has not been tested in Asia. Fans and regional partners have expressed concern in recent weeks that budget constraints, production challenges and weak audience demand could imperil the spinoff. Organizers say the contest could take time to build an audience.

Recent findings suggest that “It would be profoundly arrogant to think that just because we have a heritage brand and a huge global audience for the European show, that we would walk in and demand it to be the same here,” said Martin Green, the European Broadcasting Union’s director of the Eurovision Song Contest. “Like any new thing, it needs to find its roots.”.

Recent findings suggest that two previous attempts to take the competition to Asia were abandoned by the European Broadcasting Union, which owns Eurovision, and its partners in the region. A Eurovision spinoff in the United States, NBC’s American Song Contest, ran for one season in 2022.

The Asian edition kicking off on Saturday is far smaller than its European counterpart. Eurovision organizers mentioned they were in talks with more countries to join future seasons. They include China, Japan and India, whose music industries have global reach.

“Bringing the world together through music is a fundamentally good idea,” Mr. Green said. “Europe shouldn’t keep it to itself.”.

As Thailand’s Mang and Daou waited to learn whether they had won, the 26-year-old Mang was shown onscreen crying as if she couldn’t take the tension. That’s the sort of emotion needed to make Eurovision’s expansion into Asia a success. Acts have to want the title. But the show will also need the substantial Asian nations -- India, Japan and China -- to buy in, and much could now depend on the reaction in the Philippines to Carmelle’s win. The event was far less spectacular than a typical Eurovision in Europe. It also featured far too many ballads for my taste, but it was a fun afternoon’s viewing.

“It was nerve-wracking, nail biting suspense!” she noted. “But we know she would win! What a talent!”.

Simon Robinson, one of the fans who traveled to Bangkok for the Eurovision Song Contest on Saturday, noted that his history with the competition is nearly as long as its seven-decade run.

He was eight years old when he first watched it with his family in Yorkshire, England, when Britain's Mary Hopkin finished in second place behind Ireland’s Dana.

Sources indicate that “I really liked the concept of bringing Europe together after the war,” mentioned Mr. Robinson, 63, who now lives in Melbourne, Australia. He described the contest’s annual broadcast as a social activity for his family and neighbors.

“Eurovision helped to create the European identity,” he added.

Other fans had their own motivations for traveling to see the contest’s first Asian edition.

Some are Europeans living in Asia, on the hunt for nostalgia, while others are Americans who were eager to experience their first Eurovision.

For a competition meant to introduce the diversity of Asian pop music to the world, the lineup for the first Eurovision Song Contest Asia on Saturday had several notable absences.

In a recent development, for starters, the continent’s four most populous nations — China, India, Pakistan and Indonesia — were not there. Even Japan, a country globally considered a cultural powerhouse, was absent.

That left some of the region’s best-known musical cultures out of the mix. India has Bollywood hits. China has produced both Mandopop and Cantopop, the genre associated with Hong Kong pop stars. JPop was once huge across Asia.

As a result, the only large pop powerhouse in this contest was South Korea.

Recent findings suggest that china’s absence is relatively simple to explain. Performers are entered by local broadcasters, and Eurovision has long had a messy relationship with China’s, which are heavily regulated and try to reflect the government’s social and political values. Mango TV once had a deal to broadcast the high-camp Eurovision to Chinese viewers, but Eurovision abruptly canceled it in 2018 after Mango TV censored acts whose performances featured gay themes. It also blurred rainbow flags in the performance arena.

Frank-Dieter Freiling, a former Eurovision official, noted in an interview that such censorship was against Eurovision’s values and that the show’s organizers had historically barred broadcasters from airing the competition with delays.

It’s less clear why broadcasters from India, Pakistan, Japan and Indonesia did not participate. Assaf Blecher, Eurovision Asia’s executive producer, said that conversations with Chinese, Japanese and Indian broadcasters had not concluded in time. But he insisted that they “are knocking on our door.”.

Not participating saved those countries the potential embarrassment of being the regional power that comes in last.

Just look to Europe, where Britain and Germany often perform badly at Eurovision. In this year’s show, Britain’s Look Mum No Computer placed last, scoring only one point, while Germany finished third from last. Whenever that happens, political commentators give interviews suggesting that the results reflect those countries’ poor standing across Europe.
