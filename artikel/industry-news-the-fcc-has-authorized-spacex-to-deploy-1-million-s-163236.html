Industry News: The FCC has authorized SpaceX to deploy 1 million satellites

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In an extraordinary decision, the Federal Communications Commission on Wednesday granted SpaceX conditional authorization to deploy a constellation of up to 1 million Starmind satellites designed to serve as orbital data centers.

If fully deployed, this constellation would increase the number of satellites in orbit by roughly 50-fold.

According to recent reports, “The United States is moving decisively to ensure that this breakthrough technology creates opportunity, drives investment, and delivers lasting benefits for the American people,” FCC Chairman Brendan Carr said in a statement on Wednesday. “This action opens a bold latest frontier for the computing infrastructure needed to power America’s leadership and growth.”.

The decision, unveiled a day before the Thanksgiving holiday in the United States, represents a major regulatory victory for SpaceX and its founder, Elon Musk, who has allied himself with the Trump administration.

This announcement clears a major hurdle to deploying data centers in space, although it does not establish that orbital data centers are technically or economically viable. Nevertheless, the FCC authorization appears designed to give the firm wide latitude as it works through these remaining problems.

For example, SpaceX has seven years to deploy a single operational Starmind satellite under the order. SpaceX has until November 2035 to deploy 100,000 operational satellites, and November 2040 for the full constellation. SpaceX, therefore, has a 14-year regulatory runway to deploy the most ambitious industrial project ever approved for spaceflight.

The authorization includes some conditional aspects. SpaceX has proposed operating in six orbits, ranging from 550 km to about 1,000 km. The tech giant sought a waiver from FCC rules to allow satellites above 600 km to be disposed of into “Earth disposal orbits or heliocentric orbits.” That is, instead of burning them up in Earth’s atmosphere, SpaceX wants the flexibility to move retired satellites into other orbits around Earth, or potentially into orbit around the Sun.

The FCC did not grant this waiver request “because SpaceX has not provided sufficient information for the Commission to determine whether its alternative disposal methods are compliant with the Commission’s requirements.” The company will need to obtain a waiver before deploying any Starmind satellites that are not intended to be disposed of via atmospheric reentry.

However, in other ways the FCC appears to have been incredibly lenient with SpaceX. This is particularly the case with environmental concerns raised by numerous parties that commented on SpaceX’s application to the FCC.

Recent findings suggest that half a dozen organizations, such as the Secure World Foundation and American Astronomical Society, raised concerns about deploying so many satellites into orbit, citing collision risks, orbital debris, atmospheric pollution from so many satellite reentries, impacts on astronomical observations, and more. The FCC also received approximately 1,500 public comments concerning SpaceX’s application.

Sources indicate that some of these environmental challenges, the FCC remarked, lay beyond the scope of its review. For example, on concerns about the environmental implications of thousands or potentially tens of thousands of rocket launches, the FCC said, “Potential concerns related to the emissions of rocket launches and the subsequent impacts on the human environment as an indirect result of the Starmind system are outside of the Commission’s purview.”.

The FCC also rejected calls for further environmental scrutiny of pollution from satellite reentries, including concerns about aluminum oxides and their potential effects on the ozone layer. “Other concerns, including impacts on the atmosphere caused by satellites burning up on re-entry, remain speculative and not supported in the record,” the FCC authorization states.

Sources indicate that as part of its decision, the FCC cited a significant policy change adopted in October, when the agency concluded that space-based activities generally fall outside its obligations under the National Environmental Policy Act. The FCC reasoned that as these activities occur beyond US territory, they don’t constitute federal actions requiring environmental review. As a result, the agency declined to conduct a comprehensive environmental assessment of SpaceX’s Starmind constellation.
