Mice that lost weight on a very low-protein keto diet also developed fatty liver and lost muscle, highlighting the limitations of weight as a measure of health.

It has been revealed that ketogenic diets have become a popular approach to weight loss, despite conflicting research on their metabolic benefits. A upcoming study in mice suggests that two often overlooked factors may help explain these inconsistencies: the amount of protein in the diet and the genetic makeup of the animals eating it. The findings also raise questions about whether losing weight on a keto diet necessarily means becoming healthier.

The research, published in Cell Metabolism, began when first author Zhaoyue Zhang, a postdoctoral fellow in the Friedman lab, noticed considerable variation in the protein content of ketogenic diets. Although these diets are generally high in fat and extremely low in carbohydrates, their protein content could be contributing to differences in metabolic outcomes.

It has been revealed that zhang and her colleagues systematically tested diets containing different proportions of fat and protein. After four weeks on a ketogenic diet containing just 5% protein, mice belonging to C57Bl/6J, the most commonly used laboratory mouse strain, reduced their food intake by approximately 40% and lost weight. Mice fed an otherwise similar diet containing 10% protein ate more and gained weight. Both groups produced ketones, molecules generated when the body relies heavily on fat for fuel, suggesting that ketosis alone could not account for the difference.

“The result was so striking,” Zhang states.

It has been revealed that A closer look at the mice that lost weight revealed a more complicated picture. Although their glucose tolerance, or ability to manage changes in blood sugar, improved, they also developed fatty liver. Much of their weight loss was associated with reductions in lean mass, including muscle.

Recent findings suggest that to understand what was suppressing the animals’ appetite, the researchers investigated GDF15, a hormone released in response to cellular stress that can act on the brain to reduce food intake. When they conducted an experiment using genetically modified mice lacking GDF15, even the control mice unexpectedly failed to lose weight on the low-protein diet.

Recent findings suggest that “At first, I was a bit panicked,” Zhang states. “We had repeated the same experiment many times, and all of a sudden it didn’t work.”.

Retracing her steps, Zhang discovered that the genetically modified mice belonged to a different substrain, C57Bl/6N, which shares approximately 99.9% of its genome with the mice used in the earlier experiments.

“This realization was key, and it became the starting point for the rest of the study,” Zhang states.

The researchers eventually identified two genes that helped explain the difference: NNT, which protects cells against oxidative stress, and NLRP12, which helps regulate inflammation. The mice susceptible to weight loss, known as B6J, carried mutations in both genes. Feeding them the low-protein ketogenic diet triggered stress and inflammatory responses in the liver, accompanied by rising levels of GDF15 and an inflammation-associated protein called LCN2. Both proteins can influence appetite through their effects on the brain.

According to recent reports, restoring NNT function in B6J mice caused them to eat more and lose less weight. Conversely, disabling both genes in the genetically similar B6NJ mice, which normally carry functioning versions of both genes, caused them to eat less and lose weight. Treatment with N-acetylcysteine, an antioxidant with anti-inflammatory properties, also lowered circulating levels of GDF15 and LCN2, increased food intake, and reduced weight loss.

Industry observers note that zhang cautions that the findings cannot be directly applied to humans, particularly because the 5% protein diet contained substantially less protein than most ketogenic diets used in clinical trials. Future trials could benefit from paying closer attention to both dietary protein content and genetic differences among participants, while looking beyond weight loss and blood sugar control when assessing metabolic health.

The researchers now want to investigate whether other genetic factors influence responses to ketogenic diets and whether the changes they observed can be reversed once the diet is discontinued.

Reference: “Macronutrient composition and genetic background determine the response to a ketogenic diet in mice” by Zhaoyue Zhang, Alexandre Moura-Assis, Shanshan Liu, Alon Millet, Jordan Shaked, Divya Rajan, Katelin Chen, Anduena Toci, Chloe Schaefer, Ijeoma Obi, Sebastian Carrasco, Sandra Kelly Atkinson, Francois Marchildon, Hanan Alwaseem, Michael Isay-Del Viscio, Henrik Molina, Paul Cohen, Kivanc Birsoy and Jeffrey Friedman, 18 November 2026, Cell Metabolism.
