How Anthony Boyle Got Inside the Mind of Sam Bankman-Fried

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Anthony Boyle is in awe of the angels. “This is so good,” he says as a boyish smile forms on his 32-year-old face. Dressed in an immaculate black-and-pale-gold track jacket and pinstripe slacks, Boyle points through the Zoom screen, asking who the characters behind me are. I confess I don’t know. But the pair of fire-orange cosplay wings in my peripheral are hard to miss. “You gotta keep all this in,” Boyle says. (This is me upholding a promise.).

It’s Friday at Fresh York Comic Con when I occupy a table for a video call with Anthony Boyle. Beyond a bit part in Game of Thrones and roles in the movies Tolkien (2019) and Tetris (2023), Boyle’s growing body of work wouldn’t bring him to the East Coast’s nerd mecca. To wit: In 2024, he was on the FX series Say Nothing, with childhood friend Lola Petticrew, about the decades-long Troubles in Northern Ireland. Last year he starred in House of Guinness, a Netflix series about the famed brewery.

Soon he’ll appear in I See Buildings Fall Like Lightning, about tight-knit friends with unfulfilled dreams in the UK, which drew rave reviews at Cannes. After that, a mysterious project simply titled The Worst, “about all the worst people in the world,” Boyle teases. His costars include Keira Knightley and Alicia Vikander. There’s not a lightsaber or CGI monster in any of them.

But you might call his latest role supervillain-worthy. On the Netflix series The Altruists, Boyle steps into the shoes of Sam Bankman-Fried. The crypto billionaire and founder of FTX, whose peak net worth was an estimated $26 billion, is now serving a 25-year sentence for fraud, conspiracy, and money laundering. Boyle plays Bankman-Fried at different times in his life, from his days on the bottom rungs of trading firms to his meteoric rise and catastrophic downfall. The series also explores his relationship with Caroline Ellison (Julia Garner), the Alameda Research CEO who was instrumental in FTX’s fraud.

Part business thriller and part romantic dramedy, The Altruists is like if The Social Network cornered Natural Born Killers at a bar to talk blockchain. For its leading man, that world was foreign. When FTX declared bankruptcy in November 2022 and Bankman-Fried was arrested a month later, Boyle’s attention was … not there. “I wasn’t following at all,” he tells me. “I’m not interested in crypto or finance.” But he knows a love story, even a doomed one, when he sees it. “I was more interested in Sam and Caroline’s relationship when the script was presented to me. Crypto was a backdrop. The show is about love and the complexity within that love. If you love someone, tell them. That’s the overriding message.”.

Unlike Boyle’s portrayal of Bankman-Fried—nervous, indirect, lacking steadfast confidence—the actor himself is warm and relaxed. His blunt eloquence and honesty are flavored with his Belfast accent. Even after playing a real-life (former) tech billionaire for an eight-hour miniseries, he remains disconnected from that archetype. “The only one I researched was Sam. But I don’t think we should have a system where people can become billionaires,” he states. “It feels wrong. There’s people starving on the streets of Manhattan, where we’re talking now, and there’s a billionaire in a high-rise. That seems to me a failed system.”.

According to recent reports, before streaming demonstrates and acclaimed movies at Cannes, Boyle’s career came from especially humble beginnings. He used a wheelchair as a child, and he learned to improvise full conversations while watching other children play. While in high school, he was one of a handful of boys at an all-girls school and took on many of the male roles in the drama department.

According to recent reports, then he watched the 2006 movie This Is England. “Have you seen it?” Boyle asks me while anime characters pass by. I haven’t, though I vividly recall adding it to my Netflix queue once. “I thought it was the most incredible thing, like a lightning bolt, watching that movie. Thinking, I would love to do what they’re doing. It felt exhilarating and inspiring. It made an impression on me.”.

Boyle and Lola Petticrew, who drew acclaim for their work in the Hulu series Furious, tried out for every audition they could find. “We would Google ‘Belfast acting auditions,’ ” Boyle claims. Once, they did a production of Romeo & Juliet that Boyle says was “awful.”.

“It was set on a chessboard, for some reason. I was dressed as David Bowie, during his Ziggy Stardust era, and Lola was Gene Simmons from Kiss. I could only move in an L-shape, like the knight. Lola could only move like a pawn, and they had to curtsy every time they moved.”.

Industry observers note that he laughs thinking about it now. “You look at moments like this, when you’re doing something that you actually love, you’re like, ‘It adds up.’ ”.

In an interview with Esquire, Anthony Boyle reveals how he got into the mind of someone like Sam Bankman-Fried, his formative experience on Game of Thrones, and what cosplay he might attempt if he could ever go to Comic Con.

This interview has been edited for clarity.

Industry observers note that ESQUIRE: There are countless Sam Bankman-Fried interviews and documentaries about FTX. For The Altruists, how much did you let all this material inform your performance, and how much did you rely on your own instincts as an actor?

According to recent reports, ANTHONY BOYLE: You do a bit of both. You try to consume everything. Read all the books, watch every interview 5 million times. When you’re cycling or walking, you got headphones on, listening to his voice. It becomes part of your life—just trying to nail that voice. Trying to get into his head and mannerisms as much as possible. But on the first day of filming, I stopped. I stopped listening to his voice, stopped watching or reading anything [about him]. At day one, it became “our” Sam. Do as much research, fill your cup, then just forget about it.

After all that time listening and watching Sam, what were your impressions of him?

I was bowled over by his intelligence. I think when you’re blessed with these things like “genius,” you’re also cursed. It’s hard to function. Look at great thinkers like Nikola Tesla. He was in love with a pigeon. When you’re [one of these] great minds, you’re often plagued with not being able to have relationships with people. Sam, in his own words, struggles. It was a joy to play someone who was so different to me and so complex.

How did you embody Sam’s nervous and manic energy?

The nervousness is so important to him. In all the footage of him, you see his leg sort of going underneath the table. You listen to what he indicates about himself in the therapy scenes: “I’m a failure, people are gonna find out I’m a failure.” Repeating that in your head before a scene and having your heart rate going, just trying to constantly quell a panic attack and just thinking the pressure cooker he’s in—I love this girl and I need to be a success—repeating these things, you start to get nervous and you use that nervous energy.

Sources indicate that at the top of the series, Sam talks a lot about effective altruism. What was your understanding of that concept? Was Sam an effective effective altruist?

Being altruistic is being charitable. It is giving. To be altruistic, you could say, “I want to save lives.” Effective altruism would be “I go to Wall Street, make as much money as possible, and pay a hundred doctors to go save lives.” It’s trying to do the most good that you can do in the world. Did he succeed? In our version, he definitely tries to. He gets corrupted by power in our version of the story.

Recent findings suggest that so much of The Altruists is about this doomed love story with Caroline Ellison, played by Julia Garner. What was your experience working with her, playing out a volatile relationship?

Sources indicate that amazing. One of the best I’ve ever worked with, an incredible force of nature. It was an education to work. I honestly feel like, working with Julia every day, I picked up something. Some scenes, we’re just having fun. Whatever comes to mind, being playful and improvisational. And then there are scenes where we’re locked in, being so aggressive and nasty with one another. Those days felt good. It feels bad when you’re doing it. Your body doesn’t know you’re acting. It’s like, This is a good feeling because I know what’s happening is what I want. Working with her was like leveling up.

Is there a line of yours that best summarizes or reveals to us everything about Sam Bankman-Fried?

It has been revealed that when he speaks to Caroline and claims, “I am FTX.” That is so integral to who he is. He built this huge empire. I was saying in an interview earlier, broken men build empires. He feels broken. If he is FTX, and FTX is the biggest, the best, the fastest, the greatest, then he is. If he built this thing, then that’s who he is, not this broken individual.

Industry observers note that the Altruists positions itself as an unlikely love story set against financial fraud. But this was a real story where people lost their livelihoods. How can one reconcile the romantic-dramedy half of the show with the half where millions of real people are victims?

There’s a dissonance, isn’t there? I understand. I think [the victims] would be right about how they feel. You can’t tell a victim of a crime how they should feel. Alex Lawther’s character reminds Sam that this isn’t just ones and zeroes. This is real people losing money. This isn’t just rich people playing. It’s heartbreaking. So many people committed suicide, got divorced, lost everything. That’s the through line with Alex Lawther’s character. He’s the voice of reason that reminds our audience there’s real life at stake.

The Altruists is one of several new pieces of media about the tech industry and the billionaires behind them. There’s The Social Reckoning, Artificial, You Can See Everything. Why are we interested in these stories right now?

It’s so interesting. I didn’t know any of these projects were happening until this morning, doing this press tour. These people are shaping our lives, and people are interested in how these sort of masked men behind closed doors are controlling everything. All we’re hearing in the news is AI recently, like AI is gonna kill us all. Makes us think of I, Robot or something. It’s interesting to get behind into these people’s brains and go, “What are they motivated by? What’s pushing them?”.

According to recent reports, one of your earliest acting credits was for one episode of Game of Thrones, as a guard. What did that job mean for you as a very upcoming actor at the time?

It was great. It was my first time on a proper set. I remember a fancy car picking me up, everyone in my street waving me off, shouting like, “Go on, off to Hollywood!” I was in the front seat, beeping the horn. The driver was like, “This has never happened.” I remember not knowing what was happening on set. I remember Gemma Whelan and Alfie Allen being so kind. I had two lines, and they were like, “Do you wanna run the lines? Do you wanna run them again?” They took care of me. I think of that every time I see someone young on set. Reach out to them, tell them they’re doing a good job. Give ’em a nod. It can be a scary environment when you’re not used to it.

Recent findings suggest that your very next thing is I See Buildings Fall Like Lightning. What is it about, and what does it mean to you?

In a recent development, it’s sort of about capitalism in the way The Altruists is. This is about family and friends in a community and how the working class in England and the Midlands have been decimated by the Tory government. It’s a socialist war cry by Keiran Goddard and Enda Walsh, one of the great Irish writers. He wrote Hunger and Disco Pigs. He said he had written this role with me in mind. It was a pinch-me moment, because I’d read his plays at school and would have given my left arm to work with him. When he sent me a letter, I was overjoyed.

I also got a TV show that I’m excited about, Close to Home. It’s my first time executive producing. The guy who wrote it sat beside my brother in school. It’s about a guy who punches someone on a night out, gets arrested for it, and has to confront his life choices. It’s close to my heart because it’s about West Belfast, where I grew up.

Recent findings suggest that in the spirit of Comic Con going on behind me, do you have a weird interest or fandom? If you were here, who would you be dressed as?

Industry observers note that [Laughs.] Van Morrison. I would be dressed as Van Morrison. I’m obsessed with him. He is my favorite singer and an incredible musician. I listen to performances he’s done, like 1972 in Latest York. I listen to deep cuts. I would be at Comic Con dressed up as him, which wouldn’t be a great costume. The winged angel might be better.
