What Caused The FAA Meltdown? Duffy And Mullin Give Different Accounts

According to recent reports, the most revealing moment in last night’s press conference about the FAA ground stop came when Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin appeared to explain what Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy preferred to talk around: the government’s efforts the day before to update the system seem to have triggered the failure. That deserved a clear explanation. Instead, we got praise for President Trump, blame directed at Congress, and a demand for $50 billion.

Recent findings suggest that the FAA lifted its nationwide ground stop Friday evening after more than four hours of disruption. Getting departures moving again was welcome news. Explaining why they stopped was another matter.

Recent findings suggest that I updated our original story after the press conference last night, but left it neutral, resisting the urge the opine. It is worth watching below, particularly the exchanges with reporters (a full transcript is available here). But I knew I had to address the conference in a bit more depth (as One Mile At A Time has also done so well) .

Duffy described an aging communications system overwhelmed by data:.

“Our switches, our routers actually got overloaded with data and they went down. And — I shouldn’t say they went down; they started to give us inconsistency in the data from those routers. And because of that, we shut the system down.”.

He emphasized that the newer equipment had not failed:.

Recent findings suggest that “And our new IP switches did not go down; it’s the old hardware that went down. And I think the question for the American people is, do you want your Congress to fund this?”.

According to recent reports, but what changed Friday? Old equipment does not become old overnight. What caused the overload that made a nationwide departure halt necessary?

Mullin offered a much more specific explanation:.

“What it appears to be right now is, as Secretary Duffy and FAA was going in and putting patches into the system, they were putting new equipment and new technology, and that new equipment, new technology just wasn’t — it was overwhelming the older system, which is why Secretary Duffy made it very clear the patchwork doesn’t work.”.

There it is. According to Mullin’s preliminary account, the process of introducing patches and fresh technology was overwhelming the older system. And look at Duffy’s reaction:.

Sources indicate that those explanations are not impossible to reconcile, with the key being what Duffy left out of his explanation. Latest equipment can remain operational while changes associated with it overload older equipment elsewhere. But that makes Duffy’s emphasis on the new switches staying online an incomplete answer to the question passengers actually needed answered and my mother always taught me that a half-truth can actually be a lie.

Sources indicate that if the upgrade process contributed to the outage, saying the latest equipment did not fail does not tell us whether the transition was properly managed. Later, Duffy acknowledged the limits of what officials knew:.

“So, we’re still working on the details of what went wrong today.”.

It has been revealed that “I’ll be happy to give you more information when we have it, I don’t want to give you the wrong information. Our team is working overtime trying to navigate what happened here, what was the cause and how do we prevent it in the future should similar situations arise?”.

Recent findings suggest that that is a perfectly reasonable thing to say during an unfolding investigation. It would have been a much better starting point than presenting the outage as an obvious demonstration that Congress needed to send more money.

I am quite certain that replacing decades-old aviation infrastructure is not easy and I expect hiccups along the way as we update our aging air traffic control systems. At the same time, I expect the people responsible for the project to explain a major failure candidly.

Something like: “The government is replacing a sprawling, aging system while keeping aircraft moving and old and latest technology must coexist during the transition, but we are investigating whether a change produced unexpected consequences, and working around the clock to keep it from happening again.”.

I would have been far more understanding of that explanation.

Recent findings suggest that “Growing pains” cannot become a blanket defense for shutting down air traffic across the country if there is evidence of poorly tested updates, inadequate safeguards, or a failure to provide a workable rollback. But it would acknowledge the difficulty honestly. We can distinguish a hard project that encounters a serious setback from officials trying to sell that setback as proof of their own excellent leadership.

Early in the briefing, Mullin offered this assessment:.

“This is what the leadership of President Trump looks like by the organization and the team he’s put together. We’re built to handle crises like this, and that’s exactly what we’re doing. We’re moving at an unprecedented pace as a whole of government to make sure that we are doing the public justice by making sure everybody is safe, which everybody is.”.

In a recent development, I am thankful passengers were safe. Halting departures when communications become unreliable is the correct decision and even with this administration I have no fear that the skies are unsafe, at least when it comes to air traffic control.

But why was a nationwide operational failure the moment to congratulate the president and his team?

The public needed a straightforward explanation and some acknowledgment of the disruption. Instead, we got two cheerleaders praising the emperor with no clothes.

The administration’s performance should be judged after we understand what happened, not advertised as a success while officials are still trying to establish the cause.

And there is an awkward comparison with the response to the 2023 FAA outage. Donald Trump Jr., not the president, used that failure to attack then-Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg as incompetent:.

Breaking – All flights nationwide have beengrounded – the #FAA is facing a computer outage.

Yet another major blow to incompetent Transportation Sect Mayor Pete and people who get key jobs with no experience to check some identity politics boxes.

— Donald Trump Jr. (@DonaldJTrumpJr) January 11, 2023.

If an FAA outage demonstrates incompetence when the other party is in charge, what does an even longer nationwide shutdown under your own administration demonstrate?

Duffy was clear about what he wanted:.

In a recent development, “I’ve asked, the president has asked for $30 billion. I want $50 billion.”.

I support modernizing air traffic control. This country should have reliable aviation infrastructure, and that requires sustained investment. But a funding demand does not help with root-cause analysis.

Sources indicate that would having the entire amount appropriated have prevented Friday’s failure? Perhaps a completed replacement system would have avoided this particular vulnerability. But if the immediate problem involved introducing new technology into an older network, even a fully funded project would still go through the same transition. Money cannot eliminate the need for testing and competent implementation.

Recent findings suggest that congress already approved $12.5 billion over five years in July 2025 for air traffic control improvements and controller hiring. Earlier Friday, before the outage, Duffy and White House budget director Russell Vought sought another $30.1 billion for a package covering both air traffic control and airport infrastructure.

Sources indicate that question, though: Republicans have controlled the presidency and both chambers of Congress for nearly two years. When a reporter raised that point, Duffy’s immediate response was to ask which network the reporter represented.

The answer to a question about your record should not depend on whether the person asking works for NBC or Fox….

I think this press conference helps illustrate a larger problem for the administration.

According to recent reports, democrats have won control of both chambers of Congress. My reading of that result is that Americans are becoming impatient with a government that demands credit constantly while treating accountability as someone else’s responsibility.

There were many reasons people voted as they did, but I see a common thread between the Republican victory in 2024 and the Democratic wave in 2026: voters want results, and they are willing to punish the party that fails to deliver them.

Most people are fairly pragmatic about whether their government works. They want competence. They want officials who can distinguish between explaining a problem and trying to talk their way out of responsibility for it.

My view is that the election was a rejection of the self-dealing and incompetence that have characterized this administration, along with a Republican Congress that had two years of unified government and far too little to show for it.

It would be inaccurate to say Congress accomplished nothing. It certinlay pushed through a massive tax cut. But the Congressional Budget Office estimated that law would add $3.4 trillion to deficits over 2025–2034, excluding additional debt-service costs.

In a recent development, congress could find the votes for another enormous deficit-financed package. Now the administration wants to explain an aviation meltdown primarily by pointing to money Congress has not provided.

Sorry, but that is not a serious argumentfrom people who controlled the entire process.

According to recent reports, I still want Congress to fund necessary aviation improvements. Passengers should not suffer because lawmakers want to score a point against Duffy. But providing money and demanding accountability should happen together.

Recent findings suggest that the aging air traffic control system needs replacing. Friday’s ground stop makes the urgency painfully clear.

What the press conference did not establish is that an additional appropriation would have prevented this particular failure. Mullin’s account instead raised the possibility that the modernization process itself contributed to it, while Duffy focused on the old equipment and the need for more money.

An honest explanation about a difficult transition would have earned far more understanding from me. A clear account of what failed, followed by a credible plan to prevent a repeat, would be even better.

Instead, we got an administration congratulating itself before it could clearly explain what went wrong. After nearly two years in charge, Americans are entitled to expect better.
