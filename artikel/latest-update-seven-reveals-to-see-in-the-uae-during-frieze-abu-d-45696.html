Rizq Art Initiative, Abu Dhabi, 6 November-10 January 2027.

Recent findings suggest that in What We Left in the Sandbox, Reem Al Mubarak’s solo exhibition at Rizq Art Initiative, the Emirati artist explores how child’s play can be a powerful medium of creativity and memory. Through a series of sculptural works, etchings and drawings, Al Mubarak strives to recall specific places and objects from her own childhood, embracing the distortions of memory. A monumental sandcastle evokes a child’s perspective of space, while bubble wrap that has been compulsively popped or left intact is cast in resin, turning a disposable material into an enduring sculptural form.

The exhibition is curated by the architect and researcher Azza Aboualam, who was the curator of the UAE’s national pavilion during the 2025 Venice Architecture Biennale.

Emirati artist Shaikha Al Mazrou at the Cultural Foundation, where she is staging a solo show Courtesy the Cultural Foundation.

Cultural Foundation, Abu Dhabi, 12 November-15 March 2027.

Sources indicate that in 2022, Shaikha Al Mazrou presented her large-scale work Red Stack as part of Frieze Sculpture in London’s Regent’s Park. The work exemplifies her deft approach to defying perceptions through her choice of materials. It features a pile of red cushions constructed from fibreglass and resin on a metal framework. Despite the hardness of the materials, the creased and pillowy cushions evoke a sense of softness.

In a recent development, she receives her strongest spotlight to date with her solo exhibition at the Abu Dhabi’s Cultural Foundation. It will feature past work alongside upcoming commissions created specifically for the venue’s atrium. Al Mazrou’s work is also on view at Frieze Abu Dhabi with Lawrie Shabibi.

In a recent development, installation view: All Manner of Experiments: Legacies of the Baghdad Modern Art Group at the NYU Abu Dhabi Art Gallery, 2026. Pictured here: Rand Abdul Jabbar, Earthly Wonders, Celestial Beings (series), (2024).

NYU Abu Dhabi Art Gallery, Abu Dhabi, until 3 January 2027.

Sources indicate that in 1951, Jewad Selim and Shakir Hassan Al Said established a movement that sought to express Modernist art principles through a uniquely Iraqi perspective. It would prove to be one of the most defining moments of Arab art.

Industry observers note that all Manner of Experiments is perhaps the most comprehensive survey of the Baghdad Modern Art Group. It presents seminal works from the period as well as contemporary pieces that highlight the ongoing legacies of the group.

The exhibition at the New York University (NYU) Abu Dhabi Art Gallery brings together works from 1946 to 2023, from private and institutional collections. Selim’s experiments with abstraction are represented, as well as Al Said’s figurative expressions and his latter work that employed Arabic letters to convey an inner dimension. Key works by Dia al-Azzawi, Madiha Umar and Khalid al-Rahal are also presented, with the exhibition concluding with contemporary pieces by Mahmoud Obaidi, Walid Siti and Rand Abdul Jabbar, among others.

A key point of departure in this show is Selim’s Freedom Monument, a 50m-long sculpture in Tahrir Square that was commissioned just after the 1958 military coup that overthrew the Hashemite monarchy and established the Iraqi Republic. Though Selim died in 1961 before the sculpture fully materialised, it is considered to be one of his most enduring works, potent with the sensibilities that inspired the Baghdad Modern Art Group. Read from right to left—like an Arabic text—the monument tells the story of the revolution. Its bronze figures draw from Sumerian motifs while also employing Modernist aesthetics.

A carved ivory jewelled casket on show in the exhibition Spices and Wonders at Louvre Abu Dhabi © Department of Culture and Tourism – Abu Dhabi; Photo: Sylvie Van Roey.

Louvre Abu Dhabi, until 14 February 2027.

The Indian Ocean has been a pivotal node of cultural exchange for centuries, and an exhibition at the Louvre Abu Dhabi unravels the ancient maritime connections that linked Europe, Africa and the Middle East to South and Southeast Asia. Held in collaboration with the Guimet Museum in Paris, the exhibition charts these links through artefacts, textiles, ceramics and spices.

According to recent reports, this investigation extends to Art Here—a staple show in Louvre Abu Dhabi’s calendar, presenting works shortlisted for the annual Richard Mille Art Prize. This year’s exhibition, exploring the theme of “Confluences” is curated by Kamini Sawhney (until 28 February 2027).

Serendipitously, the theme of Frieze Masters Abu Dhabi also looks towards the Indian Ocean, presenting artefacts, maps and rare books. Presentations at Frieze will contribute to research into an understudied historical topic with implications for the modern era.

Luisa Dörr, various works from Escaramuzas, (2024–ongoing).

Photography Gallery, Sharjah, until 29 November.

Recent findings suggest that the 13th edition of Vantage Point takes place at the Photography Gallery in Sharjah, a venue that opened in November 2025. The exhibition, an annual fixture in Sharjah’s arts programme since 2013, is being held in an overhauled format. Each year, dozens of international artists have taken part in the exhibition, which celebrates photographic works across genres, from photojournalism to more experimental compositions. Vantage Point has now been pared down, exhibiting commissioned works by six photographers, and it will also be a biennial event moving forward.

In a recent development, sathish Kumar presents a series of images that focus on his brother, an oil worker in Abu Dhabi. Photographs of Kumar’s family in the industrial city compound in Al Ruwais are juxtaposed with those taken in Bengaluru in southern India. Much of the latter centre around the construction site of the family home. The series, titled Bond, alludes to the fraught experiences of many South Asian labourers who travel to the Gulf with the aim of financially supporting their parents and families back home.

The other works featured in the exhibition are equally evocative and personal. Latifa AlBokhari presents tender images that mark key moments in a woman’s life in the Gulf, from henna traditions to wedding preparations. Guanyu Xu reveals life-sized images of domestic interiors that offer clues to the personalities of the people that inhabit them. Luisa Dörr documents with painterly sensibilities a female-focused equestrian school in Mexico. Maheder Haileselassie spotlights archival images of Arbegnoch fighters, who resisted the Italian occupation of Ethiopia between 1936 and 1941. Finally, Adam Rouhana presents larger-than-life portraits of Palestinians from the West Bank, whom he photographed in a travelling studio.

Courtesy of the artist and ATHR Gallery.

The Jameel Arts Centre has given five artists each a dedicated space within the institution.

One room charts four decades of Walid Siti's practice. Sketches of dark, spiralling towers embody the human pathology of scaling literal and proverbial heights. Sculptures and acrylic paintings of mountains, meanwhile, beckon to the peaks of his native Iraqi Kurdistan. A commissioned large-scale sculpture is the centrepiece of the exhibition. It outlines a mountain in stacked polystyrene, with writing on its underside that reflects on the turbulent history of Kurdistan.

Bahraini painter Lulua Alyahya presents the urban and natural environments of the Gulf with diffused brushstrokes. Her work often teeters on the absurd, with lions scowling from computer screens and chickens hatching eggs that fry on the pavement.

Afrah Shafiq is showing Nobody Knows For Certain, an interactive video game work that drawns on a lesser-known but rich period of cultural exchange between India and the Soviet Union that took place between the 1950s and 80s.

In a recent development, newly introduced Dream Machine Project, a three-metre-high installation by Shezad Dawood, is a gyrating tower pulsing with light. Inspired by Brion Gysin’s Dreamachine, which the artist devised after his time in Tangier and after “experimenting with altered states of being” with members of the Beat Generation. The installation was prototyped by artisans in Fes, Morocco, with the final version engineered in Slough, England.

Trembling Hands (2024) by Bani Abidi gathers the artist's portraits of artists, journalists and politicians who spoke out against the genocide of Palestinians, some of whom faced backlash, censorship or repression as a result.

Installation view of Rohini Devasher, Latent Fields (Field Five) (2023); Latent Fields (Field Six) (2023).

Ishara Art Foundation, Dubai, until 15 December.

The sky held it, the earth remembered presents the work of Pakistani artist Shahana Rajani, Indian artist Rohini Devasher and Bangladeshi artist Sarker Protick, all of whom consider how memory and history are preserved and transmitted.

In a recent development, in Rajani’s three-channel video Lines that World a River, a woman paints a house along the banks of a river. The scene is vibrant with its textured blues and chalky greens. Rajani zooms in on the woman’s fingers. Her nails are streaked with the white of the paint she uses to draw the river’s foam. The drawing is of the woman’s ancestral village along the Indus River Delta—now submerged after the construction of hydraulic dams and barrages.

The work—a newly introduced iteration of Rajani’s Four Acts of Recovery, which won the Han Nefkens Foundation’s inaugural South Asian Video Art Production Grant in 2023—demonstrates how displaced communities in the Indus region have developed mural painting practices to remember their lost homes.

In a recent development, devasher’s Latent Fields comprises three sprawling silk panels that hang from the ceiling. Their dots and patterns allude to sunspots and other solar events, coming together as a monumental record of celestial time.

In Dhaka 1217, meanwhile, Protick presents 135 photographs that capture the transformation of the neighbourhood where the artist was born and continues to live.
