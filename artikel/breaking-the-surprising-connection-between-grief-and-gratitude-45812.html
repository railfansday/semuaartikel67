Breaking: The Surprising Connection Between Grief and Gratitude

Life as I knew it quietly began to unravel on a drizzly January morning in 2022.

Recent findings suggest that I was standing at the kitchen sink in a puffer jacket, staring out at my favorite persimmon tree, now bare. Only weeks before, the tree had been so full of persimmons that we collected them in huge plastic garbage cans for our neighbors to take by the grocery bagful. Our Labrador-ish mutt, Buster, was fat from persimmon overconsumption.

Now that the kids were gone, my husband Mark and I conserved energy by leaving the downstairs heat off, telling each other we couldn’t justify heating the whole house just to keep the kitchen warm. We were real empty-nesters, ready to downsize.

Industry observers note that within weeks, we stripped our house of everything personal. We took down family photos, scraped butterfly stickers from hidden corners, and painted over the teal walls preteen girls had decorated. All traces of our family life vanished. We put our home on the market. It was the end of an era.

In December, my seemingly super-fit mom was diagnosed with ovarian cancer. Our house still hadn’t sold—by the time we put it on the market, mortgage rates had started climbing for the first time in two decades—but I moved into my childhood bedroom at my parents’ home to help my dad care for my mom. She passed away four months later.

In a recent development, A week before my mom’s memorial, I myself was diagnosed with invasive breast cancer. And a few days after my first cancer-related surgery, a close cousin died of an accidental overdose just after his 50th birthday.

Sources indicate that also during that time, a dear college friend, a fit, athletic doctor, had a fatal heart attack. And while I was having radiation for my breast cancer, our beloved dog Buster was diagnosed with lymphoma and needed to be put down.

According to recent reports, other losses were expected and even positive. Our house finally sold, and so we remarked goodbye to the home where we had raised our four kids. I packed up that house through tears—for my mom, for my health, for my beloved dog and cousin and college friend, sobbing as I threw away things that marked my kids’ childhoods: boxes of ribbons from horse indicates and swim meets, SAT score reports, so many old shoes and backpacks, homemade Harry Potter wands.

Mark and I put our stuff in storage and moved in with my dad for real while we renovated a latest house in Berkeley. I loved this time with my dad; it made my cancer treatment and everything else bearable. He had always been my person, and now I got to cry and laugh with him every day, with no space between us.

Recent findings suggest that about a year after my mom died, we finally moved into our latest house. The first morning I woke up there and looked out the window at the whole Bay Area, I was astonished to see not one, but four rainbows touching down on the water of the San Francisco Bay. I felt welcomed and reassured that I was right where I was meant to be.

Sources indicate that but then my brother-in-law Brian was diagnosed with a malignant brain tumor, the kind that doesn’t usually respond to chemo or radiation. They did everything anyway—brain surgery, chemotherapy, radiation, experimental drugs, an electromagnetic cap, expensive immunotherapy from Germany—but less than nine months later, he too passed away. I’ve never known a man with a bigger heart than Brian; when he died, we lost the emotional center on Mark’s side of the family.

Industry observers note that our family was devastated. Again.

With each of these losses and life changes, I picked myself up and told my friends that at least it wasn’t my dad. When my dad dies, I’m going to stay in bed for a year, I noted. When my dad dies, expect my complete collapse. I started believing that I could bear anything except for losing my father, my oldest and dearest friend. The loss of my father would leave a hole in my heart I knew I could never fill.

And then, in April of 2025, one month to the day after we lost Brian, my 84-year-old dad passed away.

He was still driving, doing Pilates, and walking his dog Annie around his hilly neighborhood. He was still sharp as a tack, still hilarious, still going out every day to meet his many friends or his brother, Jud. He still volunteered and mentored others.

Recent findings suggest that one week, he was fully with us, bringing me a burrito for dinner at my nephew’s Little League game, taking us to dinner at the club where he’d been a lifelong member, buying tickets to a Giants game. The next week, he was gone.

My brother and I decided it “made sense” to sell our childhood home, a place that had anchored our extended family for nearly 50 years. But I could not make sense of how I could lose everything that centered me, my whole foundation, so fucking fast.

Industry observers note that still, I didn’t utterly collapse. I learned how to grieve in small moments throughout the day; how to keep putting one foot in front of the other while still feeling loss. I cried before and after work as I walked my dad’s sweet little Annie. I broke down at the grocery store, in restaurants, while hiking with friends, at the post office. I wept while packing up my parents’ house and while pulling apart their photo albums. I was exhausted, hollowed out.

My heart was broken, but I got out of bed every morning.

It has been revealed that I started fresh, truly energizing work projects and slogged through my parents’ stuff and another year of difficult firsts. The first Father’s Day without my dad, the first family birthday not celebrated at my parents’ house. As the first Thanksgiving without my dad and Brian approached, my therapist remarked that grief was often surprisingly life-enhancing. That was turning out to be true for me.

It has been revealed that thanksgiving has always been my favorite holiday. I usually host our big family, and I always have a lot to say at dinnertime about what I am grateful for. That year, the intimate connection between loss and gratitude had become painfully obvious to me.

In a recent development, genuine gratitude—when we really pause to acknowledge how valuable something is—inherently involves recognizing the impermanence of that thing. True gratitude carries the awareness that what we’re grateful for won’t last forever; it’s here now but could be gone tomorrow. That awareness is what makes a moment feel more vivid and precious.

Industry observers note that I think one gift of loss is that it can make us more intensely grateful. Not because we’re anxious about losing someone or something, but because we’ve learned viscerally that everything is temporary—and therefore extraordinary.

Sources indicate that this crucible of loss and gratitude burned away my tolerance for anything that didn’t feel essential, helping me return to a deep spirituality. Standing at the edge of my own mortality and watching the structures of my life dissolve one by one fundamentally reordered my sense of what matters. Grief is the price of love, and it teaches us that love is what matters most.

It has been revealed that so much loss has made pretense impossible for me. Grief strips us down and cracks us open, breaking down the defenses and distractions that keep us living on the surface. In the past few years, I’ve been to deep places, emotionally and existentially, that only truth can reach. I can’t un-know that now. Superficiality has started to feel like I’m being asked to forget the hard-won truth about how fragile and precious life actually is.

Recent findings suggest that when we’re grieving, we can’t pretend. We can’t avoid our own vulnerability or the fact that everything ends. And while that’s devastating, it’s also strangely liberating. I feel more at peace, more permeable, more present, more profoundly loving, more alive to the texture of my lived experience. I’m attuned to what’s real and meaningful. I feel closer to and more in love with my friends and family than ever before. I feel things so deeply, and that depth is a kind of richness.

It has been revealed that all of this is to say that growth happens in the broken-open places in our hearts. Loss and gratitude both dissolve the boundary between ourselves and the larger human experience. When we love and when we grieve, we join an ancient river of sorrow and love that connects us to everyone who has ever loved and lost.

I would never choose the losses I’ve endured. I’d give anything to have my people back, and not to have had cancer. But this is the grief-gratitude paradox: I also wouldn’t trade what grief has taught me, how deeply I feel, or who I am now because of all I’ve lost.

In a recent development, so this Thanksgiving, I’ll be at the table again, with fewer people than I’d like but more love than I ever knew I had room for. When it’s my turn to say what I’m grateful for, I won’t need to think about it. Every single moment is a gift. It’s all temporary, and it’s all extraordinary.
