Scientists Say De-Extinction Is Impossible—Because Extinction Isn’t What You Think It Is

In a recent development, what does it mean for a species to go extinct? At first glance, the idea is pretty simple: Here today, gone tomorrow. But this uncomplicated view of existence on the planet is what leads some companies to think that, if you use 21st century tools to tweak the genomes of existing animals—even those separated by millions of years of evolution—then you can effectively bring the dead back to life.

In April 2025, the de-extinction tech giant Colossal Biosciences did just that, announcing (with George R.R. Martin) the return of the dire wolf in the form of three genetically modified pups named Romulus, Remus, and Khaleesi. Almost immediately, the claims of the dire wolf’s return were challenged by scientists who noted that the resulting animals were merely genetically modified gray wolves with just 20 gene edits.

“Would a chimpanzee with 20 gene edits be human?” Pontus Skoglund, a geneticist at the Francis Crick Institute in London, wrote on Bluesky following the announcement. “If you lined up a dire wolf genome with a gray wolf genome, there would be millions of differences. These individuals seem optimistically 1/100,000th dire wolf.”.

In a recent development, now, a new article published in the journal Biology Letters argues that the very idea of de-extinction misunderstands what extinction means in the first place. The problem, according to Wheaton College marine biologist Andrew Davinack and biochemist Charlotte K. Daniels, is “lineage–genome conflation,” or the belief that reconstructing a genome is the same as bringing back an extinct lineage.

“We argue that de-extinction cannot recover extinct species because extinction represents the termination of an evolutionary lineage, not just the disappearance of genetic information,” the authors write. “Species are historically continuous population-level lineages shaped by gene flow, mutation, drift, selection, demographic history and ecological interaction. A reconstructed genome, even if highly accurate, represents at most a partial snapshot of an individual organism.”.

In the case of the dire wolf, even if Colossal reconstructed the species’ genome perfectly, one big missing piece would be the dire wolf’s ecological niche, which disappeared over the 10,000 years since it last roamed North America. As the authors note, the “prey assemblages, competitors, parasites, climate and selective conditions” differ greatly from the time of the Late Pleistocene, and a species can’t be so easily removed from its ecological context. Even the film Jurassic Park—a very common comparison to Colossal Biosciences—argued this point, saying the “dinosaurs” (spliced with frog DNA, by the way) had no real ecological role to play in the modern world.

“The dire-wolf case therefore illustrates lineage–genome conflation in its clearest form: selected traits inferred from an extinct lineage were reconstructed within the cellular, developmental and genealogical context of an extant one,” the authors write.

Recent findings suggest that but the dire wolf isn’t the only de-extinction project being eyed by Colossal Biosciences. The company has frequently communicated its interest in bringing back the woolly mammoth and even partnered with Peter Jackson (yes, that Peter Jackson) to announce its efforts to de-extinct the giant moa, a flightless bird endemic to Newly introduced Zealand.

While these are all flashy announcements, complete with celebrity ambassadors for the project, Colossal is also working on projects that Davinack and Daniels say are more worthwhile, such as the effort to introduce genetic diversity in populations of the endangered American red wolf or the black-footed ferret.

“Genomic-assisted breeding or gene editing may allow some of that variation to be reintroduced into living populations,” the authors write. “Such applications do not reverse extinctions of course, but they may slow genomic erosion, reduce inbreeding depression and restore adaptative potential before lineage termination occurs.”.

In other words, let’s take care of the animals already in crisis before we start resurrecting ones from the distant past.
