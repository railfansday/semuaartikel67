Industry observers note that maybe it is having a family that loves them. Maybe it is a warm bed, a full bowl, and a safe place to sleep. Or maybe luck is something a little more fundamental: having people who see you as an individual and take the time to understand what you need.

At Lucky Dog Rescue and Adoption Center near Lake Chapala, Mexico, the dogs may still be waiting for permanent homes, but in the meantime, they are surrounded by caring people who show up for them day after day.

At Lucky Dog, everyone has a unique way to help and collaborate.

Some volunteers walk dogs. Others train them, clean their enclosures, help with fundraising, or coordinate veterinary care. Some foster dogs in their homes. Others help with the less visible work that keeps the center running.

Industry observers note that when I visited Lucky Dog, I arrived with Mark and Nancy. Mark helped build the Thursday volunteer group, bringing together people who wanted to help in different ways.

Mark has an injured knee, and at the moment, he cannot walk the long distances over cobblestone roads around the center, but he and Nancy have their own important role. They bring chairs into the enclosures and spend about 20 minutes with the dogs, offering pats, treats, kind words, and ear scratches. The dogs happily settle in beside them.

Recent findings suggest that different volunteer groups cover each day of the week. Some take dogs for walks, while others help with training, cleaning, maintenance, administration, or fundraising.

It has been revealed that that Thursday, each person simply did what they came to do. For the dogs, every part of that effort is what makes them feel lucky.

Sources indicate that during the day, the dogs stay in pairs in larger enclosures. They are taken out for long walks or playtime in the larger garden areas around the center. In the mornings and evenings, Valentin, one of only two paid workers at the rescue, moves the dogs between their daytime and nighttime enclosures and feeds them breakfast and dinner. During the day, volunteers help with walks, playtime, training, and puppy lunchtime.

At night, the dogs return to smaller individual enclosures.

When a dog needs medical attention, a veterinarian comes to the center to examine and treat them. Volunteers then follow the veterinarian's instructions and continue treatment.

From Valentin's morning and evening routine to the volunteers who walk, train, feed, and care for the dogs, many people contribute to a Lucky Dog's day.

According to recent reports, the roots of today's Lucky Dog Rescue go back to 2001, when Geoffrey Kaye and Lourdes Avila started an animal shelter in Riberas del Pilar. When the shelter closed in 2012, the commitment to helping the area's dogs continued. Volunteers regrouped with veterinarian Dr. Antonio Ladron, and in December 2012, Lucky Dog Rescue and Adoption opened its doors.

Since then, the center has continued caring for abandoned and abused dogs while helping prepare them for permanent homes.

In 2025, Lucky Dog took in 23 dogs and placed 23 through adoption or transfer. Approximately 35 newly introduced volunteers joined, with around 30 committing to weekly shifts. The center's intended capacity is 22 dogs, yet its average monthly census was close to 30 during the year.

Those numbers give you some idea of the work happening behind every individual dog.
