Conference talks fail on slides more often than on content. The speaker knows the material and has something worth saying, but the deck is a wall of bullets, or a template everyone in the room has seen before, or forty slides for a twenty-minute slot. AI presentation makers can now produce a complete deck from an outline in minutes, which solves the time problem and creates a new one: the output tends toward exactly the uniform, stock-image, bullet-heavy style that audiences have learned to tune out.

This article explains how conference slides differ from other decks, why AI-generated decks look generic and what specifically causes it, a workflow that uses an AI presentation maker for speed while keeping the talk distinctive, how to handle the slide types that matter most in a talk (the opening, the demonstration, the data, the close), and a checklist to run before the talk.

A conference talk is spoken, timed and performed to a room. That changes the rules compared with a reading deck or a business presentation.

The causes are specific and therefore fixable.

Sources indicate that template uniformity. Every slide uses the same layout family, so the deck has no visual rhythm. Audiences read the sameness as mass-produced.

In a recent development, bullet default. Given a paragraph, the tool produces three to five bullets. Given twenty paragraphs, twenty bullet slides.

Stock imagery. Icons and photos chosen by keyword match the topic superficially and add no meaning. A talk about database indexing gets a picture of a filing cabinet.

Label titles. “Introduction”, “Background”, “Key Challenges”, “Conclusion”. Titles that name the category rather than make the point.

Sources indicate that flattened emphasis. The tool does not know which of your ideas is the one the audience should leave with, so every slide gets equal weight.

It has been revealed that no pacing. Every slide carries the same amount of content, so there is no build, no pause, no payoff.

Each of these is a default that can be overridden, either in how the input is prepared or in a short editing pass afterwards.

It has been revealed that step 1: Write the talk before the slides. Not a script necessarily, but the argument: what the audience knows at the start, what they know at the end, and the four to six moves that get them there. Write the one sentence you want them to repeat to a colleague afterwards. If this is not clear, no deck will fix it.

Step 2: Write the slide titles as the things you will say. Each title is a sentence, in your voice, that states the point of that moment in the talk. “Indexes make reads fast by making writes slower” is a title. “Index trade-offs” is not. Read the titles in order; they should be the talk in miniature.

According to recent reports, step 3: Mark the pacing. Next to each title, note whether the slide is a hold (a key point, stays up while you talk for a minute or more), a beat (a quick slide in a sequence), or a reveal (something shown, a demo, a result). This tells you where to put effort.

Step 4: Generate. Paste the titles with one or two sentences of content under each into an ai presentation maker. Ask for a slide count close to your title count. Pick the plainest theme available; distinctiveness will come from your content, not the template.

Step 5: Strip the bullets. On every hold slide, delete the bullets and leave the title, or the title plus one image or one number. On beat slides, keep at most one short line. The content that was in the bullets goes into speaker notes, which is where it belonged.

Step 6: Replace every stock image. Delete icons. Replace keyword-matched photos with your own material: a screenshot, a diagram you drew, a chart from your data, a photograph you took, a single large word. If a slide has no genuine visual, a title alone on a plain background is better than a filler image.

It has been revealed that step 7: Build the reveals by hand. Demos, before-and-after comparisons, the key chart. These are the slides the audience will remember, and they need more care than a generator can give. See the section below.

According to recent reports, step 8: Break the layout rhythm deliberately. Make the two or three most important slides look different: full-bleed image, a single enormous number, a black slide with one line of white text. Contrast is what makes a moment land.

Step 9: Rehearse with a timer. The slide count will be wrong in the first rehearsal. Cut, merge or split until the pacing matches the time.

The opening. Not a title slide with your name and a logo while you say hello. Open on something that earns attention: a surprising number, a question, a screenshot of the problem, a single provocative sentence. Your name and affiliation can come on slide two, or be said rather than shown.

In a recent development, the problem statement. One slide, one sentence, stays up while you explain why the audience should care. No bullets.

The demonstration. If the talk has a demo, record it. Live demos fail in rooms with unreliable connections and unfamiliar projectors. A recorded demo with the audio off, narrated live, gives the same effect with none of the risk. If you must go live, have the recording ready as a fallback.

Sources indicate that the data. One chart per slide, one message per chart, the message in the title. Strip gridlines, legends and secondary series. Highlight the one line or bar that matters in colour and grey out the rest. Label directly. Type large enough to read from the back.

Recent findings suggest that the “so what.” After the evidence, a slide that states what it means in one sentence. This is a hold slide; give it time.

In a recent development, the close. The one sentence you want them to repeat, alone on the final slide. Not “Thank you” or “Questions?”; those can be said. The last thing on screen while you take questions should be your point.

Because the slides are now sparse, two things need handling.

First, the speaker notes must carry what you intend to say, in cue form: the key phrases, the number you must not forget, the transition to the next slide. The generator will have put the stripped bullet content there; edit it into cues rather than prose.

Recent findings suggest that second, the shared deck. Attendees who download a sparse deck afterwards get little from it. Options: export with notes visible; publish a companion blog post or document; or build a second “reading” version with more text on each slide. Many speakers do the first and link to a post.

For a twenty-minute talk, fifteen to twenty-five slides is typical once hold and beat slides are counted. For a forty-five-minute talk, thirty to fifty. Build-up sequences of several quick beats inflate the count without inflating the time, which is fine. What is not fine is a deck of forty dense slides for twenty minutes; that is a talk that will run over and leave the audience behind.

In a recent development, rehearse at least twice with a timer, once alone and once with a listener. Cut the content you rush. Nobody has ever complained that a talk finished two minutes early.

Recent findings suggest that lightning talks (five to ten minutes). Five to eight slides, almost all holds. One idea, one piece of evidence, one takeaway. Cut everything else.

In a recent development, workshops and tutorials. More slides, more text, because attendees will refer back and follow along. The reading-deck rules apply more here; still, keep each step to one slide.

It has been revealed that panel contributions. Often no slides at all, or two or three for a specific point. Resist bringing a full deck.

It has been revealed that keynotes. Fewer slides, longer holds, more full-bleed images and single sentences. The speaker carries almost everything; the slides punctuate.

In a recent development, remote talks. The screen is small and the audience is distracted. Larger type still, more frequent slide changes to hold attention, and a recorded demo is essential because screen-sharing a live one adds another failure point.

According to recent reports, how much of the deck should the AI tool produce? The structural first draft: layouts, consistency, speaker notes populated. Expect to edit every slide and rebuild the key ones.

Is it acceptable to have slides with only a title? Yes, and it is often the strongest format for a spoken talk. The audience listens instead of reading.

Industry observers note that what about code on slides? Short excerpts only, in a large monospace type, with the relevant lines highlighted and the rest dimmed. Anything longer belongs in the companion post.

Should I use animations? Simple builds (revealing elements in sequence) help pacing. Transitions and effects distract. Keep to the former.

According to recent reports, how do I make the deck distinctive if I cannot design? Your material is what makes it distinctive: your screenshots, your data, your diagrams, your sentences. Design restraint plus real content beats decorated templates every time.
