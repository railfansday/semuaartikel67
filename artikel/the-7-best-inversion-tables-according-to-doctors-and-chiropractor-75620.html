In a recent development, using an inversion table isn’t as simple as diving into a new set of dumbbells. These devices require you to move your body upside-down, making it crucial to know what you’re doing and how to ease into the process.

It’s important to start with a “modest” angle vs. going into a full-out inversion when you’re starting out, according to Raymond Agostino, DPT, owner of Agostino Athletics in Fort Myers, Florida. “The goal is to introduce your body to the position and create a gentle traction effect without overwhelming your system,” he says.

Recent findings suggest that agostino suggests starting with a gentle recline, like 20 to 25 degrees for 30 to 45 seconds and see how you feel. “If you feel comfortable and don't experience dizziness, increased pain, or other symptoms, you can gradually increase the time or angle over future sessions,” he states. Agostino recommends only changing one variable at a time—angle and inversion time—to ease yourself in. “You don't need to go farther upside down and stay there longer all at once,” he says.

Recent findings suggest that when you’re inverted, you may feel a pressure sensation in your head or a mild “head rush,” Agostino claims. “When your head is positioned below your heart, blood pressure and pressure within the eyes can increase, which is one reason inversion feels very different from simply lying flat,” he explains. You might also feel a feeling of fullness in your head or facial pressure. “Those sensations should remain mild and should resolve when you return upright,” according to Agostino.

Industry observers note that agostino points out that you don’t get a trophy for going completely upside down. “If a smaller angle provides the stretch or relief you're looking for, there may be no reason to progress to a full inversion,” he claims.

Safety is paramount when shopping for an inversion table. "It’s ideal to find a product with comfortable ankle straps and safety locks to keep you secure and in place," says Kushagra Verma, MD, a board-certified orthopedic spine surgeon at DISC Sports and Spine Center. You also want to make sure the table can be controlled and manipulated by the user if you plan to use the device alone. Inversion tables should be used with caution, especially if you’re a beginner, says Rahul Shah, MD, a board-certified orthopedic spine and neck surgeon at Premier Orthopaedic Associates. Additionally, older adults and those who have chronic dizziness, high blood pressure, glaucoma, inner ear problems, heart disease, and/or severe injuries should avoid inversion tables without talking to their doctor first, he says. Keep this in mind, too, per Agostino: “A little pressure may be expected. Feeling like something is wrong is not something you need to train yourself to tolerate.” If it feels like your back pain is worsening, or if you develop new numbness, tingling, weakness, or radiating pain, Agostino recommends that you stop using the inversion tale.

Sturdiness and durability are crucial for preventing accidents or instability while you’re upside down, Dr. Verma says. He recommends always looking for a product made with high-quality materials, such as stainless steel, to provide better support and longevity. Be sure to read customer reviews and seek expert opinions on the machine's sturdiness when shopping. Of course, all the selections on our list have been vetted in this way.

Recent findings suggest that in addition to safety features, all experts agree that it’s crucial to consider how much weight an inversion table can safely hold. (The machine should clearly state this in the product description.) Because the machine puts you in a rather compromising position, maximum weight capacity is key for keeping you safe and at ease, Dr. Shah says. Going over the weight capacity raises the odds that the table will break or flip while you’re on it, potentially injuring you in the process, Agostino says.

Recent findings suggest that it’s vital to consider the table's length to ensure it accommodates your size and height, claims Ali Mesiwala, MD, a neurosurgeon and complex spine expert at DISC Sports and Spine Center. Most inversion tables are adjustable for a customized fit, but it’s best to ensure the maximum height is within your range for effective use. The width of the table is another key factor, as a wider table can offer more stability during inversion, particularly if you’re on the taller or heavier side, says Brian Wallace, DC, a chiropractor at Pinewood Family Care. As a general rule of thumb, the width of the table should be wide enough for your core to conformably fit and not fall to either side, Dr. Shah adds.

Recent findings suggest that depending on your needs, different inversion tables include add-on features like lumbar spine support, heat, memory foam, or massage elements, Dr. Wallace says. These extra features are not a necessity, but they can be beneficial if you’re looking for added comfort and therapeutic benefits, he says. Just remember: extra add-ons should be considered second to the table's fundamental stability, comfort, and safety.

An inversion table isn’t the cheapest tool you’ll use to reduce your back pain, but there is a range to consider. Most tables will run you anywhere from $150 to $450, with higher-priced models having additional features like heat, memory foam, and massage.

If you’re a beginner, he suggests only inverting for 30 seconds once a day as you get your bearings.

According to recent reports, the best time to use an inversion table is typically when your muscles are relaxed, such as after a workout at the end of the day, says Dr. Wallace. Remember: You should not be inverted for long periods, and it’s best to start slowly and work your way up to time and frequency.
