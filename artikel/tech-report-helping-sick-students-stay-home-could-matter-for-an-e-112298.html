Tech Report: Helping sick students stay home could matter for an entire campus

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Encouraging sick college students to stay home for three days may help reduce the spread of flu-like illnesses.

It has been revealed that staying home when sick is advice that most people have heard many times over. College students are known to attend class while ill anyway, according to earlier research on campus health.

A campus trial now suggests that a polite request to stay in may help limit the spread of illness.

Students with flu-like symptoms in that trial were encouraged to remain in their dorm rooms for three days.

In a recent development, nobody was forced to comply with the request. Snacks and drinks were delivered by trial staff so that staying in would be easier for sick students.

The analysis was led by Brian Richardson, a statistician at Wake Forest University.

Industry observers note that richardson worked with Allison Aiello of the Columbia University Mailman School of Public Health. Michael Hudgens of the UNC Gillings School of Global Public Health was also involved.

The authors describe the evidence as moderate. The data also come from a single campus.

“It seems like almost every day in class, at least one person is sick,” remarked Richardson.

Sources indicate that the data were gathered in eX-FLU, a trial led by Aiello during the 2013 flu season. The trial was carried out at a large public university.

College campuses can be particularly vulnerable to outbreaks of flu-like illness. Crowded housing and large social gatherings are the main reasons given by the authors.

A total of 579 students from six residence halls were enrolled in the trial. Many of them were recruited through friends who had already joined.

The six halls were split into 112 residential clusters for the purposes of the trial. Each cluster was then randomly assigned to an intervention arm or to a control arm.

Students in the intervention arm were encouraged to isolate in their rooms for 72 hours after their symptoms began.

Trial staff also helped those students notify professors and employers about any days they would miss.

According to recent reports, no advice about isolation was given to students in the control arm at any point during the trial. Participants were asked to file weekly reports during the 10-week study.

Each report recorded whether the student had flu-like symptoms that week and which students they had met face to face. Reported cases were confirmed by trial staff.

Recent findings suggest that flu-like illness is defined by symptoms alone. Other respiratory infections can therefore fall under the same label as influenza.

Among 93 students, researchers recorded 113 cases of flu-like illness. Of these, 44 occurred in the group that received isolation advice and 69 in the control group.

However, counting cases alone could not reveal how well the advice worked. Isolation was meant to protect others rather than the sick student.

It has been revealed that students from both groups lived in the same residence halls and shared friends. Isolation in one group could therefore prevent infections in the other.

Recent findings suggest that instead, researchers counted every reported face-to-face meeting between sick students and other participants as a possible opportunity for infection. They could not directly track whether the illness actually spread.

Recent findings suggest that suppose the advice had done nothing. In that case, students in the encouraged arm should have accounted for about 48.6% of these risky meetings. Instead, they accounted for 35.9%.

Sick students who were asked to stay in were behind a smaller share of the meetings where illness could spread.

Recent findings suggest that A gap at least this large would still appear about 8% of the time if the advice made no difference. That figure is above the 5% cutoff that many scientists use for a firm result.

Even so, the authors concluded that encouraging sick students to stay in may have prevented flu-like illness in the trial.

It has been revealed that A separate look at the students’ reported contacts pointed in the same direction. Here, sick students in the two arms were compared. Those in the encouraged arm were less likely to start or keep up face-to-face contact.

“Even though students were simply encouraged – not required – to stay home when sick, and not everyone followed the recommendation perfectly, the intervention still appears to have reduced disease transmission,” Richardson stated.

Students in eX-FLU were linked to one another through their dorms, their friendships and the way they were recruited.

Most standard statistical methods are designed for people who have no such links to each other. A new testing procedure was therefore developed by Richardson and Hudgens for the eX-FLU data.

Industry observers note that the procedure was built to check for an effect on infections, not just on social contacts. Fewer contacts would offer little public health benefit if the number of infections stayed the same.

Its performance was tested first on 10,000 simulated trials in each of three scenarios.

Physical distancing has well-documented downsides, according to the paper.

It has been revealed that mental health problems, economic hardship, disrupted schooling and poorer access to health care are all listed. Vulnerable populations are described as especially exposed to those harms.

Mandatory isolation is described in the paper as more invasive and more difficult to carry out. Newly introduced vaccines can take months or years to develop, the researchers said.

Encouragement to isolate was described as something that could be introduced rapidly in response to newly introduced outbreaks.

“People are much more likely to stay home when they’re sick if we make it possible for them to do so,” mentioned Aiello.

“Our findings suggest that encouraging voluntary isolation – and supporting it with simple resources like meal delivery and basic supplies – can help reduce disease transmission without requiring more restrictive public health measures.”.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention already tells people with respiratory viruses to stay home while sick.

Staying in after symptoms start leaves a gap, since flu can spread for a day or two before symptoms show. With COVID-19, spread before or without symptoms is even more common.

All of the contact data in eX-FLU came from weekly surveys filled out by the students themselves. Infected students listed more contacts than healthy ones, and the authors raised recall bias as one possible explanation.

Sources indicate that transmission itself was never observed in the trial. Only meetings between a sick student and another student could be counted.

The test can show whether the three-day request had an effect. It was not designed to measure how large that effect was.

Sources indicate that all 579 students were part of one connected network on a single campus. Comparisons with other strategies could not readily be made with the eX-FLU data.

A larger sample or a different test statistic might have yielded stronger evidence, according to the authors.

A subset of students in the trial were given smartphones through a side project called iEpi. Those phones recorded when two students were near each other.

Bluetooth data of this kind could be used to correct the self-reported contacts.

It has been revealed that the length of the request is another open question. A six-day version was considered in a pilot of eX-FLU. Pilot participants noted they would be less likely to follow the longer request.

Future studies could compare several isolation periods and record both adherence and infections for each.

Trials on separate campuses could also test whether encouraging every student beats targeting only high-risk individuals.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
