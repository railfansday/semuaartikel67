“He who controls the spice controls the universe.”—Frank Herbert.

Spice adds variety to your life. Especially in your microbiome. Studies are increasingly finding that spices can boost the variety of good microbes in your gut. That, in turn, can improve your mood and cognition via the gut-brain axis.

According to recent reports, A study by Regina Lamendella and colleagues, published in The Journal of Nutrition, found that blends of herbs and spices—including cinnamon, ginger, cumin, turmeric, rosemary, oregano, basil, and thyme—increased diversity of the microbiome, a measure of health. The best results were with the participants who got the largest dose of spices—about 1 ½ teaspoons a day.

According to recent reports, what is so great about spices and herbs? Their main attraction is their high levels of polyphenols—plant chemicals that provide flavor and color. People who eat plenty of polyphenol-rich foods can cut their risk of type 2 diabetes in half and improve their heart health.

Recent findings suggest that polyphenols suppress enzymes that activate inflammation. They are also antioxidants, further helping to quell inflammation. Cloves have about 15 percent polyphenols by weight, whereas veggies may have only 0.1 percent. Along with their intrinsic anti-inflammatory effects, polyphenols act as prebiotic fiber, feeding your beneficial gut microbes.

Industry observers note that the researchers saw an increase in microbes that produce butyrate, an amazing substance that nourishes and heals the cells lining your gut, ensuring that they keep toxins and pathogens out of your bloodstream. Butyrate does more: It can travel to the brain, encouraging the growth of newly introduced nerve cells, improving cognition and memory.

The gut-brain axis responds to inflammation by putting you into sickness mode, where you just want to be left alone to lie in bed while your joy evaporates. This behavior is pretty much identical to depression. Depression and anxiety overlap strongly with gut concerns, and psychiatrists are increasingly turning to prebiotics and probiotics to augment other treatments. Microbes that can improve your mood are called psychobiotics. While polyphenols boost good bacteria, they also fight harmful bacteria like C. difficile, Salmonella, and E. coli.

It has been revealed that it's not exactly news that spices are healthy. Some 2,500 years ago, Hippocrates believed that spices like cinnamon, coriander, and saffron had healing powers. Nutmeg was prescribed for flatulence, and enticingly, ginger was used as an aphrodisiac. The Kama Sutra recommends cardamom, ginger, nutmeg, and cloves for a good time. Spices were, and still are, sexy.

Industry observers note that spices were also expensive. In 410 AD, the Visigoths sacked Rome and held it hostage for a ransom of 5,000 pounds of gold and 3,000 pounds of pepper. At the time, spices were as good as gold. In those days, traders conspired to restrict the supply of spices, ensuring high prices. Their trade routes were kept secret, and excess crops were burned. It was a cutthroat business, but smuggled seeds soon cropped up in tropical farms around the world. Today, supply chains are efficient, and spices are far cheaper, so there’s little to stop you from trying some.

Don't be afraid to play with combinations of spices to create your own custom spice blend. Use it as a rub on fish or meat before cooking or sprinkle some spice on food after it’s cooked. Pro tip: Buy spices whole and grind them just before using to get the maximum joy. Spices have a lot of volatile oils that evaporate over time and deplete the magnificent aromas.

Industry observers note that if you have GERD or IBS, start slowly. Spices activate microbes, which can create gas if you overdo it. But over time, your microbes will adjust.

In a recent development, for everyone else, spices are awesome. They may not help you control the universe, but they taste great, and they can improve your mood. Once you get started, you’ll realize that life is too short to be stuck with bland food. Kick it up a notch.
