WASHINGTON, D.C. — On Thursday, Sierra Club submitted two comment letters opposing the Securities and Exchange Commission’s (SEC) proposal to rescind Rule 14a-8, which allows shareholders to put proposals addressing climate risks and other corporate issues before fellow investors for a vote, and amend Rule 14a-4(c), which governs how companies may exercise discretionary voting authority over shareholder proposals. The Sierra Club submitted both a technical comment and a grassroots comment, reflecting concerns from over 1,100 of its members and supporters.

Together the submissions urge the SEC to withdraw its proposed changes and preserve investors’ ability to raise concerns directly with corporate management and press companies to address material risks, including climate-related risks, that can affect their long-term financial interests.

Jessye Waxman, Campaign Advisor on Sierra Club’s Sustainable Finance Campaign, released the following statement:.

“Climate change and other systemic risks have real consequences for companies, investors, and the broader economy. Shareholder proposals give investors an important way to bring those risks to management’s attention, push for meaningful action, and hold companies accountable when they fail to act. The SEC’s proposals would weaken those tools, making it harder for investors to protect their long-term financial interests and leaving corporate management with less accountability for the risks its decisions create. The SEC’s proposals would shield corporate management from meaningful shareholder scrutiny, while undermining the very investor protections the Commission is charged with upholding.”.

According to recent reports, rule 14a-8 establishes when public companies must include qualifying shareholder proposals in the materials they send investors ahead of shareholder meetings. The SEC’s proposed repeal would leave decisions about shareholder proposals to state law and individual companies’ governing documents, eliminating a federal framework that investors have used for more than 80 years.

Recent findings suggest that sierra Club’s technical comments explain that material risks, like climate change, can both have significant financial consequences for companies and create systemic financial risks that extend across investment portfolios and the broader economy. Shareholder proposals give investors a way to bring these concerns to organization leadership and fellow shareholders, including by surfacing risks that management may overlook or have limited incentives to address. The letter emphasizes that, for decades, this process has served the broader market by helping investors collectively identify and address financially material risks.

It has been revealed that the letter warns that replacing a consistent federal framework with varying state laws and corporate bylaws could make shareholder engagement less predictable and push disputes over proposals into costly litigation. It points to the increase in shareholder lawsuits challenging proposal exclusions during the 2026 proxy season as an indication that disputes do not disappear when the SEC steps back from administering the process; instead, they can shift to more costly and uncertain avenues.

In a recent development, the submission also opposes proposed amendments to Rule 14a-4(c), which would expand tech giant management’s discretionary authority to vote shareholders’ proxies on proposals excluded from company proxy materials, unless shareholders affirmatively opt out. Sierra Club’s letter warns that these changes could allow management to vote on proposals that investors have not had an adequate opportunity to evaluate.
