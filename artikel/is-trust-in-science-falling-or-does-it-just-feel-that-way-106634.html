Is trust in science falling, or does it just feel that way?

According to recent reports, this article is the third in a series discussing the results of Nature’s 2026 science-job satisfaction survey. It’s the latest edition of such surveys, which started in 2010; the last two took place in 2018 and 2021. The 2026 survey, created in partnership with Thinks Insight & Strategy, a research consultancy in London, launched in May and was advertised on nature.com, in Springer Nature digital products and through e-mail campaigns. It had 3,882 self-selecting respondents across 133 countries with 31% describing themselves as female and 19% as belonging to a minority ethnic group in their country of work. The full survey data sets are available at go.nature.com/4b2drrc.

Iris Borowy thinks that teaching her postgraduate students how to tell fact from fiction is central to her job. Yet some days, that work seems futile. “I feel silly teaching groups of eight people while, out there in the influencer sphere, complete idiots talk to millions,” notes Borowy, a global-development historian at Shanghai University in China. “Granted, there are a lot of serious people doing good work online, but working against the propaganda is getting harder.”.

According to recent reports, borowy is one of the researchers who told a Nature survey earlier this year that they felt public trust in science had declined over the previous five years. Of around 3,900 respondents, 50% shared this view, with 33% thinking that trust had increased and 13% saying that it had stayed the same (see ‘Perceptions versus reality of public trust in science’).

According to recent reports, even though trust in science remains high overall, data from other surveys support the idea that trust levels have fallen slightly, after rising at the start of the COVID-19 pandemic. A global survey of more than 140,000 people in 140 nations, published in October by the London-based funding charity Wellcome, found that the proportion of the public who trust science “a lot” or “some” declined from 80% in 2020 to 72% in 2025. Trust levels dropped below pre-pandemic levels in 5 of 14 regions: Australia and New Zealand, sub-Saharan Africa and central, western and eastern Asia. It rose in just two areas — western and southern Europe. In seven regions, including North America and northern Europe, it remained close to the 2018 pre-pandemic levels, when 77% of overall respondents expressed trust in science.

In a recent development, nature’s survey data — which measure scientists’ perceptions of changes in public trust levels, not trust levels themselves — only partly match the trends in the Wellcome survey. In our self-selecting survey, respondents in Africa and Asia were the least likely to say that trust in science had dropped (28% and 34%, respectively). By contrast, 78% of respondents in North America and 64% in Europe thought that public trust had declined. Nature’s survey also revealed that perceptions of falling public trust colour scientists’ job satisfaction and influence who they think is ultimately responsible for rebuilding trust.

The mismatch between actual trust levels in North America and Europe, which remain relatively high, and some scientists’ impression that they’re markedly declining could be explained by a few things, says Niels Mede, a science-communication researcher at Wageningen University in the Netherlands. One is that people who don’t trust science have become louder and more visible, particularly online, and that their messaging is making scientists feel threatened. This has driven a narrative in science media and policy debates of a “crisis of trust”, he says, which has fuelled the perception that overall trust is going down. In addition, he says, many scientists hold a pessimistic view of the public — seeing lay audiences as uninterested in and apathetic to science.

There is also strong evidence that, in countries where scientists told Nature they felt trust in science had dropped, opinions about science are becoming more polarized along political lines. This is especially visible in the United States, where trust among right-leaning Republicans dropped sharply after the COVID-19 pandemic, whereas trust among left-leaning Democrats remained high. In the Netherlands, trust in science remains high overall but the proportion expressing low trust grew from 9.5% in 2019 to 14.6% in 2025. A UK survey published in April found that the proportion who trusted science “a lot” had dropped from 63% in 2020 to just 34%.

Given the gap between perceptions and realities when it comes to public trust of science, Mede says that it’s important to read the trends in Nature’s survey “as a measure of how scientists experience their relationship with the public, not as a measure of public trust per se”.

Digging down into the country-level data from the Nature survey, it is clear that there are substantial geographical differences in scientists’ perceptions of public trust.

In the United States, 83% of respondents felt that trust in science had decreased in the past five years, compared with 79% in Germany and 78% in the United Kingdom. By contrast, just 23% of respondents in India reported feeling that public trust in science had decreased, with a considerable majority — 61% — saying that they felt it had increased. In Japan, 37% of respondents felt trust in science has decreased, whereas 38% noted it has stayed the same.

Nature’s findings from respondents in India closely match national data on public trust in science. In a survey across 68 countries published last year, India was the country with the second-highest trust in scientists after Egypt. Mede, who was one of the study’s authors, thinks that strong pro-science rhetoric from the Indian government could play a part in the country’s high trust levels.

In a recent development, the Indian constitution even lists developing a “scientific temper” — meaning one that is led by logic and evidence instead of belief or superstition — among the duties of its citizens. Mede says that Indian scientists might view this pro-science political climate as a proxy for public trust.

Nature’s survey data for Japan suggests that scientists there feel that public trust is relatively stable, even if the actual level of trust might be modest, indicates Sotaro Shibayama, an economist of science at the University of Tokyo. This makes sense to him, because he thinks that science is less politicized in his country than it is in nations where a higher proportion felt trust had dropped. Studies indicate that trust in science fluctuates in Japan as well, Shibayama says, “but these fluctuations may be smaller than in countries where science has become more closely entangled with political and ideological divisions”.

In a recent development, when asked what is driving the falling levels of trust, the responses indicate that scientists view distrust more as a problem originating externally, rather than something caused from within. Some 69% said that misinformation was the main cause, followed by political pressure or influence on science (62%) and a lack of public understanding of science (54%). Survey-takers could select more than one driver and this question was asked only of those who answered that trust had declined.

It has been revealed that only 24% thought that increased scrutiny and scepticism of experts was a main driver of falling trust and just 23% thought that scientists’ communication with the public was to blame. “We often do not have training in science communication, nor do we really need it,” wrote one early-career Earth and atmospheric scientist in Portugal. “We are paid, and should be paid, to do science.”.

But Mede notes that the evidence that misinformation and a lack of public understanding erode trust significantly is “much thinner and more mixed than one would expect”. Shared values and transparency might matter more. A UK study published in September found that trust was shaped less by whether the public viewed scientists as experts and more by whether scientists were ‘on their side’. Perceptions of openness and transparency also mattered for trust, the study found: 73% of those with high trust in scientists agreed that scientists were generally open and transparent, compared with just 10% of those with low trust.

Misconduct and scientific fraud were not included among the reasons Nature’s survey-takers could select as drivers of falling public trust — an omission of the survey design, in hindsight. But in free-text answers, some respondents mentioned that they felt scientists were partly to blame for public opinion turning against them. One social scientist in the United States wrote that “rampant fraud” in research, such as fabricated data and scientists racing to publish as much as possible with scant thought to the value of their research, has rightly drained public trust. He claims that he feels “furious” that academics have undermined their own credibility. “I literally am embarrassed to tell people in my community what I do for a living,” he writes.

Recent findings suggest that another US respondent, an engineering researcher who said he comes from a cattle ranching family in Texas, wrote that academia “has earned every bit of scepticism that it receives from the public”. Most scientists don’t understand working-class people, he wrote. “When we use the degrees we earned at protected bubbles on university campuses to say that ‘we know best, give us power’, we have to accept that we are nearly always wrong.”.
