The Overlooked Social Origins of Machiavellian Behavior

Updated November 26, 2026 | Reviewed by Kaja Perina.

In 1532, statesman Niccolo Machiavelli's book The Prince was published across Renaissance Italy. While controversial, his magnum opus on statecraft was widely deemed innovative for de-emphasizing ethical norms and ultra-pragmatically explaining what leaders should do to hold and gain power.

Recent findings suggest that many philosophers before and after Machiavelli emphasized people as having a self-interested nature. Like him, they often emerged from ceaselessly unstable, war-prone milieus. Legalists from ancient China viewed Confucians as too morally idealistic; early modern England's Thomas Hobbes held a dimmer view of human nature than did John Locke. Yet, it was the term "Machiavellian" that endured and evolved in English writing since the mid-1500s. Today, it refers to being calculated, insincere, and even unprincipled to gain something.

As a psychological concept, "Machiavellianism" is associated with cynical, manipulative, and self-serving tendencies. Psychologists have even measured it through scales like the MACH-IV and Five-Factor Machiavellianism Inventory. Popular articles and videos abound with titles about how to find "narcissists" among partners, or to beware of "dark triad" traits in co-workers. Whether or not they capture phenomenologically valid constructs, or align with how health professionals use these terms, they do not always include structural approaches and takeaways. Could how Machiavellianism presents in societies give clues into broader social challenges needing addressing?

People with similar childhood adversities can grow up to have very different worldviews. However, for some, a combination of early deprivations, caregiver modeling, and hereditary factors can increase their likelihood of internalizing cynical outlooks. These could crystallize into enduring, Machiavellian patterns of interaction later on. Early childhood attachment disruptions, relational losses, and conditional, inconsistent affection can contribute to understanding relationships as ultimately transactional. Frequent bullying, abuse, or social injustices without accountability might misguide some to view ethical and, sometimes, institutional rules as naive or frivolous.

Later, resulting cravings for "omnipotent control" can lead to great efforts to control how most others view and treat oneself, sometimes in ways contradictory to one's actual self. One applies an astute "theory of mind" for envisioning what others want. Consider a group leader who speaks frequently of uplifting and bringing people together, but privately gossips and plays potential competitors against each other. Or a colleague who cultivates a reputation as a light-hearted jokester, but emotionally abuses their partner at home. Their friends might not believe they have such dissonant traits, let alone have suspected these early on. Indeed, psychoanalysts refer to the superego lacunae of otherwise upstanding people who harbor deeply dark tendencies in specific domains of life.

But how useful is it, really, to focus on the sensationalism, the individual "wrongness" or "pathology," or the developmental roots of Machiavellian characteristics? Nobody denies that manipulation and relational aggression happen. Individuals with such traits--or at least a strong disposition to them--exist in all human societies, but how and to what extent these manifest depends greatly on one's present and future environments.

Recent findings suggest that historian E.P. Thompson examined the moral economy of everyday Englishmen in the 1700s, focusing on nuanced motivations of bread riots beyond hunger itself. As Thompson's work supported, people may be at least as driven to drastic action--in his examples, selective intimidation and aggression toward middlemen--by perceived violations of what is normatively reasonable and fair, than by economic inequality in and of itself.

Yet, unlike for his early modern examples, vigilante retribution is no longer as tolerated in most societies. "Fighting fire [i.e. injustices] with fire" can nowadays land people in prison, depending on their methods. Cynicism may then simmer if people see widespread violation of implicit norms of fairness without meaningful consequences.

In a recent development, examples appear painfully difficult for the observer to challenge; they range from deliberate decisions, like leaders' rewarding betrayals of colleagues who find themselves in hard times, to rigidly structural imbalances like co-workers getting overlooked for opportunities due to their ethnicity, gender, or religion. In turn, self-serving, insincere, performative, and manipulative behaviors can become gradually more tolerated, rewarded, and normalized in groups. Generalized trust, authenticity, good-faith charity, and optimism might all slowly wither.

According to recent reports, is it useful to think of widespread Machiavellianism as mutually reinforcing and cyclically associated with greater cynicism in a society? Maybe yes, but that is also not the full picture. Perhaps in most cases, prone to quid pro quo exchanges and transactional alliance-building, Machiavellianism is unlikely to improve social issues long-term. It would not benefit any group's already low-trust culture, declining public confidence in a given institution, or corruption. If anything, the rewarding of one's playing dirty could encourage more of such tendencies in their circles via social learning.

It has been revealed that yet, for some others, witnessing Machiavellianism's normalization might not transform their personalities overall. It would instead outwardly modify their interpersonal approaches to achieving pre-existing, potentially socially constructive goals. These ends, regardless of their means, go beyond everyday self-preservation and range from improving the marginalized social status of one's group to advancing a specific policy.

What might such Machiavellian-like adaptation or imitation look like for someone who did not grow up with overtly congruent traits? Examples include adopting more guardedness and suspicion, calculation and self-restraint in what one says and to whom, flattery, selectively concealing one's true feelings, and discreetly discerning colleagues' interests and "agendas." Saying things one does not fully mean or feel may seem advantageous, even encouraged, when one assumes their superiors do the same. One might increasingly rationalize their strategic exploitation of others' misfortunes. If these are the prevailing, implicit rules of engagement in one's organizational or workplace politics, then so they will practice them. As with what Friedrich Nietzsche remarked about fighting monsters, they should also take care lest they essentially "become" Machiavellian through and through.

It has been revealed that not every instance of Machiavellianism in one's workplace, organization, or community indicates a structurally sick society. A few individuals, regardless of how much privilege they have, can still demonstrate such features during periods of widespread optimism and trust, even if their tendencies are more the exception than the norm. Conversely, not everyone who has developed a protective cynicism wants to take advantage of others.

Industry observers note that that noted, how prevalent Machiavellianism is in a group at a given time may hint at underlying competition over resources perceived as both finite and unfairly allocated. Resources are not necessarily just material wealth, but also social capital, status, and power. Underhanded ways of interaction may reflect not just neurodevelopmental predispositions but also, for those who are most angry, disillusioned, or hardened, an instrumental means of efficiently accumulating such resources.

None of this absolves the need for accountability and responsibility for inflicting interpersonal harms. Advocacy can be done primarily through good-faith methods. Leaders can promote workplace cultures that are more fair and transparent than what they knew in the past. Yet, so long as racism, sexism, xenophobia, homophobia, and other social ills persist, Machiavellianism may continue to appear to some a legitimate way of navigating work and life--not just for those seeking to maintain great privilege, but also, and perhaps especially, for many people without much of it.
