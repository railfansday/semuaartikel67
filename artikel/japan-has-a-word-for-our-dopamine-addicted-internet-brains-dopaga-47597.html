Japan has a word for our dopamine-addicted internet brains: Dopagaki

It has been revealed that forty-five minutes on TikTok can pass in the blink of an eye. Two minutes watching a video at normal speed? An eternity. Somewhere between scrolling through Instagram Reels, checking our phones during movies, and skipping to the best part of a song, our relationship with entertainment has become a little absurd. We're consuming more of it than ever, yet we seem to have less patience for any individual piece of it.

In Japan, there's a word for this particular kind of digital restlessness: dopagaki.

Sources indicate that roughly translating to "dopamine brat," the Japanese slang term combines dopamine with "gaki," a disparaging word for a child, to describe someone who craves a steady stream of stimulation, whether it's short-form videos, social media, or fast-paced entertainment. But while dopagaki might sound like Japan's answer to the American "iPad kid," its reported origins are a little more surprising. The expression has been linked to a viral post about the structure of a pop song, and some of the young people it appears to mock have started using it to describe themselves.

Sources indicate that so, a word associated with mocking a generation's appetite for instant gratification and excessive screen time has become a self-aware joke about the way we consume media. But dopagaki claims just as much about the entertainment itself, packaged to deliver one satisfying moment after another, as it does about the people consuming it.

After all, when something fresh is always just a swipe away, is it any wonder we've grown impatient waiting for it?

According to recent reports, dopagaki (ドパガキ) is an abbreviation of the Japanese phrase "dōpamin chūdoku no gaki," or "dopamine-addicted brat." It describes someone who constantly craves quick bursts of stimulation, whether that's endlessly scrolling through TikTok, playing video games, or skipping ahead in longer videos to get to the good parts. Even platforms like YouTube have made this kind of consumption easier, with features like video chapters that let viewers jump straight to the moments they want to see.

The word itself isn't particularly kind. In Japanese, "gaki" is a dismissive term for a child, closer to "brat" than "kid." And despite the reference to dopamine, dopagaki isn't a medical diagnosis. Dopamine plays a role in motivation, learning, and reward, and enjoying hyperstimulating entertainment doesn't necessarily mean someone is addicted to it.

But the expression's reported origins reveal something interesting about the way we consume that entertainment. It wasn't a TikTok video or a smartphone habit that inspired the term — it was a pop song.

According to recent reports, in December 2024, a Japanese user on X described the structure of "Lilac," a song by popular Japanese rock band Mrs. GREEN APPLE, using the phrase "dōpamin chūdoku no gaki." The observation centered on the song's rapid succession of musical payoffs, with catchy melodies and energetic transitions arriving in quick succession. There's barely a moment for the listener to settle into one musical idea before another comes along to capture their attention.

The original post didn't use the shortened form dopagaki, and it's unclear exactly when that abbreviation first appeared. But the musical reference offers an intriguing window into the larger cultural phenomenon. The conversation wasn't simply about people demanding constant stimulation. It was also about entertainment seemingly designed to satisfy that demand.

Sources indicate that and that idea extends well beyond a single pop song. Think about the videos that change shots every few seconds, the rise of microdramas, or the way creators are encouraged to hook viewers in the first three seconds of a video before they've had a chance to scroll away. Each new moment promises something more exciting than the last. Entertainment isn't just competing for our attention anymore. It's testing our patience.

By the summer of 2026, dopagaki had made its way into Japanese youth slang.

Sources indicate that in June, Joshikosei Lab, a Japanese marketing research group focused on teenage girls, included dopagaki among seven trending expressions identified through a survey of 60 high school girls. The group described the term as a humorous, self-deprecating way of acknowledging digital overconsumption, including the habit of watching TikTok while drying your hair or taking a bath.

It's a small survey, not a definitive measure of how widely the term is used. Still, it points to an interesting development: Some of the young people who might otherwise be the targets of the insult are now using it to laugh at themselves.

In a recent development, that doesn't mean dopagaki has lost its derogatory edge. Calling someone else a dopamine-addicted brat can still sound condescending, particularly when it's used to dismiss their tastes or suggest that an entire generation has lost the ability to concentrate. But describing yourself as dopagaki is something different. It's a way of recognizing your own habits, perhaps with a little embarrassment, and finding humor in how familiar they've become.

It's also a distinctly internet-native way of processing a shared anxiety. Rather than having a serious conversation about digital dependency every time someone finds themselves scrolling past midnight, there's now a word that captures the experience in five Japanese characters.

And apparently, the joke resonates. In a separate October survey of 94 Japanese high school girls, 16 percent named dopagaki as their most-used slang expression of 2026, while another 17 percent said they'd encountered the term on social media but didn't use it themselves. The findings suggest that the expression has gained some recognition among young people, even if not everyone identifies with it.

Perhaps that's part of what makes dopagaki such a revealing piece of internet slang. It captures both the humor and the discomfort of recognizing our own habits in a culture that rarely gives us a reason to put our phones down.

American internet culture has developed its own vocabulary for our increasingly complicated relationship with screens.

It has been revealed that there's the "iPad kid," a typically unflattering description of children raised with tablets seemingly glued to their hands. As Know Your Meme documents, the term gained traction on TikTok through videos mocking children so absorbed in their screens that they appear oblivious to the world around them. Then there's "brainrot," a term that's become both a criticism of mindless online consumption and an affectionate description of the absurd content people can't stop watching.

It has been revealed that dopagaki exists somewhere between the two. Like "iPad kid," it can carry a generational judgment, implying that young people have become dependent on screens and instant gratification. Like "brainrot," it can also function as a self-aware joke, a way of admitting that your own relationship with the internet might be a little excessive.

But there's an crucial distinction. The iPad kid is often someone else's child, usually invoked as an example of what's wrong with the next generation. Dopagaki, at least in its more playful usage, can be a label people willingly apply to themselves. And the habits it describes hardly belong to one generation.

Adults scroll through TikTok during commercial breaks, check notifications in the middle of conversations, and watch television with a second screen in their hands. The same people who complain about children needing constant entertainment may struggle to get through a quiet moment without reaching for their own phones.

In a recent development, according to DataReportal's Digital 2027 Global Overview Report, which draws on research conducted in 2026, online adults spend an average of 19 hours and 18 minutes per week using social media and online video platforms. That's more than two and a half hours a day on average spent watching, scrolling, and consuming content.

Recent findings suggest that of course, there's nothing inherently wrong with enjoying short videos, high-energy music, or entertainment that gets straight to the point. The more interesting question is how much of our appetite for constant stimulation comes from personal habit, and how much is shaped by the digital environments we've built around ourselves.

Sources indicate that social platforms are designed to keep us watching, listening, and scrolling. Creators work within those systems, learning to capture attention swiftly and hold onto it for as long as possible. Audiences, in turn, grow accustomed to an internet where the next entertaining thing is always a swipe away. Our habits shape the content we consume, and that content shapes our expectations of what entertainment should deliver.

According to recent reports, which brings us back to dopagaki. What was used in one early post to describe the structure of a pop song has become a way to capture a much broader experience of living online. There's something fitting about young people turning that criticism into a joke, particularly when the adults judging their screen habits are often just as attached to their own devices.
