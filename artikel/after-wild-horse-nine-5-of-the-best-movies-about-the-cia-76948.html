After ‘Wild Horse Nine,’ 5 of the Best Movies About the CIA

In Martin McDonagh’s latest film, Wild Horse Nine, there’s a moment where a younger CIA operative is trying to make his older comrade, who is dying of cancer and worried about his soul, feel better.

“You’re going to get to heaven, Chris,” Lee (Sam Rockwell) assures John Malkovich’s character. “There’s whole countries that wouldn’t be free if not for you. Guatemala. Indonesia. That place in Africa I can never remember the name of. Sure you killed some people. So fucking what?” Do people who work at the CIA really talk this way? God, I hope not.

But then again, Wild Horse Nine, set during the 1973 Chilean coup, isn’t really about the CIA. The dark comedy—a good bet for landing several Academy Award nominations—is about mortality and remorse and how even the most brazen people can have moments of clarity and reevaluate their past actions. It uses the CIA as a skin, the way McDonagh’s previous (and very similar) fatalistic comedy In Bruges did with contract killers.

In a recent development, although its average employee likely has a job as mundane as any other government worker, an organization as key to world affairs and as necessarily shrouded in mystery as the CIA is understandably a great font for storytellers. But I can’t say there have been too many classic films that treat the CIA seriously.

There have been some marvelous paranoid thrillers, specifically Watergate-era classics like Three Days of the Condor, which tend to get a little far-fetched by the third act. There are some exceptions, like 2005’s Syriana and 2014’s A Most Wanted Man, that show the minutiae of spy craft in a compelling way that doesn’t devolve into silliness. These are rarities. I also don’t really consider Sicario or Zero Dark Thirty CIA films, because their sweep covers multiple government agencies.

In a recent development, the lengthy 2006 film The Good Shepherd, directed by Robert De Niro and starring Matt Damon, was an attempt to show how the agency grew out of Yale secret societies and reached a crisis point during the Bay of Pigs. Despite some good intentions, though, the movie is a significant dud and has few champions. (I recently revisited the film, but found myself needing several attempts, after I fell asleep on the couch on more than one occasion.).

Sources indicate that even for pure action-adventure absurdity, Britain has James Bond, but the United States has … James Bond’s friend Felix Leiter? Nice guy and all, but not 007. Mission: Impossible’s Ethan Hunt works for the Impossible Missions Force, remember, not the CIA. In the 1960s, Derek Flint, a Bond knockoff, worked for Z.O.W.I.E. (Zonal Organization World Intelligence Espionage), and Matt Helm worked for ICE (Intelligence and Counter-Espionage). Other than Jack Ryan, whose recent Amazon series and films have been absolutely horrible, Hollywood hasn’t given us a CIA man who’s become a household name.

Industry observers note that there have, of course, been great movies about the CIA, but all of them are laced with comedy. Maybe this is some kind of national defense mechanism? Hey, don’t mind us, we may be tinkering in the affairs of other sovereign nations, but we know it’s a little naughty.

If the dark comedy of Wild Horse Nine has you itching for more examples of this curious phenomenon, here are five titles you can stream—all of which are intellectually zesty and a great deal of fun. (The Good Shepherd has value, too, should you find your stash of zolpidem depleted.) I’ve listed them from least-to-most zany.

Bryan Cranston and Ben Affleck in Argo.IMDB.

This extremely watchable spy movie—with a healthy dose of comedy—was a major financial success and also won the Academy Award for best picture. (It beat both Steven Spielberg’s Lincoln and the aforementioned Zero Dark Thirty, both of which I’d place higher, but much like an operation to destabilize a country’s democracy, Oscar success isn’t always predictable.).

It has been revealed that the story is rooted in a true event: During the Iran hostage crisis, a CIA agent (Affleck) created an elaborate cover story to smuggle home six Americans who were hiding with the Canadian ambassador. His cockamamie blueprint was to fake being a Canadian film production that was scouting for locations. To make it work, there had to be a real script (a Star Wars knockoff) plus actual Hollywood producers and craftspeople—so Alan Arkin and John Goodman (playing John Chambers, the real-life guy who did the prosthetics for Planet of the Apes) join in.

Industry observers note that naturally, there are setbacks and raised stakes as the baddies piece together which Americans are missing, then there’s a race against the clock for the big finish. Sadly (well, not for the real people involved), the action-adventure elements of the last act were made up for the movie. But Argo is still an engrossing heist-style picture. It has respect for the CIA, but also presents an agency with a prankster’s edge.

Funnily enough, a 1966 crime-comedy from Italian director Vittorio De Sica and New York playwright Neil Simon called After the Fox used the idea of a fake movie production as the cover for a grand scheme—but in their case, it was smuggling gold.

More recently, a group of Hollywood producers used the Argo technique as cover for an actual scheme in Poland, trying to recover a cache of stamps believed stolen from concentration camp prisoners. They even made a documentary film, The Stamp Thief, about the charade.

Julia Roberts and Tom Hanks in Charlie Wilson’s War. IMDB.

In the months after 9/11, like many freaked-out Americans, I would stay up until dawn watching cable news stations, which is where I first learned the term “blowback,” and about how some felt that the CIA’s backing of the Mujahideen against the Soviets in Afghanistan caused a domino effect that eventually knocked down the Twin Towers. If I tried to explain what I’d seen the night before on C-SPAN 2, I sounded a bit like a paranoid lunatic, so I was very happy when screenwriter Aaron Sorkin and director Mike Nichols collaborated on Charlie Wilson’s War, a scathingly dark CIA satire that summed a lot of this up.

It has been revealed that the always affable Tom Hanks stars as the sleazy titular congressman who, from a Vegas hot tub filled with Playboy models, sees a 60 Minutes report about Afghan refugees. It’s 1980, and he’s worried about Soviet encroachment, but also finds himself (somewhat to his surprise) genuinely caring about civilians caught up in large geopolitical events.

He finds likeminded spirits in Joanne Herring (Julia Roberts), a wealthy Houston socialite and political activist, and Gust Avrakotos (Philip Seymour Hoffman), a working-class CIA operative with a brilliant mind, well-honed bullshit detector, and extremely short temper who was just passed over for promotion. Like Argo, Charlie Wilson’s War is practically a heist picture, but about pushing levers in Washington to quietly appropriate funds and bring matériel to the Afghan fighters.

According to recent reports, the dark punchline, of course, is how our heroes are powerless when it comes time to “win the peace,” as it were, leaving a power vacuum that, it can easily be argued, was filled by al Qaeda. The film’s release at the end of George W. Bush’s presidency functioned as a needed release valve.

Walter Matthau in Hopscotch.IMDB.

Quite the opposite of seductive Sean-Connery-as-007 is hangdog Walter Matthau, but the dark comedy Hopscotch suggests they have some traits in common. Matthau plays Miles Kendig, a veteran CIA agent whose boss (Ned Beatty) takes him out of the field and sticks him with a desk job. As revenge, Kendig decides he’ll write his memoirs (much like Malkovich in Wild Horse Nine), and his spilled secrets make security agencies across the globe very nervous. As he sends salacious excerpts to international higher-ups from various international hiding spots, one of the few things the CIA and KGB can agree upon is: Get Matthau away from his typewriter!

Aiding him is an old flame (Glenda Jackson, who also starred with Matthau’s in the popular comedy House Calls) who lives in Austria, which is a nice touch because our lead character is an opera nut who likes to sing along (poorly) to Mozart in the car.

Recent findings suggest that what Hopscotch has in common with Charlie Wilson’s War is presenting CIA bosses as absolute buffoons. The best part is when Matthau uses Beatty’s own vacation home as a hideout—which America’s intelligence forces then shoot to pieces. While Walter Matthau circa 1980 isn’t the most athletic secret agent, he’s quick with disguises, voices, thinking on his feet, and laying out elaborate traps. This is a great old school comedy with just enough post-Watergate negativity to keep it sharp.

Alan Arkin and Peter Falk in The In-Laws.IMDB.

It has been revealed that hopscotch is a little wacky. The In-Laws is full-on madcap. It’s also one of the funniest movies ever made, though its serpentine plot is hard to explain in just a few sentences. But I’ll give it a go….

It has been revealed that alan Arkin plays a nervous dentist whose daughter is getting married to a nice kid, but Arkin hasn’t yet met the boy’s family. Turns out the father, played by Peter Falk, is a noisy madman who is either bank robber or a rogue CIA agent with blockhead bosses in the middle of an elaborate scheme to overthrow a Latin American dictator. Things soon spiral out of control, and this odd couple find themselves in several high-speed chases, dodging bullets while still hoping to make it to the wedding in time. The two great actors zing each other with quality barbs, and when that’s been exhausted there’s some physical comedy, too.

According to recent reports, the screenplay, written by Andrew Bergman, who also worked on Blazing Saddles, Fletch, and Soapdish, offers a much milder version of the impudence toward the CIA that you see in Wild Horse Nine. “You win some, you lose some,” Peter Falk shrugs when asked about the Bay of Pigs. Though this movie is mostly a comedic joy, the notion that U.S. intelligence is in the hands of nutcases at best—or sheer incompetents at worst—finds quick purchase here and seems to have reverberated in the decades since.

Sources indicate that brad Pitt and Frances McDormand in Burn After Reading. IMDB.

There’s a lot about John Malkovich’s Wild Horse Nine character, Chris Carlisle, that may feel familiar. In Burn After Reading, the Coen brothers’ shaggiest movie (besides The Big Lebowski), Malkovich plays Osborne Cox, a CIA agent who is demoted and decides to write a vicious memoir. But unlike the overlooked and pen-poised agents mentioned on this list already, Cox is a complete klutz.

But so is everyone in this tale, which includes Brad Pitt as dunderhead gym trainer Chad Feldheimer who, after a wild string of goof-ups and misunderstandings, ends up with a computer disc holding the first draft of Cox’s book. Feldheimer thinks he’s stumbled upon some serious material (it’s really just drunken grievances) and decides to blackmail Cox. This does not go well, and a ripple effect includes a murderer’s row of daffy performances from George Clooney, Frances McDormand, Richard Jenkins (more dumb than daffy), J.K. Simmons (exasperated by the stupidity around him, but powerless), and others.

This was the Coen brothers’ follow-up to No Country For Old Men, their Oscar-winning crime drama based on Cormac McCarthy’s dark tale of greed and fate, and it’s probably the most cartoonish thing they’ve ever done. The CIA is nothing but a punching bag for 96 minutes, but the remaining undercurrent of violence erupts from time to time in some shocking (but hilarious) moments. Burn After Reading pushes the silliness to the absolute edge, but still somehow feels like parts of it could be true.
