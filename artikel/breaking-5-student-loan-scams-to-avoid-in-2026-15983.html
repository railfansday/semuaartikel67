It has been revealed that student loan scams can be costly, but they usually leave clues. Scammers will often try to hook victims with tempting claims of “act now or miss out” and “we promise to get you student aid.".

Sources indicate that the communication might seem realistic, but unexpected fees, unknown contacts and massive promises are usually red flags. The age-old adage is true: if an offer sounds too good to be true, it probably is.

Here’s a breakdown of common student loan scams and what to do about them.

The scam: A fraudulent private firm offers to manage your student loans and help you get them relieved or even fully forgiven. These services can cost more than $1,000, and there’s no guarantee that you’ll actually get the help you’re looking for.

It has been revealed that how to avoid it: You can manage your federal student loans for free. According to the U.S. Department of Education, you never have to pay for help with your federal student loans[1]U.S. Department of Education. Avoiding Student Aid Scams. View all sources . You can work with your loan servicer to adjust your payment plan, consolidate loans and apply for loan forgiveness at no cost to you.

If you’re being promised immediate student loan forgiveness, are asked for personal identification information like your FAFSA login credentials or asked to authorize a power of attorney, proceed with caution — it’s likely a scam.

Recent findings suggest that the scam: A company charges you an upfront fee to combine multiple student loans into one new loan to reduce your monthly payment. The company may be illegitimate and is likely to pocket the fee without actually consolidating your loans.

How to avoid it: You can consolidate federal student loans for free. There is no fee to consolidate your loans through the Department of Education at studentaid.gov. If you’re looking to reduce your monthly payment through consolidation, start by contacting your loan servicer. And if you get unsolicited communication from a tech giant promising federal student loan consolidation, it's probably a scam.

According to recent reports, the scam: Scammers use transitions in federal loan programs to take advantage of borrowers. For example, the Education Department was set to resume wage garnishments after a long pause, but in January 2026 unveiled a temporary delay implement repayment reforms.

According to recent reports, when federal decisions go back and forth like this, it creates a gray area for scammers to make wage garnishment threats. For borrowers who may not know if wage garnishment restarted, it can be hard to know what to do. This goes for other federal changes, too; any uncertainty can be an opportunity for bad actors to take advantage of the confusion.

How to avoid it: If your wages are truly being garnished, the communication will come from the federal government and not a private company. Likewise, if a company offers to stop your wage garnishment for a fee, you’re probably getting scammed.

In a recent development, the scam: Criminals impersonate a real person and apply to colleges in their name. Then, they can collect financial aid, take out loans and use their college admission to net discounts on goods and services. This is known as a “ghost student” scam.

How to avoid it: Follow the steps on identitytheft.gov to report fraud and get a recovery blueprint. There are a few safeguards you can put in place to prevent this form of identity theft, such as setting up a credit freeze and monitoring your FSA ID activity.

How it works: You might receive a call from someone claiming to be from your school’s financial aid office saying you have unpaid tuition. The caller may threaten that you cannot start your classes until your tuition bill is paid in full. Scammers can be creative and convincing, even going so far as to replicate a school’s phone number to display on your caller ID.

According to recent reports, how to avoid it: The Federal Trade Commission warns that schools will not call and pressure you to pay tuition[2]Federal Trade Commission. Rather than trust the caller, use your school's secure student portal to check your account balance. Know for certain what you owe and to whom before sending money. You can also call your financial aid office to confirm the legitimacy of a phone number that has called you requesting tuition funds.

Realizing you’ve been scammed is stressful, and it’s natural to feel overwhelmed or embarrassed. But acting quickly can make a real difference. The sooner you report what happened, the better your chances of limiting the financial damage and getting your accounts back on track. Here’s what to do:.

Sources indicate that if you’ve already paid a scammer, report the problem to the bank that issued the payment. If you paid with a credit card, for instance, contact the company and tell them what happened. They may be able to freeze your account or reverse the transaction.

Reach out to your federal student loan servicer as well and report what happened. They can check to make sure no changes have been made to your account and, in the case that changes did occur, they can help you get your loan back on track.

In a recent development, file complaints with the Education Department, the Federal Trade Commission and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. Filing complaints with these agencies helps hold scammers accountable, can result in enforcement action or business shutdowns, and in some cases may help you recover lost funds.
