What a Cardboard Titanic Taught Me About Children and AI

Updated November 25, 2026 | Reviewed by Ekua Hagan.

I once had a student who found reading extremely difficult. Phonemic awareness didn’t come easily. With reading, he needed a lot of adult support. On the other hand, he was able to build a massive, two-meter-long cardboard model of the Titanic with every detail: the smokestacks, the lifeboats, the captain’s quarters with a ship’s wheel, and even a wooden popsicle stick deck.

He painted the entire thing in the same black and red colors as the real ship. His fine motor skills were exceptional, and he worked through iterations, changing, building, and rebuilding over the course of two months. If the popsicle sticks didn’t attach the right way, he built a cardboard scaffold underneath. When he wanted the lifeboats to hang, he tried different ways before settling on pipe cleaners. This wasn’t part of a history project. He was just doing it during his self-directed learning time, completely by himself. So, what does this story say about executive functioning?

His struggles in class and his well-developed design thinking skills seem to point in opposite directions, but that’s only true if we define executive functioning as a single trait that you either have more or less of. In this child, we see it full throttle in one kind of work and not another, which is why I believe it should be viewed more like a capability that grows through use, rather than a deficit. It’s actually still growing, even if not through reading, in this case. Adele Diamond and Daphne Ling reviewed the research on programs that claim to improve executive function and found that these skills improve through practice and carry over to tasks needing similar skills. The strongest gains come from activities we care about.

According to recent reports, around the same time he was building that ship, I was outside at the four square courts listening to an episode of Conversations With Tyler hosting developmental scientist Alison Gopnik, who was describing the way young children learn. “Rational Bayesians," as she called the children, run small experiments on the world and change what they believe based on what actually happens. In some respects, she claims, they do it better than scientists. Every version of a lifeboat that he tried or a smokestack that didn’t hold the cotton smoke properly—and every rebuild that followed—was one of those experiments. So what happens if we stick technology, AI, or a screen in front of this child at this moment?

Presumably, an AI tool could also have designed a proposal, the structure, and the steps for building the Titanic. Yet, isn't that exactly where Diamond and Ling say executive functioning is getting built through practice and effort?

Industry observers note that this has always been my concern when we view AI as a technological support when it risks crowding out the active, self-directed practice that builds problem-solving and executive function. Back in 2011, Elizabeth Bonawitz conducted an experiment where an adult demonstrated to a preschooler one thing a new toy could do. As a result, those children explored the toy less and found fewer of its other functions than children who weren’t shown and got to work it out alone. When the children were given the answer up front, they rationally assumed the search was over.

Recent findings suggest that gopnik’s lab also looked at the difference between children’s brains and machines in a 2023 study in which 4-year-olds started exploring how a machine functioned by working it out themselves. When the same evidence was described in words to language models, the models couldn’t find the rules. The authors' reading is that these systems are very good at passing along what people already know, and much less able to discover something fresh. The models they tested were much less sophisticated than the ones today, but the distinction drawn between imitation and innovation is something I routinely run across in my classroom. AI tools can imitate brilliantly, but a child's invention comes from variance, from trying something that wasn’t suggested.

Industry observers note that handing students AI-generated plans treats every open-ended problem as if there is some magical, single “correct” template, which consequently cuts off the messy variance, the diverse beauty, we’ve come to expect from children in our classrooms. Our students are now growing up beside this new technology, this new imitation of intelligence, that will demonstrate the function of anything, instantly, on request. Having this level of assistance at their fingertips suggests that producing something, whether that be an essay, homework, or a summary of The Grapes of Wrath, gets cheaper and easier to do.

Is this a bad thing? I’m not sure; the jury is still out. Regardless, the part that I’m 100 percent confident will remain valuable is the part the child will always be responsible for: the invention, the personal struggle, and the thinking being built behind it. The thinking that builds the Titanic.

It has been revealed that I wish I could claim credit; perhaps that I had somehow instructed him to do this as part of a project. The only thing I really did was say “yes.” His reading has improved too, though not because the sounds suddenly clicked in the traditional sense. We adapted a strategy so he builds words by piecing the sounds together in a physical puzzle, much like the way he built the Titanic.

It has been revealed that in both cases, the answer was never handed to him. What he received was a way to discover the connections by himself. That search is what a school day has to protect before any technology interferes with that self-discovery.
