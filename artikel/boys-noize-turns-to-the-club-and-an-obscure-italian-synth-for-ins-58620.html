Boys Noize turns to the club (and an obscure Italian synth) for inspiration on 7LLVSION

Sources indicate that if you’re wondering how Alex Ridha’s (aka Boys Noize) collaboration with Nine Inch Nails influenced his new record 7LLVSION, the answer is simple: It didn’t. That’s not to say Ridha didn’t learn anything from the virtuosic production team of Trent Reznor and Atticus Ross, but most of the songs on 7LLVSION had been knocking around for some time. And as Ridha is quick to point out, these are not rock songs built for live performance; they’re “battle tested” for clubs and DJ sets. “I was kind of back on the usual kind of thing,” he tells The Verge.

In a recent development, “I do the same I kind of always do. I go make coffee, go in my room, put on the machines, and I’ll start messing around with sounds and build one sound to another and there comes a record,” he indicates. Like previous Boys Noize releases, a lot of 7LLVSION starts with sound design. “It’s kind of tech-driven, in a way,” he says, “because I always look for the newest machines.”.

On 7LLVSION, Ridha frequently turned to an obscure Italian semimodular synth, the Grp A4. “It’s just so powerful, and it sounds so big,” he states. The routing options, dual filters (which he uses in parallel), and built-in sequencer kept leading him to new and inspiring places. Because of that, the A4 ended up on a number of tracks, including “Shut It Down,” “1+1+1,” and “Ycore.”.

“The exciting part is always to, you know, not repeat myself, even with the drum sounds, and try something new that I haven’t tried before,” Ridha indicates. For a few tracks, that meant getting away from the rigid, quantized grooves of techno and opting for a more dynamic, live feeling on tracks like “Shut It Down” and “1+1+1” especially. “Live drums are the new 808,” he says.

He looked to German bands of the ’70s and ’80s for inspiration, like Can, Amon Düül, and Deutsch Amerikanische Freundschaft. He also called out Conny Plank, who produced landmark records by Neu!, Kraftwerk, and Eurythmics. But in general he says, “My goal is to not sound like anything you can point to. You know, I don’t want to be a direct copy.” Instead, he was chasing the sound of “electronic punk.” That means aggressive basslines, driving drums, and stripped-down arrangements that avoid overstuffing the mix with too many instruments.

But it all comes back to the club at the end of the day. “Environment has always been the main thing in my music. I need to have a record that I want to play at the party,” he says, and that meant testing the songs off 7LLVSION extensively in his DJ sets and letting the audience tell him what works and what doesn’t. “The club is the most open and objective situation you can have,” he says. “They don’t know what I’m playing, so they hear something for the first time, and you can just look at someone’s face, or someone’s there in the room to get a cool reaction, and that usually makes me feel good about something or not.”.

Boys Noize is currently touring the world on the festival circuit, and 7LLVSION is available now on most major streaming services.
