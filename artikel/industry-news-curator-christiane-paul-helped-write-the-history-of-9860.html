Rare are the times when a New York museum has turned over an entire floor to a presentation of digital art. That makes “artport: A History of Internet Art,” opening at the Whitney Museum on Saturday, an outlier in the institution’s programming—and something of a major occasion.

Industry observers note that the titular artport is an online gallery launched in 2001 by curator Christiane Paul, who has since gone on to commission plenty of works in the intervening 25 years, by artists ranging from JODI to Mimi Ọnụọha. Officially indexed as part of the Whitney collection since 2015, artport continues to commission artists. The most recent was Jazsalyn, who, for one work staged earlier this year, proposed what she called “Black Metatheories” as means of forming something alternative to AI and called on people to respond to a range of prompts, including one that reads: “Consider blackness. How does life persist within the spectrum of impossible possibility?” Related Articles Frieze's Kristell Chadé Discusses Why the Fair Has 'Never Been About Adding Points on a Map' Newsmakers: Studio Ma's Christiana Moss on Building Museums for a Hotter, More Expensive World.

Artport’s offerings live on the Whitney’s website. Generally, however, they have not been show in the Whitney’s galleries, something that will change with Paul’s exhibition opening this week, which features more than works associated with artport. The show is yet another digital art–focused exhibition from one of the great thinkers on the subject. She literally wrote the book on digital art in 2003, and is currently in the process of writing a follow-up to it.

ARTnews spoke with Paul, a curator of digital art at the Whitney, to learn more about artport’s history and how she created such an ambitious show. This interview has been condensed and edited for clarity.

Sources indicate that ARTnews: Artport was founded in 2001, but it didn’t become a part of the Whitney’s collection until 2015. More than 10 years later, what is its place at the Whitney today?

According to recent reports, christiane Paul: I would not say it’s changed much. The original thinking, which we kept intact, was: It’s kind of weird to lock down net art in a collection. And that is why we decided to commission the works under non-exclusive licenses. But what changed in 2015 was really that we wanted to acknowledge the artist’s status in all of this. By making it officially its own little umbrella collection within the permanent collection, all of the artists gained lifetime membership status, and are now treated the same way as collection artists—that wasn’t the case before.

Also, the works are of course more integrated into the collection database. Before, when you searched, everything was there on artport, but it did not necessarily appear in collection searches, which also became problematic when we added more physical works by some of the artists—for example, a sculpture by Auriea Harvey [whose work was commissioned by artport in 2023]—to the collection. You would find the sculpture, but not the rest of her work.

When artport commissions an artist—let’s say, for its “On the Hour” series, which stages projects on the Whitney’s website at designated times—who is the commissioner, the Whitney or artport?

The commissioner is always the Whitney Museum, and I invite artists to submit a proposal. What we don’t do is show existing work. We did a series from 2009 to 2023, “Sunrise Sunset,” that marked sunrise and sunset through a 30-second intervention into whitney.org that played on all pages. I found the format highly intriguing, because it’s so net art to disrupt, particularly an institutional website. While it was very beautiful and poetic, there also was a concern: This was sunrise and sunset, Newly introduced York time only. That prompted us to change it to make it into hourly interventions.

My invitations to “On the Hour” are often based on two criteria. First of all, who are artists who can really work with that kind of seriality intervention, in terms of vignettes? I think it’s also a great way to give younger artists a chance to do something for the museum. In general, artport has always paid a lot of attention to emerging artists, but due to the format, this lends itself to being a little more experimental.

In a recent development, what did you look for when you started commissioning artists? How did you know an artist was good for artport?

Sources indicate that the main goal of artport was always to show the breadth of practice in the in the online field. When artport was launched in 2001, of course, there already was a history of net art. I mean, depending on if you’re counting by accessibility to the public, the internet launched in 1993. Artistic practice in this space started around that time and really took off in the mid-’90s. So, by the time I started, there was already a backlog of the artists who were creating the language of the field: Mark Napier, Tina La Porta, Martin Wattenberg, Marek Walczak, Amy Alexander. I was rapidly moving through those people at the time. Then I was following who was doing significant work in that area.

Industry observers note that certainly, when you started artport, institutions had already begun paying attention to net art. Documenta X in 1997 had a net art section, and Lawrence Rinder organized a net art portion of the 2000 Whitney Biennial. You did something similar for the 2002 edition, and Rhizome partnered with the Newly introduced Museum in 2003. But it seems like we’re in a very different moment now, where net art can even be the subject of major exhibitions like the artport one. What does it mean, now, to bring net art into museum walls?

I would once again say my approach has not changed that much. There’s more technological support for doing these types of demonstrates, so the infrastructural possibilities and budget certainly did change. But since the ’90s, there has been this discussion of: “Oh, this should only be seen online.” I always thought that was such an ironic misunderstanding because online is not on your couch in your house. Online is everywhere! We’re online in the museum. People are on their phones. Everything is networked.

It has been revealed that you’re definitely changing the context [by showing it in a museum], but it’s important to see this art in the context of other art forms, and that was very important to me. The challenge that an exhibition like this poses is: How do you make the work easily accessible within a gallery space while also maintaining its net-ness? On the one hand, it’s about not overwhelming people with dozens and dozens of screens, which we do still have in this exhibition. But we’re also changing formats quite a bit, and at the same time, we’re not sucking out what the online environment is really about.

How did you maintain that net-ness?

Sources indicate that the way I approached it with this exhibition was to use an open floor and a landscape of browser windows as a design conceit. There are floating walls held up by poles. There’s also a lot of cabling, as you can imagine. The walls or panels suggest open browser windows on which the screens are presented. They’re not segregating works into rooms—they’re all the web, where you might switch from one tab to another. Some works have been translated into more of an installation, like Andy Deck’s Screening Circle [2006], for example, which you use online, with different people contributing to it. You use your mouse to choose pixels and create an icon within this screening circle. And in the gallery, you will see it projected onto a platform with actually four tablets for input, so four people can use it simultaneously. It will switch between them and also bring in the online work.

According to recent reports, there are also works that have components of paintings or prints, like Michael Mandiberg, for example. They did an endurance performance, Life Study [2018], where, over the course of four years, they filmed themself doing paintings of their assistants. We’re not only showing the work and its archive, but also some of the paintings.

The exhibition is titled “artport: A History of Internet Art,” which suggests that it is a history of net art, and that there are others still to be told. What is the history you’re telling?

Julia Kaganskiy puts it nicely in her essay for the catalog. There are timestamps that are demarcating the concerns, attitudes, social norms, and media environments that have shaped our collective lives. I think any type of net art that one would have picked beyond artport from the ’90s until today would somehow tell that history. Of course, the history that artport tells is a very specific one in that there are certain choices of artists, and they tell that history in a specific way, through their personal lens. If it were a completely different roster of artists, those timestamps marking these shifts would still be there, but the story would be told slightly differently.

There’s also a fresh performance by the artist duo Operator: the third act of their ongoing “Human Unreadable” project, begun in 2022.

Operator has, for the past four years, created the series “Human Unreadable,” which is a generative choreography on the blockchain. It’s open source, so that people can use to do their own performances. In Act I, they translated sequences that were created by the choreography into large-scale black and white images that were sold as NFTs. In Act II, collectors could reveal the movement sequence that had generated the image. From the start, the work raised the question: How much of the body survives translation into code? In Act III, we have bodies without the mediation of the screen performing the first 100 tokens choreographic sequences. It really is a multimedia theater piece that also includes spoken word, and it is really about our relationships to technological systems, the artist’s personal story, and negotiating ambition and technology in your life. And its two matinees are live streamed: I think we now have 18 partner institutions. The live stream is not simply “Oh, here is a camera that shows you what’s happening at the Whitney.” It is more of an artwork in and of itself, with moving cameras and modifications, almost a little bit more like Nam June Paik’s Good Morning, Mr. Orwell minus the technological difficulties. [Laughs.].

It has been revealed that A lot of younger viewers who encounter this exhibition may not entirely understand everything involved in creating the early net art surveyed here—for example, the splash pages produced for artport between 2001 and 2006, which rely on a technology that is generally out of use in our current internet. How will you contextualize those pieces for viewers less familiar with them?

It has been revealed that there were 57 splash pages—they were done monthly—and I am only showing the ones where artists created standalone projects rather than just a splash page or gate page for their work, because that kind of browsing is better done at home. The intention is to get lost in an artist’s work and website, and that would really distract from the focus of the exhibition. That being said, we are explaining the functionality of splash pages and what they were in the section text. But I hope the art of that time, and the environment of the web at the time, really translates—to me, that is actually the most significant part of the exhibition. There are these really nice connections across the floor between these early works and later ones that show you how really our attitudes towards self-representation, identity, surveillance data processing have shifted over time.

Industry observers note that there’s an early work by Jillian McDonald that was made 20 years before deepfakes. It’s about fan culture—she inserted herself into the film Buffalo ’66 with Vincent Gallo. I have it right across from works by Tina La Porta, who did voyeur_web [2001] at a time when live casting was so popular. People would put up webcams in their homes, in their kitchens, in their bedrooms, as this kind of gesture of returning the gaze, something that people wouldn’t do anymore today. It’s a completely different attitude that gets reflected in Maya Man‘s A Realistic Day in My Life Living in Newly introduced York City [2024–25], which takes this highly stylized TikTok culture of a supposedly realistic day that is completely performed and provides a counterpoint to what voyeur_web did.

The freedom that some of this early net artists had is amazing. It’s just not possible anymore, with so much of the internet became commodified, capitalized, and cleaner.

It has been revealed that what really struck me in reviving some of the older pieces was that the first generation of net artists really used the web as material, in terms of scraping and reprocessing. The access they had, you don’t have that anymore with the corporate lockdown.

According to recent reports, and you had to have a certain degree of technical know-how, too. I was rereading a 2015 interview you did with the artist Marisa Olson for Rhizome in which you stated you were dubious that net art still existed. Do you still feel that way?

I would slightly revise my framing. [Laughs.] What I was really getting at was that in the ’90s or early 2000s, you really had a category of net artists. Those were people who really worked with the web, and that was it—that was their practice. And I think with so-called post-internet, post-digital art, this crossed over into more material form, even though the internet was still at the core of the practice. In the sense of the net artist who does work online, and that is the practice, period? I think that is much harder to find today. You still have net art, and a lot of it. I mean, Maya Man, Chia Amisola are younger artists who are really doing net art in the spirit of the online environment, but Maya also does very material works. Artists have been branching out more: They may be doing work on the web and in sculpture painting across the board. You might argue that has changed in contemporary art in general, which has become less medium-specific. But there is still net art.
