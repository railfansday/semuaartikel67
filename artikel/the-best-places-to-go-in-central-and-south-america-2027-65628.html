This is my fourth year leading our guide to the Best Places to Go in Central and South America, and each time I’m in awe of the final result—our list for 2027 is no exception. We’re talking about a region where the Pacific, the Atlantic, and the Caribbean converge; where the Andes reach for the Amazon; and where strings of puffy volcanoes and postcard-worthy islands stud the map like jewels. Within it all there are thriving cities where cultures ancient and modern collide head-on, and small towns that are timeless in a way that feels increasingly rare.

It may be a fool’s errand to distill all this wonder into a list of just 10 destinations. But the places below, as singular or more broadly representative as they may be, are the region’s answer to how we are traveling right now. For example, a pioneering set of travel specialists are ready to bring you to eco-lodges that are tucked in the untamed jungle of Guyana for wildlife sightings and citizen science experiences that will broaden your understanding of the only (official) English-speaking country in South America. If you’re looking for a sport to pick up, Preá on Brazil’s northeast coast has an open invitation on kitesurfing, the wind-driven sport this fishing village has long been known for—and the reason a set of quietly luxurious boutique hotels have been cropping up within driving distance of the rolling sand dunes of Jericoacoara National Park. If wine is what moves you, then Chile’s Central Valley should be your next stop: A slew of new culinary experiences and interesting bottles from up-and-coming winemakers make this Santiago-adjacent region the trip to take in 2027. There’s also next-gen wellness in Costa Rica, quintessential expedition cruises in Ecuador, offseason trekking in Argentina, and so much more.

Industry observers note that get ready to max out that PTO. These are the Best Places to Go in Central and South America in 2027. —Megan Spurrell.

Go for: newly introduced jungle stays, ancestral wellness experiences, and a next-level approach to ecotourism.

Tucked in the Monteverde Cloud Forest, Hotel Belmar's Artista Villas offers 10 bungalows in a reforestation site.

The chance to connect with nature has long been a draw to this region—at Hotel Belmar, that includes activities like yoga.

It has been revealed that half a century ago Costa Rica helped pioneer a newly introduced model of travel in the Monteverde Cloud Forest that would come to be known as ecotourism. Today the free-spirited residents of Monteverde, three hours northwest of the capital, San José, are once again redefining responsible travel—yet now the goal extends beyond minimizing tourism’s footprint to actively restoring ecosystems, supporting local communities, and forging deeper connections between visitor and place. That ethos is embodied by Artista Villas, a collection of 10 swish bungalows in a reforestation site at Hotel Belmar. Guests at the carbon-neutral property, which opened in June, engage with the surrounding jungle using in-room art kits, meet visiting artists and filmmakers at the property’s Cervecería Belmar, forest bathe in the mountain at Artista-owned Savia nature reserve, and learn about the source of their meals at Finca Madre Tierra. Calling this new form of regenerative travel ecotourism feels too small to capture its weight.

As conventional foreign-owned resorts proliferate along the nearby Gulf of Papagayo, Monteverde and the surrounding highlands continue to chart a different course. In March 2027 the working ranch and private reserve Hacienda Montezuma, located beneath the Tenorio Volcano (and normally reserved for exclusive buyouts), will launch the first in a series of wellness retreats bookable for up to 15 people. These will feature local practitioners leading equine therapy, meditation, and forest walks—all rooted in ancestral traditions. The region is also transitioning to a deforestation-free coffee model that is reshaping how one of Costa Rica's most important crops is both grown and experienced by visitors on educational tours. —Mark Johanson.

Go for: chef-led experiences, upcoming Cachapoal Valley bungalows, and up-and-coming wine scenes.

In Chile's best winemaking regions, there's plenty of old and latest. Case in point: Chile’s most extravagant wine hotel, VIK, expanded in 2026 with eight glass-mirrored bungalows and an art-filled wellness center, with five cabins set to open next in an oak forest on the property.

Chile’s central wine valleys have long been defined by their vines—emerald rows sprawling along riverbanks, blanketing a terrain reminiscent of the Mediterranean, yet hemmed between towering Andes and a thrashing Pacific. Recently, dynamic chefs from Santiago have begun adding their touch to the region, crafting upcoming culinary tours and elevating the work of emerging bodegas and wineries. Kurt Schmidt is among them. He first rose to fame crafting nine-course tasting menus at Santiago’s 99 Restaurante, built around the traditional foodways of Chilean viticultural regions. In 2027 he’ll be teaming up with the rural tourism cooperative Ruta de los Abastos (“The Supply Route”) on menu-to-territory day tours that will begin at 99 and expand out to wine valleys like Huasco, Maule, and Aconcagua, where Schmidt has spent years building relationships with vintners, cheesemakers, foragers, and fisherfolk (later multiday itineraries are in the works). Conveniently, Santiago lies just south of the Maipo Valley, which is famed for bold Cabernet Sauvignons and also home to the striking new Centro del Vino from Concha y Toro, Latin America’s largest wine producer. The 129,000-square-foot facility features contemporary art installations, barrel-side tasting rooms, and an interactive wine museum, plus access to manicured gardens and (for an additional fee) the 19th-century mansion of founder Don Melchor.

Sources indicate that if you have days to spare, consider heading three hours south to the Cachapoal Valley. Here, Chile’s most extravagant wine hotel, VIK, expanded in 2026 with eight glass-mirrored bungalows and an art-filled wellness center. Five cabins are next, set to open in an oak forest on the property, where winemaker Cristián Vallejo ages natural wine in clay amphorae. Continuing south, you can explore the next generation of experimental and low-intervention vino emerging from the neighboring Maule and Itata valleys, where Millennial and Gen Z winemakers are injecting fresh energy into one of South America's oldest regions at bodegas like González Bastías and Carter Mollenhauer. Add it all up and it makes for an epic wine country road trip.

As if that weren’t enough to keep you busy, know that excellent vino isn’t the only reason to dedicate your big vacation to Chile this year. Once you wrap up in Santiago, consider hopping over to Rapa Nui (Easter Island) for some extreme set-jetting. This remote Chilean territory in the Southeastern Pacific is the backdrop of Academy Award winner Martin McDonagh’s newly introduced film, Wild Horse Nine, and it’s sure to become a rising star on more travelers’ itineraries this year. —Mark Johanson.

Go for: newly introduced conservation-minded cruises, stargazing experiences, and an endless parade of wildlife.

In 2027 National Geographic–Lindblad Expeditions will celebrate 60 years since Lars-Eric Lindblad introduced travelers to the Galápagos on the first nonscientific civilian expedition voyage, establishing the islands as a premier destination for conservation-minded exploration.

In a recent development, the Galápagos Islands, an archipelago of 13 major isles and over 100 rocks and inlets off Ecuador’s coast, have long attracted travelers seeking solitude and wildlife: endemic giant tortoises and marine iguanas, native scalloped hammerhead sharks, and blue-footed boobies, for starters. Nearly 97% of the Galápagos’ land area is a single national park. Visitor access remains tightly managed, making the destination one of the rare places where travelers can experience wildlife on nature’s terms (and, as scientists are learning, occasionally encounter fresh species, like the deep-sea Microeledone galapagensis, or blue octopus). In 2027 National Geographic–Lindblad Expeditions will celebrate 60 years since Lars-Eric Lindblad introduced travelers to the Galápagos on the first nonscientific civilian expedition voyage, establishing the islands as a premier destination for conservation-minded exploration. The brand doubled its Galápagos fleet in 2025 by adding the 16-guest catamaran Delfina and the 48-guest Gemini. In January 2027 the brand will launch Ultimate Galápagos, a 13-day itinerary aboard the 96-passenger Endeavour II that visits 11 islands including remote Genovesa, known for its red-footed booby colonies. Elsewhere, Natural Habitat Adventures’ Galápagos Tortoise Camp just transitioned to 100% solar power and introduced the Galápagos’ first fully electric panga (a small skiff for excursions)—part of trips booked aboard the brand’s Petrel luxury catamaran—reducing emissions for wildlife excursions and setting a new benchmark for sustainable travel in the destination. —Hannah Selinger.

Go for: volcano trekking, peak birdwatching, price points that range from shoestring to big spender.

Guatemala, beloved for its artisan heritage, birdwatching, and volcano trekking up peaks like the Volcán de Fuego (pictured), will continue to draw a wide range of travelers in 2027.

Long a backpacking destination on the Central America 101 route known as the Gringo Trail, Guatemala is an increasingly popular destination for a generation of young travelers seeking slower, more enriching tourism that won’t break the bank. INGUAT, the Guatemala Institute of Tourism, notes that arrival numbers support the TikTok thesis—and are only projected to increase. Some of the appeal is timeless: Guatemalan day-to-day life is deeply steeped in ancient heritage and culture. Sprawling artisan markets like Nim Po’t in Antigua offer easy access to hand-carved and painted wood animal masks, for example, but deeper dives are offered by Antigua-based textile studio Luna Zorro, which organizes craft retreats throughout the country. The brand’s recently opened La Valiente (February 2026) is a two-bedroom guesthouse in the heart of the city, and almost everything at the property—from the bedding to ceramics—is artisan-made by local creators. The country’s natural beauty is also an evergreen draw, but as younger generations embrace the mores—and aesthetics—of hiking (and even birdwatching), trails like the rigorous two-day Acatenango trek are list-toppers in the central highland region that is ripe with some of the 780 species recorded in Guatemala. Many choose to continue to the Tikal National Park in the northeast, where 3,000-year-old ruins of the Maya city sit amid a lush green jungle setting. At the park’s recently reopened Conservation and Research Center, visitors can observe the behind-the-scenes preservation process of ruins and artifacts, only now visible to the public. It all makes for welcome news that Guatemala is getting even easier to access: Frontier is offering daily, seasonal, nonstop service from Los Angeles to Guatemala City from December 17 until January 4, if you need a last-minute holiday getaway, and United Airlines has a permanent direct route from Houston. —Anna Zou.

Go for: untamed rainforest, immersive eco-lodges, and easier-than-ever access.

Guyana still remains 85% forested, with sites like the impressive Kaieteur Falls—among the world's strongest single-drop waterfalls—awaiting travelers.

Guyana, South America’s only country with English as its official language, celebrated six decades of nationhood this May. Yet from a small plane bound for Kaieteur Falls, one of the most powerful single-drop waterfalls in the world, the lush rainforest below appears several geological eras older. Perhaps it’s no wonder; an impressive 85% of this country remains forested.

Most travelers start in the coastal city of Georgetown, where the country’s Caribbean identity is tangible in the annual Mashramani carnival (every February), vibrant Stabroek Market, and Gothic St. George’s Anglican Cathedral, which is made of wood. But journeys quickly turn toward the rainforest and savanna, with Kaieteur as their most dramatic introduction. At the falls, the Potaro River drops 741 feet from a sandstone ledge—more than four times Niagara’s height—yet just over 8,000 people see it each year, usually on a day flight from Georgetown. A fuller interior journey requires a separate, multiday circuit through Iwokrama International Centre for Rainforest Conservation and Development (IIC), a nearly million-acre reserve managed alongside 20 Indigenous communities, and into the North Rupununi, a landscape of savanna, wetlands, and river villages where Indigenous-led conservation becomes part of the itinerary. For in-the-know travelers, right now—when a host of upcoming expeditions and accommodations have risen to meet vanguard voyage interest—is the time to go.

Sources indicate that on that circuit, three conservation-linked stays give travelers a direct role in the work. Atta Rainforest Lodge is the base for Iwokrama’s canopy walkway, suspended 100 feet above the forest floor and managed by the reserve in partnership with Makushi communities and local tourism operators. Further into the North Rupununi, road and river lead to Rewa Eco-Lodge, operated by Rewa Village, where anglers help measure and tag arapaima before release. Another tributary leads to Yupukari’s Caiman House, a village-run guesthouse and research station where visitors help researchers study black caiman after dark. The community also raises yellow-spotted Amazon river turtles for release. Reaching these stays still requires small planes and long road-and-river transfers, though one important link is easing: In August 2026, Jags Aviation added a 19-seat turboprop service four days a week between Georgetown’s Eugene F. Correia International Airport and Lethem.

Industry observers note that back in Georgetown, the arrival of international hotel brands marks a new era at the start and finish of any trip. The 152-room AC Hotel opened in December 2025, with the 172-room Four Points by Sheraton following in April 2026. A Hilton resort is due on the seafront in 2028. And there are more reasons to linger in 2027: From October 8 to October 17, the capital will also host Carifesta XVI, which will return the Caribbean Festival of Arts to its 1972 birthplace, promising a cultural counterpart to the wilderness of the interior. —Paul Jebara.

Go for: latest off-season expeditions, and a slate of fresh lodges.

For the first time in 2026, tour company Hielo & Aventura offered its popular ice hiking excursion atop the Perito Moreno Glacier—in winter.

Los Glaciares National Park, with its toothy granite spires, windswept steppe, and glaciers that crack and roar as they calve into milky-blue lakes, has long been the crown jewel of Argentine Patagonia. Yet its main gateway town, El Calafate, hasn’t seen a latest luxury hotel in over two decades. That changes in December 2026 with Explora El Calafate, an all-inclusive 20-key lodge snaking across a Patagonian estancia near Lake Argentino. The hotel’s design leans into soothing earth tones—with giant windows framing distant glaciers—while menus highlight Patagonian lamb, trout, and wine. Guides take guests on 30 different adventures out to remote wildlands like the ice fields of Brazo Sur or the bird sanctuary of Laguna Nimez. Explora will simultaneously open a second property just across the Chilean border in its private Torres del Paine Conservation Reserve, further linking the two destinations by building out a binational circuit.

Meanwhile, southern Patagonia is shedding its reputation as a summer-only destination. Awasi Patagonia, near Torres del Paine, will now remain open through the 2027 winter season—prime time for puma safaris—while ecotourism operator Chile Nativo extols the virtue of the offseason with its Winter W Trek, tracing the iconic route past Paine Grande and the Grey Glacier in the region’s quieter season. The momentum is even greater in Los Glaciares. In 2026 park concessionaire Hielo & Aventura, responding to greater off-season demand that can help disperse the annual tourist traffic, operated its popular ice hiking excursion atop the Perito Moreno Glacier during the winter for the first time. Unlike most Patagonian mountain towns, El Calafate doesn’t shut down when crowds depart, which means there are still hot chocolates to be had after a day on the ice. —Mark Johanson.

Go for: a buyout villa in jaguar country, and groundbreaking conservation efforts.

Caiman’s upcoming private villa, Cordilheira, has seven bungalows with buyout options for larger groups, offering a new way to stay in this wildlife-rich corner of Brazil.

For flora and fauna lovers headed to South America, the Amazon has long been the marquee trip. But further south, where the waters of the Amazon trickle down into the world’s largest tropical wetland, something different awaits. The Pantanal stretches across 42 million acres in Brazil, Bolivia, and Paraguay. In comparison to the Amazon, it’s a bit more arid, with strong cowboy culture, and the foliage is sparser, making this a great place to spot jaguars in the wild. This is in no small part thanks to vital conservation work that has taken place over decades. At the upscale Caiman Pantanal, a trailblazing safari stay in the Brazilian Pantanal, jaguar rescue group Onçafari has an on-site location, allowing guests at the all-inclusive property to explore 131,000 acres of land with the big cat experts who have meticulously tagged and tracked dozens of them. Caiman’s fresh private villa, Cordilheira, was unveiled in June 2026. It brings together seven bungalows (private infinity pools included) that fit up to 16 people, making it possible for families and groups to have a buyout experience as they spend days tracking jaguars—and tapirs, and giant anteaters, and capybaras—before evenings filled with Brazilian barbecue and folk music under the stars. Operators like Natural Habitat Adventures can continue the journey northbound to destinations like Porto Jofre, where cat sightings happen by boat on the river, and jaguars can be seen chasing caimans at the water’s edge. The conservation work required to protect this rare natural landscape and its fauna is ongoing, but new projects like the Jaguar Rivers Initiative, declared in fall 2025, prove the only way is forward. This new coalition, which connects the aforementioned Onçafari with Kristine Tompkins’s Fundación Rewilding Argentina, Nativa in Bolivia, and Fundación Moisés Bertoni in Paraguay, hopes to better link and restore various jaguar habitats. The goal is, of course, ecological first and foremost—but if the preservation work in Pantanal has taught us anything so far, it’s that great conservation can lead to great wildlife experiences. —Megan Spurrell.

Go for: uncrowded surf spots, a noteworthy beach hideaway, and plenty of seafood.

Opened in February 2026, resort Inkaterra Cabo Blanco overlooks the Pacific Ocean on a coastline frequented by Marilyn Monroe and Ernest Hemingway.

The seaside towns and villages of Peru's northern shore are known for their seafood dishes, such as picante de mariscos and raw-fish tiraditos.

On Peru’s northern coast, life moves slowly in the seaside towns and fishing villages that have long drawn a quiet trickle of visitors including surfers chasing mythical waves, Limeños lured by closer-to-the-equator sun and tranquility, and culinary-minded travelers who know that nowhere else does seafood quite like this part of the country. In the past few years, an increasingly stylish crop of Airbnbs have popped up—like Kahu Beach House in Los Órganos, where modern design uses endemic natural materials and where fantastic surf breaks are visible from your breakfast table. The more recent resort opening of Inkaterra Cabo Blanco in February 2026 has shone an even brighter spotlight: On the same stretch of sand where Marilyn Monroe and Ernest Hemingway vacationed in the 1950s, this contemporary beachside stay with just 13 rooms is an elegant approach to the destination’s barefoot ease, refined coastal dining, and experiences that range from a day on the water with a local fishing collective to surfing and kitesurfing lessons just steps from the private plunge pools. (Editor's note: The resort is temporarily closed due to El Niño rains.) As for the region’s culinary offerings? You won’t have to look hard. This sun-baked stretch of coast is where carne seca dries on clotheslines and makes its way into seco de chavela (a pounded beef and plantain dish bursting with chile), and where Peruvian classics like conchas a la parmesana (scallops baked with cheese) are remixed using conchas negras, a unique shellfish found in the mangroves of Tumbes, near the border with Ecuador. Hearty dishes like picante de mariscos, a seafood stew, sit side by side with chilled raw-fish tiraditos that fly close to the sun in their spice. —Megan Spurrell.

Go for: kitesurfing for pros—and newbies—in a fishing village now home to quietly luxurious stays.

With the charm of a fishing village and powerful winds, Preá has become an ideal kitesurfing destination.

From spa treatments to horseback riding and yoga Preá's hotel scene brings a bevy of activities to travelers.

In a recent development, preá has the unmistakable charm of a Brazilian fishing village—a seemingly endless shoreline, a sandy-street village center, and beachfront restaurants serving fresh-caught seafood—with one major difference: wind, and lots of it. From July through early January, this sleepy town catches some of the world’s most consistently powerful gusts, the kind kitesurfers cross oceans for. Now, with the sport growing in popularity, beginners are starting to come too, bringing with them an exciting new energy. Kitesurfing has become so popular among Brazil’s well-heeled travelers that Marco Dalpozzo, a partner at the pioneering pousada Rancho do Peixe, dubbed the sport “the new golf, the new tennis, the new skiing.” Learning takes some commitment: First-timers start on the sand before progressing into the water, with a full beginner course happening over several days. Yet new kitesurfing locations are making it easier than ever for newcomers to join the scene.

According to recent reports, while the famed Jericoacoara next door is known for its lively nightlife and parties, Preá’s newly introduced crop of high-end boutique hotels and villas offers a more exclusive take on barefoot luxury. Think ocean-facing rooms, intimate private villas, and seaside bungalows paired with their own kite schools, full-service spas, and feet-in-the-sand restaurants. Whether you’re up for braving the wind or not, there is plenty else to do in terms of both adventure and restorative wellness.

Sources indicate that preá offers easy access to the landscapes that made neighboring Jericoacoara famous. Set off by buggy or quad to explore the region’s enormous sand dunes and freshwater lagoons that naturally occur between them, or visit the otherworldly Jericoacoara National Park. Back in Preá, book a treatment at L’Occitane spa at the Preabeach Boutique Hotel, take advantage of the yoga offerings at Hotel Kabana, or horseback ride along the shore. By sunset, everyone gathers on the beach to watch the last kiters streak across the water as what looks like a giant ball of fire slips into the ocean. Later, dinner can range from candlelit seaside dining to charming restaurants like Casinha in the rustic village center, where flip-flops are acceptable attire.

The wind is bringing in major investments too. Grupo Carnaúba is building what it envisions as the wind sports equivalent to an Alpine ski resort. Its members-only Carnaúba Wind House, opened in 2024, offers bungalow stays with a kite center, beach club, restaurant, and freshwater lagoon, while the larger Vila Carnaúba development is adding more luxury residences and beach tennis facilities. The biggest arrival yet comes in 2028, when Anantara opens its first property in the Americas: A beachfront resort complete with its own signature spa and dedicated kitesurfing school. —Carla Vianna.

Go for: cheffy restaurant openings, a revitalized downtown, and a lineup of literary festivals.

Bachour by Mata (above) is pastry chef Antonio Bachour's first Latin American pastry shop—and it joins a wave of buzzy culinary openings that have made this city an increasingly hot food destination.

It has been revealed that são Paulo is a city for those with an appetite—for business, culture, and, of course, food. Brazil’s largest and most frenetic metropolis is home to some of Brazil’s most extraordinary architectural landmarks, from Lina Bo Bardi’s museum of art (MASP) to Paulo Mendes da Rocha’s reimagined Pinacoteca do Estado de São Paulo. Meanwhile, the restaurant landscape has never been more exciting: São Paulo is now home to the only two restaurants in the Southern Hemisphere recently awarded three Michelin stars: Tuju and Evvai.

Recent findings suggest that that momentum is reshaping the city well beyond its established dining districts: Near Avenida Paulista, the Mata São Paulo hospitality complex has gathered some of the city’s buzziest names, including Mata Città, Korean barbecue spot Lavva, and pastry chef Antonio Bachour, who opened his first Latin American pastry shop Bachour by Mata here, all in one place. More is on the way, including a fresh boteco, Á Brasileira, from Janaína Torres (named the World’s Best Female Chef in 2024 by World’s 50 Best). Her beloved Bar da Dona Onça, on the ground floor of Oscar Niemeyer’s sinuous Copan building, has itself gained new neighbors, including seafood-focused Brisa do Barú and Tem Umami!, an ice cream shop with a near-permanent line out the door. Together with an observation deck, the new arrivals are turning the 1,160-apartment Modernist landmark into one of downtown’s liveliest destinations.

Also inside the Copan is Livraria Megafauna, part of a fresh wave of independent bookstores bringing literary life back to São Paulo’s streets. Its Poesia no Centro poetry festival, returning in April 2027, has already become a fixture on the city’s cultural calendar; meanwhile the growing Feira do Livro, organized by literary magazine Quatro Cinco Um, is expected to draw around 100,000 people to the Praça Charles Miller in May 2027, and it will nearly double the space devoted to its mostly independent publishers.

It has been revealed that that literary boom is part of a broader cultural surge, with major events like SP-Arte in April and the Bienal de São Paulo—Latin America’s largest visual arts event—returning to Ibirapuera Park in September. The expansion of Museu de Arte de São Paulo Assis Chateaubriand, the city’s landmark art museum, only adds to the momentum: Its newest building, opened in March 2025, increased exhibition space by more than 60% and made room for upcoming indicates and works from the collection previously kept out of public view. —Rafael Tonon.
