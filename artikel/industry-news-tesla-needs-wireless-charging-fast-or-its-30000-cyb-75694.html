Industry News: Tesla needs wireless charging fast, or its $30,000 Cybercab can’t charge at home

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that tesla is now taking orders for the $29,990 Cybercab, with the first deliveries expected in Austin next month. But there’s a problem buyers should know about: they won’t be able to charge it at home.

Recent findings suggest that the Cybercab has no onboard charger, so it only takes DC fast charging. And the wireless home charging pad Tesla has been teasing since 2023 still isn’t something you can buy.

When Tesla unveiled the Cybercab in 2024, Elon Musk remarked it wouldn’t even have a plug. “It just goes over the inductive charger and charges,” he said on stage.

That plan didn’t survive production, or at least the current version in production. Cybercabs have a NACS charge port behind a panel in the rear bumper, and Tesla has been plugging Cybercabs into regular Superchargers in Austin since they joined the fleet.

The bigger glitch is what sits behind that port. Tesla confirmed to attendees at Tesla’s Austin launch event in September that Cybercab has no onboard charger, the box that turns the AC power coming out of your wall into DC power the battery can store.

In a recent development, no onboard charger means no Wall Connector. No Mobile Connector on a 120V outlet, and no Level 2 station at the mall either. If it isn’t a DC fast charger, the Cybercab can’t use it.

Sources indicate that for a robotaxi, deleting the onboard charger makes sense. It’s one less part to buy, cool, and carry around, and a fleet car spends its downtime at a depot, not in someone’s garage. Those little things help make the car more economical and efficient.

But Tesla is now selling the Cybercab to individuals.

It has been revealed that as we reported last week, Tesla’s Cybercab autonomy agreement requires a $299 monthly subscription for the car to move at all. The same document puts charging squarely on the buyer: “You are responsible for registration, insurance, parking, charging, general cleaning and all other costs.”.

It also tells owners to “keep the vehicle charged and connected so over-the-air updates can install.” Connected to what, it doesn’t say.

The Cybercab Order Agreement limits the car to NACS DC fast charging, Superchargers included. It also notes only the registered owner can use the Cybercab, and only while physically present in it for the entire trip.

The subscription agreement carves out one exception: when service is due, the Cybercab “will travel autonomously, unoccupied to a Tesla service center.” Nothing similar exists for charging.

Recent findings suggest that so you can’t send your Cybercab to a Supercharger at 2 a.m. while you sleep, which is the one thing a self-driving car should be great at. You’d have to ride along, sit in a two-seater at a Supercharger while it charges, and ride back home.

The obvious answer is the wireless home charger Tesla keeps promising.

Tesla first showed what looked like a wireless home charging station during its Investor Day in March 2023. Later that year, chief designer Franz von Holzhausen confirmed the project on Jay Leno’s Garage, saying owners would “just drive over the pad in your garage” to start charging.

Tesla then filed patents for the charging pad and, after the Cybercab unveiling, released a video of it charging the robotaxi at 25 kW, claiming efficiency “well above 90%.” In February, the FCC granted Tesla a waiver to use ultra-wideband radios to guide the Cybercab onto the pad.

And that’s where it stops. Tesla has never sold the pad, never priced it, and never given it a launch date. It even gave up on wireless charging for the Cybertruck last year, even though the truck shipped with the connectors for it.

With deliveries estimated for December, Tesla has about six weeks to launch a home pad if it wants the first owners to charge in their own garage.

Tesla’s own aims for Cybercab charging revolve around fleet depots. In Austin, it filed plans for a hub on St. Elmo Road with 48 V4 Supercharger stalls in a first phase and cabinets to support 80 wireless chargers in a second.

In a recent development, tesla could, in theory, let private owners use it. But with the owner-presence rule, that means riding to a depot across town and waiting there. That’s a Supercharger visit with extra steps.

The other route is a home DC charger, and those exist. We covered Sigenergy’s 25 kW bidirectional DC charging module last year, which bypasses a car’s onboard charger the same way a Supercharger does. Lunar Energy also revealed a 25 kW home DC charger this year, but it’s not expected until mid-2027.

Neither is a plug-and-play answer. Sigenergy’s module is built to sit inside its SigenStor home battery system, which isn’t cheap, and Tesla would have to support third-party DC charging on a car it controls this tightly. Tesla could also build its own small DC wall unit. It hasn’t unveiled one, but CEO Elon Musk talked about integrating one into the Powerwall for years.

This is what happens when you take a vehicle designed for a fleet and sell it to consumers to hit a deadline. And let’s be honest, charging is probably not even in the top 5 of problems with selling the Cybercab to personal owners in its current state.

Musk promised a Cybercab would be sold to a customer for under $30,000 before 2027. Technically, he’s going to make it. But on the Q3 earnings call, he also stated “the vast majority” of Cybercabs will stay Tesla-owned and run as transport-as-a-service. The car was clearly engineered around that, and deleting the onboard charger is the best example.

That saved Tesla money on every unit. The cost now lands on the few people buying one.

Sources indicate that I don't see any evidence that the production Cybercab has the onboard hardware to use such a pad. Besides the wireless receiver coil it would need some kind of charging hardware to convert the pulsed wireless charging into DC to charge the battery.

Sources indicate that if you live in Austin next to a Supercharger, you’ll manage. But think about what Tesla is actually selling here: a self-driving car that can’t drive itself to charge, can’t charge in your garage, and needs you sitting in it at a Supercharger. That’s worse than owning a Model 3 with a Wall Connector, and it costs $299 a month on top.

The fix exists in Tesla’s labs. It has the pad, the patents, and the FCC approval. It needs to ship it as a product, ideally bundled with the car, or change the contract to let the Cybercab go charge on its own.

It has been revealed that will the first Cybercab owners get a wireless pad in December, or will they spend their evenings at Superchargers? And if it releases, how much will it cost? The rare few home wireless chargers that have been sold to date have been quite expensive, and the value proposition of the Cybercab, which is currently basically a $300 a month lease with a $30,000 down payment, is already pretty awful.
