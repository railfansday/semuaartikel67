Where scientists draw the line on AI agents

🌐 Source: Hugging Face • 📅 25 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 8 min read (1697 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, delegating research tasks to AI does not remove scientists’ responsibility for how the work is accomplished. For example, a researcher using AI to review the literature needs to check whether the system overlooks conflicting evidence or makes claims the cited papers don’t support. Someone handing off data analysis to an agentic system needs to understand which methods it used, which files it accessed, and whether the results can be successfully reproduced. A researcher automating part of a lab protocol needs confidence that the system respects physical safety constraints, such as temperature limits or material toxicity, while a researcher working with human-participant data needs assurance that a system protects participants' privacy and limits who can access their information. Researchers also need clear institutional guidelines for verifying and disclosing AI-assisted work.

Sources indicate that these ethical and safety questions are central to our work on AI for science. Responsible science is about more than accuracy—scientific fields have ethical research norms because of past actions and choices by scientists and scientific communities, and those norms also cover how research is conducted and what impact it has. We want AI tools to expand what scientists can accomplish without compromising their ability to scrutinize evidence, verify methods, and – perhaps most importantly – stand behind their findings.

To better understand what that requires, we interviewed 17 scientists working in the natural, applied, and social sciences about the responsible development of AI agents. These systems can use tools and carry out research tasks with varying degrees of autonomy. The interviews drew on a network of researchers established through earlier survey work—we wanted to understand how scientists would steer AI’s development to reflect the responsibilities of their fields. We asked where researchers would welcome agentic assistance, what could go wrong, and what safeguards they would need to trust agents with their own studies.

In a recent development, our interest in these questions grew out of work to evaluate the safety of models developed at Ai2. As we expand our focus on AI for science, we’ve found that much of the safety work on scientific applications emphasizes catastrophic risks in areas such as biology and nuclear physics. We also need ways to assess whether agents follow the standards of responsible conduct of research in the fields where they’re used.

Sources indicate that one of our main findings concerns trust. Across the interviews, we identified five dimensions of scientific trust: validation, privacy, objectivity, accountability, and transparency. Together, they reflect concerns that go beyond whether an AI system produces a correct answer. Researchers also need to understand how an answer was reached, protect sensitive information, recognize bias, and determine who is charged with verifying and standing behind AI-assisted findings. These are matters of responsible conduct of research.

According to recent reports, another main finding suggests that scientists should help decide which research tasks agents take on, how those agents operate, and how their performance is evaluated. Even a technically correct result may be unsuitable for scientific use if researchers cannot inspect how it was produced, or if an agent accesses confidential data or files beyond what the researcher authorized.

According to recent reports, A third key finding pertains to the future of science. Interviewees were divided between excitement about AI-driven progress and existential concern about the role of human scientists. They proposed training in AI use and named developing and enforcing guidelines as a critical, timely challenge.

According to recent reports, for many participants in our interviews, the appeal of AI agents began with time-consuming research tasks—processing large datasets, writing code, documenting experimental procedures, and handling repetitive administrative work. Automating these activities could free researchers to spend more time on fieldwork, mentoring, or developing fresh ideas. It could also make it possible to analyze volumes of data that would be difficult to examine manually.

But delegating research tasks creates a corresponding need to verify the work an AI system performs.

Several participants described checking AI-generated answers against original publications rather than accepting summaries at face value. They worried about confident responses containing subtle inaccuracies, as well as tools that reinforce assumptions by retrieving only studies that support a favored hypothesis.

Sources indicate that but checking the final answer from an AI system is only part of the challenge. Researchers may also need to know which sources an agent omitted, whether it followed the requested methodology, and what information it accessed along the way.

According to recent reports, our interviews suggest how evaluations of AI agents for scientific research could better reflect these concerns. A literature-review benchmark, for example, could test whether an agent overlooks contradictory evidence or draws conclusions unsupported by its sources. A coding benchmark could assess not only whether the generated code works, but whether the agent accesses files outside its authorized scope. Evaluations of data-processing agents could compare their methods and results with established human workflows.

In a recent development, these examples point toward a longer-term goal—a participatory benchmark in which scientists help define the research practices and safety requirements that agents should be tested against. We want to evaluate whether systems developed at Ai2 or elsewhere follow the standards of the fields where they’re used, as well as whether they complete assigned tasks.

In a recent development, these evaluations remain proposals to develop and test with scientists, rather than established solutions. But they offer a direction for our work at Ai2: building open scientific tools means making their behavior and limitations easier to investigate and giving the research community a role in defining what trustworthy performance looks like.

Verification is only one consideration in using AI tools for science. Respondents also raised a more fundamental question—what happens to scientific expertise when AI takes over parts of the research process?

Recent findings suggest that an early-career biologist described deliberately limiting AI use while acquiring skills they hoped to teach future students. Another researcher questioned whether producing a correct result was sufficient if the researcher learned nothing in the process: even with perfect outputs, “did the person who obtained the perfect outputs get anything out of this?”.

Industry observers note that some participants were concerned that AI-assisted research could diminish learning, though the value of automation depends in part on the researcher and the task. An experienced scientist may reasonably delegate a familiar procedure to save time, while someone still learning that procedure may benefit from doing the work themselves.

Participants also drew different boundaries around activities involving intellectual contribution. One biologist refused to use AI to generate research ideas, considering that a responsibility to retain personally. Others were more open to AI-assisted ideation or writing. An anthropologist, for example, argued that some sections of scientific papers, particularly routine descriptions of methods and results, are formulaic enough to benefit from automation.

According to recent reports, even researchers who tried AI for writing sometimes reconsidered. One political scientist described using Claude to help revise a manuscript, then deciding to rewrite the entire paper because the boundaries of their own intellectual contribution had become unclear. “I don’t know what was AI and what was me,” the researcher noted.

It has been revealed that context mattered, too. Having AI critique a manuscript before submission was viewed favorably by some participants, while using it to review another researcher's unpublished work raised concerns. The distinction involves more than technical capability; peer review carries responsibilities around confidentiality, attribution, and the fair evaluation of others' research.

In a recent development, these disagreements suggest that responsible automation cannot be defined by a universal list of acceptable and unacceptable tasks. Scientific fields have different norms, and individual researchers may have different goals even when performing similar work.

Recent findings suggest that the same tension applies to safeguards built into AI systems. One political scientist described how content restrictions that prevent AI tools from discussing certain topics could interfere with legitimate research on political violence or human rights violations. Meanwhile, participants working with sensitive human-subject data wanted stronger guarantees about privacy, access, and how information would be used.

It has been revealed that both are valid concerns. An agent that acts without sufficient safeguards could expose confidential data or produce harmful results, while one that’s constrained too broadly could prevent scientists from pursuing legitimate lines of inquiry.

This is why scientific communities need a meaningful role in shaping the boundaries of AI assistance. Developers need to understand not only which activities an agent can perform, but which decisions researchers want to retain and where safeguards are necessary—as well as where those safeguards might obstruct valuable work.

Our findings point toward an approach to scientific AI development that starts with the people who will use the technology.

Industry observers note that researchers should be able to examine an agent's contributions, determine which work to assign, and preserve opportunities to develop their own expertise. Achieving that will require technical features such as transparent records of agent actions and granular data controls, alongside evaluations informed by the standards and practices of different scientific communities.

It will also require continued collaboration. Our study is exploratory; the findings come from interviews with 17 US-based scientists, not a representative survey of scientific opinion or an empirical evaluation of specific AI agents. Participants discussed both the generative AI tools they use today and what they anticipate from more autonomous systems. Their perspectives identify crucial concerns and design priorities, rather than establishing a consensus about how AI should be used in science.

Still, they offer a path forward. At Ai2, we believe AI can accelerate scientific discovery while preserving the openness, reproducibility, and independent scrutiny that make research trustworthy. Through our broader efforts to develop and evaluate scientific agents, we aim to put those principles into practice. That means working with scientists to identify worthwhile uses for agents and translating their concerns into concrete evaluations, in addition to making it easier to understand both what these systems accomplish and how they accomplish it.

The promise of scientific AI isn't simply that researchers can pass off more work. It's that they can pursue questions that might otherwise be out of reach while retaining the knowledge, judgment, and agency needed to make discoveries that are their own.
