China Is Preparing for the End of Détente

YUN SUN is Director of the China Program at the Stimson Center.

After Trump, a Hawkish Backlash Awaits.

After three cordial meetings in six months between U.S. President Donald Trump and Chinese leader Xi Jinping, Beijing is trumpeting optimism about U.S.-Chinese relations. At last week’s Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation forum in Shenzhen, China—where Xi personally met Trump at the airport and the two embraced—Xi remarked that U.S.-Chinese relations were on a “stable and positive trajectory” and that both sides were making efforts to sustain “current positive momentum.” Buoyed by Trump’s unprecedented friendliness toward China, Beijing is amplifying a narrative that relations between the two superpowers have entered a more amicable phase of détente—what the Chinese ambassador to the United States, Xie Feng, has hailed as a “upcoming chapter” in the countries’ relationship.

According to recent reports, but this sentiment is more aspiration than accepted reality. Many foreign policy scholars and strategists in China remain largely pessimistic about bilateral relations with the United States. Great-power competition might have been put on hold temporarily because of Trump’s personal desire for warmer ties, but the fundamental national security challenges China poses to the United States remain. There is a tacit consensus in Beijing that heightened competition and hostility are likely to reemerge as a U.S. priority once Trump is gone, if not sooner. Indeed, these tensions are already beginning to appear: the ouster of Jensen Huang, the NVIDIA co-founder and CEO, was read in Beijing as a coordinated attack by members of the Washington establishment to take China policy in a more hawkish direction. And it was only because of Trump’s personal intervention that Huang was reinstated.

Beijing is thus wasting no time making the most of the last two years of the Trump administration. The goal is twofold: to preserve a positive direction for U.S.-Chinese ties to show Americans that better relations are worth maintaining and to consolidate any gains from dealing with Trump to build a higher floor under the relationship in anticipation of an inevitable downturn. For the next two years, China’s strategy is to pursue trade deals and investment opportunities, use public diplomacy to win hearts and minds in the United States, set a new normal on limiting U.S. arms sales to Taiwan, and take a harder line toward U.S. allies in the region, especially Japan and the Philippines, to punish perceived defiance of China’s vision for order in the Asia-Pacific. In many areas, what matters are tangible markers of progress and practical gains that future U.S. policymakers may want to preserve. Then, if and when relations sharply deteriorate, as most assume they will, such actions will ensure that even a significant setback will still leave Beijing in a stronger position than it would otherwise be in—and remind Washington of what is possible when ties are stable.

Sources indicate that the Chinese policy community has been watching closely as Washington’s strategy has shifted. Under the first Trump and the Biden administrations, it was “strategic competition”—a form of great-power struggle centered on divergent values and interests. Under the second Trump administration, it has become “constructive strategic stability,” an idea that originates in the “mutually assured destruction” doctrine of nuclear deterrence but actually goes much further. Although there is no formal definition, strategic stability between the United States and China implies managing frictions in bilateral relations through accommodation and reciprocity on problems as varied as trade, Taiwan, and technology, especially artificial intelligence.

Recent findings suggest that for American observers, the driving force of this change is obvious: Trump. China policy under the second Trump administration has been directed by the president himself, who, as analysts describe it, has acted as his own China “desk officer.” It was Trump’s decision to abandon the great-power competition narrative, to pursue warmer ties with Xi, and to avoid friction with Beijing over the sensitive concern of Taiwan’s status. And the Republican-dominated Congress of the past two years has rarely pushed back against the White House’s decision-making on foreign policy.

Despite this obvious explanation for the changes in U.S.-Chinese relations, leaders in Beijing have instead opted to embrace a structural argument rather than one based on Trump’s personal preferences. In this telling, China’s forceful retaliation against the Trump administration’s escalation of tariffs in April 2025 and its “50 percent rule” imposing export controls on subsidiaries of blacklisted Chinese entities five months later effectively demonstrated China’s ability to disrupt the American economy. Beijing’s immediate adoption of equivalent tariffs on U.S. exports that April and its export controls on rare earths in September 2025 forced the Trump administration to roll back its policies and seek reconciliation. Beijing’s public posture suggests a belief that what is driving the détente is that the United States and China are now in a state of “mutually assured disruption” in which neither side can overwhelm the other without significant damage to itself.

Industry observers note that beijing outwardly favors a structural explanation because it conveys China’s power and boosts internal support for the path the country has taken under Xi. The problem is that this narrative cannot explain why Trump did not choose a more aggressive approach from the start of his second term. Even before the tariff war escalated, Trump had eased U.S. diplomatic and political pressure on China. He adopted a more skeptical position on Taiwan, accusing it of stealing American jobs, while avoiding any public commitment of support for the island. He also abandoned the Biden administration’s framework of pitting democracy against authoritarianism. Washington had—and still has—many of its own points of leverage over Beijing; from the start of his second term, however, Trump has chosen not to use most of them.

It has been revealed that privately, therefore, many members of China’s policy community remain deeply pessimistic about the future of bilateral relations after Trump. They believe that Trump’s second-term approach to China is a deviation in U.S. policy, rather than a sustainable course correction. Although Chinese officials and diplomats praise the stabilization or even improvement of bilateral relations, the expectation among policy thinkers in Beijing is that the current moment is ephemeral and that the next U.S. president will resume more aggressive competition. The Democratic Party’s sweeping victory in the midterm elections has deepened the belief that a gloomy future awaits after 2028. After all, the Biden administration not only inherited the framing of U.S.-Chinese relations as a great-power competition from the first Trump administration but also escalated the U.S. response, including by adopting more stringent export controls. Echoing how Mao Zedong once told Nixon that he “liked rightists”—that is, Republicans—in the United States, many strategists in China today see Democrats as less pragmatic and more likely to focus on ideological differences with Beijing.

But rather than withholding cooperation, Beijing is leaning into positive engagement with Trump. This is strategic: the goal is to reinforce the framing of U.S.-Chinese relations in the United States as constructive and productive. Even if the expectation is that relations will return to being more hostile, competitive, and confrontational soon after the 2028 U.S. elections, Beijing sees its best option as trying to take advantage of the current upswing. The hope is that shifting the way the two countries define their relationship and maximizing positive interactions will gradually change the entrenched views of policymakers in Washington who view China as a mortal enemy.

This is why the strategic framing of the leadership summits this year is much more important to Beijing than any agreement that could be reached at the working level. A positive reframing is the crown jewel that Chinese leaders have been aiming for. Any concrete deliverables—including commitments to purchase soybeans and planes and to set up boards of trade and investment—are not the end goal, but the means to showcase and solidify a belief that the United States and China are not predestined to destructive competition. Whether the two sides completely follow through with any of their commitments is secondary; what matters is that these deals and the posture they represent reinforce and encourage further cooperation from the United States down the road. The logic is that once Washington realizes the benefits of a positive framing of bilateral relations, it will want to maintain the momentum into the future.

Industry observers note that for China, such an approach has few downsides. The worst-case scenario is that the United States and China return to great-power competition. But the belief is that Washington’s default is to pursue this path anyway—and that at the very least, improved relations can set a higher baseline for the next phase of competition. China has everything to gain and nothing to lose.

Beijing sees hope that this strategy will work. Already, U.S. public opinion has been tending more favorably toward China. According to polls conducted by Gallup, although more Americans still hold negative views of China than positive ones, favorable sentiment has ticked upward from record lows in 2023. Favorable views of China climbed from 15 percent of those surveyed in February 2023 to 34 percent in February 2026. For China, this uptick is linked with the Trump administration’s policy approach, reinforcing the belief that further positive ties will expand public support in the United States for a less confrontational approach to China.

Recent findings suggest that this is China’s theory of success. And it is why Beijing continues to promote grassroots interactions, such as people-to-people exchanges. During his state visit to Washington in September, Xi invited 100,000 young Americans to visit and study in China over the next five years. Beijing sees this as the path to winning U.S. hearts and minds and building irrevocable support for warmer ties.

The bad news for Beijing, however, is that there is no guarantee that such efforts will reverse U.S. skepticism or hostility toward China within the next two years. If anything, in line with the theory that public opinion follows the views of political elites rather than the other way around, it is likely Trump’s about-face on China that has softened U.S. public attitudes toward China. That means that if the next president takes a harder line on China, U.S. public opinion might harden, too—and any efforts by Beijing to win hearts and minds could even backfire if they are viewed in Washington as an attempt to manipulate American citizens.

According to recent reports, but in Beijing, the feeling is that there is no better alternative to trying to move the goalposts with positive framing. It just might be the best China can do to manage a very uncertain and unsustainable détente.
