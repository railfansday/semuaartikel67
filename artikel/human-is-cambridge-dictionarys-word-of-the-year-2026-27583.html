The Cambridge Dictionary has named ‘human’ as the Word of the Year 2026.

The year has been defined by global debates on what it means to be human when AI is blurring the boundaries between people and machines.

'Human' is defined in the Cambridge Dictionary as ‘having the qualities, faults, and feelings that people have, as opposed to gods, animals, or machines’.

In 2026, many public figures defended humans and humanity in the age of AI. Bill Gates, Chair of the Gates Foundation, made the case for “human reserved jobs”, such as caregivers, that should never be taken by machines, in a widely read essay.

According to recent reports, commenting on the selection of ‘human’ as Cambridge Dictionary Word of the Year, he said: “AI is making us think harder about what it means to be human. It could help us cure diseases, improve education, and give people more time to spend on the things that matter most.

Industry observers note that "But we need to make deliberate choices about how it fits into our lives, including where we want people to remain at the center. The measure of AI’s success should be how much it improves human lives.”.

‘AI godfather’ and Nobel laureate Geoffrey Hinton has warned that “we’re creating alien beings. I’m willing to be selfish and choose humans.” And in his Magnifica Humanitas letter, Pope Leo XIV spoke of newly introduced forms of “dehumanization” and the “pressing duty to remain profoundly human”.

Actors Scarlett Johansson and Cate Blanchett, musicians Tayla Parx, LeAnn Rimes and Billie Eilish, and authors Jodi Picoult and Jonathan Franzen challenged an "assault on human creativity" as the Human Artistry Campaign and Artist Rights Alliance opposed machine-generated replicas and theft of human-generated content by large language models (LLMs).

Meanwhile, Project Hail Mary author Andy Weir has wondered whether he "might be among the last generation of fully human writers". The Authors Guild has rolled out a ‘Human Authored’ kitemark for books, supported by Professor Dame Mary Beard and Malorie Blackman.

As AI deepfakes simulated humans, Matthew McConaughey became the first Hollywood actor to pre-emptively protect himself against unauthorised digital reproduction by trademarking clips of his voice, his face and even his catchphrase ‘alright, alright, alright’. Taylor Swift followed suit, trademarking her voice and image in April.

Professor Rachel Adams, an expert on AI governance and human rights at the University of Cambridge, noted: “AI brings us back to questions that we’ve asked for millennia: who are we, what does it mean to be human? The answer to these questions has never been fixed or stable, but the act of asking it is something we have in common.

Recent findings suggest that “AI is going to be a profound test for humanity. There’s a real risk that it deepens existing inequalities, but it also has the potential to solve them.".

Dr Tom McClelland, a philosopher of science at the University of Cambridge, stated: “I think ‘human’ is a really interesting choice because right now our understanding of humanity is changing.

According to recent reports, “We're trying to understand the difference between a human mind and an artificial mind, between living beings like us and artificial beings that we're creating. What sets us apart, is it consciousness, creativity or compassion?

“All the rhetoric around AI is about what's inevitably going to happen. But to young people growing up in an AI-enabled world, my advice would be that nothing is inevitable. What happens in the future of AI is up to us. It's up to you.".

According to recent reports, lexicographer Colin McIntosh of the Cambridge Dictionary stated: “AI is changing how we talk about being human. It’s this renewed interest in the idea of being human that captured the zeitgeist and made ‘human’ the Cambridge Dictionary Word of the Year.”.
