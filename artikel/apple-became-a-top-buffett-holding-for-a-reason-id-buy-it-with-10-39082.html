Few companies have built a business as durable as Apple (AAPL -0.79%) and that's one reason Warren Buffett made it one of Berkshire Hathaway's (BRKA -1.29%)(BRKB -1.03%) largest holdings. Plus, Apple closed fiscal 2026 with what management described as its best fiscal year ever.

Overall, with its strong brand, loyal customer base, and massive ecosystem, Apple is a stock I'd want in my portfolio, and one I'd put $1,000 into today.

But what exactly did Buffett see in Apple in the first place?

For starters, Apple has become one of Warren Buffett's core holdings for a simple reason: he came to see it less as a traditional tech tech giant and more as an extraordinary consumer business with one of the strongest ecosystems in the world.

In fact, at Berkshire's 2018 annual meeting, Buffett described Apple as having an extraordinary product and ecosystem, adding that the product was extremely sticky and supported by a huge ecosystem. That stickiness was central to his investment thesis.

Recent findings suggest that specifically, the iPhone sits at the center of an ecosystem that spans hardware, software, and services. In other words, once consumers become embedded in that system, switching becomes much more challenging.

NASDAQ · As of 9:40 AM ET 11/19/26.

Sources indicate that that customer loyalty matters because it gives Apple something Buffett always valued: a durable competitive advantage. As a result, its loyal customer base provides the organization with a steady stream of revenue from future iPhone upgrades, Mac and iPad sales, subscriptions, and App Store spending.

On top of that, Buffett repeatedly praised Apple's economics and management. For example, at Berkshire Hathaway's 2026 annual meeting, he credited much of the value Berkshire gained from its Apple investment to then-CEO Tim Cook's leadership. Indeed, he believes Cook did an exceptional job of running the organization and building on its strengths.

And even after Berkshire's leadership transitioned to Greg Abel, Apple remained in the company's portfolio.

For example, Abel highlighted Apple as one of a few core holdings that Berkshire expects will compound over decades in his first shareholder letter as CEO. That's an encouraging signal. In other words, Berkshire's upcoming leadership reaffirmed its importance to the portfolio, signaling confidence in its long-term outlook.

According to recent reports, and Apple's latest results have only reinforced that view. Specifically, its September quarter produced records for total tech giant revenue, iPhone revenue, and earnings per share (EPS), while Apple's installed base of active devices reached another all-time high across its major product categories and geographic markets.

According to recent reports, plus, services have an crucial role in the company's business model. Apple has spent years building recurring relationships around its hardware ecosystem, and the segment's continued strength gives the company another avenue of growth beyond the next product upgrade cycle.

Recent findings suggest that looking ahead, artificial intelligence (AI) could become Apple's next big ecosystem opportunity. The tech giant introduced its all-upcoming Siri AI alongside new software improvements at its Worldwide Developers Conference (WWDC26), while continuing to refresh its hardware lineup.

Sources indicate that but owning Apple isn't without risks, as the organization faces intense competition, regulatory pressure around areas like the App Store, and the need to convince customers to keep upgrading their devices. On top of that, there's execution risk around AI. In particular, if Apple falls behind competitors in making AI useful to consumers, that could weaken its leadership.

But despite these risks, Apple continues to build on the strengths that made it a compelling investment to Berkshire.

Many Wall Street analysts covering Apple rate it a buy, with an average target price of $345.63. As Apple stock at the moment trades above its average target price, any major pullback could be an attractive entry point for investors.

That makes sense because with the stock trading at a forward price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio of about 37, it isn't exactly cheap.

Ultimately, Apple's powerful brand, loyal customer base, and sticky ecosystem all made it one of Berkshire's most successful investments. Those strengths remain in place, and that's why I own the stock and would still put $1,000 into Apple today.
