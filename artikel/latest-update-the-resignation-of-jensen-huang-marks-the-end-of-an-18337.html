Latest Update: The resignation of Jensen Huang marks the end of an era. And not the one you think

Just days after the American electorate delivered a sweeping midterm rebuke that materially curtailed Donald Trump’s presidential power, the independent board of Nvidia— the world’s most valuable firm for much of the year—launched an investigation into its own CEO, Jensen Huang. The board’s focus was Huang’s affair with an alleged Chinese spy. His resignation swiftly followed. In less than a week, we witnessed the core machinery of accountability brought to bear on two of the most powerful men in the world. But while the midterm outcome is being heralded as a resounding revival of healthy democracy, the Nvidia example should not be mistaken for a corporate oversight renaissance. Instead, Nvidia’s boardroom intervention may mark the end of a governance era, especially when it comes to frontier AI firms. Nvidia has a single-class share structure, meaning the CEO—who has a minority of votes at under 4%—remains accountable to an independent board. Elsewhere however, the governance guardrails for other powerful tech executives are rapidly eroding—a dismantling revealed in IPO filings—just as these companies amass unprecedented global influence. SpaceX and Anthropic’s recent filings disclose that post IPO, their founders have a majority of shareholder votes via multi-class share structures, dual-class for SpaceX, and a novel five-class system for Anthropic. Popularized by Alphabet and Meta, this shields them from short-term market pressures, and the appeal is clear: in the U.S., a record 36% of new listings in the first half of 2026 had dual-class shares. Yet by insulating the founders, ordinary shareholders lose any real say over major decisions, leaving traditional corporate accountability resting in the hands of insiders. Both companies also took advantage of stock exchange rules for ‘controlled companies.’ This allowed SpaceX to skip requirements for an independent board and gave Anthropic leeway to bypass standard rules for how its board members are chosen. Musk can only be removed from the board or his executive positions by the vote of Class B holders, of which he personally holds approximately 91.6%. To be fired, he would have to vote to fire himself. Anthropic’s novel approach features a Long-Term Benefit Trust—whose members appoint their own successors—that chooses a majority of the board. The co-founders hold 50.1% of the votes on all other matters, including appointing the rest of the board. Anthropic argues that the structure is designed to prioritize AI safety. But is also cements the power of the founders and the trust, leaving everyday public investors with no voting leverage to hold the board accountable to either the profit motive or the public benefit mandate. SpaceX has gone a step further by relocating its corporate domicile to Texas, where the law sets a much higher bar for minority shareholders to legally challenge the board in court. The state imposes a 3% shareholding requirement, which equates to over $50 billion in SpaceX stock. Taken together, these mechanisms represent a dismantling of the corporate governance levers meant to protect shareholders and society when things go wrong. And while OpenAI has yet to file, the swift 2023 reinstatement of its CEO—and the subsequent departure of several independent directors—demonstrates the sheer leverage already wielded by those at the helm. In recent months, advanced AI agents from frontier labs have broken past security controls, accessed external systems and carried out unauthorized cyber actions. As these early AI risks begin to materialize, questions about which corporate structures are best equipped to ensure AI safety and security, along with whether and how to regulate AI, are fast becoming matters of pressing public concern. This is a pivotal moment. At the industry level, current and forthcoming IPOs look set to ‘bake in’ higher-risk corporate governance arrangements, while state jurisdictions are actively exploring legal frameworks for agentic AI organizations that legitimize the complete absence of human oversight altogether. The rapid advance of frontier AI may feel inevitable, bringing boundless potential alongside incomprehensible risks. Yet in the race to build our automated future, these firms are strategically layering available federal, state, and market levers to consolidate control. Regulatory exemptions that might be appropriate in isolation to help startups scale are being combined by AI giants to escape oversight at the exact moment we need it most. No individual is infallible—whether vulnerable to error, hubris, or compromise—which is precisely why corporate guardrails are needed to mitigate the hazards of concentrated power. Politically, there is great impetus for action. American voters have just demonstrated the power of institutions that allow people to hold their leaders to account. The lawmakers they have elected must ensure that frontier AI companies cannot engineer that accountability away. Investors may choose to accept founders’ terms. The rest of us have not agreed to accept the risks.
