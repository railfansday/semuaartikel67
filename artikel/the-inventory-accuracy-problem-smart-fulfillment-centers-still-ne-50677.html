The Inventory Accuracy Problem Smart Fulfillment Centers Still Need To Solve

Amruth Puppala | Senior Engineering Manager | Walmart.

In a recent development, ​Walk into a modern fulfillment center, and you will see how much warehouse operations have changed. Robots move products, software coordinates thousands of orders and inventory systems track merchandise across receiving, storage and shipping.

Yet, even with these advances, a familiar problem remains.

A system demonstrates that an item is available, but when someone goes to pick it, the product is not there. The inventory record may be correct on paper, but the physical reality is different. What seems like a minor discrepancy can swiftly turn into additional searches, picking delays, order reallocation or missed shipping commitments.

According to recent reports, this raises an important question. Instead of simply knowing how much inventory we have, shouldn’t we also know how reliable that information is?

It has been revealed that fulfillment centers and distribution centers handle enormous volumes of inventory movements every day. Products are received, stored, replenished, picked and shipped, often through a combination of manual processes and automated equipment.

Even with well-designed systems, discrepancies can occur.

In a recent development, A pallet may be placed in the wrong location. A receiving transaction might be completed before the merchandise is physically verified. Damaged inventory may not be removed from available stock immediately. Sometimes, a picking exception is recorded without the underlying inventory issue being resolved.

According to recent reports, warehouses already have processes to investigate these situations. Cycle counting, reconciliation and exception management are essential parts of daily operations.

However, many discrepancies are discovered only after they begin affecting order fulfillment.

By that point, the operations team is responding to a problem rather than preventing it.

One idea worth exploring is assigning a confidence score to inventory records based on the operational evidence supporting them.

Consider two storage locations, each showing 100 units available. The first location was recently verified, and its last several picking transactions were completed successfully. The second location has experienced repeated picking failures, unexplained quantity adjustments and an unresolved exception.

Both locations show the same available quantity, but they clearly do not carry the same level of operational confidence.

It has been revealed that today, many inventory decisions focus primarily on quantity, location and availability. Adding a confidence score could give warehouse systems another useful signal when making those decisions.

The score would not represent a fresh inventory balance. It would indicate how much confidence the system has in the existing record.

This could help teams identify which locations need verification and which inventory discrepancies are most likely to affect upcoming orders.

Recent advances in AI create an opportunity to evaluate these signals continuously rather than relying entirely on scheduled checks.

A system could monitor receiving transactions, inventory movements, picking results and adjustment history to identify patterns that deserve attention.

For example, repeated picking failures at the same location might suggest a discrepancy. An unusual sequence of inventory adjustments could indicate a process concern. A location that has not been physically verified for an extended period could warrant additional scrutiny, particularly if the inventory is needed for upcoming shipments.

Rather than treating each event independently, the system could examine the broader history and determine whether further verification is justified.

Sources indicate that newer AI models can also help interpret exception notes and other information that traditional rule-based systems may struggle to analyze consistently. Where cameras or robotic sensors are available, their observations could provide additional supporting evidence.

The key point is that AI should help identify where to investigate, not make inventory adjustments without verification.

Cycle counting is one area where this approach could have immediate practical value.

According to recent reports, most distribution centers already use scheduled counting programs, often based on inventory value, product movement or operational requirements. Those methods are useful and should continue.

However, a fixed schedule does not always account for problems developing during the day.

It has been revealed that imagine a location that reports 100 units in stock. Several picking attempts fail within a short period, but the inventory quantity remains unchanged. Instead of waiting for the next scheduled count, the system could recommend that the location be checked sooner. The priority could increase if those units are needed for orders approaching their shipping cutoff.

This would allow inventory teams to focus attention where a potential discrepancy is most likely to cause operational disruption. It could also help avoid unnecessary checks in locations where recent transactions consistently support the recorded inventory.

I would not recommend introducing another large platform to solve this problem. Most warehouses already generate much of the information needed. The opportunity is to make better use of it.

A practical starting point would be to combine inventory movement history, picking exceptions, recent count results and upcoming order demand.

Sources indicate that simple rules could first identify obvious discrepancies. Machine learning could then help recognize less predictable patterns, while newer language models could assist with interpreting operational notes and explaining why a location has been flagged.

The warehouse management system would remain responsible for inventory records and transactions. Any quantity correction would continue through established verification and approval processes.

For an initial rollout, I would focus on a limited number of high-volume locations and measure whether the approach reduces repeated picking failures, shortens discrepancy resolution time and improves verification productivity.

It has been revealed that accuracy matters, but so does the amount of additional work introduced. If the system generates too many unnecessary checks, it has not solved the underlying problem.

Fulfillment technology has made progress in improving speed, automation and visibility. But visibility alone does not guarantee accuracy.

A warehouse can have a view of its recorded inventory and still make the wrong fulfillment decision when those records do not match physical conditions.

The next opportunity is to make inventory reliability a continuous part of warehouse operations, rather than something evaluated through periodic reconciliation.

According to recent reports, the goal is not to eliminate inventory discrepancies. That would be unrealistic in a continuously operating facility. The goal is to recognize problems earlier, direct people to locations and prevent disruptions before they affect customer orders.
