In a recent development, grand Theft Auto VI has been dominating the lives of gamers, young and old, since its arrival on November 19th. It can seem impossible for parents to resist the inertia that settles on kids as they spend dozens (hundreds) of hours in front of screens engaged in violent role-play. While there is no easy solution, with the holidays fast approaching and gift-giving on many parents' minds, maybe there is a quaint solution to this very technological problem. Knitting.

In a recent development, A few months ago I was presenting to an audience in northern Norway where I observed something odd. At least 10% of my audience were knitting. These weren’t church ladies with white hair and compression socks (what an awful stereotype but sadly one that is portrayed far too often in our popular culture). My audience of knitters comprised, instead, younger women and men who were listening attentively to what I was saying while occupying their hands with needles and skeins of yarn. Where it has become routine to put stress balls and fidget toys on conference room tables, here was a much nicer way to evoke calm. Even better, the repetition wasn’t a wasted act of self-flagellation. My audience was engaged in a creative activity that turned a ball of wool into gifts for children or casual sofa throws for lovers and friends.

Why don’t we teach children to knit anymore? For that matter, why has it become so rare to see a child whittling with a pocket knife, sewing, building a tree fort, painting their room, drawing a tree, or creating something that is three-dimensional, tactile? So few children experience a distraction that has purpose and a tangible outcome. Video games are the opposite. Time consuming with no discernible gain.

It has been revealed that if this sounds like nonsensical nostalgia, it’s not. There is good reason to believe that one part of the solution to our children’s mental health crisis is to be found in encouraging them to do arts and crafts and other do-it-yourself (DIY) activities like woodwork and gardening. In a study of 18-24-year-olds recruited (ironically) online, those who were more engaged in extracurricular activities, including crafts, reported significantly lower risk for suicide and a greater sense of belongingness, two outcomes that are co-dependent.

That same pattern was found during the COVID-19 pandemic when some 3,725 adults were surveyed in the USA, with a clear pattern to the findings. Those who gardened, those who did woodwork, and those who immersed themselves regularly in arts and crafts all reported lower rates of mental health problems.

In a recent development, while we adults obviously see these benefits, our children may, if given the opportunity, tell us that having some fun activities to do that don’t involve screens is actually welcomed. That was the message from a 2022 report that included over 7,000 kids in Finland where, to be honest, being outdoors and working with one’s hands is more a part of the culture than in North America. Still, it seems that kids who are encouraged to engage in non-screen activities can realize a nice mental health bump.

I can certainly relate to these findings. I’ve always been someone who liked to work with my hands. I am an avid woodworker, but also knit, sew and draw. I can remember well the feeling as a child of accomplishing something concrete and sharing it with others. The reflected glory of being seen as a maker was wonderful.

It’s up to us adults to not just take screens away from kids but to also provide the resources to keep them occupied. That might be an easel and paint, a saw and workbench, a garden or maybe a pet. And yes, it could even be a set of knitting needles and some brightly colored yarn.
