How Milburn won over Labour on tackling rising youth unemployment

Labour grandee seems to have assuaged MPs’ nerves on need for welfare reform to tackle youth unemployment crisis.

In a boxing gym in Peterborough almost a year ago, Alan Milburn, freshly appointed to run a government review into the nearly 1 million young people out of work and education, said he would be “unafraid to shine a light on uncomfortable truths”.

The Labour grandee, who held three cabinet jobs under Tony Blair, warned there was a “downward escalator” taking young people into a “world of benefits” and vowed to provide radical solutions.

Industry observers note that many Labour MPs bristled. Just six months earlier, they had mounted a successful rebellion to kill off the government’s planned £5bn cuts to health and disability benefits, significantly damaging Keir Starmer’s political authority by forcing him into a U-turn on his flagship welfare bill.

That day in the gym, Milburn knew he would have to win over a party suspicious of his motives and anxious about the impact of changes on vulnerable people, even if some acknowledged that the growing welfare bill was unsustainable.

On the eve of publishing his report on Tuesday, early signs are that he has assuaged backbench nerves, succeeding where Starmer failed in moving the Overton window within the Labour party on the welfare debate around young people.

Industry observers note that milburn’s strategy was clear from the off: make the moral argument for change, make it loudly and make it often. His favourite – and oft-repeated – statistic has been that the government spends 25 times as much on benefits for young people as it does on supporting them into work.

In a series of interviews and MP engagement sessions over the following months, he described the projections – that numbers could reach 1.25 million by the early 2030s without major intervention – as a “national failure” that would result in a “catastrophic” generational fault line.

After his interim report in May, he gradually signalled that overhauling health and disability benefits for young people would be a central outcome of his review, suggesting the welfare system in its current form had failed them and was exacerbating inactivity.

Labour MPs were given time to get used to the idea and to air the arguments in public, with controversial elements of the report released early – the abolition of health benefits for under-25s, including existing claimants, among them.

According to recent reports, “Alan didn’t want any nasty surprises,” said one government source. “He wanted to engage fully with MPs and charities and anybody else with concerns. The government can’t move forward on this unless it’s able to bring people with them.”.

He had several factors working in his favour. As his days in No 10 ran out, Starmer was so weakened that Milburn was able to “bulldoze” Downing Street into supporting his proposals, according to another source, especially as Pat McFadden, the welfare secretary, was already onboard.

Secondly, several of the leading figures in the welfare rebellion were now inside government – most notably Louise Haigh, who is now Andy Burnham’s number two, and Anneliese Midgley, the chief whip.

Meg Hillier, the moderate chair of the Treasury select committee who had fronted that rebellion, last week indicated the proposals should get a “fair wind”, although she also acknowledged the difficulty of the choices ahead.

Thirdly, the drip-drip of pitch-rolling came alongside a wider sense of alarm in the UK over the prospects of the next generation, amid concerns over rising mental ill health, social media and AI upending the jobs market.

Burnham has stated he wants to cut the welfare bill “as soon as we possibly can” but has warned against “crude cuts” (which the government would struggle to get past Labour MPs anyway) in the push for “sustainable” employment.

His approach has echoes of Tony Blair, who talked of “a hand up, not a hand out”, actively pursuing a policy of reforming and investing in welfare specifically to bring down what he termed the “bills of social failure”.

The massive difference now is that the fiscal backdrop is more challenging, though Milburn will recommend that ministers spend hundreds of millions on back-to-work support in the short term in return for welfare savings, increased tax receipts and a fairer, happier country over the long term.

Recent findings suggest that the rather clunky symbolism of choosing a boxing gym for his first promotional visit last year wasn’t lost on anybody. Since then, Milburn – a former chair of the Social Mobility Commission – has been clear this is a fight he thinks the government needs to have.

His recommendations, along with those from the Timms review into health and disability benefits, which will be published in the coming weeks, will form the basis of a white paper next spring that will then become a new welfare bill, voted on by Labour MPs.

So, for now, the campaign to win them over continues. Standing alongside Milburn at a meeting of the parliamentary party on Monday night, McFadden mentioned: “What is proposed is a upcoming settlement for young people. This is not last year’s proposal with better PR.”.
