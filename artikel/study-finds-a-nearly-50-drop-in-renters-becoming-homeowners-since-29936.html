Study Finds a Nearly 50% Drop in Renters Becoming Homeowners Since 2009

As economic pressures continue to rise, a shocking new study has found that the share of renters who convert to homeownership has fallen 46% since 2009.

TransUnion, a leader in global information and insights, released its 2027 Personal and Commercial Lines Annual Insurance Outlook on Tuesday, which focuses on what the insurance industry needs to stay profitable in a changing market.

The report states that personal line underwriting has been strong throughout this year, but companies need to assess their strategies going forward as fewer people buy cars and homes, therefore reducing the pool of potential policyholders.

According to the report, the share of renters who convert to homeownership was 3.9% in 2025, a sharp decline from 7.2% in 2009.

This represents a shocking 46% decrease in the share of renters transitioning to homeownership in just 14 years.

Combined with a 2025 report from the National Association of Realtors®, which stated that the median age of first-time buyers climbed from 33 to 40 in just five years, TransUnion concluded that the pool of potential policyholders is shrinking.

The decrease in renters buying homes goes back to affordability and economic pressure, according to Ryan Thomson of The Assumable Guy.

“Renters didn't stop wanting to own. Rates tripled and prices kept climbing, so the monthly number stopped working,” Thomson tells Realtor.com®. “In Colorado Springs, where I sell, 71% of households could afford a median-priced home in 2019. By 2023, it was 25.3%. So renters stay put. Or they move wherever the payment still works.”.

TransUnion also found that Florida, Texas, North Carolina, South Carolina, and Tennessee had the greatest influx of latest residents between July 2025 and June 2026, with Florida gaining nearly 87,000 net-new residents.

The most significant outbound migration was seen in Massachusetts, Latest Jersey, Illinois, New York, and California, with the Golden State alone with a net loss of 146,000 residents.

This migration pattern is likely taking place as people chase affordability, looking for places that offer more space for lower costs.

Sources indicate that “A household may want a certain lifestyle, but not be able to afford it given the high costs of housing and of living in general in the area. State and local taxes may also play a role,” Cameron Walker, a real estate expert at Clever Real Estate, tells Realtor.com. “Texas, Florida, and the Carolinas may be more affordable and have lower state and local taxes.”.

The TransUnion report notes that the growing states all have an elevated exposure to hurricanes, severe convective storms, and other catastrophic weather compared to the states people are leaving.

While the growing states are all located near the Sun Belt and generally considered more affordable than coastal states such as Newly introduced York and California, the increased risks elevate costs beyond purchase price.

Recent findings suggest that “People may be assuming the Sun Belt states are generally affordable, and that isn't always the case,” Walker adds. “Property values are increasing in Sun Belt states because of in-migration, which increases property taxes and insurance costs, and inflates housing costs.”.

For insurance companies, TransUnion recommends strategies like focusing on retaining and creating more value from current customer, winning policies from competition, and combining risk mitigating and marketing efforts when discussing migration.

For the housing market, improving outlooks and rebuilding the renter-to-homeowner path comes down to affordability and opportunity.

“We need to increase the supply of entry-level, affordable housing. This includes promoting the construction of latest housing in high-demand, high-growth communities. The industry needs to offer new financing options and improve responsibility to help new homebuyers qualify to enter the housing market,” indicates Walker.

Sources indicate that “People aren’t giving up their dreams of homeownership. They’re postponing them because the housing market has changed and the financial requirements to homeownership are out of the reach of many. The markets that can provide jobs and affordable housing will thrive, and gain the most, from future homeowners.”.
