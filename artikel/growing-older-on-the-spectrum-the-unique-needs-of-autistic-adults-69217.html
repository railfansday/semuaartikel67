Because so few studies focus on aging in autistic adults, there is little understanding—and support—for the specialized needs they may have later in life.

Industry observers note that our understanding of autism has evolved considerably over the last 50 years. Today, many autistic adults who were overlooked or misdiagnosed in childhood are finally pursuing a proper evaluation and diagnosis.

But even as our understanding has grown, we still know very little about the unique needs and challenges of autistic people in midlife and beyond, or about the supports that help most. Why? Because less than 1 percent of all indexed autism research has focused on midlife and older age.

Sources indicate that that means we still don’t understand the cognitive, health, and social experiences of autistic adults as they age. And this lack of research influences clinical practice: Historically, clinicians haven’t been trained and equipped with an understanding of autism in older age.

An analysis of health records from the United Kingdom indicates that around 90 percent of autistic people in midlife (ages 40 to 59), and 96 percent of older adults (ages 60 and beyond), are undiagnosed.1 Until autism research and diagnosis rates improve, we won’t understand the full autistic experience, but here are the truths coming into focus.

Recent findings suggest that autism in older age is associated with complex health needs. Compared with the general population, middle-aged and older autistic adults have higher rates of several physical and mental health conditions, making frequent and proactive screenings key for this group.

In a recent development, despite the higher rates of dementia, Parkinson’s disease, and cognitive difficulties found among autistic adults, we should not assume that autism is linked to accelerated cognitive aging. Autistic people are no more likely to experience rapid cognitive aging than are members of the general population, according to research.

According to recent reports, however, one study found that about 20 percent of autistic people had highly variable cognitive profiles; this subgroup developed an old-age profile earlier than expected compared to non-autistic adults. For these individuals, red flags for cognitive decline and dementia may present at a younger age than we’d expect in the general population. Autistic adults who fit this profile may require additional help and support as they age to remain safe and independent.

Sleep typically deteriorates with age, however, adults with high autistic traits are more likely than others to report moderate or severe sleep problems as they grow older, including problems with falling and staying asleep, sleep efficiency, and daytime tiredness.

According to recent reports, sleep is a lynchpin for general and mental health. Sleep problems, for example, tend to worsen anxiety, a condition that is highly prevalent among autistic adults. Improving sleep quality by blocking out light with curtains, using earplugs or a white noise machine, choosing pajamas and bedsheets made from soothing fabrics, and/or using a weighted blanket can significantly benefit the mental health of autistic people as they age.

In a recent development, sensory processing differences are part of the autistic experience. Over a lifetime, exposure to difficult and unpleasant sensory experiences, like crowded, noisy environments, is linked to mental health problems in older autistic adults. At the same time, even small changes, such as noise-canceling headphones or wearing a face mask in public, can help. Adjustments and accommodations such as these should not be overlooked when thinking of health and quality of life.

Industry observers note that compared with non-autistic people, autistic adults experience higher rates of loneliness — the subjective feeling of lacking meaningful connection. While middle-aged and older autistic people tend to have small social networks, about one in five autistic adults has no contact with family or friends in a given month, compared with one in 20 neurotypical adults. Certain neurodivergent traits — rigidity, flat affect or an aloof personality, and communication challenges — may help explain social difficulties.

Interestingly, in the general population, we found that women tend to be lonelier than men. In the autistic population, the feeling of loneliness was more common in men.

Industry observers note that connections with family and friends seem to provide a protective mental health benefit and lead to a better quality of life. Supporting autistic people in forming meaningful relationships on their own terms and in relation to their passions and interests could be a key target for interventions.
