Posted November 23, 2026 | Reviewed by Davia Sills.

Recent findings suggest that this post is co-authored by Vicki Shabo, a senior fellow at New America, and Yalda T. Uhls, Ph.D.

Saturday Night Live’s November 14 sketch, “Sister”—a satirical, close-to-home ad for “sister” as America’s solution to a burgeoning caregiving tsunami—started a cultural conversation that has only grown. In a way that news stories, documentaries, and advocacy efforts couldn’t have dreamed of, this three-minute segment blew open a conversation America has long needed to have, not in the isolation of our own homes or whispered quietly in hours of exasperation and burnout, nor solely in the halls of legislatures, but in a true, fully open public square.

For both of us—a former movie executive-turned-developmental psychologist focused on using the reach and influence of pop culture to advance youth well-being and agency and a policy expert and advocate-turned-narrative change strategist with a focus on transforming television and film narratives about gender, work, family, and care—the ripple effects of the “Sister” sketch are a case study in how media helps people feel seen, valued, and understood—and provides hope for how media representation can spark cultural conversations that contribute to policy change.

In a recent development, “Sister” resonated because it remarked a quiet truth out loud: According to the National Alliance for Caregiving and AARP, there were 63 million caregivers to loved ones who are ill, disabled, or aging in the United States as of 2025, and six-in-10 were women—sisters, daughters, aunts, grandmothers, granddaughters, and female cousins. The experience of family caregiving is becoming more common: There were nearly 20 million more people in the U.S. providing care to loved ones in 2025 than there were 10 years before.

In a recent development, some people choose to take on the care of a loved one, but more than half of caregivers noted they had no choice. That’s, in part, because “choice” is too often a function of social expectations, gender dynamics that began in childhood, the economics of a gender-based wage gap that keeps women’s earnings lower than men’s and makes it more economically feasible for a woman to reduce work or leave a job (further exacerbating a wage and retirement savings gap).

In a recent development, why did a comedy sketch succeed in reaching so many people—traveling from private family and friend chats to social media to traditional print, radio, and television news outlets? Because stories can make complex social issues personal, relatable, and emotionally engaging. Rather than asking viewers to absorb another set of statistics about caregiving, “Sister” invited them to recognize a familiar family dynamic and laugh at its absurdity. Humor made an uncomfortable truth easier to confront, while the sketch’s specificity made it widely recognizable. And in today’s fragmented media landscape, a short, shareable piece of entertainment can move rapidly across generations, platforms, and communities, reaching people who might never read a policy report or seek out information about caregiving.

Recent findings suggest that caregiving is having a moment. The stories that “Sister” sparked, and facts about caregiving in America, have inspired public dialogue ranging from personal testimonials to a long-overdue conversation about the country’s lack of support for caregivers, including the high costs of care that the general public underestimates and the lack of policies to support families with care needs.

From a media research perspective, the power of this sketch is also no surprise. Media consumers in the United States are hungry to see stories about work, family, and care on screen. Research conducted for New America’s Re-Scripting Gender, Work, Family, and Care project in 2025 found that 92 percent of viewers aged 18 to 59 said that seeing relatable depictions of work and family woven into characters’ lives in television and film is important to them; yet as of just a few years ago, fewer than 11 percent of U.S. television shows depict caregiving.

U.S. television and film viewers—like Americans overall—are also eager for solutions: 70 percent of all viewers said in the Newly introduced America research said that America should do more to support the needs, challenges, and financial costs of family members caring for older adults, and 82 percent said they believe families and communities and the economy would be more stable if the United States had national policies like paid family and medical leave and affordable elder care that make it easier for Americans to work and care for their loved ones.

According to recent reports, young audiences are especially important to this conversation. At the Center for Scholars & Storytellers at UCLA (CSS), our annual Teens and Screens research has consistently found that young people want more authentic, relatable stories about their lives and relationships. They are increasingly interested in stories grounded in family, friendship, and community rather than sensationalized drama. These findings challenge the assumption that younger viewers are interested only in escapism. They also suggest an opportunity for storytellers to explore how young people experience family responsibilities, intergenerational relationships, and care—not just as background details, but as meaningful parts of their lives. Upcoming America’s research demonstrates the same is true for adults.

Industry observers note that we also know that changing what appears on screen doesn’t have to be left to chance. Both of our organizations work directly with writers, producers, and entertainment executives to translate research into practical storytelling guidance. At CSS, our work on the portrayal of boys and masculinity, for example, has helped creators think more deeply about vulnerability, friendship, and emotional expression. At New America, we’re doing the same for representation of how people manage the demands of work and family life with caregiving. Soon-to-be-released qualitative research reaffirms what survey data has already demonstrated: that audiences are tired of exhausted daughters in television scripts and eager to see men competently sharing, or leading on, family care. We’ve both heard directly from television creators about how these insights have influenced their thinking and storytelling choices.

Industry observers note that we know that media that sparks viewers’ imagination, engagement, and conversation often encourages producers, writers, and executives to replicate resonant and popular cultural content. And we know that more media depictions can lead to widespread culture and policy change.

What if “Sister” was just the beginning of a media representation revolution that brings family conversations about caregiving into the light? What if every show or film considered a character’s caregiving responsibilities, family relationships, finances, and job responsibilities as an element of a backstory or the on-screen story?

It has been revealed that at a time when millions of Americans are navigating the challenges of care, these stories could do more than entertain. They could help people feel seen, challenge assumptions about gender and family, and expand our collective imagination about what is possible.

“Sister” made America laugh at an uncomfortable truth. Now Hollywood has an opportunity to help us imagine something different.
