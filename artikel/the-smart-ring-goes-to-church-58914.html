The Smart Ring Goes to Church

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, stressed? Heart rate elevated? If you’re wearing a smart ring from Oura, Ultrahuman, or Samsung, the spike could prompt app suggestions like breathing exercises, meditations, or a physical reset like a short walk. So far, so Silicon Valley. But now you can add reading the Bible to that list of self-soothing activities.

It has been revealed that the first generation of Christian smart rings is here, joining other religious wearables like iQibla’s dedicated Muslim smartwatches, rings, and Hajj bands alongside “kosher” smartwatch apps. Two fresh devices in particular—Glorify’s Ring and Everblessed Technology’s Elah Ring—connect to hugely popular Christian prayer apps with tens of millions of owners, and both claim to be the world’s first Christian smart ring.

According to recent reports, both are first, in their own ways. Everblessed’s $249 Elah Ring S1 went on sale in October and is shipping in the US. The $299 Glorify Ring launched in July, with reservations open and shipping set for January 2027, but 77,000 units of an earlier beta iteration, the Amen Ring made by faith tech company ConfideIn (which merged with Glorify this May), were given away last Christmas. Everblessed manages the Blessed app, and Glorify has an eponymous app.

Another substantial player in prayer apps is Hallow, aimed at Catholics, backed by Vice President JD Vance’s Narya venture capital firm and celebrity ambassadors, with 24 million installs to date. Hallow’s toe-dip into wearables was a 2024 smart rosary with NFC embedded in the crucifix, made with Italian brand Ghirelli; Everblessed tried an earbud, necklace, and bedside device before settling on the ring design.

Head-to-head on design, the Elah and Glorify rings are both made from titanium alloy, with a thorn-inspired twist for Glorify and a minimalist band featuring a cross for the Elah S1, which is also IP68 waterproof to 5 meters.

For now, the Elah Ring is slightly techier: prayer reminders come via the cross, which glows via an LED, and “gentle” vibrations; these can pulse at certain times of day or be triggered by changes in heart rate and stress levels, picked up by a photoplethysmography (PPG) sensor. Glorify’s CEO Henry Costa tells WIRED these emotion-based reminders will land on the Glorify Ring in six months; for now, you can set the times of day you want prayer reminders, track heart rate, blood oxygen, stress, and sleep via the optical sensor.

One addition to the Glorify Ring that Costa says he personally uses “a lot” is recording prayers straight into the built-in mic. The ring has a light to signal when it's recording, and the app transcribes them. Everblessed CEO Ricky Xia says that, after consulting with pastors and customers, the feeling was that voice notes on the ring were seen as “suspicious.” It’s available in-app, but most people don’t pray out loud, and too many features drain the battery life. (The Elah Ring runs for up to six days on one charge; Glorify for four to five days.).

It has been revealed that both companies see the smart ring nudges helping wearers prioritize meaningful quiet time, away from screens, while offering in-app Christian practices as an alternative to Eastern meditation. Glorify offers Bible study, courses, and meditations. Blessed has devotional “anchors,” Bible texts and audio, plus guided breathing. Xia says he’s happy if the Elah vibrates and a user picks up a paper Bible rather than the Blessed app, while Costa says he’s “crying out to use my phone less.”.

According to recent reports, both Glorify (founded in 2020, now merged with ConfideIn and headquartered in the San Francisco Bay Area) and Blessed (founded in 2023 and based in San Jose) operate in more than 150 countries. Perhaps the biggest difference between the two rings is their relationship to AI.

Xia is all-in, describing the S1 as an “AI-native” product, with a pastor-complementing, not-replacing FaithMate chatbot in the Blessed app, customized based on preferences, congregation, and depth of learning. ChatGPT is like a “good coworker,” he claims, while FaithMate is designed to be “your friend” and an agent that can also handle secular tasks.

“A lot of our consumers not only ask questions about the Bible but also ask about themselves, their relationships with their wives, they’re being bullied at school," Xia indicates, stressing safeguards around data storage, encryption, and privacy. "Everyone is going to have some AI that they trust, and how they view that is built on their personal stuff, their private vulnerabilities.”.

In a recent development, costa is more circumspect, treading carefully on the consumer-facing side and noting that the Pope has weighed in with a 6,000-word encyclical on AI. A launch interview with CBN News garnered negative comments: “AI is Antichrist Information,” for one.

“The stance I’ve always taken is that our daily devotional is hand-authored by a team of theologians,” Costa notes. “It’s prayed over, and that is a stamp of approval.” He criticizes AI slop prayer apps as inauthentic. Still, he says the Glorify team has already tested “many forms” of AI personalization: “Any time, if we do use AI, it must be very clear to the user.”.

Smart ring owners were finding ways to add faith features to mainstream accessories before these rings came to fruition. For 20 years, timer and reminder apps have helped with prayer schedules, but recent examples are more creative. Heidi Campbell, professor of communication in religious studies at Texas A&M, refers to these DIY upgrades to the Oura ring and devices like the Muse headband as “spiritual mind-body hacking.”.

Oura owners on #christiantiktok chat about how they already use the ring’s stress and HR alerts as a prompt to pause and reflect, as per Elah.

Recent findings suggest that smart ring people flag changes in resting heart rate and sleep quality with scripture following their daily practice. “It’s really helpful for, like, what was inhibiting me from getting to a meditative or contemplative prayer state?” Campbell indicates. “It takes some of the mystery out of prayer, I think, but that can also be positive. There is a way to do prayer well and not well.” Ring hackers can also dig into health stats during Lent, Ramadan, and Yom Kippur to analyze energy expenditure and how fasting periods affect their bodies.

AI-powered voice recordings and emerging attempts at emotion tracking are part of a upcoming phase for smart rings, notes Ekta Mittal, a senior analyst of connected devices at CCS Insight. These new “purpose-driven” features extend wearable tech into “new parts of consumers’ daily routines,” Mittal says. She suggests that while Glorify and Everblessed will add value for some, the rings are “not directly competing” with established smart ring giants like Oura, Ultrahuman, and Samsung.

In a recent development, the pandemic sped up the digital transition of many places of worship when congregations could not meet up in person, encouraging experimentation with social media. So it’s early days for Silicon Valley’s impact on organized religion. Researchers at the University of Würzburg in Germany, led by Ilona Nord, are studying the theological implications of using tech, including smart rings, for spiritual practices, investigating which values are baked into secular devices and apps.

In a recent development, campbell suggests many Christian groups haven’t stopped to consider the unintentional consequences of cultivating spirituality via personal tech. “All of these things are about individualizing the practice, which is good, but the challenges are for religions which are really focused on communal aspects,” she states. Not everyone has the money or connectivity to access these gadgets, but also, more existentially, “it puts spiritual development over communal development.”.

Xia points to the Blessed app’s communities, including its Healing Room broadcast feature, which lets pastors go live with sermons. It's not in the Elah app yet, but it's on the roadmap. Costa says communal practice is a core pillar, highlighting the “social layer” of public notes made on Christian texts in-app and an upcoming Glorify Ring feature which will allow a user to pair their device with a family member’s ring: “When your loved one is feeling down, you’ll get a buzz to check in with them.”.
