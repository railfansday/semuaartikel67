According to recent reports, while thoroughbred horse racing and barrel-aged bourbon have made Kentucky famous, the state is also home to biodiverse forests and geologically rich mountains. Western Appalachia is a hiker’s paradise, with hundreds of miles of trails, accessing some of the Bluegrass State’s most striking landscapes—and the Red River Gorge is one of its crown jewels.

This ancient canyon spans tens of thousands of acres in eastern/central Kentucky. The powerful Red River carved the Gorge millions of years ago, creating an outdoor playground of cliffs, creeks, waterfalls, and sculpted rock. It has more than 60 official hiking trails and more sandstone arches than anywhere else in the eastern U.S.

Here’s what to see, where to hike, and how to play if you’ve just got one weekend in Kentucky’s Red River Gorge.

Industry observers note that every first-time visitor to the Red River Gorge must visit Gray’s Arch—no exceptions. This massive natural sandstone structure will give you a crash course in the area’s unique topography, including a diverse hardwood forest, massive rock outcroppings, and steep staircases built directly into the hillsides.

Sources indicate that while most visitors stick to the 2.5-mile out-and-back trail to Gray’s Arch before turning around, it’s worth extending your hike to 5.8 miles on the Gray’s Arch Long Loop Trail. This route takes you past the crowds that gather beneath Gray’s Arch. It winds deeper into the canyon, through the cool, damp forest, over creeks, and beneath looming rock overhangs that still hold Indigenous petroglyphs.

Whittleton Campground has a free day-use lot with a trailhead accessing multiple hikes. Two trails lead to the Whittleton Arch, and another goes to Hensen’s Arch. Try the standard USFS Whittleton Trail, a 2-mile loop that starts and ends at the campground. Or take the longer Whittleton Branch Trail #216. This 4.8-mile out-and-back accesses the arch and follows a creek for most of its length.

From the campground, you can also hike the Hensen’s Arch Trail. In less than a mile, you’ll reach a second sandstone arch and a deep sinkhole where you can descend into the Earth via a set of metal stairs.

It has been revealed that if you’re still feeling adventurous after you hike, head to SUP Kentucky for a Crystal Kayak and SUP Glow Tour, where you’ll paddle underground into a submerged former limestone mine. Thanks to limestone’s natural filtration properties, the water is astoundingly clear. Watch schools of rainbow trout swim beneath your LED-illuminated translucent kayaks, as colors refract upon the ceiling.

In a recent development, the highlight of Kentucky’s Natural Bridge State Resort Park is the massive natural bridge (for which it’s named). This impressive geologic feature stands 65 feet high and 78 feet in length.

In a recent development, there are several ways to access the arch. Hikers can take the 5.5-mile Natural Bridge and Battleship Rock Trail, which winds past five different trail shelters. Hikers walk beneath some of the country’s tallest tulip poplar trees—some standing more than 150 feet. Natural Bridge is the trail’s climax. You can view the rock formation from numerous vantage points, and even walk across the arch to the lookout point.

It has been revealed that for a longer, more challenging approach, try the 7.5-mile Sand Gap Trail. It can be done as an out-and-back, or connected with Natural Bridge Trail to make a loop.

Sources indicate that for more ridge, arch, and canyon trails, head 30 minutes north of the Natural Bridge State Resort Park to the Auxier Ridge Trailhead. From this hub, hikers can access the 6.0-mile Double Arch Loop, which follows Auxier Ridge and passes Courthouse Rock, Haystack Rock, Double Arch, and Arch of Triumph.

The Star Gap and Skyview Arch Trail also starts at the Auxier Ridge trailhead. This roughly 6-mile out-and-back hike is narrow and woodsy. It includes some overgrown sections and short scrambles with about 1,300 feet of elevation gain. But the effort is rewarded with access to Skyview Arch at the end.

Dispersed camping is allowed around Auxier Ridge. Or check out the Sandstone Drifter, a reservable cliffside treehouse accessed by a suspension bridge. But be warned: climbing its 177-stair floating staircase is a short, steep hike in itself.

It has been revealed that when rock-climbing traffic slows in winter and early spring, the Muir Valley Nature Preserve is a perfect place to see waterfalls at their full splendor. That’s when precipitation is highest, meaning larger, fuller falls and a chance to see giant icicles and frozen cascades of water. A $15 parking fee is required, and visitors must sign a waiver before entering.

Connect Horseshoe Falls, Joe Ponder Falls, Stadium Wall, and Nellie’s Falls to create a moderate, 4.4-mile hiking loop that spans three waterfalls and winds beneath sandstone overhangs. To extend the hike, add Liz’s Arch and Washboard Wall Trail for an additional 1.5 miles, exposing hikers to more arches, cliffs, and several rock-climbing areas.
