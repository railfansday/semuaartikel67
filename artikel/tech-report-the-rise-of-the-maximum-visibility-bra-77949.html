The summer of 2014, I was a college student working at a small-town lingerie store that lined its walls exclusively with neutrals. Every day, I sold women flesh-toned bras—black if they were really feeling kinky—all while filling my own intimates drawer with the same styles. Undressing in front of a guy that same summer, he voiced genuine disbelief over my nude, industrial-strength strapless bra. He wasn’t so much confused over its gravity-defying function as the fact that it was there, at all.

In a recent development, the early 2010s set a nearly impossible goal for young women: naturally perky breasts without any apparent scaffolding. But that’s all changing. Pop princess Adela’s exposed bras have become as central to her identity as her electric pink hair. Once left to jiggle freely beneath semisheer cotton, Charli XCX’s chest is now more often displayed in intricate lingerie. Several months back, Saint Laurent’s Spring 2027 front row was littered with boudoir-inspired looks while Prada and Fendi literally sent bras-as-tops down the runway. Bras have come out of hiding.

Somewhere between the “free the nipple” movement in 2012 and the impending #MeToo revolution of 2017, boobs became less of a sexualized spectacle and more of a political battleground. Victoria’s Secret canceled its fashion show as basics-driven intimates brands like Cuup and Arq gained traction for destigmatizing natural shapes. Then, breasts bounced back into cultural focus. The shift might be credited to the climate—conservatism generally propels women to take shelter in traditional femininity, or it is thrust upon them—as well as the twin forces of Sydney Sweeney and semaglutide weight loss. Either way, women got thinner while their chests grew larger—reflecting a beauty standard we hadn’t seen in a generation. Implants entered a latest era of demand and dialogue, as celebrities like Kylie Jenner and Megan Fox transparently joined the big-breasted brigade.

But boobs aren’t just bigger—they’re bolder. Gone are the days of basics and bralettes. It’s all about va-va-voom underwire, satin, and lace—even push-ups have returned to the conversation. Still, “there’s a difference between showing your bra and showing off your bra,” discerns Tara Ocello, director of brand marketing at intimates brand Skarlett Blue.

Recent findings suggest that last month, the recently revived Victoria’s Secret Fashion Show broadcast Jenner strutting down the runway, her 445-cc-moderate-profile silicones outfitted in a record-breaking $50 million bra. Bella Hadid, Candice Swanepoel, and Adriana Lima showed off the iconic mall brand’s finest embroidered balconettes, lacy push-ups, and satin plunge bras. Angels aside, the reintroduction of these runway collections is also indicative of a shift in consumer product demand—a spokesperson for Victoria’s Secret claims there’s “strong interest” in fashion-forward details like lace, satin, logo hardware, and the brand’s statement shine straps. The Very Sexy Push-Up Bra remains the number one of the three mainline bras in its “Very Sexy” category. “Today’s customer wants a bra that performs beautifully and deserves to be seen,” notes a Victoria’s Secret spokesperson. “It’s expanded how customers engage with [our bras] as both foundation pieces and intentional styling elements.”.

The luxury London retailer Agent Provocateur has been positioning its designs as statement pieces since it debuted in the mid-’90s—the last time exposed bras enjoyed prevalence. Creative director Sarah Shotton noticed the trend bubbling several years back on the Glastonbury grounds and immediately returned to the design studio. “We predicted this and designed [for it] about a year and a half in advance. I am constantly thinking, could it be worn as an outerwear bra?” she notes. “I think if customers are going to invest in it, they want it to work harder than just in the bedroom. Lingerie as outerwear is just getting bigger and bigger.”.

Sources indicate that category transcension is, of course, good for business. Victoria’s Secret reported sales up 10 percent in its second quarter, and Agent Provocateur has also seen revenue growth this year. Eager to capitalize on the movement, contemporary brands like Lioness are now engineering built-in underwear to imitate the effect of an exposed bra. This week, Charlize Theron wore a similar style by Tom Ford to the Governors Awards.

According to recent reports, I have, undoubtedly, been influenced. This year, I have spent more money at Victoria’s Secret than cumulatively on lingerie in the past decade—acquiring my own Very Sexy Push-Up, and a turquoise polka-dot number. These are bras I’ve never gravitated toward, let alone purchased, because they seemed like impractical additions to my wardrobe. I’ve had to ask myself: Can you wear leopard-print under a white tee? What if the lace breaches my neckline?

Recent findings suggest that somewhere between Erin Brockovich and Blake Lively’s character in The Town, an exposed bra—particularly a colorful one—became a media shorthand for unsophistication, and more often than not, sexual availability. In simpler terms: tacky. Now, tacky is trending. Along with diamanté-encrusted snapbacks and sky-high peep-toes, straps in dynamic hues are stretched across the décolleté of It girls en masse. Printed cups are peeking out of the necklines. Some fashionistas are forgoing the shirt altogether. “There's a real cultural shift from polished perfection toward something more expressive, unpredictable, and a little undone,” claims Ocello. Or, perhaps a little more human.

Recent findings suggest that this certainly isn’t a trend for everyone. According to Shotton, Agent Provocateur’s biggest sellers are corsets—a style that was once also considered strictly lingerie, but has since sashayed into the light. One wonders if their popularity is a gateway to full bra-baring, especially as GLP drugs become more readily available, and women more comfortable in their bodies. But with a Blue Wave washing in Woke 3.0, how long do boobs—and their bold accessories—have left? In all seriousness, might we look back on this moment with the same disdain we once reflected on early-aughts raunch culture? Only social media will tell. In the meantime, I plan for my collection to keep growing.
