📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that equity crowdfunding lets you sell a stake in your business to your own customers, raising up to $5 million in 12 months through an SEC-registered crowdfunding portal. Before you launch, budget for the portal’s fee, financial statements that anyone can read, and the yearly reports you’ll owe your new investors.

Your customers don’t need to be wealthy to invest, so regulars and fans can become backers with a reason to send you business. Unlike backers of a preorder campaign, they get a financial stake, not a product.

Industry observers note that the catch is raising the money in public, often from hundreds of investors. Under the SEC’s Regulation Crowdfunding rules, your offering statement, called Form C, goes into a public SEC database with your financial statements. After the raise, you owe annual reports, filed there within 120 days of your fiscal year’s end and posted on your website, though they need no accountant’s review.

What you sell decides what you give up:.

In a recent development, pick a portal, such as Wefunder or StartEngine, and price the raise. Wefunder keeps 7.9% of what you raise plus up to $1,000 a year, and StartEngine estimates the accountant’s review and legal paperwork at $4,000 to $10,000. You need that review to raise more than $124,000 and an audit above $618,000, though a first crowdfunding raise can use a review up to $1,235,000.

Say a neighborhood brewery raises $400,000 from 1,100 customers, most investing a few hundred dollars, to open a second taproom. On Wefunder it pays $31,600 and needs a review, not an audit. The SEC lets you test interest before you file if you say no money is being accepted and no one is committed, so poll your customers before you hire an accountant.
