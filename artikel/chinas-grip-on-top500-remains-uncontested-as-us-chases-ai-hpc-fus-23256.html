China’s grip on Top500 remains uncontested as US chases AI-HPC fusion

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In the age of AI, the top spot on the bi-annual ranking is whoever willing to run HPL instead of LLMs.

A new Top500 list of supercomputers has dropped, but little has changed. China remains uncontested with the most powerful supercomputer on the biannual ranking, and the US is not even close behind.

The Middle Kingdom's LineShine super tops the list, boasting 2.2 exaFLOPS as measured by the High Performance Linpack (HPL) benchmark. That's roughly 22 percent ahead of Uncle Sam’s biggest iron, Laurence Livermore National Laboratory’s El Capitan.

Recent findings suggest that but LineShine may not be the most efficient. Using 42.2 megawatts, LineShine’s lead comes at the expense of a noticeably higher power bill; not that that makes much of a difference. Number one is number one, and to add insult to injury, the National Supercomputing Centre in Shenzhen (NSCS) did it in spite of US export controls denying them access to American GPUs. The entire super is powered by 45,360 LingKun LX2 CPUs, each with 304 Arm v9.2 cores clocking to 1.5 GHz.

According to recent reports, the US is unlikely to reclaim the number one spot any time soon, though not for lack of trying. Under the Genesis Mission announced late last year, the Department of Energy is on track to deploy at least nine upcoming supers. It just so happens that most of these will be smaller or AI-focused machines, with few truly large systems.

Sources indicate that oak Ridge National Laboratory’s (ORNL) Lux supercomputer, built in collaboration with HPE and AMD, is one such example. Delivering 220 petaFLOPS of double-precision grunt in HPL, it’s certainly a potent system. Just two years ago it would have been a top 10 supercomputer, but it''l have to settle for number 14 on this fall’s Top500 ranking.

Now, if you’re willing to turn the resolution slider down to FP4, peak performance jumps to somewhere north of 20 exaFLOPS. That’s fine for AI inference and even some training, but for running multi-physics sims on America's nuclear deterrent, the DoE has traditionally gone for something a bit bigger.

In a recent development, ORNL’s upcoming Discovery system will certainly have enough grunt to give LineShine the boot. Details on the HPE Cray based system remain thin, but we do know it’ll be powered by a combination of AMD Venice CPUs and Instinct MI430X GPUs.

Recent findings suggest that unlike most of the GPUs sold today, the MI430X hasn’t forsaken double-precision grunt for low-precision AI throughput. Each of these multi-die chips is estimated to deliver north of 200 teraFLOPS of FP64 matrix math. According to the DoE, Discovery will deliver somewhere between 3x to 5x the performance of Frontier, which would put its Rpeak somewhere between 5.1 and 8.5 exaFLOPS at FP64.

As impressive as that might sound, China has proven that once you take power out of the equation, all it takes to claim the number one spot is scale. And if there is one thing LineShine does well, it’s scale.

In GPU-accelerated systems like El Cap, you’re lucky to push HPL to within 70 percent of the system’s theoretical roofline. But LineShine isn’t GPU-accelerated. Sure, with 16 HBM stacks, the LX2 looks a lot like a GPU, but it's really just an enormous CPU with enough vector and matrix units to put more than a few modern GPUs to shame.

With enough of them – by which we mean 22,680 dual-socket nodes – NSCS catapulted China to the top of the Top500 for the first time in nearly a decade.

The US has no shortage of systems capable of eclipsing El Capitan or even LineShine’s performance if someone were motivated enough to get HPL running on them.

SpaceXAI’s 300 megawatt Colossus supercomputer in Memphis, Tennessee, boasts 200,000 Hopper GPUs each good for 34-67 (vector/matrix) teraFLOPS of FP64 grunt. Even assuming an abysmal 25% efficiency, the cluster should be more than capable of breaking three exaFLOPs in HPL.

It might need 10x the power of El Capitan, but the thing is, beyond winning points with the White House — a ship that has almost certainly sailed for Elon Musk after the DOGE fiasco — renting out the cluster to Anthropic to train and run Claude is a far more lucrative endeavor.

The same could be said of any number of AI clusters, from Meta’s Prometheus to OpenAI’s Starship.

It used to be that the systems to summit the Top500 were among the largest computers humanity has ever built. But this hasn’t been true in some time.

The US is starving for compute. HPL or even its sibling High-Performance Gradient Conjugate just aren’t the benchmarks to beat any longer.

The new battleground isn’t nuclear proliferation; it’s one of AI models, agents, and the means to harness them.

We’re not talking about tokens, LLMs, or chatbots, but the ability to harness these models to automate laboratories and formulate and test hypotheses long after the scientists they serve have gone home for dinner.

It’s why the US’s Genesis mission puts such a strong emphasis on AI.

AI-driven labs present an opportunity for researchers to iterate faster. Rather than running hundreds or thousands or simulations trying to find a needle in a haystack, a task for which a bigger iron is warranted, AI analysis and automation could focus that search.

In a recent development, high-performance computing (HPC), and by extension the Top500, will remain relevant as long as more FLOPS means more simulations per unit of time. But these systems can only move as fast as the men and women who operate them. ®.
