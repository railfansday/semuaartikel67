Latest Update: This Unexpected French Antique Is Suddenly Making a Comeback, and You’ve Probably Seen It Before

Industry observers note that the desire to preserve mementos from a wedding day transcends centuries and cultures. Today's brides still hang onto their wedding dresses, dry their bouquets, and spend a large portion of their budgets on photography. But in 19th- and 20th-century France, the art of preserving wedding-day memories took the form of globes de mariée, also known as marriage globes.

Industry observers note that these ornate, shrine-like displays primarily showcased the bride's head crown and other keepsakes from the big day, and the additional pieces inside symbolized the couple's dreams for their life together. The cloche protected these special items from dust back then and still today.

Here's a closer look at the history and allure of globes de mariée, plus tips for sourcing and styling these gorgeous antiques.

Chris Featherston is the senior vice president of product acquisition at Austin Auction Gallery, and he and his family have been dealing in French antiques for more than 30 years. In the last couple of years alone, they've sold more than 150 globes de Mariée for an average of $400 to $600 each.

According to recent reports, but this definitely wasn't always the case. Featherstone notes that the demand for these antiques has risen significantly in the past five years alone.

According to recent reports, "In the early days of our business, most of what we sold came from house clearances in France," he recalls. "These globes were ubiquitous. At that time, we felt lucky to be able to sell them for $30 to $40 each.".

Industry observers note that anyone with a strong appreciation for historic relics can see the appeal of these antiques. For instance, Mick and Paige Whitcomb have been collecting globes de mariée for nearly a decade, and they right now own 15. Their interest in these types of treasures is reflected in their careers. Mick creates sculptural lighting from architectural and industrial salvage dating back to the 1800s through his business, Artifacts by Nomad. Paige is a tintype photographer who captures staged scenes featuring antiques from the 1800s (including her globes de mariée) using a 19th-century large-format studio camera.

"While traveling in France for a wedding anniversary, we were first exposed to globes de mariée at the Marché aux Puces antique market in Paris," Mick indicates. "While we were initially caught by the beauty of the objects, after hearing their history and connection to French marriages, it felt like a beautiful way of commemorating our anniversary and trip to Europe.".

Industry observers note that due to the highly custom nature of globes de marriée, each one is a unique find. However, Featherston notes that most contain a few similar components. For starters, the bride's headpiece often rests on a red cushion, and it typically indicates off delicate beeswax orange blossoms that symbolize purity and virginity.

Most also contain small mirrors, which carried different meanings depending on the shape. For example, diamond-shaped mirrors symbolize how many children the couple wished to have, and rectangular mirrors stood for the length of time the couple knew each other before their engagement.

In a recent development, you'll also often find a wreath embellished with brass ornaments, usually flowers, birds and tree leaves. These symbolize love, a long-lasting marriage, and happiness.

"The globes de marriées integrate beautiful symbolism in every piece, and I find them fascinating," Paige states. "Each globe is so distinctive, and tells the story of the couple that created it. You can surmise the couple's hopes and dreams by minutiae tucked away behind the dome for safekeeping. I find very intimate details incorporated into the displays, such as a prayer card of a favorite saint, to photos of the bride and groom. I have even found hair and teeth, transforming the antique into a memento mori as well.".

Industry observers note that mick has noticed that sourcing these antiques is considerably more difficult in the United States than in Europe. But with a little patience and determination, it is possible.

"Because of the glass domes, I think fewer importers are brave enough to bring them over," he says. "Despite this, we have found the occasional gem in the wild." In the States, he's had the most luck sourcing them from design-forward antique dealers, online auction sites and even prop houses that have just wrapped up a period film project.

Since there is a constant demand for these pieces, Featherston tries to feature several in every sale at Austin Auction Gallery.

Globes de mariée can add a formal and traditional flair to any space, and they even mix well with more modern accents.

"One of my favorite ways to showcase the globes de mariée is to mix them with a piece of contemporary art," Paige indicates. "I love to create a harmony between our collections of antiques and artwork from artists that are living and working today.".

For example, Mick and Paige have styled two of their favorite globes de mariée with the piece "The Gardener" by working artist L.J. Eidolon. "I find collecting modern works that have a historic feeling to them helps create balance between the multiple centuries displayed together in one scene," Paige claims.
