This story, “Cold as Death,” appeared in the October 1955 issue of Outdoor Life.

There has never been anything along the upper Mississippi, before or since, like the Armistice Day storm of 1940.

In a recent development, it started out as a mild, cloudy morning with the temperature between 50 and 60. Fellows went duck hunting in shirt sleeves. The wind picked up velocity as the day went along, but not enough to get excited about — not until late afternoon. Then, with little warning, a full-scale prairie blizzard screamed in out of the northwest.

By dark the air was smoking with gale-driven snow, and the thermometer plunged near zero. Bitter winds blew all night. The weather experts stated afterward that gusts hit 80 miles an hour. The storm laid a track of death and destruction across six states; it pounded ships to pieces on the Great Lakes, blocked roads, isolated towns, paralyzed cities, and buried cars. Hundreds were marooned on highways. A crack streamliner was 27 hours late into Minneapolis. And when the morning of November 12 broke, 20 duck hunters lay dead, frozen in stiff, grotesque poses — scattered on mud bars, sand spits, and willow-grown islands of the Mississippi bottoms between Red Wing, Minnesota, and La Crosse, Wisconsin.

In a recent development, I was among the luckier ones. I spent that terrible night with two companions under an overturned skiff on a mud bar — wet from head to foot, without fire or heavy clothing, pants frozen to my legs, and kept alive only by the body warmth of the men who lay huddled on both sides of me. The storm cost me six weeks of agony in a hospital and half of my left foot. But at least I survived.

It has been revealed that I was 14 at the time, attending high school in my home town of Winona, Minnesota. We got out of classes at 11 o’clock because it was Armistice Day. I scurried for home, planning an afternoon of duck hunting.

Sources indicate that the wind was already fairly fresh and mother was uneasy. “The storm warnings are up,” she told me while getting my lunch. But I grew up in Winona and knew the river, and at that age, bad weather didn’t worry me. The only thing it meant to me was good shooting. Ducks had been scarce all fall and maybe a blow would bring ’em down. So I hurried through lunch, impatient to get in a blind.

While I was loading my gear in the car, my cousin, Bob Stephens, indicated up with a partner, Cal Wieczorek, and they decided to join me. Bob was 20, Cal about 19.

We drove to the Prairie Island spillway at the lower end of the Winona pool, where Dad kept his boat. It was a 16-footer, pointed at both ends, with a homemade bracket for an outboard. We decided to hunt a place known as the Firing Line, along the border of the Upper Mississippi Wildlife Refuge, three or four miles upstream. Hunters lined up there to intercept ducks flying in and out of the refuge. In stormy weather the pass-shooting was exceptionally good, and this looked like that kind of day.

The wind was blowing half a gale now and was beginning to bite. Ominous dark clouds raced overhead, and scuds of cold rain pelted down as we clamped our old three-horse outboard on the boat, cranked it up, and headed out into the choppy pool. The three of us were a heavy load, and the skiff made slow headway against the wind.

In a recent development, we didn’t give it a thought then, but we couldn’t have been more poorly prepared. I was wearing cotton clothes — pants, shirt, socks, and a jacket lighter than a hunting coat. I had overshoes over my leather shoes. Bob and Cal were dressed similarly, except for hip boots. Cal and I had gloves. Bob had a heavy raincoat, and I still believe that made the difference between living and dying for me.

Recent findings suggest that we had one book of matches and no pocketknife. I was carrying a 16 gauge single-barrel Winchester; the other two had pumps. Because we intended to pass-shoot, we took no decoys.

Backed up by a series of low dams, the Mississippi is two to three miles wide in that section, flooding its bottoms and forming open lakes, meandering channels, small islands, bars, and rush beds. We threaded our way through this maze, wallowing in the heavy seas. It took us 1½ hours to cover the distance to the Firing Line.

Industry observers note that the sky was full of ducks; they were barreling across the bottoms in crazy skeins, riding the wind, and turning and twisting below treetop height. It was plain they didn’t like the weather. Before the afternoon was over I actually swung at a greenhead with my gun as he hovered above my head, almost stationary, while bucking the gale.

It has been revealed that when we finally got to the Firing Line it was too crowded. The blinds were only about 25 yards apart and all occupied. Ducks were rocketing back and forth over the marsh and there was a steady barrage of shooting. We decided to find another spot.

It has been revealed that by the time we found it — on a willow-covered island with many rushes in front — we were beginning to lose interest. The wind was howling like a banshee, down off the high bluffs to the west, with sleet and fine snow in its teeth. We hunkered in the willows for almost an hour, killed four ducks, and decided we’d had enough. The cold was already bone-chilling, our hands were so stiff we could hardly handle our guns, and we were starting to worry about getting back to the landing.

Industry observers note that two strangers came by in a little duck boat; they told us they were scared. That settled it for us, but going home was easier mentioned than done. None of us knew exactly where we were, and when we tried to shove away from shore, the seas threatened to swamp us. We finally dragged the skiff to the lee side and managed to get it afloat and start the motor. But before going 200 yards we ran aground on a mud bar, which we couldn’t skirt because of the wind and seas. We dragged the boat across the bar and started on again.

Sources indicate that we did that about four times before finding ourselves by a big open slough and admitting we were lost. It was almost dark now and snowing so hard we couldn’t see 100 feet. We couldn’t even look into the wind — the storm cut our eyeballs like a sandblast. And the slough was a welter of dirty foaming water in which our skiff probably wouldn’t last more than a minute.

It has been revealed that while we debated what to do, another boat loomed out of the stormy dusk, riding low with four hunters. Two of them were only a little older than I — one from my home room at school. The others were men who had hunted the river bottoms all their lives. When they mentioned they knew the way to Prairie Island and could make it without swamping, we decided to follow.

It has been revealed that but I couldn’t get the outboard started. Bob and Cal rowed, handicapped by cold-numbed hands, while I tinkered with it. We managed to keep the other boat in sight for a few minutes. Then, pitching and rolling, it disappeared and we didn’t see it again. When our frantic yells brought no answer we realized we were again on our own. It was days before we learned what happened to the other party. Their skiff had capsized when we lost sight of it. Three of the hunters died there, standing in the icy water, not knowing which way to go to dry land. By some miracle one of the boys survived the night and rescue parties found him on a near-by island at daybreak.

According to recent reports, only a few minutes after we lost that boat we were washed ashore on a sand spit. Bob and Cal leaped clear as we grounded and escaped a wetting. I was in the stern of the skiff, working on the outboard — right in the path of a wicked sea that smashed over us the instant we touched bottom.

Sources indicate that I can’t describe the shock that icy dunking gave me. I clawed my way out of the swamped boat, and the wind cut like a braided whip. My legs wouldn’t hold me up, so I crawled across the mud until I was beyond reach of the waves and lay there, cold and weak.

It has been revealed that cal and Bob dragged the boat up on dry land, turned it over, and propped one side up to make a shelter. When they finished I crawled under it. My pants and jacket crackled as I moved. They had frozen stiff in the few minutes I lay exposed to the wind.

According to recent reports, we still had only a vague idea of our location, but we knew that large open lake, which we couldn’t cross in our puny skiff, lay between us and any landing. We didn’t talk much about it; we didn’t need to. Live or die, we’d have to spend the night where we were.

Bob and Cal did everything possible. They gathered rushes and put them under the boat to keep me off the wet mud. Then they searched desperately for firewood, but there wasn’t so much as a scrub willow. They finally found a few pieces of water-logged driftwood, heaped them in a pyramid, and turned the outboard motor up to drain gas on it — a risky thing to do, except in a desperate emergency like ours. But the screaming wind whipped the gas into nowhere as fast as it ran out of the tank. They struck one match after another without getting a spark of fire.

The last match flared and died in the darkness, and they crawled under the boat and lay down on the wet rushes, one on each side of me. They spread their hunting coats over us and laid Bob’s raincoat on top. The raincoat kept the worst of the wind out and probably saved me from freezing.

According to recent reports, we had little sense of time and hardly talked. Bob told me afterward that I spoke only two or three times during the night, and then I worried about the anxiety my parents must be feeling.

I shivered for what seemed like hours, with my hands wedged between my thighs, since that seemed to be the warmest place on my body. I prayed silently. I didn’t think much about dying. I believed we’d get through the night somehow and in the morning find our way home. I realized finally that all feeling was gone in my feet; they were wooden stumps, no longer a part of me. I must have dozed, for I recall waking up and feeling warm, but that lasted only a minute or two and then I was shivering again.

The thing I remember best was the unending scream of the wind. It never stopped raging — searching under the overturned boat, wailing, screeching, terribly alive, terribly cold.

It has been revealed that sometime in the night Bob remembered that he had two apples and two sandwiches in his hunting coat. They were frozen hard, but we gnawed at them and they seemed to help.

In a recent development, the sound of distant gunshots reached us several times, and once we thought we heard faint, far-off yells for help. But our own guns were so crusted with ice that we couldn’t fire them, and it was useless to shout.

Recent findings suggest that we knew we weren’t the only hunters lost on the river bottom. Others were enduring the same torture. And we felt sure there must be rescue parties trying to find us, but we didn’t think we could count on them while the storm continued.

In a recent development, it was days before we learned all that happened that hard night. Rescue efforts began at dark, and men fought the storm and seas all night in skiffs and powerboats. Some even waded the sloughs to bring lost men to shore. It wasn’t a night for small craft, and no one will ever know how many boats were overturned, swamped, and driven back. But the search went on, hour after hour, heedless of the wind, cold, and snow.

The night was an awful ordeal for the hunters. One party huddled on a marshy island beside a fire started with dead grass and fed with more than 40 cedar decoys. Gas from an outboard was used to rekindle the blaze each time it died down. Another group marched, jumped, and sparred around a fire built from their boats. Still another found a patch of timber, got a fire going, and then shot dead branches off trees for fuel.

Industry observers note that they were the lucky ones; others didn’t fare so well. Two died under an overturned boat, unable to see a bonfire 400 yards away. Two more reached shore along a railroad embankment, crawled halfway up the bank, fell and froze to death, too weak to walk to safety. Others gave up, lay down where they were, without fire or shelter, and died quickly. A few drowned. Most froze. When the last of the living were rescued, the grim search for bodies continued. It was three days before all the missing were accounted for and the final toll of 20 dead was tallied.

Toward morning the air cleared enough to let me glimpse lights on the Winona dam, a few miles away. They glimmered through the driving snow, tantalizing and mocking us, but at least we knew now which direction to go when daylight came. About that same time Bob remarked that his hands were frozen and he’d probably lose them. He mentioned it quietly, as if it didn’t really matter.

Recent findings suggest that at first light the wind seemed to lose its gale force. It was still blowing hard, but when we studied the lake in the gray light of morning, we decided our skiff might weather the seas.

We were in bad shape. Cal and Bob could barely stand up; I needed help to get on my feet. Our hands were stiff and useless. The spit where we spent the night was rimmed with a 50-foot belt of ice that made launching our boat a tough job. But we didn’t have much choice. We couldn’t live through the day on that bleak mud bar, and we had no way of knowing when help would come.

It has been revealed that I crawled into the boat. Bob and Cal inched it off the bar, broke a path in the ice to open water, and rowed slowly into the lake. Their hands, however, were too numb for the oars and they lost control when the full force of wind and waves hit us.

Sources indicate that spray froze to the skiff, coating it with ice. My clothes were as stiff as armor, my legs lost all feeling, and I no longer minded the wind. In that condition, with the boat growing ever heavier from its load of ice, we were swept across the Winona pool to the Wisconsin side.

That should have ended things, but there was still more torment ahead. The marsh had frozen and we grounded on the fresh ice, 100 yards offshore. The ice gouged a big hole in the bow of the skiff, but it wouldn’t bear a man’s weight. Luckily the wind drove us up until the hole was above water, and there we sat with no way to get to shore.

Sources indicate that it was there a search plane found us an hour later. Part of the feverish search that was under way for 50 miles up and down the river, that plane — a Cub trainer piloted by Max Conrad of Winona — had been in the air since shortly after daylight. It was shuttling across the bottoms, spotting lost hunters, empty boats, deserted gear and stranded dogs, and dropping sandwiches, whisky, matches, and cigarettes. It even dipped low enough to shout encouragement to the men as they were found. Nobody in the rescue teams did a better job.

It has been revealed that conrad flew low over us, wagging his wings, and hollered a message we couldn’t understand. Then he was gone, but before long we heard the steady throb of a powerboat and saw the Army Engineers’ launch, Chippewa.

According to recent reports, she couldn’t reach us, for we were out of the river channel in shallow water. It took the crew almost an hour to break ice and get to us with a skiff. Before they were through, my dad and a friend of his, who had been looking for us all night, demonstrated up in a powerful outboard. They carried me to the launch, peeled off my shoes and frozen clothing, and for all I was only 14, gave me a man-size slug of whisky. I took it like a man, too, and never knew I’d had it.

Bob spent a week in a Winona hospital, but his frozen hands came through all right. Cal went home that same afternoon. I had a long bout with gangrene and lost part of a foot. I also lost a lot of weight, dropping from 143 to 85 pounds during my recovery. Christmas was less than a week away when I left the hospital, and I guess that was the best Christmas I ever had.

According to recent reports, I’m a teacher in La Crosse now and I still hunt ducks on the Mississippi bottoms whenever I get the chance. But, I wear special boots, even on the warmest days, and take along an emergency kit that contains wool socks, gloves, down-filled hat, compass, knife, matches in watertight container, and a small flare gun. I never go without it.

There are too many mistakes in this world to make the same one twice, and any time I’m tempted to be careless, I remember how the wind sounded that awful November night.
