Tech Report: A virus found in chimpanzee DNA explains why humans developed much larger and smarter brains

🌐 Source: Earth • 📅 17 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 7 min read (1440 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways

  • Sources indicate that humans and chimpanzees share more than 96 percent of their protein-coding DNA.
  • Industry observers note that one such difference was caused by a virus.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

An ancient virus in chimpanzee DNA accidentally shuts off a crucial brain-building gene. Humans never caught this virus.

Sources indicate that humans and chimpanzees share more than 96 percent of their protein-coding DNA. So whatever sets our brains apart must lie in that remainder, or in how shared genes are switched on and off.

Industry observers note that one such difference was caused by a virus. Millions of years ago, this virus infiltrated chimpanzee ancestors and left a copy of itself inside a gene.

The viral copy keeps the gene switched off in chimpanzees to this day, and as it turns out, that particular gene is very important. The modern human genome holds no copy of the virus.

The same gene is active in the developing human brain, where it appears to help young neurons grow the branches they use to connect.

Some of what separates the two brains, then, may come down to a gene that chimpanzees lost the use of rather than one humans gained.

Johan Jakobsson, a professor of molecular neurogenetics at Lund University in Sweden, led the work with Patricia Gerdes, a postdoctoral researcher in the university’s Molecular Neurogenetics group.

Geneticists at the University of Washington, Uppsala University and the University of Gothenburg were part of the team.

They worked with brain organoids, clumps of tissue grown from stem cells that copy a few features of a brain in its first weeks.

Industry observers note that an organoid is not a brain, and the study notes nothing about how chimpanzees or humans think. What it shows is which genes are active in each species, and where one difference came from.

Retroviruses copy their genetic material into the DNA of the cells they infect. When one infects a sperm or egg cell, the copy is inherited, and over millions of years the leftovers accumulate.

Old viral remnants make up at least 8 percent of both the human and the chimpanzee genomes.

The virus here is one that chimpanzees carry and humans don’t. Scientists call it PTERV1. It infected the ancestors of African apes roughly 5 to 7 million years ago, around the time the human and chimpanzee lines split.

Sources indicate that the virus then spread through the chimpanzee lineage between about 1.5 and 5 million years ago. Gorillas and bonobos carry copies too.

The human genome has none. A human protein that blocks PTERV1 from infecting human cells may be part of the reason.

Jakobsson’s team counted 158 copies of the virus in the chimpanzee genome, each between about 2,000 and 14,000 DNA letters long.

A retrovirus is doing the same thing right now in wild koalas in Australia, inserting itself into their inherited DNA and raising their rates of cancer.

Jakobsson said the fresh findings show that a virus can change a DNA sequence across an entire population.

Early chimpanzee brain tissue is almost impossible to study directly, so the team grew it.

Starting from stem cells from two humans and two chimpanzees, they grew organoids under near-identical conditions for 15 days.

By then each one had formed the rosettes of dividing cells typical of early brain tissue, and the mix of cell types was similar in the two species.

In an interview with Earth.com, Gerdes called the organoids a key technology that made the study possible.

“This 3D cell culture model enabled us to investigate certain aspects of early human and chimpanzee brain development in a dish in our lab,” she wrote.

According to recent reports, then the team read the chemical tags on the DNA. Cells silence stretches of DNA by attaching small chemical tags called methyl groups, a process known as DNA methylation.

In both the stem cells and the organoids, nearly every one of the 158 viral copies was completely covered in these tags.

But methylation can spread from a viral copy into the DNA around it. So the team asked a second question: did any of the 158 silenced copies also silence a chimpanzee gene nearby?

Forty-five genes that exist in both species carry a viral copy entirely inside them in chimpanzees. In the organoids, 10 of the 45 behaved differently in the two species.

It has been revealed that the clearest case was a gene called LINC00662. Human organoids produced it in large amounts, and chimpanzee organoids didn’t produce it at all.

“The gene was strongly expressed in the human organoids, but not at all in the chimpanzee organoids,” Gerdes said.

Industry observers note that LINC00662 doesn’t code for a protein; its RNA does the work directly. In the chimpanzee version of the gene, a viral copy 6,915 DNA letters long is embedded near the gene’s start. Otherwise the two species’ versions are nearly identical.

In chimpanzee cells the viral copy was heavily tagged with methyl groups, and so was the DNA on either side of it, including the stretch that switches the gene on. In human cells the same region carried few tags.

Only chimpanzees and bonobos have this insertion, so it arrived after their line split from ours.

Gorillas and macaques lack the whole region of DNA that contains the gene, and orangutans carry a different piece of mobile DNA beside it.

In cells from eight more chimpanzees, and from bonobos, orangutans and macaques, the gene was silent, so humans may be the only species with a working copy.

A silent gene beside a methylated virus could still be a coincidence, so the team removed the virus.

Using CRISPR, a gene-editing tool that cuts DNA at chosen spots, they snipped the roughly 7,000-letter viral copy out of chimpanzee stem cells. With the virus gone, the chimpanzee cells started producing LINC00662.

“This shows that it is in fact the viral insertion that is responsible for the gene being switched off in chimpanzees,” Gerdes said.

For Jakobsson, the key finding is that a repetitive piece of DNA, in this case a leftover virus, helps control a gene that matters for brain development.

According to recent reports, he sees a wider lesson in it. “This demonstrates that this part of the genome likely played an notable role throughout brain evolution and maybe also for brain disorders,” he told Earth.com.

So what does LINC00662 do in a human? In human brain tissue, the gene is most active about 10 to 12 weeks after conception, and its activity declines through adulthood.

Inside cells, the RNA stays almost entirely outside the nucleus, where the team found it bound to proteins that build a neuron’s internal scaffolding and extend its branches.

To test its job, the team silenced LINC00662 in human organoids and grew them for 15 days and for 30 days. The silenced organoids formed normally, with the same mix of cell types as the controls. At day 30, one place looked different: the zone where newly committed neurons send out their first branches.

Industry observers note that there, a marker of those growing branches was weaker in the silenced organoids than in the controls. The gap was too large to be due to chance.

The team also sequenced nearly 120,000 cell nuclei from 85 organoids. Genes that drive the growth of axons and dendrites, the long and short branches neurons use to send and receive signals, were more active in the silenced organoids, not less.

The team reads that as the cells compensating for branches that aren’t forming properly.

It has been revealed that everything here happened in lab-grown tissue a few weeks old, from two individuals of each species. The authors wrote that an organoid can’t fully capture what a human-specific gene does in a whole brain.

The authors still need to work out how methylation on the viral copy reaches the gene’s own switch. And no one has identified which of the cell’s defense proteins recognizes PTERV1 and tags it in the first place.

Why silencing the gene makes the branch-building genes more active is also unexplained.

LINC00662 is unlikely to be the only gene affected. Of the 10 genes that differed between the species and carry a viral copy, one helps nerve cells receive signals and has been linked to schizophrenia.

Jakobsson wrote that he believes elements like these play a role in human disease, and he pointed to a recent paper on Parkinson’s disease from his group .

The team plans to look next at whether other viral remnants changed genes the same way during brain evolution, and whether any of them are linked to brain disorders today.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.
