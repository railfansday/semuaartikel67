El Niño Rains Paint the Atacama

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Winter precipitation helped wake dormant seeds in northern Chile, carpeting the desert with what may be the region’s most extensive flowering of the satellite era.

In a recent development, parts of the Atacama Desert in northern Chile, normally one of the driest places on Earth, turned red-purple, green, and white in austral spring 2026 following an unusually wet winter. The rain woke seeds lying dormant in the soil, setting off what scientists say may be the most extensive desierto florido, or “flowering desert,” of the satellite era.

Industry observers note that the rains came as El Niño was strengthening in the tropical Pacific. During an El Niño, the subtropical Pacific high that normally keeps the region dry weakens, while a blocking high tends to form in the South Pacific near the tip of South America. Together, these shifts push the Southern Hemisphere storm track equatorward.

Beginning in mid-July, a series of storm fronts fed by an atmospheric river brought one of the heaviest July rain events in decades to a long stretch of Chile, from the Atacama region south to Los Lagos, according to Chile’s national weather service (Dirección Meteorológica de Chile). In August, back-to-back winter storms blanketed parts of the Atacama Desert in rare snow.

Sources indicate that the spring blooms that followed are visible in these satellite image pairs, which show the desert at different stages of flowering on September 14 (left) and on October 24 (right). The OLI (Operational Land Imager) on Landsat 8 acquired the September images, and the OLI on Landsat 9 acquired the October images.

It has been revealed that the pair above shows a part of the desert about 30 kilometers (20 miles) southwest of Copiapó, near the eastern sector of Desierto Florido National Park. The red-purple areas prominent in the October 24 scene are vast blooms of pata de guanaco, most likely Cistanthe longiscapa or another Cistanthe species, according to Roberto Chávez, a remote sensing scientist at Pontificia Universidad Católica de Valparaíso.

Chávez and colleagues photographed the Cistanthe blooms (above) in the area that same day. They were in the field to study the bloom with satellite data, drones, and digital cameras. The project aims to use these data to map how the bloom’s biodiversity changes across space and over time, to inform land-use planners and conservation managers.

Industry observers note that the pair below reveals another part of the blooming desert, about 50 kilometers (30 miles) southwest of Copiapó. It lies within a priority area of the desert cited for its biological significance, including the flowering landscape.

Recent findings suggest that cistanthe is abundant here in the October view, but the September scene (left) reveals another desert beauty. The green areas capped with white are blooms of suspiros, or Nolana, probably Nolana baccata, according to Andres Moreira-Muñoz, a scientist at Pontificia Universidad Católica de Valparaíso who studies plant geography. The researchers photographed the Nolana-covered hillsides below on September 24, 2026.

Satellite data have been key to understanding the Atacama’s blooms. A 2014 study used Landsat observations to map where flowering and fog-fed plants grow around the Copiapó dune field. And a 2019 study used NASA’s long-term vegetation record to identify 13 Atacama Desert blooms between 1981 and 2015.

Compared with those past blooms, the 2026 event stands out. “This instance of the ‘blooming desert’ phenomenon appears to be the most significant to date—in terms of its extent, intensity, and duration,” Moreira-Muñoz said, “at least since we began using remote sensing.”.
