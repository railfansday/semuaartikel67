Virtual reality can make you feel sick even when the scene around you stays still

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a five-minute VR experiment, watching a single moving ball increased nausea, eye discomfort, and disorientation as the eyes aimed and focused at different distances.

Sources indicate that volunteers spent five minutes in a virtual reality headset watching a speckled ball glide toward them and away against a black background. Afterward, they reported more nausea, eye discomfort, and disorientation than before.

The ball was designed to put two jobs the eyes normally do together at odds.

As it appeared to move closer, the volunteers’ eyes turned inward to follow it, but the headset kept the image in focus at a fixed distance.

A cap of 64 electrodes recorded their brain activity shifting while it happened.

The research was conducted by Mattie Johnson, a computer science doctoral student at the University of Maryland.

Amitabh Varshney, a computer science professor and dean at the university, led the study, and Sujal Bista of the University of Maryland Institute for Health Computing was a co-author.

The team worked with the Food and Drug Administration and Sony on the study.

It has been revealed that the team is careful about what the electrode readings mean. The scene used for comparison differed from the test scene in more than one way, and the authors can’t fully rule out eye and facial muscle activity.

So the paper calls the brain changes candidate correlates, not a signature of this kind of sickness.

Look at a real object and your eyes do two things at once: they turn inward to aim at it, and their lenses adjust to bring it into focus.

The two distances agree. A headset breaks the agreement. Its screens sit at one fixed focal distance while the 3D picture asks the eyes to aim nearer or farther.

In a recent development, the Meta Quest 2 used here has its focal plane about 4.3 feet (1.3 meters) in front of the eyes. The team measured that with a camera and a test pattern.

Within about half a diopter of that distance the mismatch is mild. Outside it, discomfort builds.

So the team indicated volunteers a black-and-white speckled ball on a black background. It glided from 11 inches (0.275 meters) out to 4.7 feet (1.425 meters) and back, one round trip every 3.9 seconds.

The ball grew as it receded and shrank as it approached, so it always looked the same size.

The researchers designed the scene to reduce the feeling that viewers themselves were moving while still making their eyes aim nearer and farther.

Forty people, 65% of them men and nearly all between 18 and 30, took part.

Recent findings suggest that the researchers analyzed brain recordings from 38 people and complete questionnaires from 38 people. Each analysis excluded a different pair of participants.

Each person sat through five minutes with eyes closed, five minutes looking at a headset’s static home screen, and then five minutes of the moving ball.

Recent findings suggest that before and after the ball, they filled out a 1993 simulator sickness questionnaire, the SSQ. It scores nausea, eye-related symptoms, and disorientation.

All three scores rose, and the total score rose by what statisticians call a large effect. Men and women both reported more symptoms, though men’s disorientation scores didn’t rise by a reliable amount.

Most participants described themselves as not at all or only slightly prone to this kind of sickness.

Recent findings suggest that the electrode cap sampled the scalp 250 times a second. Software stripped out blinks and muscle noise as best it could.

The team compared 50 seconds of brain activity near the end of the home-screen baseline with 50 seconds near the end of the moving-ball scene.

Industry observers note that in clusters of activity toward the front of the head, power in the fast beta and gamma brain waves fell. In clusters toward the back, power in the slower theta waves fell.

“In the frontal lobe, we found a statistically significant decrease in high brain wave frequencies during cybersickness, and in the occipital lobe we saw a statistically significant decrease in low brain wave frequencies,” Johnson stated.

“These changes in what we call EEG clusters could be useful predictors of a cybersickness experience.”.

The pattern is not the one Varshney’s group found in 2022 for the motion kind of virtual reality sickness, where slower waves grew stronger over the center of the head.

The studies found different patterns, but the researchers still don’t know whether the latest pattern reflects sickness, differences between the scenes, or both.

The baseline scene and the ball differed in brightness, content, and layout. Volunteers were told where to look during the ball but not during the baseline.

In a recent development, some of the drop in slow waves at the back of the head may simply be visual processing of a different picture. The front of the head is also where eye movements leak into recordings most.

The team didn’t record whether participants wore glasses or contacts, and nearly everyone was under 30.

Sources indicate that the authors also wrote that the headset’s three fixed lens-spacing settings fit a larger share of women than men poorly. That could add to symptoms in ways the study can’t separate.

Whether the brain changes track the sickness itself, the change of scenery, or both is the question the authors leave open.

Settling it would need a baseline scene matched to the test scene, which the authors recommend. It would also need eye tracking to remove the eyes’ own electrical traces, which Johnson’s team looks to add.

The goal beyond that is a headset that detects the pattern and adjusts the scene.

“Ultimately we want to inform technology to mitigate symptoms in real time, improving user comfort and productivity over extended periods of immersion,” Johnson noted.

Bista put it as a shift in who gets asked.

“The SSQ still has its uses, but now we have the tools to go from asking participants how they’re feeling to asking the brain and body what’s really going on,” he noted.

The full study was published in the journal Virtual Reality.
