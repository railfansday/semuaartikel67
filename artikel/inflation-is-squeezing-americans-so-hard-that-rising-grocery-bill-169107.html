Inflation is squeezing Americans so hard that rising grocery bills are now a top reason Black Friday shoppers are cutting back

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Black Friday has arrived, and the National Retail Federation expects about 126 million Americans to shop today, part of an estimated 182.7 million who strategy to shop over the Thanksgiving holiday weekend.

But many of those shoppers are heading out with less to spend, with the cost of groceries being a main reason why. Among Black Friday shoppers who are cutting back this year, 68% point to rising grocery costs, up from 57% in 2025, according to a fresh survey from market research firm Drive Research. Rising living costs are still the top reason, cited by 76%, up from 70%, while 61% blame inflation, up from 54%.

Black Friday budgets are shrinking as a result. Some 41% of Black Friday shoppers expect to spend less on holiday shopping than they did last year, up from 31% in 2025. The share who typically spend $1,000 or more fell to 18% from 24%, and 34% said they feel pressured to spend more on gifts than they can comfortably afford.

Everyday essentials are also on shopping lists. Household necessities like groceries and cleaning supplies are among the planned purchases for 38% of in-person Black Friday shoppers and 31% of those shopping online. Drive Research surveyed 1,405 U.S. adults from Oct. 6 to Oct. 9, and its Black Friday findings are based on the 1,025 respondents who initiative to shop over the weekend.

Grocery prices rose 0.4% in October, the biggest monthly increase since April, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, with beef up 0.9%. Over the 12 months through September, food-at-home prices climbed 1.8%, while overall consumer prices rose 3.7%. Gas has driven much of that broader increase: gasoline prices were up 37.4% from a year earlier in October, before seasonal adjustment. The BLS couldn’t publish most year-over-year comparisons for October because it didn’t collect price data in October 2025 during the government shutdown.

It has been revealed that federal Reserve officials have taken notice. Michelle Bowman, the Fed’s vice chair for supervision, said in a Nov. 10 speech the labor market had improved more than she expected this year, but that “our progress on lowering inflation has disappointed.” The Fed held its benchmark rate in a range of 3.75% to 4% at its October meeting.

Walmart remains the top in-store Black Friday destination, with 71% of in-person shoppers planning a visit—the same share for the third straight year—according to Drive Research. Emily Rodgers, the company’s director of marketing, stated when budgets are tight, “shoppers stick with the stores they already trust to have low prices.”.

Walmart CEO John Furner noted last week rising diesel and transportation costs have added about $2 billion in expenses this year, which could make it harder to keep prices low through the holidays.

“We will do everything we can in terms of efficiency,” Furner said on the firm’s post-earnings call.

Shoppers also aren’t sure the deals are real. Some 64% of Black Friday shoppers believe retailers raise prices ahead of the sales so the discounts aren’t genuine, and 72% remarked the deals aren’t nearly as good as they used to be, Drive Research found.

It has been revealed that “Black Friday used to sell itself on the date alone. Now shoppers show up with a price tracker in one hand and a healthy dose of doubt in the other,” Rodgers noted. “The retailers who win will be the ones who show their math: what the item sold for last month and what the customer actually saves today.”.

Most consumers are trying to hold the line. Some 82% are taking steps to manage holiday costs, including buying less expensive gifts (25%), cutting back in other areas (24%) and buying for fewer people (20%), according to NRF’s survey of 7,956 adults.
