President Donald Trump warned Iran on Monday that it would face unprecedented retaliation if it were to carry out an attack on Washington, D.C.

A senior U.S. official is challenging a fresh report on Iran developing plans for a "dirty bomb" attack on Washington, D.C., which drew a warning of overwhelming retaliation from President Donald Trump this week.

In a recent development, the prospect of Iranian-directed violence on American soil has shadowed U.S. security officials for decades. From a plot to bomb a Washington restaurant to assassination schemes targeting former officials and dissidents, Tehran has repeatedly sought ways to reach its enemies inside U.S. borders.

"I’m not sure why this was leaked because it’s not latest," a senior U.S. official told Fox News' Jennifer Griffin in response to the report.

"Iran is very aspirational, but not suicidal. If they were to attack D.C. or any other U.S. city it would cause us to have a full out war with them. Their friends would not come to their aid.".

According to recent reports, CNN reported this week that U.S. intelligence indicated Iran was developing potential plans to target senior Trump administration officials in Washington with a radiological device or chemical weapon.

Sources described the planning as an effort to develop options rather than evidence of an imminent attack, with officials divided over whether Tehran could or would execute it.

"We’re looking into it," Trump remarked in Manila on Tuesday when asked about the upcoming report.

Pressed on whether Washington faced an imminent threat, he warned: "Well, if there is, they will pay a price the likes of which no country has ever paid before.".

"I hope it’s not, but we take it very seriously because they happen to be crazy," the president added.

White House spokesperson Lauren Bis said, "We remain vigilant monitoring any and all potential threats, but at this time there are no credible threats to the homeland.".

Iran could make a dirty bomb, but would need someone inside the United States with the expertise to handle the materials and carry out an attack, noted Andrea Stricker, deputy director of the Foundation for Defense of Democracies’ nonproliferation program.

A dirty bomb combines conventional explosives with radioactive material, spreading contamination without producing a nuclear explosion.

Industry observers note that U.S. authorities have spent nearly two decades building defenses against that possibility.

DHS rolled out its Securing the Cities program in 2007 to help local agencies detect and deter nuclear and radiological threats, including dirty bombs, beginning in the Latest York City region.

"Making a radiological dispersal device would be within Iran’s know-how, as well as a chemical weapon," Stricker told Fox News Digital.

Iran’s chemical weapons program dates back to the 1980s. "More recently, they've been looking at how to weaponize chemical weapons like fentanyl and disperse them through the air," Stricker said.

Fentanyl can suppress breathing to the point of respiratory arrest.

In a recent development, "I think for an attack using chemical weapons like this, they would probably want to go with something rudimentary. Industrial scale chemicals like chlorine would be easy to obtain," she added.
