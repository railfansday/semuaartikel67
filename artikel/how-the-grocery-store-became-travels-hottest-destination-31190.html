How the Grocery Store Became Travel’s Hottest Destination

It has been revealed that last winter, for a friend’s birthday, a large group of us rented a chalet in Courchevel, France. There were the usual Alpine excesses—elaborate supper-club dinners, après-ski that stretched well into the evening—but, as with any ski holiday, there came a point when somebody had to buy groceries. This responsibility fell to me. I approached the task with all the enthusiasm I usually have for making my bed or taking out the garbage.

Industry observers note that I ended up at Sherpa Supermarché, an unfussy grocery chain with 116 locations across the mountains of France. I expected to be in and out in five minutes. Instead, I found myself standing before a cheese section that puts the Library of Congress to shame—there were varieties of Comté I had never heard of, some aged longer than certain marriages, others washed in wine or wrapped in herbs. The chocolate section was equally impressive. I had not known there were so many ways to make chocolate, or that I would, like a kid with eyes bigger than his stomach, start shoving them by the armful into my basket.

According to recent reports, sherpa wasn’t particularly fancy or large. It was an ordinary shop serving ordinary people, only its definition of “ordinary” happened to feel particularly French, and unlike anything we have back home. I returned to the chalet with way more chocolate and cheese than anyone had requested, and the sense that I had just experienced something meaningful.

Apparently, I’m not alone. According to Hilton’s 2026 Travel Trends Report, 77 percent of travelers said they enjoy visiting grocery stores abroad. Grocery store tourism has become one of travel’s more unexpected obsessions in recent years, with visitors increasingly building supermarket stops into itineraries once reserved for museums and monuments.

Looking back on my travels, the grocery store was always there. I’ll never forget my horror when I went to an IGA in Montréal for the first time and learned that Canadian potato chip flavors are vastly superior to our own. (If you ever find yourself north of the border, All-Dressed, Ketchup, and Poulet Rôti-flavored chips are a must.) Or on a trip to Seoul, where Lotte Mart introduced me to a wide variety of truly restaurant-quality instant noodles and elaborate packaged desserts. I’ve heard similar stories about Major C, a grocery chain in Thailand, which even offers hands-on workshops where visitors can indulge in tastings of Thai curry pastes. Doesn’t that feel a step above the free samples at Costco?

Grocery stores shape the way you see a destination. One friend keeps telling me about a to-die-for babka from Panama City’s Super K, billed as the world’s largest kosher supermarket. Would I necessarily think of kosher food in a destination best known for its beaches and tropical rainforests? Probably not. But that’s precisely the point: a supermarket can reveal an entirely different side of a place.

We’re seeing grocery store tourism get increasingly luxe, too. In Los Angeles, Erewhon has become a destination in its own right, where visitors can observe the city’s wellness aristocracy paying $22 for a celebrity-endorsed smoothie. New York City’s Meadow Lane opened in Tribeca last year with beautifully packaged pantry staples and artisanal chicken fingers so good that you don’t care about the $17 price tag. And in Tokyo, Kayanoya, a Japanese brand known for its hard-to-find seasonings and traditional ingredients, just opened a new flagship in Ginza designed by Kengo Kumba. It honestly looks about as luxe and appealing as a Prada boutique. Grocery tourism is increasingly becoming something you experience, not just a utilitarian shopping stop.

But the alpha and omega of grocery store tourism remains French butter. For years, bringing home a few blocks of Maison Bordier was the ultimate edible souvenir from Paris, and La Grande Épicerie makes the process seamless, vacuum-sealing purchases for the journey home. (I’ve also learned from TikTok that you can take your butter back to your hotel, ask for it to be frozen overnight, and, by the time you land in the States, it should be just about thawed.) And this year, Paris got another dedicated butter destination in Le Butter Shop, a retailer within walking distance, specializing in du beurre and all its regional varieties.

Which brings me back to the cheese case in Courchevel. I went in expecting a quick chore and came out with a better sense of the town than I’d gotten from any of its chi-chi, Champagne-drenched supper clubs. The monuments and must-dos tell one side of a place’s story, but the supermarkets show you another. I’ll be packing an extra bag for butter next time I’m in Paris.
