We remember routes, sounds, thresholds, and landmarks, often without knowing their exact positions or dimensions. Reconstructing Architecture Through Sound.

For years, independent investigators could study Saydnaya Prison from the outside, including through satellite imagery, but had no access to its interior. Inside the military prison north of Damascus, much of what could be reconstructed depended on the memories of people who had been held there.

In 2016, Forensic Architecture worked with Amnesty International to reconstruct the prison from the memories of five former detainees interviewed in Istanbul. Starting with a rough model based on satellite images and earlier written testimony, researchers developed the interior as survivors described cells, stairwells, and doors. The witnesses explored the rooms at eye level, corrected their proportions, and placed objects where they remembered them. Moving through the model sometimes brought back details they had never mentioned.

Mapping the Divided Memories of a City.

In a recent development, at Saydnaya, different recollections helped reconstruct the same building. In Famagusta, Cyprus, entire communities had developed different understandings of the same city.

According to recent reports, at Saydnaya, different recollections helped reconstruct the same building. In Famagusta, Cyprus, entire communities had developed different understandings of the same city.

The 1974 conflict displaced much of Famagusta's Greek Cypriot population and left Varosha, a coastal district, under Turkish military control. Former residents retained memories of neighborhoods they could no longer enter, while those who remained or arrived later experienced a city shaped by territorial divisions. Both groups knew the city, but their memories described different urban landscapes.

Led by architect and urbanist Socrates Stratis, Hands-on Famagusta explored these divided perceptions through what the team described as split mental maps. The project developed a series of counter-mapping tools, including large isometric drawings, an interactive digital platform, and a transportable physical model. These representations were used in workshops, public presentations, and discussions involving participants from across the Cypriot divide.

Sources indicate that the drawings brought together several centuries of occupation. Beneath the former courthouse lay a medieval necropolis with sixteen burials dating from the 7th to 9th centuries. The site had also accommodated the Fusina Gardens and the Palau de Belles Arts, an exhibition building constructed for the 1888 Universal Exposition. By superimposing the outlines of these different occupations onto the same surface, the installation made their dimensions and spatial relationships visible simultaneously. Visitors could move between the footprints of structures that had occupied the site at different moments, comparing their scales and positions against the surrounding urban fabric.
