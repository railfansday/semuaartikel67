📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that medicare doesn’t pay for long-term care, so in your 50s, find out what care costs where you live while your health still qualifies you for coverage. Then choose how you’d pay by what you’ve saved: your own money if you’re wealthy, Medicaid if your assets are modest, and long-term care insurance if you’re in between.

Almost 70% of people turning 65 will need long-term care, meaning help with bathing, dressing, and other daily tasks, according to the Administration for Community Living. One in five will need it for more than five years.

Recent findings suggest that your age and health set what insurance costs and whether you can buy it. Most individual long-term care policies require a health review, so a diagnosis in your 60s can raise your price or rule you out.

In a recent development, the American Association for Long-Term Care Insurance’s 2026 price index demonstrates a couple, both 55, paying about $5,000 a year combined, on average. That buys each of them a policy whose care benefits start at $165,000 and grow 3% a year. At 65, the same coverage averages about $7,000 a year.

Industry observers note that start by looking up typical prices in your area with CareScout’s cost of care calculator. Nationally, CareScout’s 2025 survey puts an in-home caregiver 44 hours a week at $80,080 a year, assisted living at $74,400, and a private nursing home room at $129,575.

Talk with your family, too, about who would give care and whether you’d stay in your own home or move to a facility. Ask your parents the same questions about their own care.

Industry observers note that get quotes for both kinds from an independent agent who sells several insurers’ policies. Identical coverage for an Illinois couple, both 60, ranged from $4,591 to $7,173 a year across five insurers in the same price index.
