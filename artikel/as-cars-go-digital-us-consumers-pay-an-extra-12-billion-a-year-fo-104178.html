As cars go digital, US consumers pay an extra $12 billion a year for repairs

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Today's vehicles need software tools to make repairs, and the subscriptions inflate insurance fees.

The computerization of cars comes at a price to US consumers, according to the Public Interest Research Group (PIRG), which states that software fees and other charges faced by mechanics push up garage costs.

An investigation by the non-profit consumer group estimates that car repair costs for Americans have been inflated by $12 billion each year, as vehicles integrate more computerized functions. Its report, Closing Shop, is available online.

To find a failed sensor, release an electronic parking brake, or perform diagnostics, a technician has to first connect a laptop or tablet to the vehicle. To so this, the repair shop has to have the right software tools, and that means paying the vehicle manufacturer subscription charges and other fees, in order to get the information they need to complete the job.

If smaller independent repair shops can’t afford all the necessary software or subscriptions, they may be forced to steer customers to authorized dealerships for some fixes – about one in five jobs – and this allows the latter to charge more.

PIRG claims that drivers pay for this through their insurance premiums, even if they never need to get their car fixed, because higher repair costs mean higher insurance claims. It states the price of auto insurance has risen three times as fast as the rest of the US economy since 2012.

Even if mechanics do choose to pay for the software, they spend an estimated collective 83 million hours a year buying, downloading, logging in, and waiting. At typical repair rates, that adds up to $12 billion.

A separate report from the PIRG Education Fund claims that a two-tiered repair model now exists, where independent sites conduct repairs with less information, less powerful diagnostic tools, and higher associated costs than the authorized dealerships.

The advocacy group wants to see legislators take this concern in hand, and has a number of recommendations for them. One is to stop manufacturers from locking out independent mechanics from simple repair jobs like changing brake pads, and require them to support a standard protocol for authenticating independent diagnostic tools.

In a recent development, another is to give owners control over their vehicle’s data via a standardized platform for accessing telematics repair information, so that the owner can give access to an independent repair shop. Car firms can use the vehicle data to tell owners that a repair is needed and to take it to an approved repair shop, without even disclosing what the issue is.

DIY repairers should be able to get everything they need to fix their own cars. Service information and basic diagnostic software should be free, and PIRG also wants a cap on the license fees manufacturers charge for their official software tools.

“We’ve allowed car manufacturers to build a toll road between the mechanic and the repair,” indicates Nathan Proctor, leader of PIRG’s Right to Repair campaign and co-author on the report.

Sources indicate that “We have a plethora of tolls on the road – subscriptions you need for manuals, diagnostic tools, secure access and more. Not only are all these tolls expensive, they also create traffic jams, slowing down the whole process, which adds to the costs for consumers.”  ®.
