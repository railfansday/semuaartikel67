Latest Update: 10 Cognitive Biases That Cost You Money – and a Habit to Beat Each One

Recent findings suggest that cognitive biases, like anchoring on the first price you see, following the crowd, avoiding bad news, or sticking with defaults, steer your money decisions in ways you don’t notice. Since you can’t count on catching one in the moment, counter each with a rule or a scheduled habit you set up before the decision comes.

In a recent development, try a quick test of anchoring first. Decide whether the world’s tallest tree is taller or shorter than 1,000 feet, then guess its height before you read on.

It has been revealed that in 2019, the world’s tallest living tree, a California coast redwood named Hyperion, measured about 380 feet. You knew the 1,000 was only part of the question, and it still set where your guess started. Each bias below shapes money decisions the same way, while every choice feels like your own.

Sources indicate that anchoring is letting the first number you see set your sense of what’s reasonable. A “was $199” tag makes $99 look like a bargain, though the $199 tells you nothing about what the item is worth or what other stores charge.

The fix is to set your price before you shop. Check what the item sells for at two or three other stores, decide what you’ll pay, and write the number down. Then judge the tag against your number, not the store’s.

The framing effect is reacting to the way a number is presented instead of to the number itself. A dealer who asks what monthly payment you want can lower it by stretching the loan over more months. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) claims a longer loan can mean smaller payments but more interest in total.

Say you borrow $30,000 at 7% interest. Stretching the loan from 48 months to 72 cuts the payment from about $718 to about $511, but adds about $2,340 in interest.

Industry observers note that to fix it, restate the number another way before you decide. Ask for the total you’ll repay over the life of the loan, not only the payment. Turn daily costs into yearly ones, too, since $5 a day is $1,825 a year.

The bandwagon effect is the pull to do something because so many other people are doing it. A stock or coin everyone seems to be buying feels safe because it’s popular. But all that buying helped push the price up, so a late buyer pays the higher price.

The fix is to write down why you’re buying before you invest, in a sentence or two, along with the price at which you’d sell. If the only reason you can write is that everyone else is buying, wait. Better yet, write your buying and selling rules down while you’re calm, and follow them.

Confirmation bias is noticing and believing the evidence that agrees with what you already think. Once you’ve settled on a car, a house, or a business idea, every good review reads like proof and every bad one like an exception.

The fix is to argue the other side before you commit. Write down what would change your mind, then go looking for it. Search the firm’s name along with the word “complaints,” and look up any bank, lender, or card company in the CFPB’s Consumer Complaint Database, which shows complaints about its financial products and how it responded.

Sources indicate that overconfidence is trusting your own judgment more than the facts support. In a budget, it means planning around what you think you spend instead of what you actually spend.

The fix is to check the record before you blueprint. Guess what you spent on food last month, restaurants and takeout included, then add it up from your bank and card statements. Build the budget on the statement total, not the guess.

Industry observers note that survivorship bias is judging by the success stories you can see, since the failures drop out of view. You hear about the friend whose side business took off, not about the people who tried the same thing and closed.

The fix is to look up the base rate, how often a move works out for everyone who tries it, before you copy a success. Of the business locations that opened in the 12 months through March 2021, 47% were still operating five years later, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reports. Before you put savings into a business, decide how much you could lose without touching your emergency fund, and put in no more than that.

It has been revealed that the ostrich effect is avoiding information you expect to be bad, like a credit card statement you leave unopened. The interest keeps adding up whether you look or not, and a late payment can hurt your credit for years. The CFPB indicates one can stay on your credit report for up to seven years.

Recent findings suggest that the fix is to check your accounts on the same day every week, such as Sunday morning, so looking is a routine instead of a choice. Open every statement, check every balance, and pay or schedule whatever is due. If you still can’t make yourself look, a financial therapist or cognitive behavioral therapy can help with the thoughts behind the avoidance.

Recent findings suggest that status quo bias is sticking with what you have, or whatever was picked for you, because changing takes effort. Economists Brigitte Madrian and Dennis Shea studied a large firm that began enrolling new hires in its 401(k) automatically. Many of those hires kept both the contribution rate and the fund the initiative picked for them, a mix few earlier hires had chosen on their own.

The fix is to re-choose your defaults once a year, on a date you set, as if you were signing up today. Start with your 401(k), since the rate an automatic initiative picks is the same for every newly introduced hire, whatever their goals.

Sources indicate that present bias is giving today more weight than any later day. If you’d take $100 now over $110 next week, but would wait for $110 in 53 weeks rather than take $100 in 52, you’ve felt it. The wait is one week both times, and it only seems longer when it starts today.

The same pull lets a credit card balance carry over month after month and pushes retirement saving off to next year, since the cost of each comes later.

The fix is to decide once and let automation carry it out. Schedule a transfer to savings, or an extra debt payment, for the day your paycheck arrives, so the money moves before you can spend it.

Sources indicate that loss aversion is feeling a loss more sharply than a gain of the same size. It can push you to overpay to avoid small losses, as with a low insurance deductible. A deductible is the amount you pay on a claim before insurance covers the rest.

According to recent reports, say a car insurance policy with a $1,000 deductible costs $150 a year less than the same policy with a $500 deductible. Over 10 years, the $500 deductible costs $1,500 more in premiums and saves $500 on each claim, so it comes out ahead only if you’d file more than three claims in that time.

In a recent development, fix it by working out what the protection costs before you buy it. Weigh the extra premium against the deductible it saves and how often you’ve filed claims in the past, then raise your deductible as long as you could pay it from savings.

Sources indicate that selling brings out a related bias, the endowment effect, which makes your own things seem worth more than buyers will pay. Another, the sunk-cost fallacy, keeps you paying into something because of what you’ve already put in.

According to recent reports, put two entries on your calendar today. Make the first a weekly account check on the same day and time, and the second a yearly review of your defaults on a date you’ll remember, such as your birthday. Those two fixes counter the ostrich effect and status quo bias, which cost you money while you do nothing at all.
