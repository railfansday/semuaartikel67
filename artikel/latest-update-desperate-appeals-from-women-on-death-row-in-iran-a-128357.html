Twin sisters Romina (left) and Taraneh Rahimi were arrested in late January, their cousin says.

Taraneh Rahimi is on death row in Iran.

In recent weeks, the 21-year-old has been unable to walk properly after her legs were beaten, and an ear-infection caused by an untreated head injury has affected her balance, her cousin says.

She is "keeping her spirits up" but "there are days she breaks down in tears," Masoud Nourmohammadi tells BBC News Persian.

He claims Taraneh and her twin sister Romina were arrested after joining the anti-government protests that swept the country in January.

When the twins' mother first saw them in prison, their faces were so swollen and bruised, and their hair pulled out, that she could only recognise them by their voices, Nourmohammadi claims, speaking from the US.

He says Taraneh signed a confession after interrogators threatened that her sister would be raped in front of her.

Taraneh is one of six women who have been sentenced to death this year in connection with the January protests, according to the UN's human rights rapporteur for Iran, Mai Sato.

In a recent development, the figure for men is much higher. However, women protesters and dissidents are being sentenced to death in numbers not seen for decades, as part of a "horrific rise" in politically motivated executions, Amnesty International's Iran researcher Raha Bahreini indicates.

January's protests began over economic grievances and spread to become Iran's largest since the 1979 Islamic Revolution.

The Iranian embassy in London told the BBC that claims of an increase in the use of the death penalty in protest-related cases were "misleading".

It categorically rejected allegations of the use of torture and any allegations that the death penalty has been imposed solely for peaceful protest, which it said is a "legitimate, and protected right".

It mentioned death sentences in relation to the "riot in January 2026" were issued in cases involving "intentional murder" or "Moharebeh (the taking up of weapons with the intent to threaten or wreck the people's lives or property)".

The twins were seized in late January, Nourmohammadi says.

It was two weeks after the end of the protests, which began in late December over economic grievances and grew to become Iran's largest against the clerical establishment since the 1979 Islamic Revolution.

The US-based Human Rights Activists News Agency (Hrana) claims it has identified more than 7,000 people killed in the crackdown that followed, most of them protesters, but believes the true figure is much higher.

The Iranian government accuses the US and Israel of fomenting the unrest, blaming "terrorists" and "rioters" for the deaths, which it indicates totalled 3,117 including some members of the security forces.

In a recent development, the twins' family did not find out where they were being held for more than three months, their cousin says. They were tried over protests in Isfahan, in an area of the city where a member of the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) security force was killed.

Taraneh loves horses and would like to set up her own breeding centre one day, her cousin notes.

Court documents have not been published, but the BBC has seen part of a sentencing document which notes Taraneh was convicted of moharebeh, which is a capital offence under Iran's Islamic Penal Code. The offence included "drawing a bladed weapon with the intent to endanger people's lives", according to the document.

Romina was sentenced to 25 years in jail for the lesser charge of aiding and abetting moharebeh, according to Sato, the UN rapporteur.

Nourmohammadi, who has been in contact with relatives and others in Iran familiar with the case, claims both women deny all the charges against them and that there is no evidence Taraneh had a "bladed weapon".

Sato says the sisters were part of a case in which 10 people were sentenced to death, but that there was "no clarification of the acts attributed to each individual".

According to sources familiar with the case, the twins' lawyers have submitted a complaint alleging they were tortured and sexually abused during interrogation, but the investigation into it is being overseen by an official they accuse of torture.

This image of Mahboubeh Shabani, sentenced to death in September, has been shared with human rights groups.

Voice messages from other women on death row contain a mixture of desperation and defiance.

Mahboubeh Shabani - widely reported to be 33 and a civil engineer - was sentenced to death in September under Iran's espionage law after transporting injured protesters, according to rights groups.

She called for help for herself and other prisoners from jail in the city of Mashhad in north-eastern Iran: "Everyone is in a terrible place mentally, especially those whose sentences have been finalised," she said.

Also jailed in Mashhad is Najmeh Amini, reported to be 23, who was initially arrested in the wake of the January protests, and later sentenced to death after being convicted of committing blasphemy in a social media post, according to rights groups.

"As long as I am alive, I have one hope, and that is to see a free and prosperous Iran before my life ends by the noose," a voice message attributed to her claims.

In addition, Mojdeh Hashemi Bazargani - reported to be aged 27 - is also facing execution, according to rights groups. They say she was convicted of charges including "espionage", after being arrested in April, during the current war between Iran and Israel and the US.

The US-based Iranian rights group Tavaana has shared audio it states is of a phone call she made from prison, describing being beaten in the head, neck and body and made to sign a confession.

In a recent development, the woman in the recording says: "They accused me of espionage. They say you've been paid for it. If I had been paid for espionage, I would have a car or a house. I have nothing.".

Najmeh Amini has been sentenced to death after being convicted of committing blasphemy in a social media post, rights groups say.

Amnesty International describes the charges in all three of these cases and also the twins as "politically motivated".

In a recent development, in response to the BBC's questions, the Iranian Embassy in London said "any allegations of the use of torture in connection with the mentioned cases are categorically rejected". It did not comment further on individual cases, but said "confessions obtained under duress have no legal validity".

At least 39 people have already been executed in connection with the January protests, according to Sato.

She notes a "sharp" increase in arrests and sentences across both men and women, and sees the rise in death sentences for women as an "escalation and expansion" of an "established pattern of repression".

Iranian human rights lawyer Musa Barzin, who is based in Turkey, claims "it appears that factors such as gender or age now carry less weight in determining the severity of punishment".

Official detail on cases is often scant and some may go unreported.

However, Sato states as well as the six women she identified as being arrested in connection with protests, a further three have received death sentences this year - for "blasphemy" and "corruption on Earth charges, in relation to the war in March" and "espionage and moharebeh".

In comparison, these nine sentences are more than Hrana recorded for women for similar offences over the whole decade to 2025 - with no more than three in any one year.

She states she is concerned by an "increasingly frequent pattern" where overturned death sentences are later "reimposed by a parallel branch of the judiciary once public attention has subsided".

Taraneh (left) and Romina both say they gave baseless confessions after being tortured.

Sato indicates there was a "turning point" for women in Iran during the 2022 protests over the death of Mahsa Amini, who died in custody after being detained over an alleged breach of Iran's dress code.

According to recent reports, women "were at the forefront of challenging state policies and structures of control," says Sato. "Their political agency, visibility and leadership have made them targets of state repression.".

The Iranian embassy noted judicial proceedings in Iran are conducted in accordance with the Constitution and rule of law. It adds: "Cases involving the death penalty have included charges such as serious criminal offences, violence, terrorism, espionage, cooperation with hostile intelligence services and Moharebeh.".

Iran executes more people per head of population than any other country, according to UN experts. Human rights groups say last year saw the highest number in decades.

Hrana recorded 2,003 executions in the year to October 2026. The vast majority - including 47 women - were for crimes such as murder and drug trafficking.

Nourmohammadi notes the family are trying as best they can to support the twins.

Romina enjoys music and often asks relatives to play songs over the phone, he states.

Taraneh loves horses and has always wanted to set up a horse breeding centre, he indicates, and they tell her they initiative to help her do so in the future: "That's how we try to give her hope.".
