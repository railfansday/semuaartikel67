Updated November 26, 2026 | Reviewed by Davia Sills.

It has been revealed that my dear friend, mentor, and pioneer in the field of human sexuality, Dr. Beverly Whipple, has died. To pay tribute, I want to fully communicate how her contributions to the field rocked the world. I want to celebrate the huge impact she had in legitimizing women’s pleasure so that women themselves would be encouraged to explore their bodies and their pleasure capacity. Beyond her groundbreaking book, The G Spot and Other Discoveries about Human Sexuality, Beverly single-handedly blazed a trail that led to some of the biggest breakthroughs in human sexuality research in our lifetime. But I also want to share the life lessons I had the privilege of learning in my relationship with her.

Sources indicate that I’m having a conversation with Beverly in my mind. As I struggle to get this tribute right, I hear her say, “Relax, just do a good-enough job!” She taught me and others not to strive so hard. To let go of being goal-directed in bed. To not pursue “great sex” in lieu of enjoying the good-enough sex we were already having, while remaining open to exploring more. Beverly’s guidance is why my book, Why Good Sex Matters, isn’t titled Why Great Sex Matters. I loosen and soften as words and tears flow.

I pause. My last blog was about grief over losing my pet. The loss-of-a-dear-friend-and-mentor grief has only made the lessons loom larger. I trust Beverly would agree: To be fully alive, we need to feel the full range of our emotions, both painful and pleasurable.

Life is full of joy and loss, and the biggest loss is not to feel it all. And pleasure, both in and out of the bedroom, is medicine. It makes our brain and bodies work better and makes life worth living. (Click here to read her paper on the benefits of sexual expression on physical health.).

Beverly had a relentless curiosity and amazing ability to observe and listen. As a nursing student, when asked by patients if they could resume sex after a heart attack, her supervisor met her questions with a blank stare; she saw the huge gaps in knowledge about sexuality. Then later, as a nursing instructor, when she tried to teach about human sexuality and was told not to do so, she promptly left to develop a sexual health curriculum to teach elsewhere.

Industry observers note that in the 1970s, she taught women with urinary incontinence Kegel exercises to strengthen the pelvic floor. She listened when some reported that stimulation of a sensitive area on the front wall of the vaginal wall was associated with intense pleasure and the release of fluid, and she got curious. While the medical field was quick to dismiss this as urinary incontinence, Beverly dug up a 1950 paper by a pioneering gynecologist, Dr. Ernst Gräfenberg, who was the first to identify this “erotic” zone and describe the ejaculate released from stimulation of this region.

In a recent development, she began work with sex researcher Dr. John Perry to investigate this phenomenon, publishing papers and eventually the book, The G Spot, naming it after Gräfenberg. In later years, she expanded the idea of a “G spot” to what is now known as the clitourethrovaginal complex, an interconnected system including the internal clitoris, urethra, and vagina that work together during the sexual response.

But this was only the beginning of her journey. Beverly listened to women who reported being able to reach orgasm without any physical stimulation at all, through “imagery” (informally called “thinking off”). This led her to partner with my Ph.D. mentor, Dr. Barry Komisaruk, complete her own Ph.D., and conduct a study of women who reported being able to have imagery-induced orgasms. The results supported that for these women, imagery-induced orgasms produced physical changes comparable to those of orgasms from physical stimulation.

Years later in my Ph.D. studies, inspired and supported by Beverly, my colleagues and I found that when women simply “imagined” erotic genital stimulation, their brains lit up areas that register actual physical touch, along with many of the same regions that get activated during orgasm. This supports the notion that the brain or mind is the most important sex organ of all.

It has been revealed that beverly continued to her work and listened to women with complete spinal cord injuries who reported that they still experienced menstrual cramps and orgasm, despite doctors telling them it was medically impossible and “all in their heads.” In 2004, Beverly and Barry conducted the world’s first-ever brain imaging study of female orgasm. The results suggested that these sensations reached the brain through the vagus nerve, a cranial nerve that runs from the cervix and uterus directly to the brain, bypassing the spinal cord.

Not all people with spinal cord injury report genital sensations, but for those who do, this validated their experience and paved the way for further brain imaging research on genital stimulation and orgasm, which I had the privilege to conduct and publish for my own dissertation. That work revealed that genital stimulation and orgasm activated widespread regions of the brain, including areas involved in sensation, movement, reward, emotion, and thinking. Thus, we conclude that orgasm is a whole-brain event.

During stimulation and orgasm, oxygen-rich blood is drawn to the widespread brain regions that become activated. A well-oxygenated brain is likely a happy, healthy brain. My research also demonstrated that many of the regions that produce, respond to, or release feel-good, good-for-you substances such as dopamine, serotonin, opioids, and endocannabinoids are active during the sexual response and orgasm.

According to recent reports, in Beverly’s work on the health benefits of sexual expression, she pointed to research linking it to pain relief, lower stress, better sleep, and enhanced quality of life. She viewed pleasure as part of health. Our subsequent studies support this: pleasure is medicine. As I like to say, healthy hedonism heals.

In a recent development, beverly was a brilliant scientist, educator, and mentor. Most of all, she was a beautiful, kind, loving human being. In tribute to her, I put together a short reel of our lab’s work, as shown on Nightline, Discovery, and other networks, as part of her legacy. She appears in the first clip, right there in the lab with us, and the work documented in the other clips all grew from the questions she asked decades ago. (Watch the reel here.).

Rest in pleasure, dearest Beverly. You live on in our hearts and our work.
