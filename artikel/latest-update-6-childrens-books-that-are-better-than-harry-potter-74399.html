J. K. Rowling began writing her debut novel, the tale of a young orphan magician named Harry, around the summer of 1990. After five years, the book finally came together as Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone (which would later be published in the US as Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone). After several publishers kept rejecting the initial manuscript, the eight-year-old daughter of Bloomsbury's chief executive fell in love with Rowling's writing so deeply that the publisher decided to add this to their growing portfolio of fantasy children's literature by upcoming writers.

Sources indicate that the rest is history. Philosopher's Stone was a smash hit and a cultural phenomenon, sparking an equally successful book series and eventually leading the Harry Potter franchise to becoming one of the biggest multimedia franchises in the history of commercial entertainment. However, there are six particular children's books from throughout history that are even better than the Harry Potter book series as a whole. From foundational classics like The Little Prince to more relatively modern masterpieces like The Golden Compass, these absolute masterpieces are arguably the very best of the best when it comes to children's literature.

Inspired by his real-life experiences as an aviator and his longing for home and childlike wonder during World War II, Antoine de Saint-Exupéry wrote the children's novella The Little Prince while living in Upcoming York away from occupied France. In it, a stranded desert pilot befriends a young prince from a tiny asteroid who's looking for a way back to the rose he left behind. At less than 100 pages in length, it's a remarkably easy book to read in its entirety in one sitting; but even though it was undoubtedly written for children, it's such a timeless and universally appealing masterpiece that everyone with the ability to read should check it out at least once.

Everyone is able to draw a different message from it depending on what stage of life they're at when reading it.

In a recent development, what's more, a single read-through will likely not be enough, since this is one of those classic books worth reading over and over again. The prose is beautiful, the emotional sincerity is remarkable, and the book's philosophical core makes it so that everyone is able to draw a different message from it depending on what stage of life they're at when reading it. Needless to say, it's significantly less vast and ambitious than an entire seven-book MG and YA fantasy book series, but its thematic depth is more existential and universal, its narrative economy is more admirable, and its easier re-readability makes it arguably superior to Harry Potter.

Industry observers note that american writer and essayist E. B. White was best-known for his work in children's literature, and even though Stuart Little is probably what his name is most often immediately associated with, Charlotte's Web is not to be underappreciated. It's the tale of the titular barn spider, who cleverly weaves messages into her web to make a runt pig famous and save him from being slaughtered. It's easily one of the best children's books of all time, directly informed by White's own struggles raising animals that would eventually be slaughtered at his farm in Maine.

According to recent reports, what makes Charlotte's Web so wonderful and timeless is White's refusal to condescend to his young audience, crafting a story whose emotional honesty, lyrical prose, and quiet philosophical weight make it deeply endearing regardless of the reader's age. There's zero bloat here, every moment being written with maximum emotional and thematic impact in mind. Though Harry Potter's classic brand of "Chosen One" heroism has a charm of its own, the way Charlotte's Web celebrates quieter and more ordinary kindness is arguably even more special.

There are remarkably few fantasy books more influential than Harry Potter, but there's one in particular that can be traced back as far as the mid-19th century: Lewis Carroll's Alice's Adventures in Wonderland. Back in its time, the book was absolutely revolutionary for how it broke away from the contemporary children's literature tradition of moralizing to kids. Never before had a children's book been so preoccupied with entertaining its readers and purely cultivating their imagination, and the genre was never the same again as a result.

Industry observers note that inspired by Alice Liddell, a 10-year-old girl who Carroll entertained during a fateful 1862 boat trip, Alice's Adventures in Wonderland is one of the most deliciously absurd and surreal children's books ever written, which only adds to its timeless entertainment value. With an exquisitely creative and twisted use of language, a limitless use of his imagination, and a clever subversion of adult authority, Carroll broke new ground in children's literature, paving the way for books like Harry Potter to exist in the first place. It doesn't really have much of a plot, but that's precisely where its inimitable charm comes from.

Where the revolutionary work of his dear friend J. R. R. Tolkien was the most influential and groundbreaking work of the 20th century in the field of general fantasy literature, C. S. Lewis wrote one of the most foundational children's fantasy novels of the 20th century: The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe, the first chapter in the iconic Chronicles of Narnia. In it, four evacuated siblings during World War II are transported to a magical winter world through a wardrobe in a house in the English countryside, where they find themselves having to ally themselves with the lion Aslan to overthrow the White Witch.

A masterpiece which completely changed the portal fantasy subgenre forever.

Recent findings suggest that the result of such a premise is one of the most essential classic fantasy books to read before kicking the bucket, a masterpiece which completely changed the portal fantasy subgenre forever. It's one of the most direct literary ancestors of the kind of epic, good-versus-evil brand of children's fantasy that would eventually birth Harry Potter, and it's every bit as much of a must-read—or perhaps even more so. Full of mythic wonder and bolstered by a universally endearing Christian allegory of sacrifice and grace, it's a masterclass in warm and meaningful writing for children, which it achieves in under 200 pages.

Northern Lights (published as The Golden Compass in North America and a few other regions) is the inaugural chapter in Philip Pullman's His Dark Materials series, one of the most vital and foundational in the history of '90s fantasy literature. It's one of the most essential fantasy books of the 1990s, telling the tale of a fierce young girl journeying into the Arctic to rescue kidnapped children and uncover the dark secret behind a mysterious substance called Dust. Inspired by classic literature, philosophy, and visual art, Pullman wrote the story around themes of growing up and questioning authority.

According to recent reports, pullman didn't actually write the book specifically for children (which probably explains the overtly anti-organized-religion attitudes that have made the novel so controversial throughout the years), but rather as a tonally and thematically layered story for all ages. But even though the book has been embraced as a classic of children's and YA literature mostly for marketing purposes, one thing is certain: Few books compare to The Golden Compass. Full of philosophical depth and intellectual ambitiousness, complete with an absolute gut-punch of an ending, it's the kind of book that all those who think Harry Potter is the best that children's literature has to offer should read.

Industry observers note that before its sequel became the most important fantasy novel of the entire 20th century almost a couple of decades later, The Hobbit became an absolute smash hit in 1937, immediately establishing J. R. R. Tolkien as a household name. It's the story of the home-loving hobbit Bilbo Baggins embarking on a treasure-hunting adventure with a powerful wizard and 13 dwarves, and it just so happens that it has only gotten better with age. It's one of the bestselling fantasy books in history and one of the most widely celebrated and studied classics of 20th-century children's literature, the kind of pop-cultural institution so influential that anyone who loves speculative fiction should consider it an absolute must-read.

Recent findings suggest that on top of being one of the best cozy fantasy books of all time, The Hobbit is genuinely one of the most perfect fantasy novels ever written. It's the foundational bedrock of modern fantasy, a traditional Hero's Journey tale elevated by a masterful pace, beautiful prose, and Tolkien's unmatched sense of depth in terms of world-building and plotting alike. It's almost unfair to compare any other fantasy children's book to it, but if one were to put it side by side with the Harry Potter series, it would have to be the clear winner. It's tightly structured, full of masterful world-building and memorable characters, wonderfully written almost as though it were a magical piece of folklore, and brilliant in how it subverts the Chosen One trope by having an ordinary hobbit be its hero.
