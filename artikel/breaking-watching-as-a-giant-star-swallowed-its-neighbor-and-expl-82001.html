Breaking: Watching as a giant star swallowed its neighbor and exploded

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that in the Andromeda Galaxy, a giant star about seven times as massive as the Sun spent nearly a decade slowly swallowing its much smaller companion. The process, known as common envelope evolution, was proposed in the 1970s to explain how some of the tightest pairs of stars in the Universe form, but no one had ever caught it in the act.

Sources indicate that in a fresh Nature study, astronomers led by Viraj Karambelkar, an astrophysicist at Columbia University, finally witnessed the entire spectacle from the decades before it began to the eruption that ended it in late 2025. “It lasts for a few years, maybe a few decades,” Karambelkar said. “Stars evolve on time scales of millions of years. So, it’s really like a blip on the entire cosmic time scale.”.

Some pairs of stars orbit so close to each other that they could not have formed at that distance, so their orbits must have shrunk. Common envelope evolution is the leading explanation for that shrinkage. When one star in a binary grows old, it expands into a giant, and its outer layers can engulf its companion. The two then share a single, loosely bound envelope of gas. As the engulfed star drags through it, it loses orbital energy to friction and spirals inward. Depending on how much of the envelope gets lost, the pair either ends up in a much tighter orbit or merges.

Karambelkar’s team spotted a candidate system where this might be happening while searching Andromeda for stars changing in brightness, using the Wide-field Infrared Transient Explorer (WINTER) telescope at Palomar Observatory and data from NASA’s Near-Earth Object Wide-field Infrared Survey Explorer (NEOWISE) mission. One giant had been brightening for seven years. “When a star first swallows its companion, it has a very subtle effect on its brightness,” Karambelkar remarked. “It slowly brightens, and a lot of light comes out in the infrared.”.

In April 2025, Karambelkar’s team published a paper suggesting that this system, named AT 2025abao, was undergoing an engulfment in progress. Back then, though, they couldn’t rule out a competing explanation: a single star with instabilities deep within its core. So, they kept watching.

Sources indicate that the researchers reconstructed the history of the system using observations from about a dozen telescopes and surveys, going back to photographic sky plates from 1989. Until 2018, the star barely changed. Modeling those quiet years revealed a giant late in its life, about 350 times wider than the Sun. Its companion was impossible to see, however. “The giant star outshines the smaller star by a factor of about 18,000,” Karambelkar remarked.

Industry observers note that to get around it, the team estimated the mass of the smaller companion based on the amount of dust the system had shed before the engulfment. The estimates suggested a star of roughly 0.7 solar masses.

Industry observers note that between 2018 and early 2025, the giant brightened two- to threefold in visible light, swelling while its temperature stayed nearly constant. Karambelkar compares the process to a spacecraft reentering Earth’s atmosphere. The companion, skimming through the giant’s outer layers, was gradually seeing its orbit shrink, and the friction was heating up the envelope. The energy released by that drag matched the extra light the team observed.

According to recent reports, then, the star’s visible light dropped by half while its infrared glow kept rising. NASA’s SPHEREx space telescope happened to catch it in July 2025, revealing that the giant had swollen to some 860 times the Sun’s width and wrapped itself in fresh dust.

In a recent development, in October 2025, the star brightened roughly 100-fold in just 25 days, shining like 1.5 million Suns at its peak. “At one point, the rate at which the orbit was decaying became very, very fast,” Karambelkar said, comparing the release of the gravitational energy to an explosion. “The companion just fell very promptly, very deep inside the envelope of the star, and it crashed into the core. That violent crash set off a bomb inside the star, and that explosion caused it to brighten very quickly and eject some of the material.”.

The eruption had all the hallmarks of a luminous red nova, a type of outburst known to come from merging stars—but an event the researchers weren’t expecting. “Honestly, our minds were blown,” Karambelkar noted. He said that this was evidence that this explosion was a consequence of the inspiraling of a binary companion. What made AT 2025abao special, though, was the giant itself.

All previously known luminous red novae came from younger stars with dense, tightly bound outer layers, which inevitably drive both stars toward a merger. This one was an old giant with a loose, churning envelope—the setup that can also leave behind a surviving tight binary. To find out if the companion actually survived, the team looked at how AT 2025abao’s energy was spent.

In a recent development, to predict how such events end, astronomers use a formula with an efficiency parameter describing how much of the energy lost by the sinking companion goes into blowing the envelope away. AT 2025abao demonstrated that the energy goes to different places at different stages.

Recent findings suggest that during the slow spiral, most of the energy escaped as light. In the final plunge, the companion released about a hundred times more energy, but only around 1 percent of it was radiated away. Nearly all the rest went into hurling a tenth of the envelope outward at about 10 times the speed needed to escape the giant’s gravity. The rest of the envelope stayed put, which suggests the companion merged with the giant’s core instead of surviving as a tight partner.

“Initially, when the rate of energy deposition is low, the star is able to puff up and adjust to this newly introduced energy source inside it,” Karambelkar said. “But once the energy deposition rate increases very swiftly as the companion spirals even closer, the star can no longer adjust itself. At that point, it needs to throw out some of its material.”.

The efficiency the team calculated fits values inferred from known post-engulfment binaries. But the formula assumes the energy is mostly spent expelling lots of the envelope. Here, most of it went into flinging a small amount of gas at high speeds. It turned out the formula works as a rule of thumb but misses some of the physics.

In AT 2025abao, gas shed during the slow spiral piled up in the plane of the binary’s orbit, so the explosion’s ejecta could expand freely toward the poles even though it was blocked around the equator. That, Karambelkar argues, explains the hourglass-shaped nebulae seen around dying stars. “It had been an idea for a very long time that those hourglass shapes are shaped by binary interactions,” he said. “It seems like what we’re seeing here confirms that hypothesis.”.

Sources indicate that the team admits it can’t yet be sure the stars merged, because dust formed from the ejected material hides whatever is left inside. “We want to continue monitoring the system for several years, possibly even decades, to watch the dust clear out and whatever is remaining inside emerge,” Karambelkar stated. “That will conclusively tell us whether this merged or the binary system survived.” In the meantime, the team hopes to find many more such events involving stars of different masses and types.

Telescopes we have today can detect them only as far as Andromeda, but the Vera Rubin Observatory and the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope should spot them in about 100 Milky Way-like galaxies within some 50 million light-years. “We can have an influx of several of these events per year,” Karambelkar remarked.
