Recent findings suggest that A remarkable archaeological discovery in southern Saudi Arabia is revealing what life was like 125,000 years ago, when parts of the Arabian Peninsula were covered in lakes, grasslands, and thriving wildlife. Researchers have uncovered ancient stone tools, fossils of large animals, and rare plant remains that offer an unusually detailed look at how early humans survived in this dramatically different environment.

Sources indicate that the discoveries come from Uruq al-Julaybah, an ancient lakeside site in the Mundafan region. Confirmed by the Saudi Heritage Commission, the findings are the result of a joint investigation involving the Commission, King Abdullah University of Science and Technology (KAUST), and Griffith University. Published in Science, the research brings together specialists in geology, archaeology, botany, and micropaleontology, along with experts from several international institutions. Their work provides notable latest evidence about prehistoric human activity, environmental change, and early human presence across the Arabian Peninsula.

A Lost Green Arabia Filled With Lakes and Wildlife.

Today, much of this region is dominated by desert, but the landscape looked very different during the last Interglacial period, around 125,000 years ago. This warm, humid chapter of Arabian history is often called Green Arabia, a time when plentiful water and vegetation supported large animals and human populations.

Sources indicate that using satellite-based terrain modeling, scientists reconstructed an enormous ancient lake covering approximately 340 square kilometers. The lake reached depths of up to 29 meters. Evidence from sediments, the surrounding landscape, and the remains of aquatic organisms indicates that it was a relatively stable freshwater environment bordered by open grasslands and vegetation.

Sources indicate that the researchers identified more than 500 plant and animal fossil specimens, including invertebrates, spread across more than 130 locations. Among the animals that once inhabited the area were hippos, extinct long-horned buffaloes, camels, Arabian oryx, gazelles, and ostriches. These discoveries reveal a rich prehistoric ecosystem that bears little resemblance to the dry landscape found there today.

Thousands of Ancient Stone Tools Reveal Human Ingenuity.

In a recent development, alongside the fossils, archaeologists recovered 5,511 stone tools measuring more than 2 centimeters from the main excavation area. More than 98% were made from flint, and analysis showed that the people who created them used Levallois techniques, a method of shaping stone to produce carefully prepared flakes.

To better understand how the tools were made and used, researchers conducted systematic surveys using a grid system to document and collect artifacts and fossils. They also applied two dating methods to sand and fossil teeth, used three-dimensional techniques to determine how stone fragments fit together, and examined tool surfaces with microscopic and chemical analyses.

In a recent development, these investigations uncovered intriguing evidence of how early humans used the plants around them. Residues preserved on stone tools included traces from several types of vegetation, some of which contain toxic compounds. Resin and woody material suggest that certain stone tips were attached to handles or shafts to make hunting weapons.

According to recent reports, other plant residues point to the processing of tubers, bushes, and palm leaves. This suggests that the people living around the lake relied on vegetation for a variety of purposes, from obtaining food to carrying out everyday activities.

Industry observers note that perhaps most surprisingly, the findings raise the possibility that prehistoric hunters incorporated poisonous plant compounds into their hunting technology. Although this interpretation remains tentative, it represents some of the earliest potential evidence of such practices identified in Saudi Arabia. The discoveries also include some of the region's earliest known evidence of palm-related vegetation.

A Rare Glimpse Into Everyday Life 125,000 Years Ago.

What makes Uruq al-Julaybah especially valuable is the unusual combination of discoveries preserved at one location. The animal fossils, manufactured tools, and plant remains were all recovered from the same dated archaeological setting (animal remains, stone tools, and plant material), allowing scientists to piece together a remarkably complete picture of prehistoric life.

Industry observers note that the shores of ancient Lake Mundafan appear to have provided an ideal gathering place for early humans. Freshwater, animals, vegetation, and materials suitable for making tools were all available in the surrounding landscape.

Recent findings suggest that evidence suggests that people used this environment for numerous activities, including manufacturing stone tools, preparing hunting equipment, processing animals and plants, and working with plant fibers. Taken together, the discoveries indicate that these early populations were highly adaptable and made sophisticated use of the resources available to them.

"It is very exciting to see how the collaboration of so many different specialists allows to reconstruct this uniquely complete picture of life, behavior and the environment in the distant past," noted Professor Frans van Buchem, KAUST Professor of Geology and co-lead scientist.

Industry observers note that dr. Faisal Al-Jibreen, a Heritage Commission researcher and professor of Paleolithic archaeology who participated in the project, emphasized the importance of the discovery at Lake Mundafan in the Empty Quarter. He described it as a significant archaeological find that helps explain how the ancient environment shaped the lives of humans and animals in southern Saudi Arabia during early prehistory.

Reconstructing a Forgotten Chapter of Human History.

Sources indicate that "This site gives us a rare and richly detailed view of how people lived in a landscape that looks nothing like Arabia today," noted Professor Chris Clarkson, co-lead scientist and Professor of Archaeology at Griffith University's Australian Research Centre for Human Evolution. "The combination of animal remains, stone tools and plant material in a single, dated context is what makes this discovery so significant.".

It has been revealed that by bringing together different kinds of archaeological and environmental evidence, the researchers have reconstructed a period of Arabian history that is difficult to imagine from the region's present-day appearance. Their findings offer vital clues about how early humans moved across the Arabian Peninsula, developed technology, and adapted their behavior to changing environmental conditions.

"Saudi Arabia has an exceptional archaeological heritage. Building Heritage Science capabilities in the Kingdom will enable us to turn the smallest traces into the biggest stories," noted Professor Laurence Hapiot, co-author and Head of Heritage Research at KAUST.

The investigation reflects an extensive international research effort to better understand the Arabian Peninsula's ancient human and environmental history. The Saudi Heritage Commission supported fieldwork during the 2023 season, helped fund the research, and facilitated scientific activities.

Along with KAUST, Griffith University in Australia, and King Saud University, the project involved international universities and research centers specializing in archaeology, ancient environments, history, and laboratory analysis. This collaboration demonstrates how combining expertise from different scientific fields can uncover extraordinary details about human life thousands of generations in the past.
