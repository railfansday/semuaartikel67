Oobah Butler Wants to Expose the World’s Stupidest Industries, but They Get Worse Faster Than He Can Plan His Stunts

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that it’s a spectacular time to be a grifter. Basic truths keep getting harder for people to agree on, marketing is more dependent on hackable algorithms than traditional gatekeepers, and it seems like everyone is willing to uncritically listen to anyone offering them a shortcut. As Oobah Butler puts it in the first episode of his fresh HBO series “Scams for a Better Tomorrow,” we’re living through a “gold rush of bullshit.”.

It has been revealed that if that’s the case, Butler is a prospector who hits a paystreak everywhere he looks. After beginning his career as a musician, the British performance artist has devoted much of his past decade to the art of the scam. In 2017, he made headlines for creating a TripAdvisor profile for a nonexistent restaurant that briefly became the highest-rated eatery in London. His penchant for scams has only grown since then, culminating in “Oobah: Scams for a Better Tomorrow.” The five-episode, “Nathan for You”-esque series sees him grift his way to the top of some of the slimiest industries on the planet including wellness supplements, wine, and dating apps with an approach that blurs the line between satire and how-to guides. In a recent development, when we spoke over Zoom, it was the first time I had ever interviewed a scammer. I naturally had to ask Butler if that was his preferred term (he had no problem with it), and how he would characterize his own unorthodox line of work.

Sources indicate that “I think it sits somewhere between gonzo journalism and comedy. And I think we’re in a moment, in a culture, that’s just so rife with inauthenticity and scamming,” Butler stated, explaining that just watching run-of-the-mill advertisements on a streaming service are enough to convince you that we live in an insane world. “One in three [ads] are AI now. And I don’t know why, that stuck in my mind recently where I was like, ‘Wow, these aren’t real people trying to sell me a product. They’re like prompts in a machine.’ So it’s an interesting line of work, but I think it feels very now, to be honest.”.

The series should be essential viewing for anyone who either wants to laugh at the state of our seemingly elite spaces or just wants to make a quick buck in a dirty industry. Episodes illustrate how ecosystems of influencers and online ratings can be easily manipulated in pursuit of accomplishing ludicrous goals, like turning an undrinkable bottle of smoke tainted cabernet into one of Napa’s highest-rated wines of the year or marketing a skincare product as “endorsed by Cameron Diaz” because a random Hispanic guy was willing to slap his name on it for $50.

In a recent development, it’s a funny enough premise, but Oobah and his small team (which included frequent Safdie collaborator Ronald Bronstein) found that their best laid intends left them insufficiently prepared for the greed and stupidity that they’d encounter in the real world. The first episode begins with a scheme to market a newly introduced skin serum that’s allegedly made from the tears of small children. They thought that ridiculous idea could serve as the climax of an episode, but they soon found that the grifters they encountered had little issue with the idea — some of them even thought they could make money on it with the right tear-shaped bottle. The joke remained in the show, but it had to be reduced to a starting point before the episode devolved into crazier plans.

Sources indicate that “We had a tip from a journalist that dermatologists can be full of shit and that they often can take money in exchange for recommendations. So we set up a lab where I had these kid actors with goggles on with tubes coming off them, and it was like we were milking the kids for the tears or whatever. We were like, ‘Right, we’re going to invite this dermatologist down here,’ a high-end Manhattan dermatologist, and we expected the dermatologist to laugh us out of the room,” he stated. “What we couldn’t initiative for doing that stunt is that the dermatologist would be like, ‘Awesome. Yeah, this is cool. Yeah, all right, interesting product. Maybe we could keep on working together, but I expect an endorsement fee.’ And I’m like, ‘Oh, you don’t give a fucking shit. You don’t care.'”.

Recent findings suggest that the incident cuts to the central problem of a show like this: it’s hard for satire to keep up with a world that only seems to get crazier every day. But Butler is not shying away from the challenge. He works quickly (the show began shooting this spring and wrapped in October before premiering on November 22), and is willing to change his intends whenever it turns out that a sincere actor has beaten him to one of his comedic scams. He already has plenty of ideas for a hypothetical Season 2, including taking on the recent trend of hotels and vacation rentals using AI-generated images that look nothing like their actual rooms. But the one thing he can count on is that the bullshit gold rush is not going to end any time soon.

Sources indicate that “It’s hard to satirize something that’s so insane and fucked up,” he noted. “That’s why in the first episode, we go and make a fresh product because it felt like the children’s tears thing wasn’t crazy enough. You know what I mean? So I literally go and make a human meat supplement, a lab-grown human meat supplement, and we go to this lab in Austin, we take stem cells from my stomach, we grow it over months and we create this thing. Because the actual reason we did that was like, ‘Oh, I don’t think children’s tears is scary because influencers would promote that.’ So I suppose that the crazier things get, the crazier the satire has to be able for it to be effective.”.

Sources indicate that “Oobah: Scams for a Better Tomorrow” airs Sunday nights on HBO at 11 p.m. ET/PT before streaming on HBO Max.
