📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a recent development, few superheroes have a television legacy as rich and varied as Batman. From the campy action of Batman 1966 to the groundbreaking animated noir of Batman: The Animated Series and the 1940s reinvention of Batman: Caped Crusader, the Dark Knight has flourished on television just as successfully as in theaters. The many Batman TV highlights can feel worlds apart, but each offers an endearing and worthwhile take on the traditional mythos.

In a recent development, however, one of the best Batman TV series barely features him at all. Instead, it takes Batman’s story as the foundation for an ambitious, original crime saga set decades before his emergence, withholding the fully realized Caped Crusader until its final episode. That series is Gotham, and for many longtime fans it’s still considered one of the greatest small-screen Batman stories despite making huge lore changes and completely reinventing the origin of not just Batman himself, but his allies and rogue’s gallery too.

In a recent development, following Jim Gordon (Ben McKenzie) and the young Bruce Wayne (David Mazouz) immediately after the murder of Bruce’s parents, Gotham styles itself as a detective series that traces the city’s descent into supervillain chaos. It turns familiar bombastic supervillains into (initially) grounded characters with their own ambitions and lives beyond fighting Batman. Since its conclusion after five seasons in 2019, few superhero series have managed to make such sweeping changes to a cherished comic-book mythology while still being embraced by devoted fans of the source material.

According to recent reports, making a Batman TV show without Batman in it was always going to be a huge gamble. Gotham makes it work incredibly well, and there’s a key reason why. Rather than ignoring Batman completely for its prequel story about Jim Gordon’s rise through the GCPD, Gotham instead ensures his shadow still looms large by spending 100 episodes creating conditions that make his arrival feel completely necessary by the time he emerges in the finale. The show details Gotham City’s decline from a city plagued by mob rule to a one filled with supervillains that only a costumed hero feels equipped to deal with.

Gotham does this by completely rebuilding the mythology around Batman and the young Bruce Wayne. The network of villains and allies surrounding Batman begins to emerge much earlier than it does in the comic books, and it’s much more interconnected. The villain origins especially change. For example, The Riddler (Cory Michael Smith) begins as a police forensic scientist who works with Jim Gordon and, at one point, is even his romantic rival. Twins Jerome and Jeremiah Valeska (both Cameron Monaghan) play versions of the Joker with connections to Bruce Wayne that mean, unlike the comics, Batman is fully aware of the identity of his greatest foe. Camron Bicandova’s Selina Kyle, the eventual Catwoman, is repositioned to be a childhood frenemy of Bruce’s rather than a thief he meets and falls in love with as Batman.

Giving Batman’s enemies such detailed personal histories is a substantial change to the dynamics audiences expect. It also makes Batman’s arrival feel totally necessary. If there were any chance that the likes of the Joker, Riddler, or Penguin (Robin Lord Taylor) could be redeemed or rehabilitated by traditional means, it would have happened during the years Jim Gordon spent facing them in Gotham. By the time Batman emerges, they’ve become so entrenched in their villainous identities that an equally outlandish figure like the Dark Knight genuinely feels like the only way to keep the city safe from them.

Gotham’s huge transformations also work because they open up storylines that couldn’t happen in the comics, but none of these newly introduced narrative arcs feel like they prevent the Gotham City fans already know from being able to happen. Giving Ed Nygma a sympathetic history doesn’t mean audiences can’t imagine him eventually becoming The Riddler and fighting Batman as an outright villain. Gotham makes substantial changes, but each one feels deliberate and serves the central goal of creating a setting and story that convincingly leads to Bruce Wayne becoming Batman.

Gotham finished its five-season, 100-episode run in April 2019. Its 2014 premiere came before the movie-connected streaming era of Marvel and DC, and before shows like Prime Video’s The Boys and Netflix’s The Umbrella Academy made superhero subversion mainstream. However, despite the fact that superhero media has changed considerably since then, Gotham still holds up. Watching it in 2026 hardly feels like revisiting a bygone era of the genre. The reason why is simple: its appeal was never dependent on being the latest franchise event or cashing in on the superhero boom that began in the late 2000s and early 2010.

The visual identity of Gotham has proved essential for the show’s longevity. It doesn’t feel tied to any specific era, instead having a noir flavor that perfectly fits its unfolding detective story. Old-fashioned cars, imposing architecture, dim police offices, and flashes of modern technology create a city suspended between decades. This production design gives Gotham an atmosphere that can survive changing trends in other superhero media and even in other cop and crime reveals.

In a recent development, its ensemble roster of characters, and the Gotham cast members who play them, are just as key to its legacy. Gotham doesn’t just give Batman characters fresh storylines to pad out time until Batman arrives, it gives them incredibly satisfying narrative arcs in their own right. Gordon’s compromised idealism provides a human center that drives all five seasons, while watching characters like Nygma and Cobblepot trying to turn the pain of their loneliness and social isolation into a meaningful destiny never gets stale. Even Bruce Wayne’s new pre-superhero story feels worthwhile. There’s something incredibly endearing about a Batman who takes up the mantle not just because of the death of his parents, but because of multiple events and encounters with criminals that lead him to decide there’s no alternative to taking up vigilantism.

It has been revealed that the show also grows stranger and more theatrical as it continues, allowing its crime drama to accommodate increasingly grotesque humor and horror as the emergence of Batman draws closer without ever losing its noir detective identity. Most importantly, though, Gotham delivers a complete journey that makes its inevitable endpoint feel earned. The eventual appearance of Batman rewards everything that came before. The ending of Gotham gives its long descent into chaos a purpose, and makes it worth revisiting again and again.
