Tesla on Autopilot/FSD was going 74 mph before fiery crash that killed 3

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that A Tesla Model Y left a parkway in El Dorado Hills, California, hit a tree and burst into flames in September, killing three of the four people inside. Tesla’s own report to federal regulators states Autopilot or “Full Self-Driving” was engaged, with the car doing 74 mph.

According to recent reports, the crash was widely covered in the Sacramento area as a fiery single-car wreck. None of that coverage mentioned a driver-assist system. It’s the latest case in our ongoing investigation into fatal crashes hidden in Tesla’s NHTSA data.

Late on Saturday, September 12, 2026, a 2024 Tesla Model Y with four people inside was heading west on Serrano Parkway, between Terracina Drive and Silva Valley Parkway. That’s a residential part of the Serrano community in El Dorado Hills, east of Sacramento.

It has been revealed that according to the CHP’s Placerville office, the Model Y left the road “for unknown reasons,” hit a tree on the north side of the parkway, and caught fire. Officers got the call around 10:38 p.m. By the time they arrived, the car was fully engulfed in flames.

It has been revealed that charles Anthony Cameron, 30, of Sacramento, was thrown from the car and died at the scene. Alejandra Mendoza, 28, of Sacramento, and Kailauna Viola Mills, 28, of Roseville, were found dead inside once firefighters put out the fire. One of the two women was driving; CHP hasn’t publicly said which.

Recent findings suggest that mills lived in Arizona and was visiting family. Her uncle told CBS Sacramento she worked in the medical field and was supposed to get married next April.

A fourth person, a 28-year-old Sacramento woman, survived with major injuries.

CHP states the cause is still under investigation. Speed, impairment, and how the car left the road haven’t been released. And nobody has noted a word about Autopilot.

Under NHTSA’s Standing General Order, automakers have to report crashes where a Level 2 driver-assist system was engaged within 30 seconds of impact and someone is killed or hurt. Tesla filed a report on this one.

It lists a 2024 Model Y, a consumer vehicle, and marks the “Engagement Status” as “Verified Engaged.” That’s Tesla confirming through its own telematics that Autopilot or FSD was on. It logs a pre-crash speed of 74 mph, a fatality, and the California Highway Patrol as the investigating agency.

Then it redacts the narrative, the software version, and whether the road was inside the system’s operating domain, all as “confidential business information,” the same treatment Tesla gives 99.9% of its reports.

The rest of the report is strangely thin. Tesla lists the roadway type as “Unknown” and what the car hit as “Unknown,” even though police said it was a tree. It checks only one source for how it learned of the crash: the media. So Tesla found out about a triple-fatal crash in one of its cars from the news, then went into its own data and confirmed its driver-assist was on.

How we matched it: Tesla logged the incident time as 05:36 UTC, which is 10:36 p.m. Pacific on September 12, within two minutes of the 10:38 p.m. call CHP responded to. The city, the 2024 Model Y, the month, and the fatal outcome all line up. It’s the only fatal Tesla driver-assist report from El Dorado Hills in the data. It’s the same crash.

Two of the three victims were found inside the car after the fire was out. The survivor got out of, or was pulled from, the wreckage, depending on the report.

It has been revealed that CHP hasn’t said whether the doors played any role, and we’re not suggesting they did. But it’s a fair question to ask about any Tesla crash with a fire, now that NHTSA is writing upcoming rules for emergency door releases after years of complaints about people getting trapped. We’ve asked.

Sources indicate that this is the second triple-fatal crash in Tesla’s latest NHTSA filings where its driver-assist was verified engaged and the public never heard about it. The other one was on I-75 in Naples. Add Vineland and July’s four fatal crashes, and the pattern is hard to miss.

But it couldn’t fill in what the car hit, and it learned about three deaths in one of its cars from the news. Then it redacted the one field, the narrative, that would tell us what the car did.

Sources indicate that tesla wants regulators and customers to trust FSD with no one watching. That trust has to be earned with data. A firm that seals the account of what its car did at 74 mph in a neighborhood, in a crash that killed three people, isn’t earning it.
