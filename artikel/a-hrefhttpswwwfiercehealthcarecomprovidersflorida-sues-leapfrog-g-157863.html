Florida’s attorney general has filed a lawsuit against hospital safety watchdog The Leapfrog Group alleging that it misled consumers about how it determines the grades assigned to hospitals, particularly those that declined to voluntarily submit data to the group.

The complaint, which was filed late last week and lists President and CEO Leah Binder alongside her organization, follows a similar suit brought by Tenet hospitals in the state that convinced a judge that nonparticipating hospitals were being “unfairly” penalized in Leapfrog’s methodology.

It has been revealed that the group was ordered to withhold safety grades for those hospitals and in grade releases since has voluntarily chosen not to award ratings to hundreds of others that did not submit recent quality data to its surveys. Leapfrog appealed that decision in September, and has stated it is reviewing a potential methodology change for upcoming gradings to incorporate non-submitting hospitals.

The Florida Office of Attorney General James Uthmeier’s lawsuit cites that case. It argues that Leapfrog’s poor scores for “at least seven Florida hospitals” misled patients, and outlined specific cases in which patients had declined care over the perceived poor performances of those hospitals.

“The Leapfrog Group told Florida patients its hospital grades were peer-reviewed and backed by experts. That was a lie,” Uthmeier remarked in a Friday release announcing the litigation. “When hospitals declined to fill out its survey, Leapfrog handed them the worst scores in the country without a shred of data, and patients refused surgery because of it. Leapfrog and its CEO should expect to be held accountable.”.

Sources indicate that the office requested that the court permanently bar Leapfrog from “misrepresenting to Florida consumers … the data, sources, methods or expert approvals underlying any hospital grade or score they publish,” or from representing the scores as peer reviewed. It also seeks to limit Leapfrog’s solicitation of charitable contributions, make corrective disclosures, and pay out other relief to providers, patients or other affected individuals and groups.

Leapfrog, in a statement to Fierce Healthcare, stated the office’s case “poses the specter of outright government censorship,” and was “piggybacking” on the Tenet hospitals’ case “with old allegations Leapfrog strongly refuted.”.

“Leapfrog looks forward to answering in court and defending our First Amendment rights and most importantly, patient safety,” the group’s statement reads. “Too many patients are harmed or killed by medical errors and infections in hospitals, and Leapfrog will always stand up for their lives as we have for 26 years.”.

The methodology change at the heart of the cases came ahead of Leapfrog’s fall 2024 safety grade publication. It more heavily weighed “imputed” scores for four of Leapfrog’s 32 safety measures that were assigned when recent safety data for the two preceding years were unavailable. The Tenet hospitals, in their case, highlighted internal communications and other testimony that questioned the validity of the changes. Both lawsuits suggest the changes were made to maximize hospital participation in Leapfrog’s survey, which would bring downstream increases to revenues it collects from allowing hospitals to advertise their grades.

Uthmeier’s office has also filed a friend of the court brief to the appellate court now considering Leapfrog’s appeal, where it similarly argues that Leapfrog’s conduct is a violation of Florida’s consumer protection laws and not protected by the First Amendment.

The latest complaint comes the same week as Leapfrog’s latest hospital safety grade release, which, similar to the spring release, withheld grades for 447 hospitals.
