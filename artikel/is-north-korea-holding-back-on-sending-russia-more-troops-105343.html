Is North Korea holding back on sending Russia more troops?

Over the summer, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy remarked that he expected between 30,000 and 50,000 North Korean soldiers would be deployed to Russia as part of Pyongyang's ongoing support of Moscow's war effort.

However, it now appears that troop buildup has yet to materialize, and it is unclear whether North Korea will continue sending large numbers of troops to Russia.

Writing on Telegram on November 9, Andrii Kovalenko, who resigned in September as head of Ukraine's Center for Countering Disinformation, said an August intelligence assessment indicated that the Kremlin had requested more than 30,000 additional North Korean troops for an autumn offensive.

He remarked a summit between North Korean leader Kim Jong Un and Russian President Vladimir Putin was expected in September, but did not happen. Kviy has claimed that North Korea previously sent thousands of troops to Russia's Kursk region in 2024, where Moscow was fighting to repel a Ukrainian incursion.

Kovalenko believes Pyongyang turned down Moscow's request because taking part in offensive operations in Russian-occupied parts of Ukraine's Donetsk region may still be a red line for North Korea.

However, Kovalenko added that Russia still relies on North Korean material support, particularly ammunition, while assessing that another major deployment of North Korean combat troops was unlikely this winter.

Han Ki-bum, a former deputy director of South Korea's National Intelligence Service, told DW that the lull in fighting and the onset of winter give Pyongyang little immediate reason to send reinforcements.

He added that a visit this week to Moscow by Jo Yong-won, a senior North Korean official and close aide to Kim, could shed light on any future plans.

Han stated it remained to be seen whether the visit was intended to deepen political ties between the ruling parties, building on their military cooperation, or to lay the groundwork for a visit by Kim himself.

Ahn Chan-il, a former North Korean soldier who defected to South Korea in 1979 and now heads the World Institute for North Korea Studies in Seoul, said Moscow also had reasons to limit the deployment.

He told DW that he expects Russia to weigh its battlefield needs against the costs of bringing in more North Korean troops. Unless the war expands, he noted, Moscow is unlikely to request a larger contingent.

Ukrainian official Kovalenko remarked North Korea still had a missile unit in Russia's Voronezh region and around 8,500 support personnel in rear areas, including drone operators, engineers and sappers.

He added that Pyongyang was ramping up ammunition production and showcasing newly introduced drones and missiles.

In a post on X on November 17, Zelenskyy stated that Russia was receiving dozens of North Korean ballistic missiles each month.

He warned that their use in Ukraine was helping Pyongyang refine its weapons and creating security risks for countries around the Korean Peninsula. Battle-testing weaponry is one of North Korea's primary advantages from military cooperation with Russia.

Kovalenko noted Pyongyang appeared to have adopted what he called a "containment strategy," limiting its military commitment to Russia's war while maintaining ties with both Moscow and Beijing.

In his assessment, this approach allows Pyongyang to retain the benefits of its alliance with Russia while reducing its exposure to the outcome of the war.

Analyst Ahn said he expects Pyongyang's military cooperation with Moscow to continue because Russian assistance is crucial to North Korea's efforts to develop drone technology and strengthen its navy.

Nevertheless, the extent of this cooperation may be more limited in the future, and Pyongyang is also able to turn to China as a hedge.

Ahn stated that if Russia limits the deployment of North Korean troops, Pyongyang's foreign currency earnings from those deployments would have less room to grow, giving it another reason to seek more support from Beijing.

Jeong Joon Hee, a former South Korean Unification Ministry spokesperson, told DW that recent moves towards building better ties with Beijing may also reflect Pyongyang's dissatisfaction with what Moscow has offered in return for North Korea's help.

By strengthening its relationship with China while keeping ties with Russia steady, he said, Pyongyang could also be trying to persuade Moscow to give it more.

According to recent reports, kim Hyung Suk, a former South Korean vice minister of unification, told DW that North Korea needs China's economic, political and diplomatic backing alongside the support it receives from Russia. He expects Pyongyang to keep pursuing China's support regardless of how it manages its role in the war.
