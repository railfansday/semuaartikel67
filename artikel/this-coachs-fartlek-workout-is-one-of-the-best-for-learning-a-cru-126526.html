This Coach’s Fartlek Workout Is One of the Best for Learning a Crucial Running Skill

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Whether you’re racing your dream marathon or tackling a weekly speed workout, finding the right pace is crucial to your success. Go out too hard and you may struggle to hold on. Run too conservatively and you might miss your goal finish.

Learning to recognize different paces takes practice, run coach Matt Fitzgerald, cofounder of 80/20 endurance, tells Runner’s World. One workout he recommends for building this skill is a fartlek he learned from fellow run coach Brad Hudson, his coauthor on the book, Run Faster from the 5K to the Marathon: How to Be Your Own Best Coach.

Moving you through several race paces, this workout challenges you to find the subtle differences between them. The goal? Building both aerobic fitness and a greater sense of pace control.

This workout helps improve your ability to settle into the right effort earlier, as well as enhance your capacity to hold those paces for longer—a perfect session for both optimizing your weekly speed run and helping you run your fastest race.

After an easy running warmup, you’ll run five faster segments, separated by an easy jog of the same duration.

It has been revealed that the base workout uses three target paces: 5K pace for the one-minute segments, 10K pace for the two-minute segments, and half-marathon pace for the three-minute segment. If you don’t know your race paces, you can also go off of rate of perceived exertion (RPE), with 10 equaling all-out effort. You should still be able to have a conversation around 4 RPE.

It has been revealed that fitzgerald states the workout should feel “fun hard,” meaning the faster running should feel challenging, but you shouldn’t finish the workout feeling like you just went all out in a race. Each surge is relatively short, so focus on hitting your intended effort rather than pushing as hard as you can.

Industry observers note that more experienced, higher-volume runners can extend the pyramid to 1-2-3-2-1-2-3-2-1, again jogging for the same duration after each faster segment. This version includes 17 minutes of faster running and 17 minutes of recovery jogging, compared to just nine minutes of each in the original version.

In Fitzgerald’s experience, many runners tend to think of their effort in two broad categories: slow and fast, but this workout challenges that by demanding more care in differentiating between faster paces.

The one-minute 5K pace efforts should be faster than the two-minute 10K pace efforts, which should be faster than the three-minute half-marathon effort, explains Fitzgerald. The differences between these three paces are small—often just a few seconds per mile—so learning to recognize what each one feels like can help you become a more efficient and more confident runner.

Before each segment, think about the effort you’re trying to find. For the first one-minute interval, Fitzgerald suggests literally imagining that you’re starting a 5K race. You wouldn’t want to sprint the opening minute of that race and flame out, so resist that urge here, too.

Try to settle into the right pace by feel, then use your watch to check and adjust. Over time, you’ll grow more attuned to the connection between how you feel and how fast you’re moving. “Your watch can’t [run the workout] for you,” Fitzgerald notes. “It can tell you how fast you’re going. It can’t tell you how fast you should go.”.

Sources indicate that learning to run by feel translates to race day, when you want to hit your goal pace. Your body’s response to the conditions and the effort helps you decide how to proceed through each mile, slowing down or speeding up.

This workout doesn’t ask you to spend a long time at any single pace. Instead, it gives you short exposures to several intensities within one session.

Hudson, who’s coached multiple Olympians and world-class athletes, tells Runner’s World that moving through a range of paces during a single workout helps with not only pace control, but also running economy or the energy required to run at a given speed.

Additionally, Hudson explains the continuous nature of the fartlek session gives it an aerobic emphasis because you never actually stop running throughout the entirety of the workout. “The faster portions [of the workout] increase oxygen demand, but as you go longer in a workout, the more it becomes aerobic, no matter what,” he claims. “If you’re keeping the running continuous, the aerobic energy systems just keep going up and up and up.”.

Fitzgerald agrees, although he also notes athletes get some anaerobic stress during the shorter, faster efforts. Think of it as a way to work on several aspects of your fitness at once, rather than a session designed to train just one. Plus, switching between paces can make the workout more mentally engaging than returning to the same effort over and over again.

The first time you try the workout, you might overshoot a target pace or find it difficult to feel the difference between efforts. That’s okay. Fitzgerald indicates trial and error is an notable part of developing the skill.

Sources indicate that when you repeat the session, look for signs that your pacing is becoming more controlled: You start each segment closer to your intended effort, settle into a steadier rhythm, and maintain a clear difference between your 5K, 10K, and half marathon paces. You may also find it easier to keep your recovery jogs consistent.

Recent findings suggest that the point is, you don’t need to nail every second of this workout. Learn from your mistakes, adjust when necessary, and remember Fitzgerald’s advice: “Progress over perfection.”.

In a recent development, it may be tempting to think of the easy jogs simply as passing time between hard segments. But both coaches emphasize the value of continuous running and implore runners not to lose focus during the recovery intervals.

Fitzgerald notes teaching your body to recover from a faster effort while still moving presents a unique challenge. Your muscles must learn how to continue producing the energy needed to keep you running efficiently while your body works to recover from the harder interval at the same time. This adaptation translates directly to race day when you have to settle back into race pace after surging to pass another runner or gliding a little too fast down a hill.

The key is paying attention to how your recovery intervals feel. If you have to slow them down more and more to prepare for the next segment, Fitzgerald says that may be a sign you’re pushing the faster portions too hard.

Industry observers note that the beauty of this workout is if you realize you’ve gone out too quickly, you can easily adjust so you can still complete the workout. If you overcooked one of your speedier segments, Fitzgerald suggests tacking on another minute or so to the easy jog until you breathe easier and lower your heart rate—then adjust your effort level on the next interval.

Recent findings suggest that because the workout touches several intensity levels, Fitzgerald recommends it during base training or in the off-season. It can help runners maintain well-rounded fitness before they begin building toward a more specific goal.

Industry observers note that it can also work as the de facto speed workout during a lighter training week or serve as a secondary quality workout during half-marathon or marathon training. In this case, the session offers a chance to maintain some faster running while the biggest emphasis remains on your long runs and race-specific efforts.

Hudson uses the workout as a key tool in returning to training post-marathon. After a week off, he generally has his athletes begin with easy running. Then, he adds this workout as a way to reintroduce different intensity levels and prepare his athletes for more demanding work ahead.

Whenever you use it, keep its purpose in mind: It’s designed to help you practice changing gears and build general fitness, and using it to support key workouts specifically tailored to your next race makes it even more effective.
