Breaking: Did a Single Router Ground the Nation’s Flights? Experts Express Doubt

🌐 Source: Cnet • 📅 16 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 5 min read (1040 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways
  • The humble internet router is at the center of another national firestorm.
  • Secretary of Transportation Sean Duffy said Sunday that a single router in Arkansas was to blame for the nearly 5-hour-long ground stop on Friday.
  • FAA investigators zeroed in on a routine network upgrade at a radio facility in El Dorado, Arkansas, where a copper wire line was being upgraded to fiber optic cable by the contractor L3Harris.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The humble internet router is at the center of another national firestorm.

Secretary of Transportation Sean Duffy said Sunday that a single router in Arkansas was to blame for the nearly 5-hour-long ground stop on Friday. It was only the second nationwide halt to US air travel since Sept. 11, 2001, leading to the cancellation of 12,462 flights on Friday alone.

FAA investigators zeroed in on a routine network upgrade at a radio facility in El Dorado, Arkansas, where a copper wire line was being upgraded to fiber optic cable by the contractor L3Harris. A new router that came online during the upgrade may have overwhelmed the older equipment, investigators say.

“Either through contractor error or a faulty piece of equipment, that router that was being replaced as we switched from copper to fiber, it started flooding other routers in our air traffic control system,” Duffy said in an interview Sunday.

This sent a barrage of data through the FAA’s internal communication system — about 1,000 connection attempts every 4 to 5 seconds – that air traffic controllers use to help guide pilots through the country’s airspace. When that fresh router was turned on, agency officials noticed the same problems at a radar site in Amarillo, Texas, which had undergone similar work weeks before, Duffy remarked. The routers at the Arkansas and Texas facilities led to 17 out of the FAA’s 22 Air Route Traffic Control Centers experiencing network instability, according to the agency.

But some aviation experts say this official explanation strains credulity.

“I don’t think it’s plausible,” Robert W. Mann Jr., the founder of airline analysis and consulting firm R.W. Mann & Company, told CNET. “Given the network architecture, which is fully duplicative and has fallback capabilities on every link and every piece of physical hardware, it’s not credible that one or even two routers would be an problem.”.

It has been revealed that the impacts of the nationwide ground stop are reverberating on the other side of the weekend. As of 3:30 p.m. EST, 333 flights within, into or out of the US have been canceled Monday, according to flight tracking website FlightAware, and more than 3,800 have been delayed.

The FAA router that grounded tens of thousands of flights looks a lot different from the one that sits in your home. These industrial-grade devices, called switches, are much larger than home Wi-Fi routers and built to withstand harsh conditions. While the agency pointed to the single router in Arkansas as the cause of Friday’s complications, it claims it disabled 60 similar units as a precaution.

In a recent development, FAA Administrator Brian Bedford said Saturday that the agency is in the process of replacing around 2,000 Cisco routers with 600 Juniper routers. Could any one of those routine equipment upgrades suddenly overload the FAA’s communications system?

In a recent development, “What surprised me most is the redundancy part for this type of national infrastructure,” Ilia Rabinovich, vice president at the cybersecurity firm Sygnia, told CNET. “This is one of the first things we discuss with clients in terms of preparedness – having backups, having redundancy connectivity, having different connections that you can migrate to different places.”.

Mann thought the timing and symptoms pointed to a new $875 million AI-supported air traffic management tool called Strategic Management of Airspace, Routes, and Trajectories, or SMART.

“What they did starting in September at Baltimore-Washington is at a very low level, for about 60 days. It had managed about 180 flights in total over 60 days,” Mann said. “And then suddenly in November, they revealed they would implement that system across all 22 continental US air traffic control centers.”.

“You went from subscale to superscale based upon 60 days of very, very limited testing, and within two days, you had the meltdown that they should have known was likely to occur,” Mann noted.

According to recent reports, on Oct. 13, more than 25 lawmakers sent a letter to the FAA asking it to pause the rollout of the tool, citing a lack of transparency and training for air traffic controllers.

“We have heard from several sources that frontline aviation safety workers were notified of the SMART tool rollout mere days in advance or not at all, giving them no time to learn how the system works, how it is incorporated into their workflow, and what oversight and accountability mechanisms maintain aviation safety standards,” the letter indicates.

Sources indicate that the FAA says in an FAQ page on the incident that the SMART system wasn’t responsible for the matter. A representative for the agency didn’t immediately respond to CNET’s request for comment.

The FAA’s acting CTO, Rebecca Guy, said at a press conference Saturday that the FAA identified the routers in Arkansas and Texas and disabled “both of those right away and were able to stabilize our network.” But the Transportation Secretary’s interviews over the weekend didn’t instill a lot of confidence in the FAA network’s resilience.

“We have old switches, old routers. We use 1980s little copper lines. It’s an old system that went down and went down everywhere. It got overloaded,” Duffy said at a Friday night news conference.

Industry observers note that the FAA has upgraded over 3,500 of its communication lines to fiber, but more than 5,000 copper lines are still scheduled to be replaced by the end of 2028. It’s eye-opening to see how reliant the FAA still is on copper lines, a technology that the Federal Communications Commission has for years deemed insufficient for average home internet users.

It didn’t take long for officials to point to funding as the ultimate cause of Friday’s groundings. Duffy repeatedly called for more funding over the weekend in order to upgrade the outdated system.

“The government and FAA are looked on as the piñata here because every time you beat on FAA or beat on the government for not doing a good job, money falls out,” Mann said. “It didn’t take, what, 6 hours, before we had a cabinet secretary basically saying if we gave him $50 billion, he would fix all this.”.

Duffy had asked for $30 billion for the FAA in September. After Friday’s fiasco, he increased his request by $20 billion.
