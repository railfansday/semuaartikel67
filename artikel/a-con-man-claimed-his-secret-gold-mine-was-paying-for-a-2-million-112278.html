Sources indicate that A man nicknamed "Death Valley Scotty" said his gold mine paid for Scotty's Castle. That wasn't true. Here's what really happened.

In the 1920s, reporters who made the trip to a remote canyon in northern Death Valley found a Spanish-style mansion rising out of the desert.

According to recent reports, walter Scott, better known as “Death Valley Scotty,” had an explanation for it. He was building the two million dollar home, he noted, with profits from his secret gold mine.

In a recent development, there was no mine. The house belonged to Albert Mussey Johnson, a Chicago insurance millionaire who had been sending Scotty money for years, according to the National Park Service.

When reporters asked Johnson about it, he agreed that Scotty owned the place and described himself simply as “Scotty’s banker.”.

A century later, the estate still carries the con man’s name. But Scotty’s Castle has been closed to regular visits since a flash flood in October 2015, and in October 2026 the park stated the earliest it could fully reopen is November 2028.

It has been revealed that scott was born in Kentucky in 1872. He ran away as a boy to join two of his brothers in Nevada, worked at the Harmony Borax Works and rode in Buffalo Bill Cody’s Wild West Show, according to the park.

After leaving the show in 1902, he turned to gold prospecting and convinced several wealthy businessmen that he had a claim to a fabulous gold mine in Death Valley.

Scott offered to split the profits if they first put up money to extract the ore. When no gold arrived, most of his investors decided they had been conned and backed out.

Recent findings suggest that in July 1905, Scotty traveled by train to Chicago with a roll of money, falsely claiming to have struck it rich in a Death Valley mine. The trip broke the travel time record and opened doors among the wealthy elite.

According to the park’s biography of Johnson, he had already invested in Scotty’s mine in 1904, and he kept sending money even after the person he sent to check on Scott reported that there was no gold mine.

In 1906, Johnson tried to visit the mine himself. On the way into the valley, his party was attacked in what became known as the Battle of Wingate Pass, and Scotty’s brother Warner was shot. The park describes Scotty as a flamboyant con artist and showman.

When Johnson finally made it to Death Valley, Scotty took him on a grueling trek by horseback, apparently expecting the desert to be too much for him.

Johnson had been badly injured in a train accident in his youth. Instead, he stayed nearly a month, and his health improved dramatically in the dry, sunny climate.

It has been revealed that he never saw the mine and was almost certainly being swindled, the park claims, but he did not seem to mind. The two men became lifelong friends.

Johnson went on to buy land in spring-fed Grapevine Canyon, about 3,000 feet above sea level, eventually owning 1,500 acres.

His wife, Bessie, suggested building something more comfortable than the canvas tents they had been using, so they could “get away from the rattlesnakes and scorpions.”.

Work on what they called Death Valley Ranch began in the 1920s, and the garage built in 1922 served as the workshop for the construction.

Scotty, who recognized a good story, told everyone the house was his. Even the concrete posts along the property’s fence were stamped with an “S” for Scotty and a “J” for Johnson, the true owner.

Construction stopped in 1931, when Johnson discovered that, because of a surveying error, he had been building on federal land.

The Great Depression was beginning to take hold, and the work never resumed. The castle was left incomplete, and its swimming pool was never finished.

Johnson died in 1948 and left most of his fortune and property to the Gospel Foundation, which he had founded two years earlier.

Scotty had the right to live at the castle for the rest of his life. He died in January 1954. Johnson had hoped the National Park Service would one day buy the property, and in 1970 it did.

For decades, rangers in period costume led tours through the house and told the story of the Johnsons and their friend.

On the night of October 18, 2015, Scotty’s Castle received 2 3/4 inches of rain, about a year’s worth, in five hours, after a half-inch the day before.

In a recent development, A flash flood of mud and rocks poured down Grapevine Canyon at an estimated 3,200 cubic feet per second, according to the park. On steep slopes like these, heavy rain can trigger flash floods faster than expected.

Rangers sheltered with about 20 stranded visitors on the rim of Ubehebe Crater that night, and one ranger watched power poles and metal dumpsters float out of the canyon. Nobody was hurt.

According to recent reports, the main house mostly escaped the flood. The historic garage, which served as the visitor center, did not. The water broke through its windows, doors and walls and left four feet of mud and rocks inside.

The unfinished pool filled many feet deep with mud and debris, and most of the 8-mile road through the canyon was destroyed.

Repairs were under way in April 2021 when contractors arrived one morning to find the garage on fire, with flames spreading to the neighboring Longshed. The garage was destroyed.

A state investigation found the fire started inside the building but could not determine a cause and found no evidence of arson.

The park has given two figures for how far the garage repairs had gone, 37% in a June 2021 release and 60% on its recovery page. One of the upgrades being installed was fire sprinklers.

In 2019, the park expected to reopen the castle in October 2021. After the fire, the target moved to 2023. It is now November 2028 at the earliest.

The park lists funding and staffing problems, legal reviews to protect historic and natural features, contracting challenges, unforeseen conditions, the pandemic, more flooding and the fire.

The repairs cost about $71 million, split into separate projects so they could be funded from different sources.

Some of the work is done. Newly introduced berms and flood walls finished in January 2023 protected the historic district when the remnants of Hurricane Hilary hit the park that August. The road through the canyon has been rebuilt.

The next phase, expected to start in 2027, covers the parking lot, the Longshed and the historic Gas Tank House, which will become a small visitor contact station in place of the burned garage.

After that, curators will return the furnishings and museum collections to the house before interior tours can resume.

Until then, the only way in is a guided tour. This season, rangers and guides from the Death Valley Natural History Association are leading exterior tours of the grounds on most Saturdays and Sundays through April 17.

Tickets are sold through the association. Bonnie Clare Road, the 8-mile route through Grapevine Canyon, remains closed to the public, including pedestrians.

Once the castle is fully reopened, the park prepares to open it each year from late fall through early spring.

The information was obtained from the National Park Service’s October 2026 announcement on Scotty’s Castle tours, its pages on the Scotty’s Castle flood and recovery, building the castle, Death Valley Scotty, Albert Mussey Johnson and the showmen of Death Valley tourism, and its releases on the 2021 fire, the fire investigation and the repairs four years after the flood.
