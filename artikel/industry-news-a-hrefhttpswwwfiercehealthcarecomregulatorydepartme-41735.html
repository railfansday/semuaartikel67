Sources indicate that the U.S. Department of Labor proposed new policy this week that would end fast-track green cards for healthcare professionals like nurses and physical therapists—the latest in a string of policies from the Trump administration raising the bar for foreign-born workers and students across healthcare and other industries.

Recent findings suggest that currently, nurses, physical therapists and other workers “with exceptional ability in the sciences or arts” are granted an exemption from an administrative test in the permanent labor certification program (PERM), a months-long administrative process in which an employer must prove there are no able, willing, qualified and available U.S. workers for a permanent job ahead of sponsoring a foreign worker.

The exemption, referred to Schedule A, is given to occupations where the DOL recognizes a nationwide shortage of U.S. workers. Nurses and physical therapists have been listed under Schedule A for more than 30 years.

The Department of Labor, in its newly proposed rule, remarked it wants to eliminate Schedule A as it cannot “demonstrate that the occupations at the moment listed in Schedule A are still shortage occupations.”.

It cited in the proposed rule a late 2023 request for information published under the Biden administration seeking input on potentially expanding Schedule A. Despite more than 2,000 responses, DOL now says it didn’t receive the information it would need to establish a methodology for regularly reviewing and updating Schedule A occupations, and questioned whether it has the statutory authority to make such occupational exemptions in the first place.

According to recent reports, “The elimination of Schedule A would enable the Department to better ensure that U.S. workers have an opportunity to apply for available job opportunities, and adhere to the statutory requirements to protect U.S. workers assigned to it, as intended by Congress under the [Immigration and Nationality Act].”.

In a recent development, the proposed rule, which is open to public comment for 60 days, also includes requirements for employers to advertise their open jobs more broadly and for a longer period of time to demonstrate a good-faith effort to recruit American workers before turning to a green card sponsorship. These and other proposed changes would be the first “comprehensive” reforms to the PERM program in almost two decades, the Department of Labor remarked.

If finalized as-is, employers who want to sponsor professional nurses, physical therapists and other affected roles “would be required to go through the lengthier and costlier PERM labor market testing process, adding time, cost and administrative work to a pathway that hospitals, nursing homes and rehabilitation providers have historically relied on to fill persistent shortages,” Caroline Tang, an Austin shareholder at labor and employment law firm Ogletree Deakins, told Fierce Healthcare.

It has been revealed that industry associations as well as other sections of the government have widely projected shortages in the healthcare workforce, including nurses, for the coming years. The Heart Resources and Services Association’s National Center for Health Workforce Analysis, for instance, projects (PDF) national shortages of registered nurses through at least 2038, with a projected 8% shortage in 2028 that is more severe in non-metro areas.

The proposed policy change is among several brought by the Trump administration in line with an order from the White House to prioritize domestic hiring—though many have been stymied in the courts.

According to recent reports, among the most prominent is a $100,000 supplemental fee the Department of Homeland Security attached to H-1B petitions that was vacated by two separate judged, and in August was succeeded by the somewhat altered proposal of an additional H-1B petition fee of $103,265. Another policy from the same department tightening how long nonimmigrant students may remain in the country (referred to as “duration of status”) was temporary blocked hours before implementation in September and fully vacated on Monday (PDF), though the former decision is already being appealed.

The Department of Labor has also proposed increasing the prevailing wage floors for several green cards that legal experts expect will be challenged by employers that would shoulder the resulting financial burden.
