📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that some travelers spent the entirety of Friday night in airport terminals. Others scrambled to find rental cars, bus tickets, or another flight home. Even after the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) lifted its nationwide ground stop, airlines canceled thousands of flights on Saturday as they tried to get planes and crews back where they needed to be.

In a recent development, by Monday, President Donald Trump was facing a different question. Had his focus on White House construction projects distracted him from the travel chaos? Trump brushed off the suggestion, defended the upcoming ballroom and helicopter landing pad, and blamed former President Joe Biden and aging air traffic control equipment.

It has been revealed that "I don’t take any responsibility," he told reporters Nov. 16, saying Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy was in charge. He argued that money had been wasted and stated the government could replace the old equipment while keeping the current system running, provided Congress funded the work. "What you saw on Friday was very, very old equipment that had a heart attack," he told reporters Nov. 16.

In a recent development, when a reporter asked what he was doing while flights were grounded, Trump remarked he was at the White House meeting with Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy and others involved in the response. Later, when pressed on whether his focus on White House construction had distracted him from the disruption, Trump defended the latest helicopter landing pad and the ballroom under construction.

According to recent reports, "If you take a look at the White House, it's flawless...we had 18 broken windows. The plaster, the exterior plaster, was falling off the wall and it was an embarrassment," he noted. "The helipad has gotten great reviews." Recent findings suggest that at the end of the interview, when asked again about the outage, Trump mentioned, "Incredibly, everybody was safe. Nobody was hurt. No crashes, no nothing." Trump argued that the system should have been upgraded decades ago and said he expected the disruption to spur Congress to fund the overhaul.

Industry observers note that over the weekend, Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy walked through how the problem spread. FAA contractor L3Harris was replacing an aging copper line with fiber at a remote site when a router began flooding the agency’s network with more than 1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds.

According to recent reports, "What that did was give us complications with our communication with airplanes and it also caused some disruption with our radar feeds," Duffy noted. The FAA paused departures nationwide, and Duffy said the more than 3,000 flights already in the air landed safely.

It has been revealed that on NBC's Meet the Press on Sunday, Duffy stated investigators were still looking at whether the contractor made a mistake during installation or the router itself was faulty. Asked whether he owed travelers an apology, Duffy pointed to decades of underinvestment under both parties. "It happened on my watch," he said, adding that "I can’t apologize for what was passed off to us.".

The funding debate was already underway before travelers got stuck. Congress approved $12.5 billion for air traffic control upgrades in 2025, and Duffy notes that money is earmarked for things like radar and radios. On Thursday, one day before the ground stop, he and White House budget director Russell Vought asked Congress for another $30.1 billion.

Part of that request would go toward replacing the communications network that Friday’s router problem overwhelmed.
