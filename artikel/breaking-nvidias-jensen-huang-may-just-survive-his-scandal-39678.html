Breaking: Nvidia’s Jensen Huang May Just Survive His Scandal

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a recent development, exactly three years ago today, the tech world found itself in an all-too-familiar crisis. On an otherwise ordinary Friday afternoon, the board of OpenAI abruptly fired Sam Altman, following a secretive deliberation about what was believed to be a pattern of deceptive behavior by the CEO. A shock wave of panic rippled through the industry: Employees rallied around Altman, who set up a makeshift war room in his Russian Hill home; investors made furious calls to the board to demand a reversal; and Satya Nadella offered to create the conditions within Microsoft for Altman and his loyalists to rebuild OpenAI from the ground up.

It has been revealed that as the members of OpenAI’s board racked their brains for who might make a worthy replacement, the extent of their miscalculation quickly dawned on them. OpenAI could not survive without Altman, and the tech industry might not survive without OpenAI. Within four days of Altman’s firing (an event so fleeting that it came to be known as “the blip”), he was reinstated as CEO, and the board coup that failed to kill him only made him stronger.

Three years later, the foibles of a different man at a different AI organization—Jensen Huang, who resigned from his post as CEO of Nvidia last week after the revelation of several compromising affairs—have thrown the industry and the stock market into tumult.

It has been revealed that not one to go down without a fight, Huang has reportedly hunkered down with his inner circle to plot his own comeback. Meanwhile, a petition to reinstate him, pending an independent investigation not unlike the one that exonerated Altman, has garnered nearly 200,000 signatures, including from the likes of Elon Musk, Travis Kalanick, and Garry Tan. Even President Trump has weighed in, noting that he “would certainly not mind” if Huang were reinstated. The news from today’s earnings report that the firm had brought in a record-breaking $100 billion+ in sales last quarter under Huang’s leadership will only help their cause.

Sources indicate that while an inside source told CNBC yesterday that Nvidia’s board is not considering reinstating Huang, the former executive is continuing to court public opinion independently. “I’ve seen an outpouring of calls from Nvidia’s employees, investors, business partners, and customers for me to return as CEO,” Huang stated in a public statement Tuesday. “If the board of directors decides that my return is in the best interest of our shareholders, our employees, the company, and America, I would be honored to answer these calls and return as CEO.”.

It would be difficult to overstate Huang’s influence on the AI industry and US tech policy at large.

This entire mess has felt uncannily validating of the thesis behind Vanity Fair’s New Establishment list this fall, for which I wrote, “Never in the list’s history have the scale and the stakes of individual influence felt so high,” noting the “Ouroboros of investments and insular networks that make you realize how small and self-serving circles of power can be—and how swiftly they could come tumbling down.”.

We’ve officially entered the age of the too-major-to-fail CEO, in which our economy increasingly hinges on—and endlessly forgives—the moods and misdeeds of a handful of powerful men with few accountability structures. It’s not just the companies they founded that have become entwined with these founders. “The whole economy has basically been financed by Nvidia and in turn by Jensen’s ability to raise money,” says Yoni Rechtman, a partner at Slow Ventures.

Sources indicate that huang deserves a great deal of credit for having built his company from the ground up. But his trespasses—namely, becoming romantically involved with someone the State Department has linked to Chinese intelligence, and who has undeniably helped get America’s high-performance chips into the hands of our adversaries—are more than worthy of the scrutiny they’ve received.

Industry observers note that like a person who’s sick with a life-threatening illness but refuses to make a will, Huang demonstrated a staggeringly hubristic leadership. He had no real succession plan, installed a board and executive staff of yes-men and women, and created a “culture of omertà,” as his biographer, Stephen Witt, put it, that allowed his affairs to become an open secret within the firm. Nvidia didn’t even protect itself with basic D&O insurance, which would have shielded it from the kinds of shareholder lawsuits it could now face. The Dow, S&P 500, and Nasdaq have all continued their precipitous decline since the news of his departure was announced last week.

As a result, how many of us have found ourselves asking, Why should the fate of my 401(k) have anything to do with one man’s sex life?

If you need another example, consider Musk, who has gained almost single-handed control over our space and satellite infrastructure. At SpaceX, he’s also become a master of the newly popular dual-class voting structures that have allowed founders like himself and Mark Zuckerberg to consolidate control of their companies. On more than one occasion, his erratic whims have left our geopolitical fate hanging in the balance.

In a recent development, perhaps start-ups do need these larger-than-life characters to get off the ground. As Rechtman puts it to me, “Great businesses don’t get built by committee. They get built by small groups of people and singular leaders. And necessarily, in the pursuit of some huge and lofty project, a person becomes elevated to the status of ‘great.’”.

But as companies mature—and especially as they go public—they owe it to themselves and to investors to outgrow their founders. Plenty of the companies keeping our economy afloat, like Apple, Amazon, Microsoft, and Netflix, have indeed survived the parting of their founders.

Unfortunately, public markets are now falling victim to the same hype frenzy as VCs. AI-linked stocks now account for nearly half of the S&P 500, and 47 of those stocks made up three quarters of the index’s gains in the first half of this year. Nvidia, in one way or another, is connected to just about all of those companies through an intricate web of loans, partnerships, and investments.

In a recent development, so how do we deal with tech’s “great man” problem? This extreme concentration of power has helped foster a startling lack of resilience in our geopolitical and economic systems. But ask any antitrust expert, and they’ll remind you that it can also erode product quality, endanger consumer protections, and raise prices.

The answer cannot simply be for these men to improve their moral character, and it cannot be for us to become more forgiving. After all, any one of them could drop dead of a heart attack and leave us in the same predicament.

Walter Isaacson, the Silicon Valley biographer who is now interviewing many of the field’s “great men” on his newly introduced podcast, The Builders, believes the only possible answer “is to have more entrepreneurs who can build competing products, who can shoot off rockets and get them into orbit and create satellites and create chips and build fabrication plants.”.

Rumors are already circulating that if he does not return to Nvidia, Huang may start his own competitive chip manufacturer, which helps explain why VCs are lining up to demonstrate their goodwill.

It has been revealed that in the meantime, as we prepare for more mega IPOs, we would be wise to examine the structure of these deals to see what checks on power exist within these companies, from strong succession looks to empowered boards. Unfortunately, Anthropic is preparing super-voting shares for founders, which will give them decisive control over the company following its $1.4 trillion IPO. But hey, at least there are seven of them.
