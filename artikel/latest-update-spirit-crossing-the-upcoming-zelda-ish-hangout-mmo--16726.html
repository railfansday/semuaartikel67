According to recent reports, newly introduced multiplayer game Spirit Crossing offers up a visually blissful, but inwardly draconian world in which other people are not allowed to talk to you – indeed, not permitted access to the visible electromagnetic spectrum – unless you have given them a high five. By default, players appear to each other as shadows, all exploring a skybound landscape of magic animals and layer-cake mountains that reminds me of both Sable and The Legend of Zelda: Tears of the Kingdom.

Sources indicate that deign to slap somebody's hand, and they'll pop into colour and be able to join you for various activities. For example, going fishing, playing instruments round the campfire, playing dress-up and pulling emotes in group photos, bouncing around on strangely inflated yaks, or questing forth to heal troubled spirits. There's some material gathering and crafting, and there are recurring storms that make the ambience more threatening, but overcoming a challenge doesn't feel like the point of Spirit Crossing. The "missions" are just an excuse to hang out together.

There's nothing forcing you to do any of this in company, mind. "Stay a mysterious shadow, lend a hand when you feel like it, and disappear before anyone can make it weird," the devs explain, adding a hug emoji to make plain that they don't think you're some kind of smelly misanthrope for shunning other players.

I, personally, am a smelly misanthrope, and will only ever appear in your game as a skulking opacity, peering through the window of your personal gingerbread house, with its tastefully accessorised lava lamps and throw rugs. No, I will not join you for a group hang-glider tour. No, I do not want to play the banjo with you. You will experience me only as the nagging sensation of being watched, while you chat to the local "Stylesmith" about fetching rucksack patterns.

Spirit Crossing has an upfront cost that "gives players full access to the complete game experience in perpetuity", but you can also take out a subscription for some cosmetic items and access to a "limited resource exchange shop". They're avoiding traditional microtransactions, which is nice.

It has been revealed that spry Fox are also the developers of Cozy Grove - "the best grove since Byker", wrote Graham, in one of his regular 'senior moments'. I myself know them as the studio behind wintry puzzler Road Not Taken, with its randomised woodlands, and block-merging city builder Triple Town, with its unexpectedly terrifying bears. Little about Spirit Crossing seems terrifying, unless you count the shadow loitering at the edge of your conversation pit.

It has been revealed that you can read more about the game on Steam. I find Spirit Crossing quite novel, especially when it dispenses with the WholeCosy "discover your inner fashonista" stuff and leans into the supernatural elements: I like the idea of hunkering by the campfire during a storm, watching the clouds hurtle past. Still, I'm disappointed to hear that Spry Fox have made use of generative AI during its creation.

Recent findings suggest that as has been pointed out to us on Bluesky, the developers called on Anthropic's Claude tools for various behind-the-scenes tasks - the example they give in a Discord post is editing several config files at once. There's no disclosure on the game's Steam page because Spry Fox haven't used AI tools to generate any of the art or music - Valve rewrote Steam's disclosure requirements in January to exempt the usage of generative AI for "efficiency gains", which the above presumably falls under.

It's hardly a full-throated endorsement of generative AI, but it still forms part of a political and cultural project I consider reckless and morbid. For a longer account of my objections, albeit one that doesn't cover Claude in particular, here's my longform thinkywangle from July last year.
