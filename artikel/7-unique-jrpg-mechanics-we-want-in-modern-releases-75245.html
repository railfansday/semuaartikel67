7 Unique JRPG Mechanics We Want in Modern Releases

🌐 Source: Dualshockers • 📅 20 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 6 min read (1219 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

  • When I first played Vagrant Story, I barely knew what I was doing.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that JRPGs are evolving in a fairly consistent way. While modern titles keep several staples of the genre, such as a fixed story, a focus on character development, innovation in the battle system, and some wacky progression systems, sometimes it feels like they leave behind certain mechanics that could be put to great use today. Many JRPGs of yesteryear featured unique mechanics that were either never re-explored, or, if they were, remained confined to indie games and flew under the radar of many players.

Today, I want to explore some JRPG mechanics I would love to see in modern releases. Most are battle-related, though a few, if properly implemented, could expand exploration and even improve how the game's systems interact. That is not to say they have never appeared in other games throughout the years, but I would love to see them become much more mainstream.

When I first played Vagrant Story, I barely knew what I was doing. When chaining attacks, you can aim at enemies' limbs, yet all I ever did was try to go straight for the head or body to defeat the foe as fast as possible. How naive I was, because against bosses, that mindless strategy never paid off, especially if I was not wielding an appropriate weapon.

This mechanic of targeting different body parts has popped up in other JRPGs, though usually you can only hit distinct areas when an enemy is preparing a specific attack. I would love to see a JRPG reuse this mechanic exhaustively, letting players willingly target different enemy limbs and trigger distinct tactical outcomes.

According to recent reports, one of Star Ocean's greatest strengths is its Item Creation system. To me, gaining levels wasn't about learning latest combat skills, but it was all about earning points to invest in Customization and unlock new crafting potential, whether forging an endgame weapon at the start of the game or diving into other side activities. Still, the most entertaining feature, even at the cost of my relationship with my party members, was Pickpocketing.

In a recent development, leveling up your Pickpocketing skill lets you steal from every NPC in town and score the wildest variety of items. The sheer thrill of not knowing what you would get, whether a simple consumable or an insanely overpowered piece of gear, was endlessly fun and had me making the rounds through town as if I were living inside GTA. As mentioned, doing so affects how your party members perceive the protagonist, so if you are aiming for a specific ending, watch out for the consequences of your actions.

According to recent reports, this entry is less about battle mechanics and more about dungeon delving. As much as I love modern JRPGs, especially for their evolution in combat gameplay, narrative, and character development, I feel some are still a bit lacking in the level design department. Too many dungeons are simply streamlined corridors, featuring an occasional puzzle that solves itself before you even begin thinking through the solution.

According to recent reports, plenty of classic JRPGs boasted incredible level design, but if I had to highlight just one, it would be Wild Arms. This series blended the interactive tools of action-adventure games and let the scope of exploration expand incrementally. Moreover, acquiring new tools encouraged backtracking and re-exploring older dungeons, much like a Metroidvania, to hunt down hidden treasures.

I never had much contact with Sega consoles growing up, which led me to neglect their JRPG catalog for quite a while. I played Phantasy Star IV fairly recently, and during my playthrough, a friend told me to set up Macros using specific skills while emphasizing that the execution order mattered. So I complied, and when I stepped into battle, oh boy, what a beautiful surprise.

Industry observers note that for those who don't know, depending on the sequence in which you cast spells and skills, they fuse together to unleash an entirely new combo attack. It is a deeply entertaining combo system that later surfaced in future JRPGs like Breath of Fire IV. It feels distinct from two characters simply chaining standard turns together, because there is a real sense of mystique in discovering the combinations, and I will confess that without a GameFAQs guide, I never would have figured them all out. Still, it is pretty amazing and elevated turn-based combat to new heights.

According to recent reports, staying on the topic of skill combos, Chrono Trigger pulled off something similar yet distinctly its own. If two or three party members mastered specific techniques and had their ATB gauges filled simultaneously, they could execute a Double or Triple Tech, each carrying a unique effect. The very first time I discovered this, as I imagine most players did, was with Crono and Lucca's Fire Whirl. A whole upcoming world of tactical possibilities opened up in that exact moment.

Sources indicate that chrono Trigger pushed the concept even further, and just when I expected the game to stick to Double Techs, the JRPG introduced Triple Techs. I don't know about you, but I built my entire party around these Techs, and luckily, nearly every character paired well with the rest of the roster. Triple Techs were slightly more restrictive, yet they still allowed for remarkably efficient setups, especially when hunting for Techs capable of healing the entire party at once.

Industry observers note that breath of Fire is my favorite JRPG series, and within it, Breath of Fire III stands as my absolute favorite entry. Whenever people ask me why, a massive part of my reasoning rests on its Gene splicing system, a feature Breath of Fire IV dropped for reasons I still don't understand, which was a real shame. I loved transforming into dozens of distinct dragons, even if Ryu and Fou-Lu's half-dragon forms looked awesome in their own right.

Industry observers note that essentially, you discovered Dragon Genes scattered across the world and could combine up to three at once to transform Ryu into wildly different dragon forms, each boasting unique strengths and abilities. The thing is, I don't necessarily need the exact Gene system back, but I would love a transformation-focused JRPG where characters can shift forms mid-battle and completely alter the flow of combat. Even better if those transformations feature distinct variations that actively reward exploration.

I know there are many, many caveats surrounding the progression mechanics of Final Fantasy VIII, though I feel the bulk of player complaints stems from the Draw system rather than the Junction system itself. There is a historical reason behind that, as Hiroyuki Ito, creator of both the ATB and Junction systems, left the Final Fantasy VIII team before finalizing the battle mechanics to direct Final Fantasy IX, leaving his original vision somewhat incomplete.

Sources indicate that just imagine what it could have been if fully realized. The Junction system is remarkably dynamic. Beyond simply slotting spells to boost each character's raw stats, you could attach status ailments to basic physical attacks, build elemental resistances, tailor one character toward magic and agility while turning another into a pure strength powerhouse, and so on. Pair that flexibility with specialized commands learned through Guardian Forces, and what appears on the surface to be standard turn-based combat suddenly gains deep strategic layers.
