Breaking: Harvard Study Finds Fossil Fuels Power Most US Hyperscale Data Centers

A Harvard-led study published November 16, 2026 in Nature Sustainability estimates that 403 US hyperscale data centers consumed approximately 82 terawatt-hours (TWh) of electricity under its central scenario, corresponding to about 1.8% of total US electricity consumption.

Fossil fuels supplied roughly 54% of attributed generation, mainly natural gas at 38% and coal at 15%. The central estimate for attributable carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions was approximately 25.7 million metric tons, within a scenario range of 21–31 million metric tons.

Across facility-load scenarios, estimated electricity consumption spanned approximately 68–99 TWh. Harvard mentioned the upper estimate approaches annual electricity use in states such as Indiana, Michigan, and Tennessee.

Industry observers note that the estimates cover May 2024 through April 2025. Researchers used satellite imagery to compile the facility list and modeled annual electricity consumption for each site under a range of operating scenarios. They then used Environmental Protection Agency data to attribute emissions according to the mix of power plants in each facility’s regional grid.

The paper describes its headline accounting as a location-based, total-output balancing-authority attribution method. In practice, the approach allocates generation and emissions from plants in the relevant regional grid to the facilities. The result is a facility-resolved estimate of electricity sources and emissions, rather than direct meter readings from every site.

On the paper’s electricity-weighted, total-output measure, carbon intensity was approximately 314 grams of CO2 per kilowatt-hour, compared with a US total-output average of approximately 348 grams. The lower aggregate sits alongside substantial regional variation: about a quarter of the electricity was consumed in grid regions with carbon intensity above the national average, and many facilities were concentrated in areas where coal-fired generation contributes heavily to supply.

According to recent reports, virginia, Oregon, Ohio, and Iowa together accounted for more than half of the electricity use examined. A national average therefore cannot represent every campus; the emissions attributed to a facility depend on the generation mix of its regional grid.

In a recent development, available data did not cover every US hyperscale data center, and the researchers could not separate electricity used for AI-specialized operations from general-purpose computing at individual facilities. The facilities support services including cloud computing, large language model training, and global deployment of AI tools, so the totals cannot be interpreted as AI-only consumption.

Lead author Gianluca Guidi said in Harvard’s announcement that the reported electricity use and carbon footprint “could already be substantially larger now in 2026.” That is a projection based on facilities built and campuses expanded after the study period, together with continued growth in AI computing demand, rather than a new measurement.

The study did not directly measure health effects, which the researchers plan to investigate in future work. Corresponding author Francesca Dominici described two pathways: carbon dioxide drives climate change, affecting health through heat waves, wildfire smoke, flooding, and other pathways; fossil-fuel plants also release fine particulate matter, nitrogen oxides, and sulfur dioxide linked to heart and lung disease and premature death. People living near and downwind of those plants may bear health costs even when they are far from the data center itself.

Dominici remarked the goal is to give the public, policymakers, investors, and developers a more concrete basis for decisions about where data centers go and how they are powered. The framework moves that accounting beyond a single national total to regional grids and their power plants—the level at which hyperscale siting, electricity supply, and attributable emissions meet.
