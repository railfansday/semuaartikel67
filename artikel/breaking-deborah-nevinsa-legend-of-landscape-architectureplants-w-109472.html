Sources indicate that when my partner and I were starting a family, we searched high and low for a place outside Fresh York where we could imagine our future children growing up. We looked upstate, in Connecticut, and at all those charming hamlets strung along the eastern end of Long Island known as the Hamptons. Toured hundreds of houses, clicked thousands of listings. But not one felt like home.

Nevins in her garden in East Hampton.

It looked like something out of a medieval fairy tale. A great furrowed trunk rising from a froth of flowering vines that split into enormous limbs sweeping improbably far across the garden. Its arrangement was so baroque—veiny, muscular wood twisting and reaching, dissolving overhead into a canopy of green delicacy. We could picture our children climbing its winding branches, little hands against its ridged, mossy bark.

Industry observers note that our twins’ arrival was imminent, so we made an offer in February, closed in April, and brought home babies Grace and Noah on Memorial Day weekend. Now we joke that we didn’t buy a house—we bought this tree.

But what is it? A butternut tree? An oak? A black walnut? Deborah Nevins, one of the most revered living landscape designers, created this garden for the previous owners in 2004. In the fall, she invited me to her white-shingled cottage in Springs, East Hampton, to find out. Her living room has triple-hung windows like those in Monticello, Thomas Jefferson’s house in Virginia, which go down to the floor so it’s easy to wheel in her potted lemon trees when the seasons change. “Winter is divine; it’s when they bloom,” she coos. “The house smells of lemon blossoms.”.

David Geffen’s pool at his former Beverly Hills estate, once home to Warner Bros.’ Jack Warner.

“Butternut!” she declares, slamming the book shut with all the force of her petite five-foot-nothing frame. “For sure.”.

This is our tree. A butternut that likely sprouted generations before she came along. “It’s a native tree, probably a hundred years old. But I’d have to know the caliper—the diameter about a foot up—to be more precise.” She decided it’d be the anchor for our yard design the moment she saw it—existing mature trees are “sacred” and must be preserved at all costs. At its base, she dared to plant clematis, usually a climbing vine, that now rambles across the ground, softening the old tree’s trunk with a tangle of flowers. The butternut divides the rest of the property into distinct passages, including an ornamental reflecting pool bordered by hornbeam hedges, a lap pool shaded under locust trees, and a gravel path lined with rounded boxwoods.

GREEK MYTHOLOGY It took Nevins eight years and some 20 trips to Greece to complete her collaboration with architect Renzo Piano on the Stavros Niarchos Foundation Cultural Center. What was once a parking lot is now planted with more than 1,500 trees, 128,000 shrubs and perennials, and 164,000 grass plugs.

A rendering of the 40-acre park at the Stavros Niarchos Foundation Cultural Center in Athens.

To Nevins, the tree is an excellent example of her greater gardening philosophy: “I’m interested in creating unfolding spaces which intensify [the viewers’] experience, your sense of place and movement through a garden,” she claims. She frequently builds hedges and allées to control viewers’ attention, like horse blinders. “Not every aspect of the garden is seen at once. You have to stop and think.”.

Sources indicate that over the last four decades, Nevins has been entrusted with the grounds of some extraordinary spaces. Her clients include media mogul David Geffen, fashion designer Tommy Hilfiger, former Disney chairman Michael Eisner, and hotelier Ian Schrager. Commissions can range from tiny residential spaces (like mine) to huge commercial projects abroad. It took her eight years and some 20 trips to Greece to complete a 40-acre park at the Stavros Niarchos Foundation Cultural Center in Athens. That was a collaboration with architect Renzo Piano on a landscape that climbs over the buildings themselves, planted with native grasses from the hills of Attica and fragrant Mediterranean herbs, including lavender, rosemary, and oregano. Nevins’s mantra for the project was simple: “Speak Greek.” The execution was anything but simple. More than 1,500 trees, 128,000 shrubs and perennials, and 164,000 grass plugs fill the public work. Nevins conceptualized the totality of the park. “I chose every single tree!” she states. “We had to propagate hundreds of thousands of plants throughout Greece, and we decided to hire various nurseries so that if there was a disease or fire, the entire crop would not be wiped out.” Her effort impressed Piano. “It is always special when, beyond the work on a project itself, you realize that you share the same values and ideas,” says Piano of Nevins, whom he describes as a “wonderful professional and human adventure.”.

“There is discipline in her work but never rigidity,” says Scott Mitchell, the West Coast–based architect who met Nevins at a dinner honoring architect Richard Meier in 1997 and went on to work with her on properties for Geffen in both California and Upcoming York. “Her landscapes don’t compete with architecture; they give it a place to belong.”.

Sources indicate that he recalls the “emotional power” of her work for Geffen at the Warner estate in Beverly Hills, former home of Warner Bros. cofounder Jack Warner. “Endless sweeps of white azaleas, a driveway lined with ancient, gnarled native sycamores, and 11 acres of rolling meadow beneath giant coast live oaks,” Mitchell states. “World-class projects require cohesion between architecture and landscape. They have to play from the same sheet of music. Debby totally gets that. Her response to architecture is clear and harmonious—confident but never overstepping.”.

Tommy Hilfiger and Dee Ocleppo at their Mustique estate.

In a recent development, gil Schafer, a Upcoming York–based architect who has worked with Nevins on more than 25 houses and gardens, also commends her command of structure. At his own house near Millbrook, she built a 12-foot-high hornbeam enclosure nestled into the surrounding farm fields that has, he claims, “an almost abstract sculptural quality—as if Richard Serra had suddenly decided to work in hornbeam rather than Corten steel.”.

Nevins was born in Manhattan at what was then the Doctors Hospital on the Upper East Side. The family resided in the penthouse of the same building where Greta Garbo lived. Her father died of a brain tumor at 43, when Nevins was six months old, and so her mother had to return to work. At six, she was sent to Camp Taconic in Massachusetts. “I was tiny! My mother might have been arrested today.” She remembers walking in the woods, finding a strawberry, and reporting the discovery in a letter home. The seed had been planted—pun quite obvious.

Fleur Cowles, the formidable editor behind the short-lived, extravagantly inventive magazine Flair, was a maternal relative and had an outsize impact on her childhood. “She knew everyone, and she was so brilliant,” Nevins states, offering me an issue of Flair from another bookshelf like a cherished family heirloom. Another influence was her mother’s sister, Miriam Sampson, an interior decorator who lived across from Carnegie Hall. “In this massive apartment, she had her office in one room, and she had little models of apartments. I was so intrigued,” Nevins recalls. She’d cut out pictures of interiors and gardens from magazines and assemble them in scrapbooks. “But I didn’t even know about this idea of being a landscape architect.”.

In a recent development, when Nevins was 14, her mother remarried and the family moved to Upcoming Haven, Connecticut. She went on to attend Boston University for undergrad and returned to New York for graduate school at Columbia. “They didn’t think that landscape history, which is what I wanted to study, was academic enough. And they probably had no one to teach it anyway,” she notes. So she earned an MA and an MPhil in art history. Travel became a form of individualized study. “I learned by looking,” she says. She sought out Le Corbusier’s buildings in France, including La Tourette, Maisons Jaoul, and Villa Savoye, and drove from Paris to Santiago de Compostela looking at Romanesque and Merovingian churches. She made her first of many pilgrimages to Sissinghurst, a garden in Kent created by the writer Vita Sackville-West and her diplomat husband, Harold Nicolson. “There’s nothing more beautiful than Sissinghurst,” she sighs.

One of Nevins’s greatest champions has been Geffen, whom Victoria Newhouse, the renowned architecture critic and widow of longtime Condé Nast chairman Si Newhouse, introduced her to. On her first meeting with Geffen, Nevins arrived to present her work with a carousel of 80 slides. “After maybe 30 slides, he states, ‘That’s enough. You’re hired.’ And I thought to myself, Who is this guy?” When she looked him up, she read that one of his attributes is spotting talent. “And that was that.”.

At the Warner estate in Beverly Hills, she removed one swimming pool to open up the landscape, built a new one with a new patio house where Warner had a putting green, and relocated several mature oak trees on the property. “David is not afraid of big moves,” she notes. (They preserved the fountains and terraces Warner had commissioned from Florence Yoch, who designed the grounds of Tara for Gone With the Wind, of course.) However, according to Nevins, when Warner’s daughter came to see what Geffen had done, she looked around and asked if they had changed anything. “ ‘What did we change? We changed everything!’ ” Nevins remembers Geffen saying. “The idea that we didn’t do a thing is crazy, but that it looked like it was meant to be—that’s a good thing. The ultimate compliment that looks like I did nothing.”.

An aerial view of Deborah Nevins’s Hamptons garden.

Hilfiger hired Nevins for a property in Mustique in the early 1990s, and again for his Denbigh Farm in Greenwich in 1995. In the Caribbean, she created palm promenades and a courtyard of white flowers; in Connecticut, she brought the elements of an old estate into a coherent whole. “I knew she was incredibly passionate about her work, and that always interested me because I love working with people who are passionate about what they do,” Hilfiger says. “Her execution was impeccable, and she delivered exactly what she promised.”.

Nevins’s office is based in Fresh York City, and she keeps between 5 and 10 new projects going at all times. “Almost any job is interesting unless I hear in advance that the people aren’t nice,” she says. “Also, art collectors, in my experience, have been great patrons. If you’re an art collector—and you’re truly not collecting just to make money from art—you have an interest in beauty and understanding.”.

PLEACHY KEEN “She is very intuitive, so she needs to walk a site and feel what it has to say,” architect Gil Schafer notes of Nevins. “She feels this deep kinship with the natural world, with plants and with the trees of a site, which guides how she approaches her interventions on a property.”.

Industry observers note that on average, it takes two years to get a garden up and running, three years if she needs to build new structures. But a garden is never finished. Roberta Denning, who with her husband, Steve, commissioned Nevins for her family’s property in Greenwich in 2006, describes the pleasure of living with something that acquires depth while continuing to change. “What she created has grown more valuable with time,” Denning notes of her property, which includes a walking labyrinth among chestnuts and beech trees. “Nearly 20 years on, the gardens feel both that they have always been here and that they are continually renewed.” Nevins says clients have joked that hiring her is like a marriage because it’s a long-term relationship, and it requires work. “So I guess I’m a polygamist?” she smiles.

It has been revealed that patience is a massive part of her process. “She is very intuitive, so she needs to walk a site and feel what it has to say, and this is one way that we are so similar in our approach,” Schafer says. “You need to give her the space to read the land and sort out how she wants to approach it. I think she also feels this deep kinship with the natural world, with plants and with the trees of a site, which guides how she approaches her interventions on a property.”.

Industry observers note that people joke that Nevins speaks to the plants, but Schafer isn’t sure it’s an act. “I think she really does!”.

Industry observers note that “I do love them,” Nevins says of her botanical family. “I was at a nursery with someone the other day, and I noted, ‘It’s problematic. I get crushes on plants, but sometimes they don’t really have a place, and it’s so disappointing. It’s like a failed romance.’”.

A walking labyrinth in Greenwich, Connecticut.

Currently her crush is Stewartia pseudocamellia. “It has amazing bark, and the flower looks like a camellia, but it’s not a camellia, it’s a tree.” One plant she doesn’t love? Flowering forsythia, which Schafer reports she often refers to as “spring vomit.”.

According to recent reports, nevins believes her greatest asset is the ability to blend both architectural history and botanical skill. “I have such a strong academic background in architectural history,” she says. “I know exactly what these references are that they make to historical, even modern historical buildings.”.

In another part of Long Island, Nevins combined the elements into what she calls a “beech house”—pun absolutely intentional. Martin Filler, an architecture critic and longtime contributor to The Fresh York Review of Books, met Nevins when they were graduate students in Columbia’s art history department in the early 1970s. At the time, his wife, Rosemarie Haag Bletter, an architectural historian and professor emerita of art history at the CUNY Graduate Center in New York City, was working on her PhD and teaching undergraduates. According to Filler, Nevins promised back then to design them a garden one day. “Since we didn’t have a house and never expected to, we mentioned to ourselves, ‘Yeah, right,’ ” he states. “But who knew that someday we would, and that gardening would become such a large part of our lives, especially Rosemarie’s?”.

In the mid-1990s, they bought a cottage that had been part of Stanford White’s Breese estate, The Orchard, in Southampton. “Debby said that she would now make good on her promise to design a garden for us,” Filler remembers. “By that time she had become one of the top landscape designers, but she insisted on doing it and refused to be paid.” She stayed with them for a winter weekend and spent hours walking the property. “Then she sat down at our dining table with a roll of yellow tracing paper and the surveyor’s map, and she designed the whole thing within a few hours. She joked, ‘Don’t tell anyone how fast I was.’ ”.

In a recent development, THE GIVING TREE When Derek C. Blasberg first saw the majestic tree in the backyard of a property in the Hamptons, he knew he’d found a home for his family. The hunt to identify it led him to Nevins’s larger body of work. “We could picture our children climbing its winding branches.”.

According to recent reports, the Beech House, a 16-foot-by-16-foot-by-16-foot living gazebo made of 26 purple beech trees (Fagus sylvatica “Dawyck Purple”), is the centerpiece. The trees were sourced from Buffalo, trucked to Southampton, and replanted in the same orientation in which they had been grown. (“Each tree had to be marked with a dot of blue paint to indicate due north and then planted in that same geographical direction,” Filler remembers.) They were wired and trained over a wooden inner framework shaped like the schematic outline of a house and then clipped into shape on the exterior. The floor was paved with bluestone squares and rectangles in a random-looking pattern Nevins designed.

According to recent reports, “Each year when we meet with our accountant to do our tax return, he’s always shocked that we spend so much on the garden,” Filler says. “We tell him: ‘You have clients who have boats, you have clients who have classic cars—well, this is our pleasure, our indulgence.’”.
