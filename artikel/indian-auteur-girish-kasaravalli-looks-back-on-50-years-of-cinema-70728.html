Indian Auteur Girish Kasaravalli Looks Back on 50 Years of Cinema at IFFI Masterclass: ‘All My Films Talk About Othering’

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Indian auteur Girish Kasaravalli looked back on his career in Kannada-language cinema at the 57th International Film Festival of India (IFFI) in Goa, in a masterclass titled “The Moral Universe of Parallel Cinema: 50 Years of Girish Kasaravalli.”.

In a recent development, the 75-year-old spoke about the moral contradictions of society and the relationship between cinematic form and social content. He also argued that even entertainment is a political act. The session was moderated by Variety‘s Asia editor Naman Ramachandran. Across films such as “Ghatashraddha” (1977), “Tabarana Kathe” (1986), “Thaayi Saheba” (1997), “Dweepa” (2002) and “Gulabi Talkies” (2008), Kasaravalli has kept returning to one question: how societies exclude individuals and communities, and how people who are themselves victims of oppressive structures can also become complicit in perpetuating them.

Recent findings suggest that asked whether the 1977 Kannada classic “Ghatashraddha,” which examines religious orthodoxy and social exclusion, still spoke to contemporary India, Kasaravalli said: “All my films talk about othering. I always talk about how society looks at some people from a different lens, and in that process they make them ‘other.'” He described this as “the biggest cruelty an individual or a group of people can impart on another person.”.

Sources indicate that asked whether he considered himself a political filmmaker, Kasaravalli remarked: “Whoever makes something, whether he makes mainstream cinema or art cinema, he is making a political statement, either in support of the dominant ideology or opposing the dominant ideology. He cannot avoid it.” There is nothing called only entertainment, he added, and even entertainment is a political form.

On his characters, he stated: “I don’t want any of my characters to become a kind of a mouthpiece to a certain ideology.” Drawing on Jean-Luc Godard, he compared understanding a character to buying a house: one must examine it from the outside and then from within before making a judgment. Similarly, a filmmaker must inhabit a character and then step back to assess it. For Kasaravalli, characters are too complex for a filmmaker to easily take sides.

On the evolution of his cinematic language, Kasaravalli described the visual construction of “Ghatashraddha,” as “ritualistic cinema.” “The style is not imposed on it; it comes from within the content itself,” he said. Since “Ghatashraddha” was about a ritual, he felt its cinematic form should reflect that structure.

Kasaravalli said he could not write a script until he had clarity about its images. “For me, the film is not merely making statements; it’s an attempt by the filmmaker to impart an experience.” He described writing detailed instructions for his crew, down to which hand a character uses to lift a glass, because even that changes the composition of a shot.

It has been revealed that this attention to the relationship between character and environment shaped “Gulabi Talkies.” Kasaravalli told cinematographer S. Ramachandra that the coastal community had to be as important as the protagonist. He wanted the images to feel like “actuality coverage,” not a predetermined visual construction. Rejecting conventional scenic shots of the sea and rocks, he told his cinematographer: “Film should not have any pretty image.” They chose 16mm, allowing the grain to contribute to the film’s sense of actuality.

Recent findings suggest that the discussion of visual aesthetics brought him to Cuban filmmaker Julio García Espinosa’s idea of “imperfect cinema,” which, for Kasaravalli, challenged the assumption that Western aesthetics should define cinematic perfection. “I don’t want to take your aesthetic sensibility as my sensibility,” he said, quoting Espinosa. Kasaravalli said each country and each region has its own aesthetic concerns. “We should make films according to that condition; we should never imitate the West. Why should I be perfect? I should be loyal to my own milieu.”.

According to recent reports, asked whether parallel cinema belonged to a particular period, Kasaravalli disagreed, pointing to notable films being made across the country today. The larger problem, he mentioned, was reaching audiences. Of streaming platforms, he said: “We all thought it is going to be a solution for all of us. But OTT [streaming] is exactly doing what the mainstream distribution network was doing earlier.”.

In the audience Q&A, he addressed open endings and whether a filmmaker should provide answers. “A filmmaker who claims that he knows answers for all the questions in life is telling a lie,” he said. “He is also as confused as you are. He only throws up options so that you can start thinking. There is nothing called closure… life is always open.”.

As the session concluded, IFFI festival director Ashutosh Gowariker described Kasaravalli’s work as “textbooks,” saying he had returned to “Ghatashraddha” while developing a upcoming film. He also singled out the filmmaker’s close-ups. “You let your actors simply be. You make the audience think and anticipate what the character is going through,” Gowariker mentioned.
