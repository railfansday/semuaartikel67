We love outdoor gear, but it’s hard to think of something we need less: Year-round, Backpacker‘s editors test tents, footwear, kitchen gear, and the rest of the latest and greatest hiking equipment to hit the market, and our closets are full to bursting. Still, every once in a while, something comes around that impresses us so much, we’re still thinking about it long after it’s been replaced or passed on to another tester.

We asked our editors to share the gear that’s at the top of their wishlist right now. Some of these wants are modest—a gourmet backpacking meal, a closed-cell foam pad, a trucker hat. Others, like an American-made packraft or a home freeze-dryer, are not. All of it is exceptional. Whether you’re shopping for a hiker who seems like they have everything or adding to your own wish list, this gear is far from ordinary.

Industry observers note that on Backpacker’s recent Editors’ Choice trip along Wisconsin’s Ice Age Trail, we used 10-liter Ursack bear bags to keep our smellables safe, and I fell in love with them. They’re lightweight at just 7.6 ounces, made with bullet-proof Spectra Fabric, and compress as you eat your way through their contents. I wouldn’t throw out my trusty hard canister if I got an Ursack for the holidays, but I’d sure use it a lot. —Will Brendza, Associate Editor.

There are so many flashlights out there, and they range in quality from amazing to shoddy, that I have shied away from actually shopping for one for years. But the LD31 RENEGADE flashlight from Fenix jumped out at me recently. Not only does this puppy pack 3200 lumens in a perfectly sized 5-inch package, but its rechargeable battery also doubles as an emergency external charger and can easily attach to a backpack strap, pocket, or bill of a cap thanks to a two-way clip. On top of all of that, this thing is completely dustproof and waterproof up to 2 meters. —WB.

Sources indicate that I tend to be pretty picky when it comes to outdoor and hiking hats. A lot of them look dorky, use cheap materials, or aren’t comfortable when you wear them. That’s why I was so stoked to find Fractel—a running hat tech giant from Australia that blows most American outdoor hats away. The T-SIERPINKSKI Trucker Hats are stylish, lightweight, breathe well, and come in some wild colorways.—WB.

Leave it to Hyperlite to launch a product I didn’t know I needed, can’t really afford, and desperately want right before the holidays. The brand, known for its ultralight packs and shelters, relaunched its apparel line in 2026, and in November dropped an anorak made with waterproof 3-layer Dyneema. It weighs just 7.5 ounces. Do I need a new anorak? Absolutely not. Would I love to keep this sweet little number in my backpacking kit? You bet. —WB.

It has been revealed that I’m not usually the kind of hiker who’s willing to give up a hip belt on my pack, but the Volpi FastPack 30 was so much fun to use, it made me want to bring less stuff just so I could justify using it. The vest-style carry on this frameless model was comfortable, and stretch storage pockets on the straps and at the bottom (for a rain shell), plus a strap on top for carrying my sleeping pad, made organization simple. —Adam Roy, Editor-in-Chief.

If I have a superpower, it’s being able to sleep anywhere, under any circumstances. Why not take advantage of that? I wouldn’t call Gossamer Gear’s Thinlight pads comfortable—at an eighth of an inch thick, they barely qualify as pads at all—but at just 2.7 ounces, they put even most cut-down closed-cell foam pads to shame when it comes to weight. —AR.

There’s a lot to like about the Soto Windmaster, which our testers picked out as the best backpacking stove of 2026. At just 3.1 ounces, it’s featherweight, and it performs consistently in the worst conditions, putting out a reliable flame without chewing through its gas supply even when it gets breezy. Thanks to its regulated design, it’s a solid pick for winter camping, too—a situation where some of my lightweight canister stoves have struggled. —AR.

Industry observers note that I’ve used a wide variety of inflatable craft during my time at Backpacker, from paddleboards to canoes. But few have impressed me as much as Alpacka’s Chinook, which tracked steadily and sliced through the water on an early-summer trip through Ruby Horsethief Canyon on the Colorado River. With its prominent bow and stern and removable skeg, the Chinook has some kayak DNA in its design, and paddles more like one than some of the squirrelly packrafts I’ve used. At 9.1 pounds, it’s light enough to haul, and in-tube storage and a clip-in point for a deck bag give it enough capacity for deep backcountry expeditions. —AR.

After more than a decade of use, my trusty lightweight tent from Brooks Range Mountaineering (RIP) is about to give up the ghost. (The company closed in 2018, if that tells you anything about how long I’ve been using it.) In my search for a replacement, I got to test out an X-Dome this fall, and I now see what all the fuss is about. This tent has a cult following for a reason: It’s featherweight at 44 ounces but doesn’t skimp on livability; the tall ceiling feels like a luxury for sitting up and changing inside. A simple pitch and details like the large doorways and generous internal pockets put this at the top of my list. —Zoe Gates, Senior Editor.

Food is my love language and there’s nothing better than a consumable gift. I evangelize Stowaway Gourmet freeze dried meals to my backpacking friends any chance I get, because they are just so darn tasty. Perfect for a stocking stuffer, or spring for a 72-meal variety pack to feed your favorite hiker for a year. My faves: Drunken Noodles, Kimchi Jigae, Wild Boar Bacon Stew, and Cereal Killer. —ZG.

The time has come: After years on the trail, I am finally over inflating my sleeping pad manually. Over the years, I’ve tried all the methods, from lung power to foot pumps to inflation sacks. I’m ready to take one dreaded camp chore off my plate. A pad inflator is the height of luxury, and I can’t wait to indulge. —ZG.

If someone really wants to spoil me this year, they’ll invest in a hobby I’ve always wanted to try: freeze drying my own backpacking meals. A dehydrator is an affordable gateway to the world of homemade trail meals, but freeze dryers (larger, more expensive) open up a whole new world of food preservation. Freeze dried meals last longer than dehydrated ones, and maintain better texture and nutritional value—not to mention the wider range of ingredients that hold up to freeze drying. Armed with this appliance, I can spend my whole winter prepping meals for summer backpacking trips—and create some stocking stuffers of my own for the next holiday season. —ZG.
