Archaeologists Found a Castaway Camp on an Island at the End of the World. Did a Legendary Sailor Build It?

Sources indicate that while hunting penguins for oil (yes, people really do that) in 1873, famed Argentine sailor Luis Piedra Buena wrecked his schooner brig Espora off the southern tip of South America. He and his crew regrouped on a deserted beach, spent about two months building a latest cutter, the Luisito, and sailed safely to Chile to tell their tale. Now, researchers believe they may have found pieces of a shipwreck and the remains of a “castaway camp” possibly tied to Piedra Buena on Isla de los Estados, an island “at the end of the world.”.

In a recent development, in a fresh study published in the journal Antiquity, lead author Nicolás Ciarlo of Spain’s University of Cádiz and his colleagues describe how their research into the maritime history of the island, which lies across the Strait of Le Maire from the tip of Tierra del Fuego, led them to a castaway camp. They believe the camp may link the island’s Lacroix Inlet in Franklin Bay to the more than 150-year-old survival story.

Recent findings suggest that isla de los Estados is probably best known for a lighthouse that inspired Jules Verne’s novel The Lighthouse at the End of the World. The island forms the easternmost point of Argentina’s Tierra del Fuego Province, and from the 17th century onward it lay along a key sailing route.

By 1870, the Argentine government had granted the island to Piedra Buena. Although he never established a settlement there, he sailed past it countless times while pursuing his interests throughout Patagonia. Then disaster struck. “During a campaign for exploiting penguins for oil, he and his crew lost the schooner brig Espora,” according to the study.

In a recent development, the crew survived, built a cutter, and sailed to Punta Arenas, Chile. There, Piedra Buena described the time they had spent at Lacroix Inlet, although experts have long doubted that he identified the location correctly.

Recent findings suggest that to test his account, the research team visited Lacroix Inlet in January 2026 with help from the Argentine Navy. There, they found the “remains of a shipwreck and a temporary camp” on a wind-battered stretch of coast at the base of a cliff, exposed to strong southwesterly storms. The “possible castaway camp” sat near a stream.

“Although there is still no common agreement, Piedra Buena’s account and other documentary evidence suggest that this bay is likely the place where the Espora sank,” the authors wrote. “The location and material culture of the temporary occupation at Lacroix Inlet point to a connection between this site and the nearby shipwreck remains and thus, possibly with the Espora and her crew.”.

At the camp, which the team described as a “short-term occupation,” they found glass beads, buttons, and a bottle, along with nautical items such as a deck prism and a rudder hinge. Elsewhere in the inlet lay the remains of three wooden hulls, including timber that may have been used to build a structure. All the wood likely came from ships’ hulls, but it’s unclear whether it all came from the same vessel.

The team called the finds “novel data” on the history of seafaring around the island. Their preliminary assessment points to at least one shipwreck that led to a temporary settlement. “The identity of the vessel/s and the castaways’ trajectories are the subject of ongoing inquiry,” they wrote, adding that a thorough study of the timbers may provide definitive answers.

In a recent development, some artifacts are especially telling. “The deck glass located on the surface is one of the most revealing artifacts,” Ciarlo told Discover Magazine. “To assess the identity of the Espora—as it happens with other shipwreck sites—is like putting together a puzzle. It does not rely on a unique proof, but on a critical analysis and merging of multiple lines of evidence.”.

Sources indicate that still, questions remain. In the study, the authors acknowledged that they found no trace of the site where the Luisito was built, and it’s unclear whether the Espora was ever completely taken apart. They wrote that more work is needed at the camp and on the shipwrecks in the weather-beaten inlet.

“As archaeologists,” Ciarlo mentioned, according to Phys.org, “being in this isolated and wild place allowed us to taste what a small group of castaways saw and felt about 150 years ago.”.
