Pain: It's All in the Mind

Posted November 24, 2026 | Reviewed by Margaret Foley.

The title of this post sounds a little provocative, but it is in fact true. Pain normally starts in peripheral sensory nerves, and the painful signal is then transmitted to the brain through the medulla and the thalamus. But it is the brain (which, ironically, lacks pain sensors for its own tissue), and by extension the mind, that actually "feels" the pain, by processing the painful nervous signal and constructing the conscious experience of the pain, together with its emotional content.

Pain is subjective and variable between individuals. There is normally a correlation between the severity of the bodily injury and the intensity of the perceived pain, but this is not always so. In the case of back pain, which is an exceedingly common modality of human suffering, studies have shown only a weak association between vertebral disc degenerative changes and symptoms: Some people with serious degeneration in their scans are almost asymptomatic, while in some other cases the opposite is the case. This variability helps illustrate the fact that pain is not a wholly predictable process in which a given stimulus always produces the same response, constant and fixed both in quality and in intensity. Instead, individual physiological and psychological factors are involved in the processing and modulation of the pain.

Industry observers note that acute pain has a function, which is to alert us that something is wrong in our body and needs attention. It tells us where the problem is and how serious and urgent it is. Chronic pain, on the other hand, often persists well beyond its initial utility. It is in this regard comparable to anxiety, which also alerts us of possible dangers, but in some cases, like chronic pain, persists after its usefulness has expired. It is a malfunction.

Industry observers note that it comes as no surprise that pain should have an vital emotional component. It is, after all, meant to be scary and distressing in order to be physiologically useful. When the pain becomes chronic, this arouses a whole range of negative emotions, which may too become chronic. It is also inevitable that the sufferer will attach a meaning to the pain, which may result in a catastrophizing response, or even a damaged self-esteem, as if the sufferer had internalized the painful experience as evidence of a low personal value. In this unfortunate situation, the individual suffers the bodily pain, as well as the psychological hurt that the pain carries with it.

According to recent reports, an excessive bodily vigilance and attention to the pain, and a fear of it re-emerging after it has subsided (perhaps only temporarily), have been identified as psychological factors implicated in the amplification of the pain experience. Research has also shown that pain is more intense in those who carry a sense of grievance, interestingly. With all this in mind, it is not surprising that chronic pain is associated with an increased risk of depression.

Given the strong psychological component found in pain, both as a direct result of it and as a catalyzing factor that amplifies the painful experience, it is often worthwhile exploring the possibility of deploying psychological treatments in order to improve the quality of life of the sufferer. Cognitive behavioral therapy, which is widely available, has been found to be clinically effective. Antidepressants, such as SNRIs or the older tricyclic types, can sometimes kill two birds with one stone in certain pain modalities (such as facial pain or fibromyalgia) by targeting the pain neurologically while at the same time providing a useful antidepressant action.
