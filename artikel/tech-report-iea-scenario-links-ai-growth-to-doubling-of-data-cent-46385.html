The International Energy Agency released its World Energy Outlook 2026 on November 19, 2026. In the report’s Stated Policies Scenario, AI-related investment helps double global data-center electricity consumption to 1,000 terawatt-hours by 2030.

Sources indicate that the finding puts AI’s electricity footprint inside a wider system-planning problem. The report’s scenarios show data-center capacity approaching three times its present size by 2035 as AI and digital services expand. Grids, supply chains and, in some cases, public acceptance are emerging as constraints around the investment boom.

According to recent reports, the Stated Policies Scenario incorporates policy measures formally proposed but not yet adopted, while the Current Policies Scenario examines existing settings without further policy action. The full outlook states that none of its scenarios is a forecast, so the 2030 result is a conditional pathway rather than a prediction.

The Stated Policies Scenario includes 26 million kilometers of latest power lines by 2035, while about 16 million kilometers of existing lines need replacement or refurbishment. Renewables supply half of global generation by 2035 in the same scenario, tying the network expansion to a changing generation mix as well as growing consumption.

Recent findings suggest that grid spending rises 75% over the next decade even in the Current Policies Scenario. That investment requirement sits within broader electrification: the full report projects more than 10,000 terawatt-hours of additional global electricity demand over the same period, with electric vehicles, cooling, heat pumps, industrial motors and data centers all contributing.

Industry observers note that supplies of copper, transformers, cables and other electrical equipment needed for that expansion are tight. The energy-security chapter projects 40% to 50% growth in peak electricity demand by 2035 across scenarios. Adequacy therefore requires new capacity and robust grids, while flexibility calls for more storage, demand response and diverse generation.

The report identifies a dual role for AI: it could help optimize electricity systems and reduce costs, while current data-center demand is amplifying strains in some markets and supply chains.

An earlier IEA analysis found that data-center electricity demand grew 17% in 2025, compared with 3% growth in global electricity demand. Supply chains were tightening for gas turbines, transformers, advanced chips and IT components, while planning and regulatory delays held up grid connections and other approvals.

The earlier analysis found rapid declines in power consumption per AI task, but rising adoption and energy-intensive uses such as AI agents outweighed those efficiency gains at the system level. That helps explain why more efficient computing can coexist with fast growth in total electricity demand.

Industry procurement is already responding. The earlier analysis reported that technology companies accounted for about 40% of corporate renewable power-purchase agreements signed in 2025. The pipeline of conditional agreements for data-center operators to buy electricity from small modular reactor projects grew from 25 gigawatts at the end of 2024 to 45 gigawatts when that analysis was published.

According to recent reports, many onsite natural-gas projects intended to work around slow grid connections remained at early stages. The earlier analysis described rapid, large swings in AI data-center demand that can stretch the technical capabilities of onsite gas plants, making battery storage increasingly key for reliable operation and potentially for grid support with suitable incentives.

Large, concentrated data-center loads can also trigger investment in generation and networks, creating particular affordability challenges. The earlier analysis found that demand growth does not necessarily raise prices when the right policies and infrastructure investment are present. Smart grid integration and incentives for flexible operation are among the available responses.

Industry observers note that onsite generation and batteries offer partial workarounds, while grids, electrical equipment and flexible operation remain system-wide requirements. For data-center developers and their investors, power-system delivery is therefore a core constraint on expansion and a central part of AI infrastructure planning.
