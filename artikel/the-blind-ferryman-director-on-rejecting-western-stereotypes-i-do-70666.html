‘The Blind Ferryman’ Director on Rejecting Western Stereotypes: ‘I Don’t Want to Make a Movie Asking for Sympathy’

According to recent reports, for Iraqi-Swiss filmmaker Ali Toffan Al-Fatlawi, the idea behind “The Blind Ferryman” began with a thought that struck him during Iraq’s civil war in 2006. Living in Switzerland and watching the violence unfold in his homeland, he thought of two of his aunts who were blind and found himself wishing that blindness might spare his countrymen from the violence they were inflicting upon one another.

Recent findings suggest that that idea became the starting point for his fiction feature debut, which explores what it means to see, believe and trust our own perceptions. The film had its world premiere at the 47th Cairo Intl. Film Festival. “I think we lost the ability to think with our feelings,” Al-Fatlawi tells Variety. “Or, as we say in Arabic, ‘Seeing things with our heart.’”.

Recent findings suggest that set in Iraq’s southern marshlands, the film follows Ayoub (Ahmed Moneka), a blind boatman who falls in love with Genya (Sam Rahmani), a mysterious woman whom nobody else can see. Convinced she exists only in his imagination, his community mocks him, forcing Ayoub to defend a love in which only he believes.

“When you love someone, why do you need the agreement of other people?” Al-Fatlawi asks. “It’s your love, it’s your own decision.”.

The character of Genya draws on stories Al-Fatlawi heard from his father about a mythical woman who appeared to men in the marshlands, as well as ancient Sumerian mythology and the “Epic of Gilgamesh.” The filmmaker was particularly fascinated by the prominence of female deities in ancient Mesopotamian mythology, questioning how those figures gave way to the predominantly male image of God that characterizes contemporary religion.

The marshlands themselves were equally key to his vision. Beyond their mythical associations, they offered Al-Fatlawi an opportunity to portray a side of Iraq rarely seen on screen, challenging the familiar images of war, poverty and destruction that have long dominated international representations of the country.

Sources indicate that “I don’t want to do a movie which demonstrates that we are poor people, asking for sympathy,” he insists. “I’m really very careful not to make a movie which makes the West feel good about their life.”.

In a recent development, his determination to challenge preconceived notions also informed his casting. Ayoub is a Black Iraqi, while the threatening outsider who arrives from the city is a white, blue-eyed man dressed in religious clothing, a deliberate challenge to stereotypes about who looks like a hero and who looks like a villain.

In a recent development, that tension between appearance and reality runs throughout the film, shaping everything from its characters to its visual language. A longtime painter and performance artist who later expanded into filmmaking, Al-Fatlawi developed the screenplay through drawings, often painting scenes before putting them into words.

“I paint and then I write, I paint and then I write. That’s my way to write,” he explains.

Working with his cinematographer, he chose the 4:3 aspect ratio to bring characters closer together within the frame, while composing wide shots that divide the image equally between the landscape and its reflection in the water.

“I told him, I want really in the middle, 50%,” he recalls. “There is the image, and the reflection of the image in the water.”.

That same attention to perception shaped his direction of the actors. Al-Fatlawi instructed Moneka to keep his eyes open and fixed throughout the film, resisting conventional portrayals of blindness that might invite pity.

It has been revealed that “I don’t want people to feel sorry for you,” he recalls telling the actor. “I want you to be strong. You see more than everybody who has eyes.”.

For Rahmani, the challenge was to embody Genya as both a flesh-and-blood woman and a potentially mythical presence. Al-Fatlawi asked her to play the character as 50% real and 50% imaginary, a delicate balance they worked through in rehearsals and during filming.

“I had to portray the whole spectrum without losing credibility as either,” Rahmani tells Variety. “Sometimes I went against my instinct, but that’s why I give so much credit to Ali as the creator of this whole world.”.

The experience was also a personal homecoming for the actress, who grew up in Jordan and Canada and had never lived in Iraq. After years of speaking Iraqi Arabic almost exclusively at home, spending nearly four months in the marshlands gave her the chance to use her native dialect freely, even in banal everyday encounters at supermarkets and restaurants. Rahmani says her time there brought her closer to her Iraqi heritage.

In a recent development, “It was such a surreal and, I don’t know, special feeling full of relief,” she recalls. “I can only describe it as not having to mask all the time and code switch. I could just be myself.”.

Sources indicate that “The Blind Ferryman gave me that gift, Ali gave me that gift of being more comfortable in my own skin,” she says. “It was healing for me.”.

The project began development in 2015, receiving support from the Doha Film Institute, El Gouna and Red Sea film festivals and participating in Cannes’ L’Atelier program in 2022. That same year, it secured approximately $2.5 million in financing.

After a 45-day shoot in Iraq, however, Al-Fatlawi still had one crucial sequence to complete: an underwater scene he had envisioned years earlier.

Recent findings suggest that rather than abandon it or spend an estimated $100,000 to $150,000 on a water tank, he found a cenote near Playa del Carmen, Mexico, whose submerged vegetation bore a striking resemblance to a painting he had made in 2021. Working with two underwater photographers who had never shot a feature film, he completed the sequence for a relatively modest $15,000.

At CIFF, Al-Fatlawi states he has been particularly moved by audiences discovering meanings in the film that he had never consciously intended.

In a recent development, “They add things which make the movie even richer,” he reflects. “They have the doors open. They can enter from everywhere.”.
