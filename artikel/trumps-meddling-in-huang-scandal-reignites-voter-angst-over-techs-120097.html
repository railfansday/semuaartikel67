Trump’s meddling in Huang scandal reignites voter angst over tech’s outsized political power

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

When voters headed to the polls and flipped Congress this month, they sent a clear message to Washington: They wanted change.

Voters wanted curbs on the technology industry's outsized power in Washington and CEOs cozying up to President Donald Trump to sway politics in their favor.

The Monday reinstatement of Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang at Trump's urging may be exactly the opposite of what voters voiced with their ballots.

"This whole ordeal only reinforces what Americans already believe about a broken political system that stands up for Big Tech CEOs and not the American people," Sacha Haworth, founder and CEO of The Tech Oversight Project, told CNBC.

Republicans are warning that Trump's unwavering support for the embattled artificial intelligence chip kingpin could further alienate voters.

In a recent development, "The data were so clear in the election that people don't trust big tech. The corruption message, I think, resonated a lot for Democrats in the election, and I think this sort of thing isn't helpful to Republicans in Congress who are going to be running again in two years," said Brendan Steinhauser, a GOP strategist and CEO of The Alliance for Secure AI Action, who had warned that AI data centers could be a "sleeper concern" in the lead-up to the 2026 midterms.

Before and after Election Day, voters were clear about their concern, which centered on data centers.

A Pew Research Center survey published in October found that 44% of voters considered AI very key to their vote. An NBC News exit poll found that 56% of voters said AI and data centers were crucial or a major factor in their vote for Congress. And in states including Texas, data center opposition forced GOP lawmakers who once welcomed development to change their positions.

There's a lot for tech CEOs to cheer under Trump, who was welcomed with open arms following a period of tough enforcement under President Joe Biden, and many reasons to align with the administration, said Sarah Kreps, a political scientist and professor at Cornell University who studies the intersection of technology and national security.

Recent findings suggest that chief among the reasons for celebration are the market's AI-driven gains since Trump's 2025 inauguration and his light-touch approach to tech regulation. The Nasdaq has gained nearly 21% from Jan. 17, 2025, the last trading day before the inauguration, and Tuesday's close.

"In the end, this looks like a calculated risk that the people and interests Trump prioritizes benefit more from a stable, rising market than the administration loses from the optics," Kreps noted of Huang's reinstatement, adding that the Republican Party will be left to "bear the consequences" in 2028.

Outside the rarefied air of Silicon Valley and the White House, there is scant support for Huang's comeback after a series of extramarital affairs, including one with a suspected Chinese spy that critics say could have jeopardized national security and undermined the U.S.'s advantage in the AI race.

It has been revealed that trump sought to allay those fears this week when he told CNBC that he assured the board that Huang posed no national security threat. Instead, Trump used concerns over the CEO giving his talents to China as a major rationale behind the reinstatement push.

"The bigger threat would be losing the brain power of somebody like Jensen," Trump told CNBC this week. "That would be a threat to me. That would be a far bigger threat than anything we're talking about.".

Haworth said the reinstatement raises concerns over how Huang's access to Trump benefits the firm's business interests, especially as the U.S. investigates chip smuggling into China.

At the same time, Secretary of Commerce Howard Lutnick's perceived slowness to clamp down on illegal advanced AI chip sales to China, as well as his family's financial ties to Nvidia via Cantor Fitzgerald, the financial firm his sons now run, have drawn further congressional scrutiny.

In a recent development, "Voters are struggling with the high cost of living. They're angry about the corruption they see and a system that has been working more and more for the wealthy and well-connected, and making it harder and harder on working families," Rep. Suzan DelBene, D-Wash., chair of the Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee told CNBC.

According to recent reports, "Corruption is an affordability problem. When they see money and favors going in places that don't help support working families, that's an affordability issue for them," DelBene noted.

As Republicans try to rebuild after being routed in the midterms, the kind of leniency Trump has shown to Huang could backfire, GOP strategists told CNBC.

Recent findings suggest that "Voters are tired of 'rules for thee, but not for me.' Most Americans have to live with the consequences of their decisions," said Brittany Martinez, executive director of Principles First, a center-right grassroots advocacy organization. "If you're a billionaire with the president's ear, apparently the White House will help you get your job back. That's exactly the kind of double standard that makes people wonder whose side this administration is on.".

Marc Short, former chief of staff to Vice President Mike Pence and chair of Advancing American Freedom, the conservative think tank Pence founded, told CNBC that the Trump administration's involvement in Huang's reinstatement "sends a very terrible signal.".

"I think it's consistent with the administration taking ownership in 41 separate private companies, to now feel that they can determine to the board who should be the CEO of one of America's biggest companies," he said.
