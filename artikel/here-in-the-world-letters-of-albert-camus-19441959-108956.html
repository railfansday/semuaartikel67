Here in the World: Letters of Albert Camus, 1944–1959

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that what an amazing behemoth of a book! I’ve been lugging it around for weeks. Nearly a thousand letters: the intimate correspondence of Albert Camus and Maria Casarès. When they first met in Paris – on D-Day, 1944 – Camus had already published L’Etranger (1942; The Outsider). Born and raised in Algeria, he was in Paris when the Second World War broke out, and later joined the French Resistance. Camus’s wife, Francine Faure, a mathematician and pianist, remained in Algeria. Maria Casarès was originally from Galicia, the daughter of an exiled former prime minister of Spain, Santiago Casares Quiroga. In 1944, Casarès was already known as an actress, having played the titular role in J M Synge’s Deirdre of the Sorrows. She later starred in Les Enfants du paradis (1945), Cocteau’s Orphée (1950) and La Lectrice (1988), among other films and plays.

On D-Day, Camus was thirty; Casarès was twenty-one. They began an affair, but this ended when Camus was reunited with his wife. In 1945, Faure gave birth to twins: Catherine and Jean. In June 1948, Camus and Casarès met again and embarked on a long relationship, which continued until Camus’s death in a car crash on 4 January 1960. Afterwards, the poet René Char found Casarès’s letters in Camus’s apartment and discreetly returned them to her. In the 1990s, not long before she died, Casarès offered the letters to Camus’s daughter, Catherine.

Sources indicate that the correspondence was first published in French in 2017; now it has been (brilliantly) translated by Sandra Smith and Cory Stockwell. The letters are passionate, voluminous, at times torrential. Everything is discussed: love, sex, books, plays, films, beauty, art, family, madness, fear of failure, fear of death. Also logistics, travel aims, inadequate postal services, money troubles, indigestion, hives, headaches.

In a recent development, in her preface, Catherine Camus writes: ‘Their letters make the earth more vast, space more luminous, the air lighter, simply because they existed.’ Casarès, she believes, is ‘like the ocean: breaking, crashing, re-forming and starting over again with stupefying vitality’. Camus is pretty oceanic as well. Both lovers travelled extensively; the letters are often scrawled in haste, sent from Paris, Cabris, Oran, from North and South America, written on ocean liners, or ended abruptly to catch the post. Sometimes, too, they are written as a sort of diary, across several days. The tempo changes. There are lists, schedules; they want to know what the other has been doing, hour by hour. How many glasses of wine they’ve drunk. What they’ve eaten. Who they’ve seen. There are ecstatic protestations of love. And questions, lots of questions. What happened last night? Are you happy? Did you see a doctor? They both emerge vividly – fascinating, erudite, witty, anxious; flawed and paradoxical.

As Catherine Camus indicates, there is great luminosity; many descriptions of humid summer nights, bright oceans, shimmering mountains. Also, a rich sense of being finite and alive, briefly here in the world: ‘I didn’t sleep any better last night, and tonight it’s so hot, there are so many crickets and stars that I have no hope of falling asleep. At least I can write to you…’ (Camus). Or, ‘Today the sky, which was a drab deep gray this morning, gradually brightened up, and around 2:00, you could just about see the sun peeking through a soft veil’ (Casarès). They struggle with grief, sorrow and anxiety, with physical asperities. Camus endures savage bouts of TB, ‘getting undressed again, letting myself be poked and prodded’. For a while, Francine Faure is depressed, consigned to a hospital, repeating the name ‘Maria Casarès’. Camus and Casarès agonise about her. Sometimes, they argue furiously: ‘You’re stupid, my poor darling!’ writes Casarès, ‘and we must wait for it to pass!’ The war has ended, cities lie in ruins, everything feels untethered and surreal: ‘Of course, the world is still sick, even if I’m well,’ writes Camus. ‘Will we always have to love each other during the Apocalypse, in anguish, in torment?’.

Industry observers note that often, they discuss books and authors. They’re not fans of Hemingway. On reading To Have and Have Not (1937), Casarès writes: ‘I certainly found some well-written pages but, my God, how dusty, dull, gloomy it all is, and it smells so much of rooms with peeling wall­paper, all of them a mess, with the odor of sheets, night sweats and dirty clothes!’ Camus’s response is yet more scathing: ‘Hemingway? It serves you right! Why read phonies with no talent?’ Proust gets a mixed review: Casarès is ‘hooked’, loves the way he writes about nature, but finds the ‘political gossip’ boring. In January 1950, Camus sends ‘bad news’:.

Industry observers note that george Orwell died. You don’t know him. A very talented English writer, who had the same experience as me, more or less (even though he’s ten years older), and exactly the same ideas. He’d been fighting tuberculosis for years. He was one of a very small number of men with whom I shared something.

According to recent reports, both Camus and Orwell opposed the adamantine absolutes of communism and fascism; both were attacked for their political views. They had arranged to see each other in Paris in 1945, but Camus was afflicted by another bout of TB and the meeting never happened. A few months after Orwell’s death, Camus gives a copy of Nineteen Eighty-Four to Casarès. She isn’t convinced: ‘I didn’t learn anything from it that I hadn’t already imagined, and it left a weak impression on me.’.

Recent findings suggest that when it comes to his own books, Camus is full of self-doubt, grappling with his ‘tormented, mistrustful soul’. He works ‘like a madman’ on L’Homme revolté (1951; The Rebel), but fears it is a failure: ‘I only hope that this book won’t be too far from what it was meant to be. I hope it will help people to live – that it will say that all is not lost – that it will give everyone with whom I feel solidarity the strength to hate nothing, and to create.’ Even after receiving the Nobel Prize in 1957, Camus is still ‘trying to find some equilibrium within myself’. As for Casarès, in one of the last letters she describes herself as ‘here, a little shapeless, it’s true – a little like you, perhaps, or like the world that surrounds us – but I’m here nonetheless, whole and alive’. It’s impossible to know if they wrote with any sense of eventual publication in mind, or simply and entirely for each other. Camus usually drafted and redrafted his published works. Even his Notebooks were edited – by Camus himself, and by Francine Faure, among others. In the end, these letters are so surreally intimate, so poetic and unbridled, that it feels a bit like reading an extraordinary epistolary novel: about love, art, war, finitude and crazy-­beautiful optimism. All is not lost indeed.
