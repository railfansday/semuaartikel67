Jeff Bezos’s $41 billion AI startup Prometheus is shrouded in secrecy. We found out who’s building it

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that for many in the tech industry, physical AI is the next world to conquer. While developers of large language models like OpenAI and Anthropic seek to push reasoning models distilled on billions of streams of text to their limits, an emerging crop of companies are focusing on an even more ambitious goal: developing AI programs designed to speak the language of physics. Should they succeed, these firms have a stake in everything from building models that might power self-driving manufacturing or enable drug discovery to those that can take on plane design and weapons development. The promise is enormous. Whoever cracks physical AI could control a slice of the software stack that becomes critical to a host of industries—and worth many, many billions. Already, a growing list of companies are racing to stake their claim. Among the buzziest is Prometheus, a startup backed by Jeff Bezos that’s already flush with cash. Bezos isn’t just bankrolling Prometheus. He’s also coleading the manufacturer, marking his first executive role since stepping down as Amazon’s CEO in 2021. His cofounder is Vik Bajaj, a Stanford professor who previously led science operations at Verily, Alphabet’s health technology venture. Prometheus was incubated in 2024 at Foresite Labs, which Bajaj leads, with support from the venture capital firm Arch Venture Partners. A number of Prometheus employees also appear to have come from firms associated with Bajaj. By combing through LinkedIn and researchers’ personal websites, Fast Company identified 130 people who say they’re currently working for Prometheus, and assembled what appears to be the first public database of its kind. Prometheus employs approximately 250 people, including numerous accomplished AI researchers, some of whose names have not been publicly disclosed. The company is recruiting not only AI model developers but also experts in fields like aerospace and chemistry, a strong hint that it may one day target manufacturing and healthcare customers. Employees bring expertise in ocean engineering, energy engineering, mathematics, and even high-performance computing. Thus far, Prometheus has built a neural surrogate for numerical simulation and has used the technology to design a range of hardware, including an experiment that involved having the company’s models redesign the Mars helicopter. This next AI revolution needs AI tools trained not on text but on data from the physical world, as well as subject-matter experts who can ensure that models are distilling a common core of engineering principles that can extend across myriad domains. This, in turn, involves recruiting not just AI coders but plenty of people with expertise in the physical realm, whether that’s manufacturing automotive parts or running biology labs. “Most fundamentally, the biggest challenge is just the sheer breadth of what we’re doing. Engineering is vast. There’s no such thing as an engineer,” one leading AI expert and early-stage Prometheus employee, speaking on the condition of anonymity, tells Fast Company. “This idea of building something that’s general enough to cover all of that space, that’s been a really, really huge challenge—and a really interesting one.” What Prometheus, and physical AI, aim to achieve News that Prometheus (formerly called the Prometheus Project) was under development went public only last year, and its leadership has been tight-lipped about what it’s actually trying to build. We do know that the company was incubated at Foresite Labs, which is housed within the health and life sciences investment firm Foresite Capital. (In addition to Prometheus, Foresite Labs has backed companies like AI drug discovery startup Xaira and disease therapy outfit Alumis.) Still, Prometheus’s bare-bones website includes only links to its LinkedIn and X pages, and government documents show that Prometheus used a code name (“FL2024-008 INC”) for a small amount of lobbying and when applying for H1-B visas for some employees. We also know that Prometheus has attracted significant capital from investors interested in healthcare, AI, and tech manufacturing. The company originally raised about $6 billion in funding, and then another $12 billion—for a $41 billion valuation—earlier this year. Its backers, Semafor reported in June, include JPMorgan; BlackRock; and Arch Venture Partners. Other backers include Powerlaw, a closed-end fund that has also invested in high-tech companies like SpaceX, Colossal, and Waymo, as well as RoboStrategy, a venture capital fund that states it owns shares in the company. Bezos has said that the company isn’t focused on robotics, though that’s what many people first associate with the term physical AI. Instead, Prometheus aims to develop what it calls an artificial general engineer, which Bezos likens to a much more advanced version of computer-aided design, or CAD, powered by artificial intelligence. “My through line for the last few years has been AI,” Bezos said in an interview with Squawk Box anchor Andrew Ross Sorkin earlier this year. “My time at Amazon is spent on AI. My time at Prometheus is spent on AI—Prometheus is really an AI company. And my time at Blue [Origin] is largely spent on AI. And [at] Prometheus . . . the tools that we’re building are going to help companies like Blue immensely.” A trademark application makes clear that Prometheus’s aspirations extend far beyond healthcare, spanning much of the engineering and manufacturing pipeline. The document references physical simulations and digital twin technology, along with structural, thermal, fluid, acoustic, and electromagnetic analysis, and even “acoustic and vibration analysis, noise prediction, and acoustic design optimization.” The application also discusses 3D printing, semiconductor manufacturing, and developing factory layouts. It’s not hard to see why this interdisciplinary approach might one day prove lucrative. Companies spend an extraordinary amount of time researching and developing upcoming components and hardware, a process that can involve the arduous work of finding and vetting suppliers. There’s also the expensive, resource-intensive process of simulating and testing how new systems perform once they’re built and under different conditions. Another challenge: developing production lines capable of manufacturing that hardware at scale. Consider the development of a jet engine, a process that can require thousands of workers and more than a decade of design, prototyping, testing, and manufacturing. Prometheus envisions AI tools that could dramatically compress that timeline, from, say, a decade to just a few years. The company sees similar opportunities across everything from household products to large-scale industrial systems. The aspiration for the physical AI industry is to automate as much of the process as possible and make real-world testing and evaluation far more efficient. The idea is that this could speed up hardware development across a wide range of industries. Indeed, Prometheus reportedly already has its eyes on a network of spin-offs in which it might one day own stakes. “Today it takes days to weeks for engineers to set up one simulation for one design. There are a lot of repetitive, manual, low-added-value tasks that they have to go through while using this software,” explains Thomas von Tschammer, cofounder of Neural Concept, a physical AI startup and Prometheus competitor. “The opportunity with AI is first of all to automate and compress a lot of this low-added-value work from those engineers so that they can actually spend time engineering.” Inside Prometheus One early Prometheus employee, an expert in neural networks who was among the company’s first hires, was excited to “get back to basics” and pursue fundamental research. At the Bezos-backed firm, the employee says he’s working on building an “end-to-end” system at the intersection of physics and AI, developing “accurate, high-fidelity simulations” that could help engineers with initial designs. The goal, the employee says, is to create an agentic artificial engineer that could one day drive “simulations and use them to make design decisions at the highest level.” Such an AI system would ideally be generalizable across engineering disciplines, much as current LLMs can be applied to a wide range of text-based tasks and types of content. “If we can get a tool that across many different domains is able to simulate faster and more accurately, you can see how this generalizes to fields that wouldn’t otherwise have had contact very much at all,” the Prometheus employee says. A good number of staffers come from other major AI companies, including xAI, and many have particular experience in AI-based materials science. Notably, a number hail from companies where Bajaj was formerly affiliated, including Google, Foresite Labs, and Foresite-backed Xaira. Before leaving Foresite, Bajaj helped incubate a range of biotech firms and filed a patent for AI-based nucleic acid sequencing. Several employees have also come to Prometheus from General Agents, the applied AI research lab that Prometheus quietly acquired last year. The lab released “Ace,” a real-time computer autopilot, last year, and its former CEO, Sherjil Ozair, is now on Prometheus’s technical staff. Other staffers have far more eclectic backgrounds. In its quest to build powerful simulations, Prometheus has hired at least one former employee of video game developer Epic Games, and a former AI researcher from Meta’s Reality Labs. The company has also recruited employees from Waymo and Red Bull Advanced Technologies, which helps translate Formula 1 innovations into applications for the automotive and aerospace industries. Prometheus’s hires also suggest that it may eventually want to sell to the government. The company’s team includes a technical adviser who also serves as a chief scientist for the U.S. Army’s ground systems research. Another Prometheus adviser is Chase Johnson, who previously served in the Marine Corps’ innovation unit and worked as an attorney for defense tech company Anduril. Johnson, in his LinkedIn bio, suggests Prometheus could one day transform the “defense industrial base.” Another employee who works at Prometheus government relations tells Fast Company that what brought them to the Bezos-backed firm was the desire to work on “cutting-edge” problems. In particular, they’re interested in how an AI engineering agent could aid industries with critical supply chains, including the automotive and semiconductor sectors. “We are entirely focused on building models and products for the physical economy, and to accelerate the dream to build loop,” the employee says. “We are exclusively focused on design, engineering, and manufacturing, and I think that is an advantage. Focus is always an advantage, and we know that we are purpose-built for design, engineering, and manufacturing.”  The quest for physical AI Bezos may already have a ready-made customer for Prometheus: himself. His space company, Blue Origin, is developing reusable rockets to launch satellites and humans into orbit, with ambitions that extend to orbital data centers. (At that Semafor event, Bezos said Blue Origin was already using some Prometheus tech.) All of that requires extensive manufacturing and engineering work, which could benefit from the kind of AI-powered software Prometheus is developing. Bezos has acknowledged the potential connection, though it remains unclear whether—or how—Prometheus might ultimately integrate with his other businesses. Then there’s Amazon, which does plenty of its own in-house manufacturing, maintains frontier AI and robotics research groups, and has invested heavily in warehouse robotics. Bezos has also invested in Physical Intelligence, a startup developing AI models for the physical world and robotics, though there doesnt’ appear to be any formal relationship between the two companies. Taken together, these interests raise the possibility that Bezos is positioning himself to build a new, far-reaching empire centered on physical AI. In the meantime, a range of other startups are competing in the physical AI race. Anima Anandkumar and Benedikt Jenik were once approached about leading Prometheus before launching their own startup, Accelerated Understanding. (That offer, Reuters previously reported, came from Bajaj and would have included a major stake in the company and a million-dollar salary.) Their focus is on building models capable of making predictions in four dimensions, at any resolution. To do that, they say they need a team that isn’t constrained by traditional disciplinary silos or the limitations of conventional LLMs. “Even if one were to use LLMs to automate execution, the real world stays the bottleneck,” Anandkumar tells Fast Company. “If we instead teach AI models to directly simulate and understand physics, that bottleneck goes away.” Other firms envision a similar formula for building physical AI teams: a mix of machine learning generalists and specialists with engineering and manufacturing experience. Paul Eremenko, of P-1 AI, which makes an agentic AI tool called Archie that works directly within engineering teams, argues that it’s often much easier to train someone with an aerospace background in the latest AI research than to teach an AI specialist the nuances of aerospace engineering. “We don’t compete with OpenAI or Anthropic for talent,” Eremenko says. “The prototypical AI researcher or AI engineer for us is somebody who comes from a physical world background—a mechanical engineer, aerospace engineer, electrical engineer—who became very interested in AI.” Back at Prometheus, the competition for talent is only expected to intensify. An employee working in government relations tells Fast Company that the Prometheus expects to expand its workforce exponentially, even as hiring remains highly selective. For the early employee specializing in AI, the recruiting pitch comes down to the opportunity to tackle problems that have long eluded conventional engineering tools. “In terms of wooing them, I think I’m often selling people on the novelty and the excitement.”.
