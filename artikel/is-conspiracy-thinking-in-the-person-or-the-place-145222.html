Is Conspiracy Thinking in the Person or the Place?

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Posted November 26, 2026 | Reviewed by Lybi Ma.

Sources indicate that here are three statements. How much do you agree with each?

Food should be chosen based on each person's blood group.

Evidence of alien contact is being concealed from the public.

These seem to have little in common, but people who agree with one tend to agree with the others (Lobato and colleagues, 2014).

All three statements come from questionnaires that measure what researchers call epistemically suspect beliefs: confident claims about how the world works that go beyond, or against, the available evidence.

According to recent reports, because these beliefs hang together so well, many researchers treat them as something close to a personality trait, a stable way of seeing things. Others see them as a response to the world. Conspiracy theories, for example, tend to spread during periods of social crisis (van Prooijen and Douglas, 2017).

Recent findings suggest that if these beliefs are traits, they should stay roughly the same from year to year, regardless of what happens around a person. A response to the world would instead rise and fall as people’s view of that world changes. Most studies cannot tell which is true, because they measure people only once.

Sources indicate that in a paper just published in Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, my colleagues and I tested the two views (Alper and colleagues, 2026). The project, led by Sinan Alper of Yaşar University. was funded by TÜBİTAK, Türkiye's national science agency.

We surveyed the same adults in Türkiye and the United Kingdom five times, once every six months. At the start, 2,077 people participated in Türkiye and 959 in the UK. Each time, they reported these beliefs and rated corruption among public officials, business executives, and the media, as well as other attitudes.

The two countries differ sharply on corruption. On Transparency International’s (2026) Corruption Perceptions Index, the UK ranks 20th of 182 countries, and Türkiye ranks 124th.

The three beliefs moved together.

Sources indicate that on average, the beliefs declined slightly over the two years, except for conspiracy belief in Türkiye, which revealed no clear change. I wouldn't read much into this, since answering the same questions repeatedly can itself lower scores. What struck me more was how tightly linked the three beliefs were. At the start, the correlations between them were strong, ranging from .65 to .86. Change was linked even more tightly. When a person’s belief in the paranormal fell, their belief in pseudoscience and conspiracy theories tended to fall with it.

It has been revealed that one possibility is that all three reflect a single underlying tendency. Simpler explanations, such as people answering the survey less carefully over time, cannot yet be ruled out. Either way, much of the change is shared, but not all of it.

According to recent reports, in Türkiye, people who saw more corruption over the past two years also became more open to conspiracy theories. Their paranormal and pseudoscientific beliefs did not follow. In the UK, we found no such link.

This may seem to clash with the previous section, and in part it does. Most of the change in these beliefs is shared, but each may keep some room to move on its own. In Türkiye, corruption may have tracked the portion attributable to conspiracy beliefs alone. It makes sense that belief in conspiracy was the one to move. A conspiracy theory claims that powerful people are secretly working together for their own gain. Corruption is often a true version of that story. Black cats and blood-type diets have nothing to do with it.

Sources indicate that at the start of the study, though, the link between perceived corruption and conspiracy belief was about twice as strong in the UK (r = .61) as in Türkiye (r = .32). The difference may come down to how common corruption is. Where it is relatively rare, seeing it everywhere may say more about the person than about the country and may function as a stable trait. Where corruption is widespread and changing, people’s sense of it may follow those changes, and conspiracy beliefs may shift accordingly.

Our answer is both. These beliefs are steady and hang together, but they can still change.

Some factors seem to set a person’s starting point. People higher in authoritarianism, who value discipline and following leaders in unity, endorsed all three kinds of beliefs more in both countries. But changes in authoritarianism did not track changes in belief. Perceived corruption works differently, and whether it moves these beliefs seems to depend on where you live.

It has been revealed that this fits earlier work. Conspiracy beliefs are more common in countries with widespread corruption and in less stable societies, where education offers less protection against them (Alper, 2023). In another study I coauthored, education was also less tied to trust in science in highly corrupt countries (Alper and colleagues, 2024). In such places, people might learn to distrust authority from experience.

When someone you know shares a conspiracy theory, ask two questions. Is this how they see everything, from health advice to the evening news? Or is it a response to something they have watched go wrong? The first kind of belief tends to change slowly. The second should be taken seriously, because sometimes suspicion points at something real.

According to recent reports, careful thinking still matters. In a recent experiment, my colleagues and I found that training designed to encourage reflection lowered conspiracy beliefs (Bayrak and colleagues, 2025). But when institutions keep giving people reasons to be suspicious, better thinking can only do so much. I guess that in some places the best answer to conspiracy thinking is institutions that give people less to suspect. Survey data like ours cannot settle that.

It has been revealed that sinan Alper and I have begun to test it experimentally. In two preregistered studies with nationally representative US samples, we described an unnamed US state or a fictional country as either highly corrupt or very clean (Alper and Yilmaz, 2026).

According to recent reports, people found conspiracy claims about the corrupt place far more believable, and this was most closely tied to a sense that resources were being shared unfairly. The paper is still under peer review, and I will write about it once it is done.
