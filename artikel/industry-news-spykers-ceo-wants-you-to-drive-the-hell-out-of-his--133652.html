Industry News: Spyker's CEO Wants You to ‘Drive the Hell’ Out of His $1.8 Million Supercars

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

When people spend nearly $2 million on a two-seat, mid-engine car, you can safely assume it is not the only car in their garage. It makes sense that it will not be driven every day; however, often times cars like this are not really driven at all. They spend their entire lives in climate-controlled garages, tucked beneath soft covers. And when the car in question is something like a one-of-25 C8 Preliator XXV Coupé, the temptation to preserve it as a collectible is understandable.

Sources indicate that but Spyker CEO Victor Muller has a different idea. He wants his customers to get behind the wheel and drive. "We prefer to sell our cars to people that actually use them," Muller told me during an interview at Retromobile New York. "Because that's what I made them for. To drive the hell out of them.".

According to recent reports, that's a refreshing philosophy from someone selling a car this pricey. Particularly when that car is the new C8 Preliator XXV Coupé, which looks as much like a piece of fine jewelry as an 800-horsepower road rocket. Unfortunately, Muller admits that many of Spyker's previous creations haven't enjoyed particularly active lives.

Recent findings suggest that "Many of our cars, of the 252 we made so far, have just ended up in dungeons, in vaults, in spaces where nobody will ever see them," Muller mentioned. He's hoping to change that by organizing more owner events and encouraging customers to spend time enjoying their cars; Spyker has joined the Supercar Owners Circle, a global network of car collectors, and is planning gatherings in Europe and the United States.

The tech giant is also in a position where it can be selective about who gets its cars. According to Muller, all 25 Preliator XXV Coupés sold during their debut at The Quail, a Motorsports Gathering in August. Every single one was snapped up in a day. "I've never seen anything like that in my entire life," he said. Spyker prepares to build 25 Spyders and 25 Sedancas in 2028, he added, once production of the Coupés is complete next year.

Industry observers note that who is buying these cars has changed dramatically over the last quarter century. "In 2000, my typical customer was a fifty-five to sixty-year-old industrialist—old money, car collector, very astute buyer," Muller explained. "Fast forward twenty five years. Your average age is thirty-five years. Tech investor. Huge collection. Knows his cars completely. But nobody asks how much it is.".

It has been revealed that muller notes approximately 40 percent of his upcoming buyers are existing or former Spyker owners, and roughly 80 percent of the new cars are expected to go to the United States. But no matter how old the buyer is or where they live, Muller wants them to drive the cars. This makes sense when you understand his approach to engineering; The new car has no massive infotainment screens or hybrid systems, and electronic intervention is largely limited to essential safety features like ABS and stability control.

“If you mis-shift in a Spyker and you drive two hundred miles an hour and you shift to second gear, you blow up the engine," Muller said. "And I think it has to be that way in Spyker world." For Spyker, the connection between driver and machine is more important than chasing lap records, acceleration figures and electronic nannies. "If you don't like that, buy something else," he added. "If not, man up—drive a Spyker.".

Sources indicate that another way to demonstrate that these cars are meant to be driven is to get the brand back into racing. Muller revealed that Spyker is taking steps toward returning to professional endurance competition. The firm competed in the 24 Hours of Le Mans eight times, most recently in 2010. Naturally, I asked whether another trip to Le Mans might be in Spyker's future.

"Absolutely," Muller mentioned. "We'd love to go back in racing." Muller indicates Spyker is preparing to re-enter endurance racing, although he stopped short of announcing a timeline, championship, or confirmed race entry. He certainly likes the idea of seeing his latest creation on the track. More importantly, he's already started putting people in place to make it happen.

Recent findings suggest that "I actually hired my former GT2 race director again," Muller remarked. "He started a week ago today.".
