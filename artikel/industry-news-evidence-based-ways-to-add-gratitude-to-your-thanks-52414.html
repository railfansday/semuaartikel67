More than 90% of Americans celebrate Thanksgiving – typically with roasted turkeys, football games, and family gatherings. It’s widely considered the biggest and busiest holiday for travel, and it’s commonly viewed as the start of the holiday shopping season. Beyond all of these traditions, research shows there are major benefits to remembering the sentiment that accompanies Thanksgiving: gratitude.

The research clearly establishes that gratitude promotes well-being. While we may feel thankful for something specific, like a gift or a meal, a broader outlook of gratitude — the mindset of noticing and appreciating the positives in your life — is proven to protect people from psychological distress.

In a recent development, “Gratitude has been shown to have many benefits: Grateful people are happier, less envious, sleep better, are more resilient when facing trauma, and need fewer visits to the doctor,” explained Cornell social psychologist Tom Gilovich, director of the Judgment and Belief Lab. “The benefits of gratitude, furthermore, extend to those who cross paths with grateful Individuals, as grateful individuals are more generous to others.”.

The evidence on the benefits of gratitude continues to build. A 2023 systematic review combined the data from 44 articles that included more than 16,500 participants to look for connections between gratitude and life satisfaction. It found a strong connection between dispositional gratitude — a general tendency to notice, appreciate, and respond with grateful emotions in your daily life — and life satisfaction. It also found that gratitude at earlier points in life predicted life satisfaction later in life.

Industry observers note that there’s a growing body of research demonstrating that it's possible to encourage gratitude in people of all ages and backgrounds. Another systematic review from 2023 combined 64 randomized-controlled studies measuring interventions designed to boost feelings of gratitude in participants. It found that, on the whole, the interventions led to more feeling of gratitude, fewer symptoms of anxiety and depression, greater life satisfaction, and more positive and lower psychological pain scores.

Despite the research, critics question whether gratitude could also make people complacent. The concern stems from the idea that gratitude can create feelings of contentment, which may lead to a lack of action.

Industry observers note that A recent study published by Cornell researchers including Gilovich took on this argument. Researchers analyzed three separate data sets: a survey of more than 2,000 Americans, a longitudinal study of more than 200,000 people from 22 countries, and a field study that surveyed individuals at blood drives and flu shot clinics. They found that people who felt grateful were more likely to engage in costly prosocial actions.

Recent findings suggest that “Although some have expressed a concern that feeling grateful might make people feel satisfied and therefore complacent about pressing societal problems, research shows that that concern is misplaced,” Gilovich noted. “Grateful people are more likely to volunteer their time at civic and religious organizations, donate money to charity, and participate in blood drives. Gratitude not only makes you feel good, but it helps you live up to your best self and be a better member of society.".
