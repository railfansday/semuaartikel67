Freelance Writing Rates – Quote by the Hours a Piece Takes, Not the Word Count

🌐 Source: Moneycrashers • 📅 19 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 2 min read (494 words, 📖 Standard)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that base every freelance writing quote on all the hours the piece takes, including research, interviews, and revisions. Time a few typical pieces from start to finish, multiply the hours by the hourly rate you need, and quote the total as a project fee. Charge more for specialist topics, rush deadlines, and contracts that buy all rights.

A per-word offer can sound fair and still pay badly, because the word count claims nothing about the hours the piece takes.

Industry observers note that say a 1,500-word article takes you 6 hours to research, write, and revise, and you need to earn $75 an hour. Quote at least $450, which works out to 30 cents a word. A client offering 10 cents a word would pay $150 for the same 6 hours, or $25 an hour.

According to recent reports, thirty cents a word is in line with the Editorial Freelancers Association’s 2026 rate chart, built from a survey of more than 1,100 editorial freelancers. Its median range for work-for-hire articles and essays, which the client owns outright, runs from 25 to 45 cents a word.

In a recent development, start from the hourly rate you need, which is the yearly income you want plus your business costs and self-employment tax, divided by the hours you can bill clients for. Then time three typical pieces from first research to final revision, including interviews, client calls, and email. Multiply your average hours by that rate, and quote the result as a flat project fee. For a client who pays by the word, divide the fee by the word count to get the per-word rate to ask for.

Specialist knowledge, technical or regulated subjects like medicine, credentials such as a CPA license, and rush deadlines all justify a higher fee. In the same rate chart, median ranges for work-for-hire medical and technical writing start at $1 a word. Selling more rights should cost the client more. A contract that buys all rights, or makes the piece work for hire, leaves you nothing to resell or reprint, so price it above one that buys only the right to publish the piece first.

Over time, collect testimonials, quote each new client above the last, and win more clients directly, since no platform takes a share of what they pay.

Divide every offer by the hours the piece will take, and counter any that fall short of your hourly rate with your project fee.
