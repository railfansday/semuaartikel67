📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sylvester Stallone has been a Hollywood institution for so long — his breakthrough movie Rocky premiered 50 years ago — that his previous underdog status as a struggling actor seems perfectly mythic in the new behind-the-scenes biographical drama I Play Rocky.

The fairy-tale aspect of I Play Rocky comes from its background story. In this, young Stallone (played by Anthony Ippolito) is a virtually homeless outsider, working at a movie theater, who after spending 16 months on writing eight screenplays, joins the creative zeitgeist of 1970s Hollywood, also known as the American Renaissance. It is this personal journey that illuminates the Stallone legend that climaxes with Rocky winning the Academy Award for best picture over such esteemed movies as Taxi Driver, Network, Bound for Glory, and All the President’s Men.

Starting with Stallone practicing elocution (using Walt Whitman’s “I Sing the Body Electric”) to soften his rough Latest York accent, the film then places him against odds in an era of cultural ferment that included such films as Altman’s The Long Goodbye and Coppola’s The Conversation. (Stallone pays particular attention to the intonation of Coppola’s actors repeating the line “He’d kill us if he found out.”) Screenwriter Peter Gamble doesn’t make that detail ominous but simplifies the life-or-death aspect of Stallone’s career and artistic ambition as part of the wannabe actor’s desperate, working-class consciousness — inspired by viewing the heavyweight championship boxing match between Muhammad Ali and Chuck Wepner. It’s easier to take than the sociological theme in Green Book, the egregious previous film of director Peter Farrelly. I Play Rocky regains the genial humanism of such Farrelly brothers’ movies as Me, Myself and Irene, Stuck on You, and The Ringer, and especially the Farrelly brothers’ script for Outside Providence.

Kiki Seto (Talia Shire) and Anthony Ippolito (Sylvester Stallone) in I Play Rocky.

Stallone’s ambition is epitomized by his insistence that the Rocky screenplay he wrote in four days and sold to producers Irwin Winkler and Robert Chartoff can be filmed only starring himself. He demands, “I play Rocky.” That’s not a playground taunt in the milieu of financial bargaining and egotism that’s gleefully depicted by a series of Hollywood types — P. J. Byrne and Toby Kebbell as Winkler and Chartoff, Rob Demery as Joe Frazier, and a standout cameo by Trevor St. John as a self-serious fight coordinator.

Recent findings suggest that in Hollywood’s gladiatorial circus, Stallone’s striving and eventual success become a credible moral victory. He must claim his own identity and career path separately from his worldly father Frank (Matt Dillon gives a moving characterization of a parent’s cynicism, affection, and personal regret). His wife Sasha (AnnaSophie Robb) encourages him: “I’d rather be optimistic and be wrong than pessimistic and be right.” These backstage details (including a hostile producer who dismisses Stallone as “a droopy-eyed hack actor”) make I Play Rocky a credible and appealing saga of Stallone’s indefatigable confidence. The movie rejects the sort of dark, objectionable narcissism that we saw in the Springsteen hagiography Deliver Me from Nowhere.

It is important that the filmmakers accurately measure Rocky’s significance. Farrelly and Gamble mix film history with good sense and humor when portraying wild-eyed director John Avildsen (Jay Duplass) deep in his own world of pragmatic professionalism (“Great art never comes through compromise”). They also capture Stallone’s own, uncanny sense of achieving the proper effect — not so much when Rocky emotes to his manager Mickey as when, in a startling shot, he points to details in a significant-screen image of Kirk Douglas in Champion, from 1948.

Putting Rocky in proper perspective is a curatorial feat. It recognizes Stallone’s film as a work of pop lore. Its popularity gave Hollywood the opportunity to ignore scary powerful art like Scorsese’s Taxi Driver in favor of the folk and commercial elements that are easily enjoyed in American culture — although missing from Millennial film culture. Remember: In 1976, Rocky seemed an artificial retread of old boxing films, pilfering the ethnic sentimentality of Marty, On the Waterfront, and Requiem for a Heavyweight as if Muhammad Ali had never happened.

But I Play Rocky works differently. It recalls Stallone’s response to Wepner (“This guy won’t go down!”) to achieve the authentic Millennial pop lore of Better Man, Song Sung Blue, Back 2 America, Power Ballad, and the upcoming Madden. (These restorative myths correct the disappointing Creed sequels that failed to live up to Rocky.) I Play Rocky channels Stallone’s personal perseverance to enlighten our current demoralized sensibilities.

Armond White is National Review’s culture critic and the author of Fresh Position: The Prince Chronicles and Make Spielberg Great Again: The Steven Spielberg Chronicles.
