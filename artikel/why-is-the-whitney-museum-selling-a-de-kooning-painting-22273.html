Why Is the Whitney Museum Selling a De Kooning Painting?

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that looking to cash in on marquee week in Newly introduced York, the Whitney Museum of American Art has consigned a Willem de Kooning painting from its collection to Christie's for its 20th-century evening sale tomorrow, November 17. Once a promised gift to the museum, de Kooning's “Yellow River” (1958) is expected to fetch up to $18 million.

The practice of museum deaccessioning has long been a subject of controversy. In 2022, the Association of Art Museum Directors (AAMD) loosened its policy to allow institutions to apply funds from the sale of deaccessioned art toward the “direct care” of their collections.

A lot description for “Yellow River” on Christie's website notes that the proceeds will benefit the Whitney's collection. The Whitney has not yet responded to Hyperallergic's request for comment, but a museum spokesperson told Artnet News that deaccessioning is “a component of the Whitney’s collections strategic strategy, allowing the museum to thoughtfully evaluate and refine its holdings in support of its long-term collection priorities.”.

It has been revealed that the de Kooning painting going under that hammer had a rooted connection to the Whitney before it was bequeathed to the museum in 2023. Postwar art collector, attorney, and museum trustee David Solinger, who would go on to become the museum's first president beyond the Whitney family in 1966, purchased the work from de Kooning's sold-out exhibition at Sidney Janis Gallery in 1959.

Sources indicate that “Yellow River” emerged from a series of “highway" paintings representing the artist's shift from cacophonous urban compositions to more airy landscapes, designating the freeway as a conduit connecting New York City to the Hudson River Valley and Long Island's South Fork. In vibrant warm yellow, earthy brown tones, and stretches of deep blue, de Kooning's decisive gestures direct viewers across overlapping paths and visual rest stops throughout the canvas.

In a recent development, “I love to be on those highways,” de Kooning said to British critic and curator David Sylvester about the expansive roads during a BBC interview in 1960. “And they are really not very pretty, but the big embankments and the shoulders of the roads and the curves are flawless.”.

“Yellow River” hung at the foot of Solinger's bed for some time, serving as a testament to Solinger's fascination with the artist. His wife, Betty Ann Besch Solinger, took ownership of the work after Solinger passed in 1996, and in 2011, she designated the work as a promised gift to the Whitney in honor of her late husband. It officially entered the Whitney's holdings in 2023, the same year that the museum deaccessioned seven works, including an Edward Hopper landscape, and sent them to auction to benefit the collection.
