Latest Update: Experimental Drug Shrinks Tumors in Two of the Hardest-To-Treat Cancers

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A single experimental drug designed to block signals from several cancer-driving mutations has shown encouraging early results, raising the possibility of treating multiple hard-to-treat cancers.

Industry observers note that when pancreatic or lung cancer continues to grow despite several treatments, doctors can find themselves with few remaining options. In some patients, the difficulty lies in genetic mutations that keep cancer cells growing and have historically been difficult to target with drugs. An experimental cancer drug called ERAS-0015 has shown promising results in an early clinical trial, shrinking tumors in patients who had already undergone other forms of treatment.

The drug targets RAS, a family of genes that provide instructions for making proteins involved in controlling cell growth and division. In healthy cells, RAS proteins act like molecular switches, turning on when cells receive signals to grow and turning off when those signals are no longer needed. Certain genetic mutations can leave these switches stuck in the “on” position, continuously instructing cells to grow and helping sustain cancer.

Sources indicate that for years, scientists struggled to develop drugs capable of interfering with these faulty proteins. ERAS-0015 takes an approach known as a pan-RAS molecular glue. It works by bringing proteins together at the molecular level and is designed to bind to RAS while the switch is active, blocking growth signals associated with several different cancer-driving RAS mutations.

Sources indicate that “The overall goal of precision oncology is to match a patient’s mutation, or the ‘Achilles Heel’ of a specific individual’s cancer, to a drug that directly targets that mutation. This is what we are attempting to do by studying the effectiveness of ERAS-0015 in patients with RAS alterations,” said Dr. Judy Wang, a medical oncologist at Florida Cancer Specialists and Research Institute in Sarasota, Florida.

Wang presented findings from 148 patients participating in the phase I AURORAS-1 clinical trial on November 19, 2026, at the 38th EORTC-NCI-AACR Symposium on Molecular Targets and Cancer Therapeutics in Barcelona, Spain.

Recent findings suggest that the trial began in June 2025 and is evaluating ERAS-0015 both alone and in combination with other anticancer therapies. Participants include people with pancreatic cancer, colorectal (bowel) cancer, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) that has spread beyond the original tumor site, and other solid tumors driven by RAS mutations. The results presented at the symposium included 83 patients with pancreatic cancer and 37 with NSCLC. The trial is continuing to enroll patients.

“AURORAS-1 has enrolled patients that have tried a number of other treatments for their cancer. In this context, ERAS-0015 treatment caused a 44% response rate in patients with RAS-mutant pancreatic cancer and a 60% response rate in patients with RAS-mutant lung cancer. A ‘response’ means the patients experienced considerable tumor shrinkage, and these response rates are notable as they show clinically meaningful activity from ERAS-0015 treatment in patient groups that have few if any therapeutic options to address their cancer. In addition to this, ERAS-0015 was well tolerated with few severe side effects,” Wang told the symposium.

These response rates came from relatively small groups of patients receiving a 32 mg dose of the drug. Tumors shrank in 11 of 25 patients with pancreatic cancer who had previously received chemotherapy and in three of five patients with lung cancer who had previously been treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors, drugs that help the immune system recognize and attack cancer, or platinum-based chemotherapy.

Researchers gradually increased doses of ERAS-0015 from 2 mg to 40 mg to evaluate how well patients tolerated the drug. After a median follow-up of just over four months, there was no indication that the maximum tolerated dose had been reached.

At doses of 16 to 32 mg, the most common side effects, affecting at least 10% of patients, were rash, diarrhea, nausea, and inflammation of the lining of the mouth. Twelve patients (9.8%) experienced severe treatment-related side effects, classified as grade 3 or higher. No dose-limiting side effects were reported.

“We are encouraged by the preliminary clinical activity and tolerability of ERAS-0015 in patients that have tried a number of other treatments for RAS-driven lung and pancreatic cancer,” remarked Wang.

According to recent reports, “Our goal is to give clinicians new, better options for treating their patients with RAS-driven cancers. Early results suggest ERAS-0015 has the potential to be one of these options.”.

Phase I clinical trials involve relatively small numbers of patients and are primarily designed to evaluate safety and determine appropriate doses. Although the tumor shrinkage observed in this trial provides an early indication of the drug’s potential, larger phase II and phase III clinical trials will be necessary to confirm its safety and effectiveness.

According to recent reports, “If these early results are confirmed, ERAS-0015 could offer patients with RAS-driven cancers a fresh option. Clinical development of ERAS-0015 would be impossible without the patients, caregivers, investigators and participating trial centers that put their trust in us to take part in our study. We are deeply grateful for their contribution to AURORAS-1,” Wang concluded.

Professor Elena Garralda, chair of the EORTC-NCI-AACR Symposium and co-director of the Vall d’Hebron Institute of Oncology’s clinical research program, was not involved in the study. She commented: “This is the first presentation of these preliminary results from the AURORAS-1 clinical trial and they have been eagerly awaited. They show that the RAS inhibitor, ERAS-0015, has encouraging anti-tumor activity in cancers that are hard to treat successfully and for which there are few treatment options available. We look forward to further results from larger clinical trials, which are investigating whether ERAS-0015 can help a broader group of patients.

“This is one of several studies being presented at the EORTC-NCI-AACR Symposium that are beginning to show it is possible to design drugs that target cancers driven by RAS mutations, which was not thought possible until recently.”.
