Industry News: Only 18% of Gen Z workers want to build AI skills. A Wharton professor claims using AI is riskier for them

The generation with the longest careers ahead of them appears to be the least interested in sharpening their AI skills, according to research from talent services firm Seramount released Thursday: Only 18% of Gen Z employees want to develop or improve their AI skills, compared with 36% of millennials, 46% of Gen Xers, and 43% of baby boomers.

For younger workers, using AI could come with a different set of professional risks than it does for their more experienced colleagues.

“Because younger workers haven’t had the same amount of time in their career fields to build up their standing as experts, using a technology that blurs their own contribution to their work can feel especially risky,” Jessica Reif, an assistant professor of management at the University of Pennsylvania’s Wharton School, told Fortune.

The survey of more than 1,800 U.S. white-collar employees at companies with at least 500 workers, conducted in June through NORC’s AmeriSpeak panel, found that 58% of Gen Z employees use AI in their current roles, compared with 60% of baby boomers, 63% of millennials, and 67% of Gen Xers. Seramount defined Gen Z as workers 29 and younger.

In a recent development, that leaves a puzzle. Gen Z workers, often cast as digital natives, use AI at nearly the same rate as baby boomers but show far less interest in getting better at it.

Younger workers are considerably more concerned about what using AI might say about their own abilities.

According to recent reports, some 22% of Gen Z employees surveyed by Seramount worry that using AI could lead others to question their skills, compared with 10% of millennials and Gen Xers and just 8% of baby boomers. Only 27% of Gen Z employees feel comfortable acknowledging their use of AI for a work task, compared with between 47% and 53% of their older colleagues.

Those concerns are not entirely unfounded. In four experiments involving more than 4,400 participants, Reif, then at Duke University, and her co-authors Richard Larrick and Jack Soll found in a study published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences that employees described as receiving help from AI were perceived as less competent, less diligent, and lazier than those described as receiving other forms of help or whose use of help wasn’t specified.

Industry observers note that the stigma could also influence hiring decisions. In one experiment, managers who used AI less frequently themselves favored candidates who did not use AI, while managers who were more frequent AI people favored candidates who used it daily.

The penalty in those experiments held regardless of the age of the employee being evaluated. But Reif remarked its consequences could be more substantial for workers who are still trying to establish themselves professionally.

“A senior employee who uses AI has a more substantial record of results that can offset doubts about their effort or ability, but an early-career employee doesn’t have that record yet so others might weight their AI use more heavily when evaluating them,” Reif mentioned.

Sources indicate that the pressure can work in the opposite direction, too. A survey of 3,001 professionals worldwide released Tuesday by the London School of Economics’ Inclusion Initiative and consulting firm Protiviti found that 48% of employees worry about being judged for not using AI enough at work, compared with 20% who worry about being judged for using it.

For workers at the beginning of their careers, that presents a dilemma: They need to demonstrate their ability to work with AI while also proving what they are capable of doing themselves.

Beyond concerns about their professional reputation, younger workers appear less convinced of the benefits of using AI in the first place.

Only 30% of Gen Z employees in Seramount’s survey believe AI helps them work more efficiently, compared with roughly half of their older colleagues. And 41% believe it improves the quality of their work, compared with 56% of Gen Xers.

Young people are also questioning whether the immediate benefits of AI could come at the expense of their long-term development. A survey by Gallup, the Walton Family Foundation, and GSV Ventures of 1,572 Americans aged 14 to 29, released in April, found that 80% believe using AI tools is very or somewhat likely to make learning more difficult for them in the future.

In a recent development, among employed Gen Z respondents, 48% believe the risks of AI in the workforce outweigh its benefits, compared with 15% who believe the opposite. Some 69% place greater trust in work completed without AI than in AI-assisted work.

That skepticism, as Fortune has reported, partly reflects how poorly schools, employers, and government have prepared young people for an AI economy.

Younger employees’ attitudes toward AI may also reflect the environments in which they are being asked to use it.

Only 27% of Gen Z respondents in Seramount’s survey stated their organization provides support to help them integrate AI into their work, compared with 38% of millennials and 43% of Gen Xers. Younger employees were also the least comfortable seeking help from colleagues on how to use AI tools, with only 18% saying they felt comfortable doing so, compared with 34% of baby boomers.

Seramount suggested that junior employees may also have less need for AI in their current roles or more limited access to enterprise AI tools.

The LSE and Protiviti research points to the importance of training in AI adoption across generations: 93% of employees who had received AI training used the technology at work, compared with 50% of those who had not.

Not all research suggests young workers are being left behind on training, though. The LSE and Protiviti study found that 66% of Gen Z employees had received AI training in the past year, compared with 40% of baby boomers. That study polled professionals worldwide, while Seramount surveyed only U.S. workers.

For employers, failing to engage their youngest employees in AI adoption could create a longer-term talent development problem, echoing warnings that automating entry-level roles could cost companies their future workforce.

“If Gen Z falls behind on AI, employers risk weakening their future leadership pipeline,” Diana Forster, senior director of research at Seramount, said in a press release. She called on employers to provide clearer guidelines, training, and opportunities for young employees to practice using the technology.

Despite the challenges young workers face in embracing AI, the technological disruption could also work in their favor.

OpenAI CEO Sam Altman believes that periods of rapid technological change give people at the beginning of their careers a chance to gain a competitive advantage. As AI transforms how jobs are done and opens up new business opportunities, those with fresh ideas and new skills could have an edge over more established players.

“I think you want to start your career at a time of the greatest change, when the playing field is most open,” Altman said during an interview with Stanford’s Graduate School of Business in October, as previously reported by Fortune.

He added, “I think people with high potential, drive, ideas, a new way of looking at things, and new skills tend to do relatively well when the ground is shaking the most.”.

For workers concerned about being judged for using AI, Reif offered some advice.

“No matter their age, employees are increasingly in a position where they need to demonstrate their value above and beyond what AI can do,” Reif remarked. “Employees who are proactive in communicating how AI enables them to contribute more or in ways that would not have otherwise been possible may be able to counteract some of these perceptions.”.
