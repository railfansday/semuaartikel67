My cat, Mitzi, is 16 years and 8 months old. She has early-stage kidney disease and hyperthyroidism, which she is on medication for. About 2 months ago, she became deaf, and since then has refused to leave the master bedroom. I have moved her water bowl and litter box there and feed her there.

Is there anything I can do to get her to come out?â.

According to recent reports, at her age, sweet Mitzi has certainly earned the right to enjoy her golden years at her own pace, but I can imagine it is difficult to see her withdraw from the rest of the house, especially when you remember how she used to move around. However, I would not necessarily interpret her reluctance to leave the bedroom as something we need to correct. Losing her hearing can be quite disorienting. Imagine walking into a room where you can no longer hear footsteps, familiar voices, or the little sounds that help you understand what is happening around you. For Mitzi, the bedroom may now feel like her safest little world, where everything is familiar and predictable.

Sources indicate that I actually think you have done a wonderful job adapting her environment by bringing her food, water, and litter box closer. Rather than encouraging her to leave right away, I would focus on helping her feel confident enough to explore if she wishes. You could start by sitting with her near the bedroom doorway, offering a favorite treat or a little affection. Gradually place treats a short distance beyond the door, allowing her to decide whether she wants to investigate.

Sources indicate that take care to only approach her within her visual field, avoid startling her while she sleeps, and keep pathways clear and nonslip. Think of it as extending her latest world one comfortable step at a time, rather than asking her to return to her old routine.

There is one thing I would not overlook, though. Mitzi has both chronic kidney disease and hyperthyroidism, and at her age, reduced mobility or withdrawal can also be associated with arthritis, high blood pressure, visual changes, or inadequate control of her thyroid disease. I would recommend a senior cat veterinary checkup that includes blood pressure measurement, assessment for arthritis, and follow-up kidney and thyroid testing. Sometimes what looks like a behavioral change is actually a senior cat quietly telling us that they are in pain and something does not feel quite right.

Most importantly, please do not feel that you are encouraging a bad habit by keeping her essentials in the bedroom. You are adapting her home to meet her changing needs, and that is a beautiful expression of your love and care for her. If the bedroom is where Mitzi feels safe, eats well, drinks, uses her litter box, and enjoys your tech giant, that can be a perfectly good quality of life.

At this stage, happiness is not measured by how much of the house she explores, but by how comfortable and secure she feels in the little world she chooses.
