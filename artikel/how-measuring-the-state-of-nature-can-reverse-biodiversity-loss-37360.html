How measuring the state of nature can reverse biodiversity loss

Biodiversity is the cornerstone of human survival — providing us with food, shelter, medicines, clean air and water, and contributing to our general well-being. Its continuing loss is a major risk to us and to the global economy.

Sources indicate that extreme degradation of ecosystems is projected to cost the world at least US$2.7 trillion, or 2.3% of global gross domestic product, each year by 20301. That’s almost three times more than the annual cost of protecting nature (up to $967 billion)2, a figure that happens to be similar to the amount governments paid in 2024 to subsidize the fossil-fuel industry ($920 billion; see go.nature.com/4ejtafn).

Sources indicate that those stark numbers should drive efforts to protect nature. In 2022, the Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework set out an ambitious pathway to reach the vision of a world living in harmony with nature by 20503. But what’s missing is a way to measure progress. A systematic approach to tracking biodiversity losses and attempts to reverse them is urgently needed.

The Nature Positive Initiative (NPI) has begun to do this by developing metrics to record the ‘state of nature’. Launched in 2023, the NPI is a consortium of the world’s largest sustainable business and finance coalitions and conservation organizations.

In a recent development, the metrics aim to provide a globally consistent minimal framework — a simple common language — for tracking changes in nature across countries and sectors. They were developed through a consensus process and measure ecosystem extent and condition, species extinction risks and population trends across terrestrial, freshwater and marine realms4.

They are also designed to integrate with existing global standards and frameworks for reporting and disclosing nature-related financial risks, such as the Science Based Targets Network (SBTN)5 and the Taskforce on Nature-related Financial Disclosures (TNFD)6. This compatibility increases the likelihood that scientifically sound metrics will be reported and embedded by businesses and financial institutions in their disclosures and policies, and in systems for risk management and investment — all of which are essential to decision-making.

Industry observers note that so far, the metrics have been tested with 29 companies across 23 countries at more than 50 sites. However, they still need rigorous, independent assessment in real-world contexts to ascertain whether they are practical to implement and really improve environmental outcomes. Without empirical validation, there is a risk that these nature metrics might generate misleading signals to investors, fail to detect meaningful ecological change or impose monitoring cost burdens that limit the uptake of biodiversity projects.

Here we highlight four challenges and a way forward.

Industry observers note that although they are called metrics, the NPI measures are in fact a broad framework of indicators and recording approaches. Their focus on assessing the broad ‘state’ of ecosystems, rather than concentrating on the pressures on nature or actions for protection, is an important shift. It moves companies away from simply reporting their activities, instead making them and others accountable for outcomes. But it leaves latitude in how organizations will interpret and apply the metrics, potentially weakening their overall impact.

Industry observers note that for example, the metrics have three levels of detail or precision — preparatory, medium and high — to recognize the uneven global distribution of data, expertise and resources. Although this flexibility makes them easier to apply in diverse settings, it creates challenges for comparability and interpretation when assessments are undertaken on different spatial scales or levels of precision.

The Tiaozini wetlands in eastern China are a crucial ecological hub.Credit: Costfoto/NurPhoto via Getty.

The idea is that companies will get on board early with the preparatory level of precision and then progress towards more high-quality, ecologically meaningful measures and outcomes. A preparatory-level assessment might use data with relatively coarse spatial resolution and classify land cover as natural or not natural, for instance. More-advanced assessments would have higher resolution and use more-precise ecosystem classifications.

The concern is that organizations might cherry-pick ‘easy’ ecosystem characteristics or species to measure, potentially biasing their reporting towards apparent gains rather than describing those that are meaningful. For instance, common species are more likely to respond well to ecological improvements than specialist species are, even though the specialists might be a higher priority for conservation.

Over-reporting of improvements, particularly in relation to ecosystem connectivity and extent, can provide a misleading sense of how well species can survive genetic and demographic challenges. Better understanding of these measures is also key in guarding against changes that might not only bring benefits but also could do harm, such as when connectivity promotes the movement of invasive species that alter the local ecosystem.

In a recent development, another challenge is that nature is forever changing through a myriad of interactions between living species and the environment, as well as with shifting global processes such as climate change. With simple metrics, there is a risk that complex, dynamic processes and non-linear changes will be missed and the damages and risks underestimated.

Measuring the number of hectares restored, for instance, can indicate where conservation action has occurred, but not whether nature has recovered, ecosystem functions have been reinstated or threatened species populations are increasing.

It has been revealed that the inclusion of ecosystem condition and species status measures in the NPI metrics aim to address this complexity. But this will work only if they are not simplified to the point at which they no longer provide an accurate and comprehensive assessment of how a site is changing.

According to recent reports, reconciling the tension between a global approach for nature impacts and the local ecological and sociopolitical experiences of these impacts is challenging. Although global consistency is essential for corporate reporting so that investors can make informed comparisons, at the local level, biodiversity protection and the financial impacts and dependencies of communities on natural resources are inherently context-specific. Metrics designed to support global comparisons between organizations might overlook ecological, cultural and social values that are notable locally.

Many owners of the state of nature metrics and global biodiversity frameworks will draw heavily on global data sets. Such resources include the Global Forest Watch Open Data Portal and the Copernicus Global Dynamic Land Cover product for ecosystem extent and condition, and the IUCN Red List of Threatened Species as a proxy for species that are at risk of extinction.

Industry observers note that but without on-the-ground verification, the metrics will fail, particularly in data-poor regions, by misrepresenting fine-scale ecological conditions and changes. Relying on remote-sensing products that describe only the tree canopy, for example, can miss much about the structure of the forest below. Indigenous knowledges might also be disregarded. Overlooking local values and knowledge is concerning because it reinforces the broader global pattern of marginalizing local communities in environmental decision-making.

Implementation of the metrics and the incentives behind them that push companies to engage with nature measurement are likely to have both positive and negative impacts on nature and communities. What these impacts look like is currently unknown. This is why the metrics need to be independently tested to assess their value for nature, businesses and communities.

It has been revealed that impacts on nature will be driven by how priority species are determined, how the metrics are combined and compared and the time frame in which changes will be measured. For example, should equal weight be given to population change in critically endangered species and to species of important cultural value to local communities?

And how should owners compare shifts in the components of an ecosystem that typically change over decades (such as growth of a forest) to those that develop much more rapidly (such as the life cycles of pollinating insects)? Developing guidance on how to grapple with these complexities will be essential if the metrics are to describe the state of nature.
