It’s a funny account of what hard-to-fit feet can drive a person to do, and a reminder that the right bootfitter can change everything.

In my quest for ski boots that fit, I’ve seen bootfitters in at least seven states and two countries. I’ve visited Evo and REI, small independent shops, local chains, and garages-turned-ski shops. I’ve consulted boot designers, brand reps, professional athletes, and gear reviewers; tried Intuitions, ZipFits, and custom footbeds. I’ve seen one orthopedic doctor, three acupuncturists, and two physical therapists. I’ve stayed up at night thinking about boots, and developed recurring stress dreams about them.

In a recent development, last season, I was beginning to consider looping in a tarot reader or astrologer. Maybe I needed a psychotherapist, or a priest. Maybe I’d have to just quit skiing. And then, finally, I found my bootfitter. But not before resorting to some questionable behavior along the way. Here are seven desperate things I did in my search for a bootfitter who could solve my ski boot concerns.

According to recent reports, every pair of ski boots I wore from ages two through 22 were hand-me-downs from my older brothers. Then I started working in the ski industry. While skiing around Massive Sky during a media event, a kind man working for Nordica looked at my feet with a frown.

“What are those?” he asked. I was sliding around in some decade-old size 26.5 monstrosities. He shook his head and invited me to swing by the Nordica headquarters in Fresh Hampshire. There, he measured my feet and sent me off with a pair of 25.5 Nordica Promachines.

Everything changed. Suddenly I was in an aggressive stance, my shins met real resistance, my power transfer improved dramatically. I got a taste of what a good boot can do, shortly before developing a wicked case of tendonitis in my ankles and shins.

Recent findings suggest that after two seasons of excruciating, day-ending pain, I booked an appointment with an orthopedic foot and ankle specialist. I knew my boots were the culprit, and couldn’t be sure a doctor would understand the whole foot-encased-in-rigid-plastic thing. I lugged a giant bag with both my alpine and touring boots into the orthopedic practice and pulled them out for show-and-tell.

According to recent reports, the physician kindly assured me he knew what ski boots were, and that I wasn’t dealing with any serious medical issues. I’d have to take my boots back where they belonged.

In a recent development, the owner of Santa Fe’s one dedicated ski shop sized me down yet again, into the stiffest 24.5 he had on hand—a 110 from Fischer. I felt like I was skiing in socks and a vise-grip at the same time, and I lost feeling in my feet on the first lap. Mercifully, he let me return them.

I’d hit the central problem: boots significant enough to be comfortable gave me tendonitis, and boots small enough to prevent it gave me bunions and nerve pain. So I took my boots to Taos Bootdoctors, where a whole team of bootfitters worked patiently to expand the forefoot. I’d ski for a day or two and come back, over and over, looking for just a few more millimeters. I brought six-packs and did my best to be charming, and the lovely staff never made me feel less crucial than the tourists dropping thousands of dollars on gear. Still, I couldn’t finish a day without pain.

I soldiered through the season, eyeing an upcoming hut trip with dread. I could manage shorter tours, but a week of daylong missions posed some real problems. Once we were in the mountains, I knew no amount of bunion donuts and anti-inflammatories would save me. I had to instate boots-off breaks in the middle of eight-hour ski days, carefully pulling my socked feet out of jail and propping them up on a backpack in the snow while my friends took an extra lap. Sweet, stupid relief.

Recent findings suggest that finally, I heard a rumor about a man in Seattle working out of his garage who was willing to do unorthodox things to ski boots. He had a history of custom-fitting plug boots for ski racers, which seemed like a good sign. While visiting family in Washington, I brought him both pairs of boots.

He looked at my feet, listened patiently, and rattled off a comprehensive plan that involved several devices I’d never seen before. Overnight, he molded both pairs into a completely new shape. I never had to touch either boot again, and proceeded with great pleasure to ski 12-hour days in the backcountry and endless resort laps with happy feet. I had found him—the bootfitter I was looking for. Then, he retired.

Recent findings suggest that when my touring boots started to go, I bought a replacement pair and nobody could make them fit. The experience began to take on mythic proportions. I felt like the princess with a pea beneath her many mattresses, or an ugly stepsister trying to cram her foot into a crystal slipper: all discomfort and wrong fit.

It seemed my trusty Tecnicas were the problem. I tried on every touring boot at ski shops in three states. I ordered over a dozen pairs online, obsessively double-checking return policies and momentarily racking up a very scary credit card bill. Eventually, I came crawling back to my old Tecnicas with no initiative, two full seasons into the search.

I hesitated to say yes to ski trips, afraid I’d have to sit out ski days because of pain or busted gear. Eventually, it happened: I took my tired old touring boots to Rogers Pass in B.C. and, on day three, broke the upper cuff clean off the bottom section of the boot. Thankfully we were just a few hundred yards from the truck. I spent the entire day sitting at the trailhead.

In the meantime, my alpine boots had begun to warp. I found a replacement pair and was back at square one: home in Fresh Mexico, with two pairs of ski boots that didn’t fit, rapidly dwindling patience, and no bootfitter.

I considered that this might be the end of the road for me as a skier. Then, I learned that one of the best skiers I know had been contemplating quitting the sport because of foot pain. He’d even gone so far as to teach himself to snowboard. But then he found exactly what I’d been looking for: yet another fit-obsessed industry veteran working out of his garage.

I visited another home workshop in western Washington and the bootfitter listened carefully as I explained the problem. Then, he overhauled both pairs of boots, tirelessly stretching the forefoots to give my feet room to breathe. They took on a completely new shape: generous and wide. I took both pairs out for a spin and sighed in relief.

According to recent reports, there are a lot of great bootfitters out there. But for a freak like me, I’ve learned I need something of a mad scientist. If you’re plagued with bone spurs, high insteps, wide feet—there’s hope, I promise. With a little faith, a lot of persistence, and maybe something like divine intervention, I trust you’ll find the bootfitter you need. Let’s just hope they don’t retire.
