Industry News: AI boom or bust? 10 under-the-radar stock picks and investing ideas for either scenario

🌐 Source: Fortune • 📅 27 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 7 min read (1376 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways
  • Industry observers note that investors don’t have to decide whether AI will change the world.
  • According to recent reports, the same goes if the bubble bursts—a risk that Nvidia’s recent troubles heighten.
  • To help navigate both sides of the trade, Fortune has identified six investment strategies for 2027.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that investors don’t have to decide whether AI will change the world. They do, however, have to determine how much of their portfolio should depend on it. After all, AI bets now extend far beyond the obvious megacap tech stocks. Utilities and power-equipment makers have been swept up in the data center build-out. Even parts of the energy sector now trade on the promise that AI will consume an enormous amount of electricity.

According to recent reports, the same goes if the bubble bursts—a risk that Nvidia’s recent troubles heighten. Because classic AI stocks are weighted so heavily in indexes like the S&P 500, even a standard 60/40 portfolio can mean betting big on this nascent technology. Investors looking to hedge must find something that doesn’t depend on the very boom they’re worried about.

To help navigate both sides of the trade, Fortune has identified six investment strategies for 2027. Three are built to ride the boom—and three will help you survive a bust.

The VanEck Data Center Supply Chain ETF allocates to companies that help power, operate, and equip data centers. It’s a bet on the physical infrastructure of the AI boom rather than solely the hyperscalers and software developers themselves.

Industry observers note that that’s because the fund gives investors exposure to several layers of the data center supply chain. Think chipmakers, along with companies that make notable computing components, like Nvidia, Broadcom, AMD, and Micron. Industrial companies like Eaton, Vertiv, and Johnson Controls that provide the electrical equipment and cooling solutions for these energy-intensive facilities are included as well. Meanwhile, Quanta Services and Amphenol add exposure to the construction and connectivity themes.

Tom Essaye, founder of Sevens Report Research, likened the broadening AI trade to “water sweeping across a floor,” reaching “places that people would not think had anything to do with AI.”.

Bryan Armour, director of ETF and passive strategies research for North America at Morningstar, points to RACK as a way to look beyond traditional tech funds and growth stocks to capitalize on the AI trade. “You need to build the data centers, you need to build the chips and the GPUs and memory and all the different components,” Armour noted.

The Roundhill Neocloud ETF bets on the landlords of AI computing: businesses that build specialized data centers packed with GPUs and rent out the processing power AI companies need to train and run their models.

In a recent development, two neocloud providers, Nebius and CoreWeave, make up more than half of the fund; many of the rest have been in Bitcoin mining and are retooling for AI. The bet is that rising demand for computing will fill up these facilities and lead to upcoming contracts.

Sources indicate that the Roundhill Photonics & Optics ETF (LYTE) and the KraneShares Photonic and Optical ETF (LUMA) both target a key bottleneck in the AI build-out: moving enormous amounts of data promptly and efficiently. Both funds offer exposure to companies developing optical technologies that increase bandwidth and reduce the energy needed to move information between chips and across data centers as AI computing demands grow.

These ETFs invest in the suppliers behind those connections. LYTE holds stocks such as Ciena and Eoptolink, while KraneShares’ LUMA counts LandMark Optoelectronics, Soitec, and Marvell among its major holdings.

Sources indicate that “AI will still have legs next year,” said Todd Rosenbluth, head of research at VettaFi. “There will still be demand for companies that offer [this] exposure.”.

It has been revealed that still, investors allocating to these more specialized funds must be prepared: All the names here rolled out in 2026, so they don’t have long track records and tend to swoon when the AI trade comes under pressure. “The narrower you invest in the market,” Rosenbluth remarked, “the greater the volatility.”.

Think the AI boom is overheated? These strategies can help hedge your portfolio if the AI trade falls back to earth.

The Invesco S&P 500 Equal Weight ETF offers a straightforward way to loosen AI’s grip on your portfolio. Unlike the traditional S&P 500 index, which gives the biggest companies the largest weighting, RSP resets its holdings to equal weighting each quarter. That puts chipmaking giant Nvidia on par with any other company at each quarterly rebalance. If megacap tech underperforms while the rest of the market holds up, RSP could lose less than the traditional S&P 500 index. That stated, for the first nine months of 2026, RSP returned roughly 9.9%, including reinvested dividends, lagging the broad-market index’s 12.7% total return.

The Invesco S&P 500 Low Volatility ETF (SPLV) buys the 100 S&P 500 stocks with the smallest price swings over the previous 12 months. SPLV can offset some AI risk because the fund allocates to seemingly steadier businesses, rather than companies with exceptionally high growth expectations. The fund has little exposure to Massive Tech and much larger allocations to utilities, financials, and real estate. Because SPLV counts utilities among its holdings, however, it’s not completely AI-free.

Sources indicate that although SPLV lagged the S&P 500 during the first nine months of 2026, it remained positive for the year through Sept. 30. The fund provided some downside cushioning in 2022, when the Federal Reserve started aggressively hiking interest rates. That year, in total returns, the S&P 500 dropped 18.1%, while SPLV fell only 4.9%.

Sources indicate that the iShares Core High Dividend ETF (HDV) targets high-paying dividend stocks. The appeal is less “this manufacturer could change the world” and more “this company can pay shareholders now.” Investors who want a stake in established businesses rather than expectations of explosive growth might consider HDV. It’s been a banner year for the fund, with a strong first nine months in 2026. Including reinvested dividends, HDV returned approximately 17.5%, beating the S&P 500’s total return of 12.75%.

It has been revealed that the strategy is not foolproof, though. Companies can cut their dividends, and the fund can dive with the broader market; however, it can be a different and welcome source of returns.

According to recent reports, the Vanguard Morningstar Value ETF (VTV) invests in large-cap stocks that are considered relatively inexpensive against their business fundamentals. Where the AI trade often asks investors to pay up for future growth, value investing means finding assets that are trading for less than their intrinsic worth. That makes VTV a potential hedge if the market sours on these growth stocks.

JPMorgan Chase, Berkshire Hathaway, ExxonMobil, and Johnson & Johnson are among the fund’s holdings, as well as a smattering of tech names like Micron. For the first nine months of 2026, VTV returned approximately 14.8%.

Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities offer a hedge against a less obvious risk of the AI boom: that building the future makes everything more expensive. A massive build-out of data centers, power plants, and transmission lines could weigh on supplies of equipment and skilled labor. That can push inflation higher.

These government bonds adjust their principal with the consumer price index. Their fixed interest rate applies to that adjusted balance, meaning payments rise alongside inflation. Investors can access TIPS through funds such as the Vanguard Short-Term Inflation-Protected Securities ETF (VTIP) and the iShares TIPS Bond ETF (TIP). Although both lagged the S&P 500 during the first nine months of 2026, when inflation was rampant they held up much better. (In 2022, on a total return basis, including distributions, the S&P 500 tumbled more than 18%, while VTIP and TIP shed roughly 3% and 12%, respectively.).

VettaFi’s Rosenbluth warns that inflation is not in the rearview mirror, in his opinion. “The environment we’re in today, where companies are spending a lot to support artificial intelligence, that can and has been contributing to inflation being elevated.”.

Industry observers note that for Rosenbluth, that concern fits into a broader challenge for 2027, when some view this breakthrough technology as overhyped and others believe it’s still in the early stages. Making sure a portfolio has room for more than one outcome is key.

Recent findings suggest that “Everyone is long AI,” he stated. The priority is “just having a balanced portfolio, because there’s just such a wide range of potential outcomes next year.”.
