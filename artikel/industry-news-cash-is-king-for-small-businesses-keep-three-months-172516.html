Industry News: Cash Is King for Small Businesses – Keep Three Months of Fixed Costs in Reserve

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that keep a cash reserve worth at least three months of your business’s fixed costs, the bills that don’t shrink when sales do. Hold it in a separate business savings account that pays interest, so a slow season or a customer who pays late doesn’t force you into costly borrowing.

It has been revealed that in 2025, the typical small business with a Chase business account had cash to cover less than 18 days of its usual spending if no money came in, the JPMorganChase Institute found. With that little cash, one massive customer paying a few weeks late can leave you short for payroll.

Size the reserve by fixed costs, not total spending. When sales slow, you buy less inventory, but rent, insurance, loan payments, subscriptions, and the pay of the staff you keep come due in full. If your sales are seasonal, hold three months of those bills plus the part of them your slow-season sales don’t cover, since that gap returns every year.

According to recent reports, to build it, pick a share of a typical month’s profit and set up a recurring transfer of that amount into a separate business savings or money market account that pays interest. Kept out of checking, the reserve won’t get spent on everyday bills, and it stays apart from your household emergency fund, which covers your own bills if the business can’t pay you.

Say a dental practice’s rent, staff pay, equipment loan payment, and insurance come to $45,000 a month. Its three-month reserve is $135,000, built over two years at $5,625 a month. When an insurer holds up the practice’s claim payments for six weeks, the practice covers payroll from the reserve instead of borrowing, then refills it as the payments arrive.

Spend the reserve only on a real shortfall, such as a late payment, a slow season, or a broken machine you can’t work without, never on an opportunity like a bulk discount. Refill it from the next money that comes in, before you spend on anything that can wait. With a 13-week cash forecast, you can see a shortfall weeks before it hits. A business line of credit, opened while sales are strong, can cover a gap that outlasts the reserve.

Pull last year’s bank statements, total the fixed costs, and divide by four for your three-month target. Then set up the recurring transfer before this month ends.
