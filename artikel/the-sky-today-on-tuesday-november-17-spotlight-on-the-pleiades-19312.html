Sources indicate that early this evening brighter targets are best, as the Moon doesn’t set until late. Ready for viewing as the winter constellations start to dominate earlier each day is the Pleiades, an open cluster cataloged as M45.

Known since antiquity and readily visible to the naked eye, the Pleiades contains several bright stars arranged in a tiny dipperlike shape. Located in northwestern Taurus, the cluster is sometimes mistaken for the Little Dipper, although that asterism is much larger and lies in the northern sky, anchored at the end of its handle by Polaris, the North Star. The Pleiades shines with a collective magnitude of 1.6 and spans roughly 110’ — a huge swath of sky. It lies above the sideways V-shape of Taurus’ face as the Bull rises in the east this evening, and is already 30° high by 7:30 P.M. local time. How many stars can you make out with the naked eye? Most observers see six or seven.

Once you’ve taken in the cluster without any optical aid, zoom in with binoculars or a finder scope. (Any higher magnification and you won’t see most of the cluster at once, but instead be limited to a smaller portion.) Many more stars will now pop into your field of view. If you do opt for high magnification, look for a faint grayish haze around the stars — this is nearby dust reflecting the stars’ light, and requires clear, dark conditions to make out.

It has been revealed that the Leonid meteor shower peaks tonight, with the best meteor viewing overnight into the early-morning hours of November 18, as the Moon sets around midnight and the radiant rises higher. Keep reading for more detail below on how to observe the shower.

The peak of the Leonid meteor shower, caused by the dusty debris of Comet 55P/Tempel-Tuttle, is best seen early on the morning of the 18th, after the Moon has set and the shower’s radiant rises high in the hours before dawn.

Recent findings suggest that two hours before sunrise, Leo is high in the southeast, with bright Jupiter and Mars joining the curved shape of the Sickle asterism. The Leonids’ radiant is located just inside the cuved blade’s inner edge, near 3rd-magnitude Zeta (ζ) Leonis to the upper left (north) of Mars and Jupiter. The two planets appear roughly side by side in the sky around this time, with ruddy Mars at left and more golden-yellow Jupiter at right. Regulus sits just to their lower left.

It has been revealed that to catch the most meteors, you’ll actually want to look a little ways away from Leo, about 40° to 60° to the left or right of the constellation. This is where meteors with the longest trains will also occur. Although this shower isn’t particularly strong, you can expect to see roughly a half-dozen or more Leonid meteors per hour. The Leonid shower produces some of the fastest-moving meteors we experience all year, streaking through the sky at some 44 miles (70 kilometers) per hour.
