During the midterm elections, Americans made one thing very clear: Data centers must be reined in.

A vast majority of communities across the country voted to ban or restrict the construction of data centers, as concerns about their impact on the environment and public health grow.

A fresh study by Harvard researchers indicates that these fears are not unfounded. The findings published today in Nature Sustainability show that the carbon emissions and energy use of hyperscale data centers amount to as much as those of entire states and more than those of the country of Denmark.

I spoke with Gianluca Guidi, a postdoctoral fellow in the Department of Biostatistics and lead author of the paper, to discuss how the team collected data on energy use despite a lack of industry transparency, the rapid evolution of AI infrastructure and future research goals.

Hyperscale data centers are the largest and most powerful data center facilities, featuring thousands of servers to power cloud computing, model training and the global use of AI. Guidi said he was inspired to look into data center emissions and the broader impacts of generative AI after reading a paper on bitcoin mining’s electricity use, which found that just 34 mining facilities, combined, consumed as much electricity as the entire city of Los Angeles.

The study narrowed in on 403 of these facilities to provide the most up-to-date and transparent measures of their electricity use and carbon emissions between May 2024 and April 2025.

Tech companies are not quick to disclose their energy use, so Guidi and his team had to circumvent these obstacles to get accurate metrics. The team used satellite imagery to narrow down 675 candidate sites to 403 verified facilities, estimated annual electricity consumption, and linked them to Environmental Protection Agency data to measure each facility’s carbon emissions.

“I would say the data preparation, data collection and validation were 100% the toughest part of the whole project,” Guidi said during a phone interview, explaining that there is a need for transparency and people should have access to this data.

Industry observers note that during the study, the team found that these hyperscale data centers emitted 25.7 million metric tons of carbon dioxide, more than the entire country of Denmark and far exceeding the last peer-reviewed estimate from 2018. The 403 facilities also used between 68 and 99 terawatt-hours of electricity during the testing period — about 1.8% of all US electricity consumption.

Recent findings suggest that A majority of the electricity (54%) that powers these hyperscale data centers comes from natural gas and coal, despite many tech companies signing corporate pledges to make substantial investments in renewable energy. Fossil fuels have long been known to generate massive amounts of carbon dioxide, exacerbating global warming and climate change, despite many of these tech companies. “We need to distinguish between what companies commit to on paper, and then the actual physical emissions associated with the whole powering of these infrastructures,” Guidi said.

The Harvard team also pointed out that the paper’s data is from last spring, meaning the industry’s 2026 footprint is certainly substantially larger today.

According to recent reports, “I feel like the pace at which the industry is growing is really outpacing the pace at which people like us — scientists — are working in order to understand (HDC) impacts,” Guidi said. “And we’re lagging behind because of the way the system is made.”.

As data centers scale and expand, the research will need to as well, the researchers mentioned. The team is already working to expand its dataset to investigate the broader public health consequences of hyperscale data centers, including water consumption and noise pollution.
