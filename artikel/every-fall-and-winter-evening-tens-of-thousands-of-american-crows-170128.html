Every fall and winter evening, tens of thousands of American crows fly to the same spot to sleep, and scientists can only guess why they do it

American crows gather in huge winter roosts, possibly for protection, warmth, access to food and opportunities to share information.

On fall and winter evenings, American crows leave their daytime haunts and fly to shared sleeping sites called roosts.

Some roosts hold fewer than 100 crows. Others hold hundreds of thousands, or even more than a million. Roosts that number in the tens of thousands are common.

Dr. Kevin J. McGowan of the Cornell Lab of Ornithology addressed the question in a guide last updated in 2010. Why crows do it is “largely a matter of conjecture,” he wrote, listing four hypotheses.

It has been revealed that american crows often live in family groups made up of a breeding pair and offspring from the previous two years. The whole family helps raise the young.

Their diet runs from grains, seeds, fruits and insects to small animals, carrion and garbage. They are also known for solving problems and finding unusual food sources.

Recent findings suggest that partners in Flight estimates the species’ global breeding population at 28 million. Numbers declined slightly between 1966 and 2019, according to the North American Breeding Bird Survey.

Industry observers note that during the nesting season, most breeding crows sleep on their own territories. Afterward they join the roosts.

“It appears that all crows will join winter roosts, even territorial breeding crows,” he wrote.

Non-breeding crows may stay on the home territory, wander between foraging flocks or leave the area for months.

Roost numbers peak in winter and fall away near the start of the breeding season, usually in March.

An hour or two before full darkness, crows collect in an area away from the final roost. There they spend their time calling, chasing and fighting.

Recent findings suggest that groups from several of these congregation areas may merge at one staging area. At dark, the main body moves to the roost itself.

That final site can be a single dense woodlot or a spread of many suitable trees.

A 1972 report put one roost at Fort Cobb, Oklahoma, at more than two million crows.

It has been revealed that the first hypothesis is about the site. Crows may gather because one spot offers good shelter, protection from predators or suitable trees.

The guide compares that kind of roost to a crowded hotel, where the guests share a building but do not really interact.

In a recent development, the second idea is safety in numbers. Large owls are the predators crows fear most, and a crow in a group may be safer than one alone.

“Owls probably are regular attendants at crow roosts,” he added.

Crows also work together to harass or drive off predators, a behavior called mobbing.

Recent findings suggest that under the third hypothesis, the roost works as an information center. A hungry crow could watch for well-fed arrivals in the evening.

It could then trace their flight paths back, or follow them at dawn, to a rich food source.

Recent findings suggest that the fourth is called patch-sitting. Roosts may form close to food that is reliable and impossible to defend.

Landfills, commercial composting facilities and certain farm fields all qualify. Sleeping nearby gives the crows breakfast before they disperse.

A 1997 study in The Auk, led by Donald F. Caccamise, followed color-marked and radio-tagged crows.

By day, most stayed faithful to their group territory all year. They frequently left it to forage up to 2.5 miles (4 kilometers) away.

Recent findings suggest that at night they roosted either at home or at a large roost about 11 miles (18 kilometers) away, next to a landfill. They slept on their territories 87 percent of the time in spring and 42 percent in winter.

Each crow reached the communal roost on its own, not with its group.

Group cohesion was high on territories. The team tied keeping a territory to holding a breeding site.

The team linked communal roosting to likely foraging benefits. Those count most in winter, when food on a territory is scarcest.

In a recent development, crows have gathered in large fall and winter roosts “for as long as there have been crows,” according to McGowan. By 2010, the roost at Auburn, New York, was downtown.

In a recent development, A large roost was recorded near that town in 1911-12. In the winter of 1932-33, about 25,000 crows slept 3 miles (4.8 kilometers) south of it.

Hunting is one possible reason. The Migratory Bird Treaty Act was extended to crows in 1972, and firing a gun inside city limits is prohibited.

Cities are also often 5 to 10 degrees Fahrenheit (about 3 to 6 degrees Celsius) warmer than the surrounding countryside. Great horned owls should be scarcer in urban areas.

According to recent reports, streetlights and parking lot lights may help crows spot a threat such as an owl and find a fresh perch at night. Large old trees in parks and cemeteries may be especially attractive for roosting.
