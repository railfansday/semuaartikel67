Ofqual will propose changes to non-exam assessment in selected A Level subjects and require stronger action against misuse of wearable technology, Chief Regulator Sir Ian Bauckham stated in a letter to leaders at some awarding organisations published on November 18, 2026. The intervention applies to England.

Industry observers note that the forthcoming proposals will cover A Level history and the three A Level English qualifications. Ofqual will also consider changes in other subjects and qualifications where risks to authenticity require action. Awarding organisations have been invited to share their views on the regulator’s direction before any formal consultation.

Bauckham’s concern focuses on work produced without supervision, particularly extended writing. In the full letter, he stated it is increasingly difficult to establish reliably whether such work is entirely the student’s own. He explicitly acknowledged that not all non-exam assessment is inherently vulnerable to AI, while identifying particular risks in some forms of assessment.

“Where we can no longer have sufficient confidence in the authenticity of assessed work, we must consider whether the assessment itself needs to change,” he wrote. That position connects malpractice controls to assessment design: when the conditions surrounding an assessment no longer provide reliable evidence of authorship, detection measures alone may not resolve the underlying problem.

Industry observers note that alongside the assessment proposals, the letter addresses exam security as wearable devices become more sophisticated and AI-enabled. Awarding organisations must take all reasonable steps to protect assessment integrity and ensure that required assessment conditions are implemented effectively and consistently.

Relevant centre staff, including invigilators where appropriate, are expected to receive effective training using clear, consistent and practical resources to identify and respond to wearable-technology misuse. The training should be in place for the summer 2027 exam series. The response cannot rest with invigilators alone, and organisations were asked to consider additional preventive and detection measures.

Sources indicate that responses are requested before November 30, 2026. Bauckham asked organisations to describe their risk assessments, preventive action, support for centres and any further measures they consider necessary. Those planning collective action were also asked to explain the arrangements they will use to coordinate effectively with other awarding organisations.

Industry observers note that the new intervention follows Bauckham’s March 3, 2026 letter, which requested stronger measures against mobile phones and other devices in exam halls and AI misuse in non-exam assessment. The earlier letter reported that 2025 penalties included 1,125 cases in which students lost an entire GCSE or A Level and nearly 2,000 cases in which marks were deducted.

Almost half of approximately 5,000 malpractice cases reported annually over the preceding three years involved students taking mobile phones or other communication devices into exams. Detected AI misuse cases reported to Ofqual remained relatively low at that time, although the letter recorded significant concern among teachers, leaders and others about the real extent of misuse.

In a recent development, ofqual previously asked exam boards to improve awareness of what constitutes misuse and to strengthen deterrence, detection and prevention, including more rigorous candidate and teacher authentication of work. The November letter adds named A Level subjects for possible assessment changes and a specific training timetable for wearable risks.

Ofqual’s direction therefore extends beyond policing individual misconduct. The open question for the forthcoming proposals is which forms of extended writing can remain reliable measures of a student’s own achievement when work is produced without supervision.
