Key Hegseth adviser faces questions over ties to Israeli defense contractor and Saudi Arabia

A government watchdog has also labeled Hegseth’s defense policy appointee a ‘walking conflict of interest’.

One of defense secretary Pete Hegseth’s key advisers faces questions about potential conflicts of interest over his many roles, including advising the US defense department, Saudi Arabia, and an Israeli defence contractor.

Recent findings suggest that former Republican senator Norm Coleman emerged as an informal yet omnipresent guide for Hegseth in December 2024 as the former Fox News host navigated confirmation hearings for his nomination as defense secretary. Hegseth now finds himself under fire after articles of impeachment were filed by Republican congressman Thomas Massie charging him with “high crimes and misdemeanors,” including waging war against Iran in violation of the War Powers Act, but Hegseth’s imprint on the Pentagon goes beyond the disastrous war against Iran.

At the start of his marathon 4 hours and 24 minutes hearing before the Senate armed services committee – a hearing that delved into allegations of sexual misconduct, workplace drinking and marital infidelity – Hegseth took time to “thank the former senator from Minnesota, Norm Coleman, for his mentorship and friendship in this process”.

Coleman’s service to Hegseth appears to have been rewarded in June, when the secretary of war appointed Coleman to the Defense Policy Board, uniquely situating Coleman to advise on defense procurement decisions of interest to the former senator’s clients.

“Serving as a sherpa to rich people seeking to climb Mount Everest is dangerous and thankless work that is often underappreciated by Westerners,” remarked Jeff Hauser, founder and executive director of the Revolving Door Project, a group that scrutinizes executive branch appointees to ensure they use their office to serve the broad public interest. “But serving successfully as a sherpa to a controversial nominee tends to breed enormous goodwill.”.

In a recent development, coleman is a man of many, often highly remunerative, hats. He is the chairman of the Republican Jewish Coalition (RJC), a group comprised of a who’s-who of pro-Israel Republican megadonors. He chairs the Congressional Leadership Fund, one of the biggest super Pacs supporting House Republicans. And he serves as a lobbyist for Saudi Arabia and now as vice chairman of the Defense Policy Board, a Pentagon advisory committee focusing on “matters pertaining to strategic planning, the policy implications of U.S. force structure and modernization, regional defense policies, and other defense policy and national security problems of special interest to the Department”.

Last year, Coleman added another job to his resume: senior adviser to Kinetica, an Israeli weapons organization ideally positioned to profit from deeper integration between the US and Israeli weapons development industries, posing a potential conflict of interest for Coleman as he advises the Pentagon and Republican candidates on matters related to the defense budget and weapons procurement.

“Norm Coleman is a walking conflict of interest on the Pentagon Defense Policy Board,” stated Craig Holman, a government affairs lobbyist at Public Citizen, a consumer advocacy organization that opposes corporate influence in government. “Most other advisory boards will not allow the appointment of registered lobbyists.”.

It is not illegal to simultaneously work as a lobbyist and advise the Pentagon.

Questions about the national security implications of US-Israeli defense integration came to a head earlier this year with the introduction of Section 219 of the House National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA), legislation that would more deeply intertwine the US and Israeli militaries and commit the US to bilateral research and development and co-production of weapons, joint ventures and licensing agreements.

Mark Dubowitz, CEO of the Foundation for Defense of Democracies (FDD), a group whose mission includes “provid[ing] education to enhance Israel’s image in North America”, explained that intertwining the US and Israeli militaries and weapons development systems is a necessary step in the face of polling showing rapidly diminishing support for Israel among Americans of all political stripes. “Those figures should concern Israel and its friends because, ultimately, public opinion shapes policy,” said Dubowitz, writing in Israel Hayom, an outlet owned by pro-Israel megadonor Miriam Adelson, last month. Dubowitz went on to advise: “Most importantly, Israel and the United States should deepen their military, technological, intelligence and economic integration to such an extent that inflicting financial harm on Israel would also carry a significant cost for Washington.”.

FDD, which endorsed congressional efforts to mandate US-Israel defense integration, has a unique tie to Coleman’s Israeli client: its board member Isaac Applbaum is chairman of Kinetica. And Kinetica claims it is uniquely situated to benefit from such an intertwining of the US defense sector with Israel.

Sources indicate that kinetica reported in February that Coleman, speaking in his capacity as an adviser to the company at a conference in Tel Aviv, “shared his perspective from his deep proximity to the U.S. defense establishment.”.

Recent findings suggest that “He highlighted that the U.S. Department of Defense is actively seeking latest technologies to integrate into its systems – a shift from a landscape once dominated exclusively by large defense primes to one that is increasingly open to emerging innovation,” wrote Kinetica on LinkedIn. “For Israel, as a strategic U.S. ally, this represents a significant opportunity to scale into the American defense ecosystem.”.

Industry observers note that speaking to the Defense Brief in June, Kinetica founding partner Aaron Applbaum (Isaac’s son), reiterated this sentiment, explaining the business strategy behind the Israeli startup in a broader ecosystem of growing military spending in the US, Nato, Japan and the Middle East. “It’s a generational opportunity to start a new platform,” said Applbaum.

In a LinkedIn post later that month, the company cheered its bet on Coleman as a validation of its business strategy, celebrating his appointment as VP of the Defense Policy Board, writing:.

Sources indicate that “Congratulations to Kinetica’s Senior Advisor, Senator Norm Coleman, on his appointment as Vice Chair of the Defense Policy Board, which advises the U.S. Secretary of Defense and senior Pentagon officials on strategic and national security concerns.

“As an advisor to Kinetica, Senator Coleman brings a rare combination of policy, leadership, foreign affairs experience and a deep understanding of the U.S.-Israel strategic relationship.

His appointment reflects an important part of the Kinetica thesis – defense policy, technological superiority and allied innovation are no longer separate conversations. They are part of the same national security mission.”.

Coleman’s exact role with Kinetica is unclear, but his work for other weapons manufacturers and contractors reveals Pentagon lobbying efforts in the months leading up to his appointment to the Defense Policy Board, raising questions about potential conflicts of interest.

“Human beings cannot actually firewall parts of their brain from each other, which means that it is impossible for one’s work for the United States to fail to influence one’s work for Saudi Arabia or defense companies - and vice versa,” noted Hauser. “The difference is that Saudi Arabia and defense contractors want their paid agents to have multiple loyalties so that they can be more effective in return for cash, whereas the US government ought to reasonably expect for the undivided loyalty of those who serve it.”.

In his capacity as senior counsel at legal giant Hogan Lovells Cadwalader, Coleman served as a lobbyist on the firm’s account for Hidden Level Inc, a manufacturer producing drone detection systems. The firm’s work included outreach to the departments of defense and homeland security and the White House as part of efforts “regarding drone airspace management and security capabilities”, according to lobbying disclosure filings.

Coleman also helped in “raising the profile” of the SkyWater Technology foundry “as a trusted domestic semiconductor manufacturer”, according to the lobbying firm’s description of its work lobbying various Washington agencies, including the Pentagon, on behalf of Coretsu, an AI and technology firm seeking to promote “technology applications for legal and regulatory analysis”.

Sources indicate that after his appointment to the Defense Policy Board, Coleman continued to be listed on the firm’s lobbying disclosures, revealing he was a lobbyist on at least one account that lobbied the Department of Defense: Bear SVTP Development LLC. The company retained Coleman and two other lobbyists to assist with problems related to “[l]and development with respect to AI Data Centers and federal regulation”, according to lobbying disclosures.

“It’s a very suspect situation that Norm Coleman is manipulating: he’s advising the Pentagon on what policies to do while he’s also advising contractors on what to do,” said Holman. “But it doesn’t seem to be illegal, just self-serving and unethical.”.

“The Federal Advisory Committee Act just bans individual lobbyists, not those who serve as lobbyists for another firm like Hogan Lovell,” said Holman, referring to legislation dictating the operations of advisory committees like the Defense Policy Board.

Members of the Defense Policy Board seemingly holding significant conflicts of interest is not a newly introduced or unusual occurrence. A Center for Public Integrity investigation in 2003 found that nine out of thirty members of the Board had ties to contractors with more than $76bn in Pentagon contracts and four were registered lobbyists, including its chairman, Richard Perle, who had just resigned his chairmanship. Perle drew fire for his business relationship with Global Crossing Ltd and satellite maker Loral Space & Communications Ltd, but a heavily redacted report by the Pentagon inspector general concluded that he did not violate any ethics laws.

In a recent development, lobbyists face greater scrutiny after the Republicans’ midterms debacle. Stephen Miller, Trump’s new chief of staff, issued a memo ordering White House staffers to get approval before meeting lobbyists.

Coleman, Hogan Lovells Cadwalader and Kinetica did not respond to requests for comment.
