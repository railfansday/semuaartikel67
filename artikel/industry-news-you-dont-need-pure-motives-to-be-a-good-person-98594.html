In one of my business ethics classes, a student questioned how to think about his own volunteering. He mentioned that he enjoyed the work and cared about the people he worked with, but he also knew that it would look good on his resume. He wondered whether having mixed motives took something away from volunteering. When I looked around the room, I saw most, if not all, heads nodding, sharing his doubts.

Recent findings suggest that we sometimes assume that for an action to be truly moral, it must be altruistic. Neera Kapur Badhwar has shown the prominence of this idea in modern moral philosophy: that only acts motivated to help others for their own sake count as moral. This idea assumes that being self-interested means focusing on ourselves to the exclusion of others. That is why my students worry that wanting to volunteer but also seeing its benefit tarnishes the goodness of volunteering.

In a recent development, this form of thinking doesn’t distinguish between benefiting from an action, being motivated by that benefit, and acting exclusively for that benefit. It misunderstands human motivation and what it means to be selfish.

If you’ve ever watched television shows like Succession, you can appreciate how easy it is to become cynical about people’s motivations. Every action is strategic, every act of generosity comes with conditions, and every relationship is ultimately a calculation for the sake of personal benefit. Yet, while people certainly can rationalize selfish behavior or disguise selfish motives with moral justifications, for most of us, personal benefit isn’t the only thing motivating us. In fact, Grant and Berry have shown that prosocial and self-oriented motives need not be at odds. When people care about helping others, they are more likely to take other people’s perspectives and channel their intrinsic motivation into ideas that are both novel and useful.

According to recent reports, so, while a desire to boost one’s resume may motivate people to do something, it doesn’t mean that the choice of what to do is insincere. My student volunteered both because he wanted to help others and because doing so might benefit him. Moreover, the relative importance of each motive may change depending on the day, the circumstances of the situation, or the task you are performing.

It has been revealed that recognizing that we have multiple motives for doing something is not an admission of moral weakness. It is an appreciation for the complexity of human motivation.

Recent findings suggest that self-interest means being concerned about your own well-being and pursuing things that you care about. Selfishness means giving your own interests disproportionate priority when the legitimate interests of others should also matter. The difference between self-interest and selfishness is in how we consider other people’s legitimate interests when deciding what to do.

Industry observers note that for example, wanting to advance your career is self-interested, yet it only becomes selfish when you take advantage of or disregard others to do so. When my student decided to volunteer, he was self-interested, yet, at the same time, he considered the needs of others in choosing to do so. He did not use others merely as a means for his own career advancement.

Sources indicate that in his book The Reasons of Love, philosopher Harry Frankfurt gives an example of when self-interest and selflessness align rather than conflict. He writes that when you genuinely love someone, you do not care about their well-being simply because it makes you feel good. You care about their well-being for their sake. Yet, because you love them, their well-being has also become something in which you are personally invested, and caring about them does in fact make you feel good. Arthur Aron and colleagues have shown that when people become close, they incorporate aspects of the other person into their own self-concept. When this happens, caring for oneself and caring for another person need not be opposing motivations.

As a parent, I derive tremendous joy from seeing my children happy. Yet I do not want my children to be happy simply so that I can experience that joy. I want them to be happy because I love them. The satisfaction I derive from caring for them is not what motivates me, but it is a benefit that I enjoy.

Recent findings suggest that when we love someone, their well-being becomes something we care about for its own sake, even when pursuing it requires us to set aside something else we want. When we choose a loved one’s well-being over other wants we have, we do not need to see this as acting self-sacrificially. We can recognize that we are pursuing something that we want over other things.

As our commitments to others and our communities grow, acting for the sake of others may not simply be a means for self-interested reciprocity, but rather can be grounded in a desire for relationship. Rather than making our actions less ethical, it deepens the moral community in which we live.

Rather, it means developing the capacity to care about others and to consider their interests alongside your own. When we do this, we can recognize that it can—and should—feel good to do good things.
