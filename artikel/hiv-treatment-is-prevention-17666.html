HIV Treatment Is Prevention

Posted November 16, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk.

Industry observers note that HIV treatment, antiretroviral therapy (ART), has seen dramatic advancement in the past four decades. HIV is now a chronic condition. When people take their ART as prescribed and maintain an undetectable viral load and a high T-cell count, life expectancy for those living with HIV approximates that of those who are uninfected (Trickey et al., 2023).

It has been revealed that in addition to the direct health benefits of maintaining an undetectable viral load, there is an additional prevention benefit. When someone living with HIV has an undetectable viral load, they are unable to transmit HIV through sex (Eisinger et al., 2019). Public health campaigns promoting ART adherence have called attention to this phenomenon, terming it Undetectable = Untransmissible or U=U.

Sources indicate that we might assume that prevention messaging that draws on U=U would be most effective among people living with HIV who are partnered with someone who is uninfected. Having an undetectable viral load would decrease or eliminate the potential for sexual transmission of HIV to their partner. Alternatively, people living with HIV may be generally motivated to take their ART and maintain an undetectable viral load when they are having sex with partners who may be uninfected regardless of relationship status.

In two recent studies (Hillesheim & Starks, 2026; Starks et al., 2024), our team examined relationship status group differences in ART adherence and viral detectability in large samples of sexual minority male adults.

According to recent reports, ordinarily, we expect people in relationships to have better health outcomes compared to single people (Kiecolt-Glaser & Wilson, 2017). In both of these papers, findings suggested this was true for sexual minority men living with HIV in non-monogamous relationships and those whose partners were uninfected. These groups were more likely to be adherent to ART and report an undetectable viral load compared to single sexual minority men. Presumably, these are groups where men may be most likely to consider preventing transmission of HIV to sexual partners.

Sources indicate that there was also a warning sign in both papers. Sexual minority men in relationships where both partners were living with HIV were significantly less likely to be adherent to their ART and achieve an undetectable viral load. For these men, prevention motivation may be of limited relevance. It may be notable to emphasize the personal health benefits of HIV treatment to maximize HIV outcomes in these subgroups.

Effective HIV treatment not only prolongs the lives of people living with HIV, but it also eliminates the potential for sexual transmission. This prevention motivation may explain why rates of ART adherence and achieving an undetectable viral load are higher among sexual minority men in non-monogamous relationships and those with uninfected partners. We may need different messages, emphasizing the personal health benefits of HIV care, to reach subgroups who have low levels of sexual HIV prevention motivation.
