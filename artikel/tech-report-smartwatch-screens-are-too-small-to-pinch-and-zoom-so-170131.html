Tech Report: Smartwatch screens are too small to pinch and zoom, so engineers turned the forearm into a touchpad

A radar wristband turns the forearm into a smartwatch touchpad that reads two-finger gestures and press force, beating a touchscreen in tests.

On a phone, pinching to zoom or scrolling with two fingers is second nature. On a watch face the size of a postage stamp, there is no room for it.

Engineers have now moved those gestures off the screen and onto the skin of the forearm, turning it into a touchpad for the smartwatch.

Recent findings suggest that A small radar on a wristband points at the skin next to the watch. Fingers pressing there leave tiny dents, and the radar reads where the fingers are, how many there are, and whether a press is gentle or firm.

In tests with 20 volunteers, the system recognized gestures about 93 percent of the time after a calibration of under four minutes. In a head-to-head task, people missed their targets less often on their own arm than on a watch touchscreen.

The work comes from Chenhan Xu, an assistant professor of computer science at North Carolina State University, and doctoral student Yuliang Fu, with Rakshita Ranganath and Huining Li. They call the system EpiPad.

“Smartwatch screens are too small for the touch interactions people use every day on phones and laptops, such as two-finger scrolling, pinch-to-zoom, or pressing harder to trigger a different command,” Xu said.

“Our goal was to develop a small, wearable technology that would provide that level of interactive control for people.”.

On a watch touchscreen, a fingertip covers a small target it is trying to tap. The authors wrote that a typical on-screen keyboard on a commercial smartwatch gives each key about 0.12 inches (3 millimeters) of width.

Earlier attempts to use the forearm skin as a touchpad mostly tracked a single finger, or relied on landmarks such as knuckles, which limited taps to a few fixed spots.

Rotating, zooming, and other two-finger moves were out of reach.

The back of the forearm offered room to work with. The system turns a strip of forearm skin about 6 inches by 1.6 inches (15 by 4 centimeters) into a touchpad input area. The padding of muscle under that skin makes a hard press comfortable.

The sensor is a millimeter-wave radar chip with one transmitting antenna and three receiving ones. It sends out a pulse every thousandth of a second and times the reflections.

Because the radar faces across the skin from the side, a press straight down barely changes the distance it measures.

So the team mounted the chip about 0.55 inches (14 millimeters) above the skin, roughly the thickness of a chunky smartwatch case, and angled it slightly downward.

“With the EpiPad, a compact millimeter-wave radar worn on a wristband reads the tiny dents fingertips make as they press the skin, so the system tracks multiple fingers and how hard each one presses,” stated Fu, the paper’s first author.

The system distinguishes two levels of pressure, gentle and hard. The hard press is where the signal gets tricky, because a finger arriving on the skin shifts the radar reading in the same way the dent does.

The team found the pressure signature in the moment the finger holds still after landing.

The 20 volunteers, 14 men and six women, were all young adults. Each spent about an hour and a half tapping, pressing, sliding, rotating, and zooming on a numbered grid stuck to the left forearm.

According to recent reports, people press differently and have different skin. A model that was trained on everyone except one volunteer, was then tested on that volunteer. It recognized gestures only about half the time.

According to recent reports, each new user therefore performs a short set of standard taps and gestures first, a calibration that takes just under four minutes. Afterward, the system identified the gesture 92.8 percent of the time and told a gentle press from a hard one 90.2 percent of the time.

Sources indicate that the system also placed a fingertip to within about 0.4 inches (10 millimeters). In other words, the calibration brought a fresh user almost up to the accuracy the system reached on people it had trained on.

Recent findings suggest that ten of the volunteers came back twice more over three months and put the wristband on again. The original calibration still held, at about 91 percent for gestures.

Twelve participants then raced a cursor between targets on a smartwatch display, using their forearm skin as a touchpad. The standard pointing test required them to select each target with a hard two-finger press. The smallest targets were 0.05 inches (1.28 millimeters) across, narrower than a key on a watch keyboard.

The same people did the task on a touchscreen and with a tilt cursor steered by angling the wrist. On the touchscreen they missed 21.7 percent of targets.

With EpiPad they missed 6.5 percent, and they moved between targets faster than with either other method.

“EpiPad outperformed the other two tasks with regard to both error rate and efficiency,” Fu noted.

According to recent reports, the system responded in about 90 milliseconds, within the range of commercial devices. Reading pressure took longer, about half a second. The authors found that most hard presses lasted at least that long anyway.

According to recent reports, in a test on two people, a tight sleeve barely mattered. A loose jacket dropped gesture recognition to 72 percent and pressure detection to 63 percent, because the fabric moves on its own and reflects the radar.

According to recent reports, models calibrated for the index and middle fingers also stumbled when people switched to thumb and index, until they recalibrated for the new pair. Five volunteers who walked slowly while using it kept accuracy near 90 percent.

The prototype is a wristband with a circuit board sticking out, and it still needs a laptop to process the signal.

The authors wrote that custom hardware with more transmitters could let the radar sit lower, to fit thinner watches. Longer studies in daily life, they wrote, would be needed to judge how people take to it.

Xu stated the parts cost less than $10 and could go into smart glasses and other screenless wearables, using “the wearer’s own skin as a full touch surface.”.

Industry observers note that the paper was presented at the UbiComp/ISWC conference in Shanghai in October. The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the ACM on Interactive, Mobile, Wearable and Ubiquitous Technologies.
