Do fitness trackers help people exercise more? The answer is complicated

Fitness tracker users were more likely to meet exercise guidelines, but a newly introduced study cannot show whether the devices made them more active.

Step trackers, health apps, and patient portals offer different ways to monitor and manage health.

An analysis of a national U.S. survey found that these tools were not equally associated with getting enough exercise.

In the analysis reported by Heejun Kim and colleagues, adults were sorted into groups based on the tools they used.

Among adults in the wearable-centered group, 31.4% met both federal exercise guidelines.

According to recent reports, among adults who used few tools, 18.2% met both guidelines, compared with 19.9% of those centered on a patient portal. No clear difference could be found between these last two groups.

A patient portal is a secure website where patients can view test results and exchange messages with their health care providers.

The results suggest that the mix of tools may reveal more about exercise habits than any single device.

Kim, who led the study, is a doctoral student in sport analytics at the University of Mississippi. Kim worked with Ju-Pil Choe, also a doctoral student, and Professor Minsoo Kang of the Department of Kinesiology and Sport.

Because participants answered the survey only once, the findings show associations and cannot establish whether these tools make people more active.

It has been revealed that choe described two people as an example. The first person has a smartwatch and checks the step count on it each day.

The second person opens a patient portal for lab results or a message to a doctor. That person has never owned a fitness tracker.

“Both are using digital health tools, but in very different ways,” Choe remarked.

Data came from the 2024 Health Information National Trends Survey, a nationally representative survey of U.S. adults run by the National Cancer Institute.

Wearable users had been compared with non-users in studies of the 2019 and 2022 rounds.

Among the users, the odds of meeting activity recommendations were found to be higher in both. Those studies could not show which other digital tools the same people used.

Kim’s team instead took 17 questions from the survey, each of which could be answered yes or no. All of them referred to the 12 months before the survey.

The questions covered wearables, health apps, patient portals, telehealth visits, online health searches and health-related social media posts.

According to recent reports, there were 5,804 adults who had answered every one of these questions. Based on their response patterns, the team sorted them into four groups.

The largest of the four groups, which made up 34.3% of the adults, was the one centered on patient portals. Its members messaged providers and viewed test results online, but very few of them used a wearable.

The broad group, representing 32% of adults, used almost every tool covered by the survey.

A third group of 11.8% relied on wearables and apps, whereas portals were used much less.

The remaining 21.9% of adults were placed in a limited group, in which few digital health tools were used. Most of its members did, however, use social media and look up health information online.

Recent findings suggest that two targets are set for adults by the federal Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans. The first is at least 150 minutes a week of moderate aerobic activity.

The second is muscle-strengthening activity, which is to be done on two or more days of the week.

Of the 4,930 adults with complete answers, 25.1% met both exercise guidelines. However, the rates varied between groups.

In the broad group, 32.7% met both exercise guidelines, compared with 31.4% in the wearable group. The rates were lower in the portal group (19.9%) and limited group (18.2%).

These differences remained after researchers accounted for age, sex, income, education, race, ethnicity, body mass index, smoking and other factors.

Compared with the limited group, the broad group had 2.8 times the odds of meeting both guidelines, while the wearable group had 2.1 times the odds.

The difference between the portal and limited groups was too small to rule out chance.

Recent findings suggest that for strength training, the picture was not the same as for aerobic activity. Higher odds of meeting the strength guideline were seen in all three other groups, including the portal-centered group.

Those odds were about 2.6 times (broad), 1.6 times (wearable-centered) and 1.6 times (portal-centered) the odds of the limited group.

The share of adults who met this guideline was 32.5% in the limited group and 49.5% in the broad group.

For the aerobic guideline, it was only the broad group that clearly differed from the limited group.

The lowest aerobic rate of the four groups was reported by the portal-centered adults, at 34.5%. In the limited group the rate was 40.2%, but this gap too was small enough to be due to chance.

A possible reason for the difference was offered by the authors. Strength training may be a more deliberate and planned kind of exercise than aerobic activity is.

As such, it could fit the broader habits of health management which portal use reflects.

According to recent reports, the authors wrote that aerobic minutes can be accumulated through both structured workouts and everyday movement. For this reason, aerobic activity may be tied less closely to any one set of digital habits.

It has been revealed that kang said that a person who has more digital health tools is not necessarily a more active person. What counts for more, in Kang’s view, is the way in which people use the tools from day to day.

Recent findings suggest that in the limited group, 12.9% of adults had not finished high school. A household income of less than $20,000 a year was reported by 25.7% of the group.

Among the other three groups, the share that had not finished high school stayed between 1.1% and 4.2%. The share living in households below that income stayed between 8.6% and 11.7%.

According to recent reports, the limited group was also set apart by age. Of its members, 10.2% were people aged 75 or older, and in no other group was this share so large.

Industry observers note that the broad group had the most college graduates, at 47.3%. The broad group also had the most adults in households which earned $100,000 or more, at 49.8%.

Recent findings suggest that more digital tools were not simply a mark of better health, however. Of the four groups, the broad one had the highest average body mass index (29.5).

It has been revealed that choe stated that people may be missed when exercise programs are built mainly around apps and wearables. These, Choe said, are people who would benefit from extra support.

The survey cannot prove that trackers increase exercise. Active people may be more likely to buy them. Digital skills or health care access may also play a role.

An earlier review found that trackers can help when used in behavior-change programs, which this study did not test.

In a recent development, people reported their own exercise and may have overstated it. The survey also missed vigorous activity, possibly counting some active adults as falling short.

Of 7,278 respondents, 2,348 (32.3%) were excluded due to missing answers.

Industry observers note that among those excluded from the digital-use grouping analysis, 52.7% were 65 or older, compared with 17.7% of those kept. Those excluded also had less education and lower incomes.

Industry observers note that A second analysis included people with missing answers. The same four groups appeared, with 97.7% staying in their original group.

In this analysis, the portal group had 1.5 times the odds of meeting both guidelines compared with the limited group. The wearable group also had higher odds of meeting the aerobic guideline.

The authors suggested different ways to help each group exercise more.

Tracker users could get feedback and goals, while portal users could receive reminders through messages or appointments.

People who use few digital tools might need more in-person or community support.

It has been revealed that the study did not test these ideas. More research is needed to see whether this support helps people exercise more.

The full study was published in the journal Frontiers in Physiology.
