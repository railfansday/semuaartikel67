Industry observers note that the banking business certainly seems complicated on the surface. The major companies in the industry operate multiple profit centers while simultaneously managing the challenges that come with sheer size. Maintaining regulatory compliance is another aspect of the complex machine.

Recent findings suggest that and yet, at its core, the banking business is philosophically simple -- banks aim to earn more in interest payments than they must pay in interest themselves. Everything else is ultimately a means to this end, or add-on revenue.

It has been revealed that here's a closer look at this core business metric you may know as net interest income, although we're even more interested in a derivative figure called net interest margin. Only a couple of significant banks saw improvement in this measure during the third quarter.

In a recent development, contrary to a common misconception, banks don't keep huge stacks of cash in their vaults to lend out when borrowers apply for loans. Most banks keep a relatively small amount of cash in their vaults, in fact. There's not even enough actual cash on hand at your local bank branch to cover the simultaneous closure of every checking or savings account serviced by that branch.

Industry observers note that rather, it's more cost-effective and logistically easier for your bank to borrow money from other customers' deposits, checking accounts, and certificates of deposit as it's needed to make loans, even knowing doing so will drain cash from your bank money. The bank will charge its borrowers a higher interest rate than it pays depositors, and then pocket the difference. This difference results in what's called net interest income.

According to recent reports, yes, net interest income can and does ebb and flow with the economy and changes in interest rates. A strong economy creates strong demand for borrowing, providing banks with more opportunities to make interest-generating loans. And although banks can earn net interest income at any interest rate, lending tends to be more profitable when rates are higher.

Sources indicate that this measure isn't quite the end-all, be-all indication of how well a bank is managing its assets and loan portfolio, however. While obviously more net interest income is better than less, it's the net interest margin figure that indicates how well a bank is using the assets it's got to work with at any given time.

Sources indicate that simply put, net interest margin is the amount of net interest income earned relative to the amount of interest paid. Although almost always in the low single digits, a bigger number is still better than a smaller one.

It has been revealed that it's absolutely possible for a smaller bank with fewer assets to report a higher net interest margin rate than a bigger bank. The key is to maintain a high-quality loan portfolio while ensuring you retain customers' deposits.

Neither the domestic nor the global economy is in a recession at this time. In fact, although persistent inflation is making most consumers miserable, the economy is technically strong, with the U.S. unemployment rate at a historically low 4.1% and third-quarter gross domestic product (GDP) growth exceeding 3%.

Recent findings suggest that nevertheless, there's no denying the lending business is experiencing turbulence. The Federal Reserve recently reported that as of the end of the third quarter, 5% of loans to U.S. borrowers are in some form of delinquency, led by auto loans and mortgages. Many banks are tightening their lending standards as a result, hoping to steer clear of any future loan write-downs or charge-offs. Higher interest rates are also discouraging some would-be borrowers, of course.

A couple of familiar banks, however, appear to be pushing through the headwind.

Bank of America (BAC -3.15%) is one of them. Its net interest income grew from a little less than $15.4 billion in the third quarter of 2025 to a little more than $16.6 billion in Q3. Moreover, its net interest margin -- what it calls net interest yield -- improved from 2.01% a year earlier to 2.16% in the third quarter. It's also worth noting that while its loan and lease portfolio grew more than 7% year over year, its provision (money set aside) for losses on loans was actually slightly reduced compared to Q3 2025. BofA is clearly managing its business well in this tricky environment.

JPMorgan Chase (JPM -3.03%) too is doing well, even if not quite as convincingly as Bank of America. Last quarter's net interest margin rate (excluding capital-markets-related assets) came in at 2.48%, up from a year-earlier 2.45% and from Q2's 2.40%. And like BoA, its interest-earning asset base grew from just under $2.5 trillion to almost $2.65 trillion.

Not every big bank is doing as well. Wells Fargo (WFC -2.47%) saw its net interest margin slip from 2.61% in the third quarter of last year to 2.43% in the three months ended in September, despite net interest income growth of nearly 5% to a little more than $12.5 billion. Charge-offs and loan-loss provisions were also up from year-ago levels for both its consumer and commercial lending businesses.

Citigroup (C -4.04%) also bumped into a bit of a headwind in Q3. Although its net interest margin rolled in at 2.43% versus the year-ago figure of 2.4%, that was also down from Q2's 2.54% despite a 12% increase in its interest-earning asset base. Like Wells Fargo, Citi's provision for credit losses is inching higher, although the biggest crimp may be that its average effective yield on assets slipped from 5.89% in the third quarter of last year to 5.54% this time around.

Recent findings suggest that there's always more to the story, of course. Widening net interest margins don't necessarily mean that bank's stock is a better buy. Narrowing margin rates don't inherently mean a bank is in trouble either. And one quarter's worth of data rarely means very much.

It's one of several important fiscal metrics to monitor all the same, since it indicates how well a bank minimizes its interest costs while maximizing its interest revenue. Indirectly, it points to the quality of loans a bank is making, and how cost-effectively it's retaining customers' deposits. These seemingly little things can actually end up being a pretty big deal.
