3-step method to influence people and win them over, from a Stanford expert: ‘Your voice is like a muscle’

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Think back on the most memorable, inspiring speeches or presentations you've witnessed. They probably weren't delivered in a monotone, Matt Abrahams says.

"The human brain is designed to pick out and focus on things that are novel and new," says Abrahams, a Stanford University lecturer on strategic communication and host of the "Think Fast, Talk Smart" podcast. "So anything that stays the same, like a monotonous voice or cadence, we don't pay as much attention to.".

Indeed, successful leaders often have an uncanny ability to win over a crowd: The power of persuasion lies not just in what you actually have to say, but also in how you say it, research demonstrates. The topic has taken on new relevance this week as Nvidia interim CEO Colette Kress steps into one of the most closely watched leadership roles in corporate America.

In appearances this week, including Nvidia's quarterly earnings call and an interview with CNBC's "Mad Money" host Jim Cramer, Kress has discussed the tech giant's performance. She's also addressed employees and investors in a time of leadership upheaval, following Nvidia co-founder Jensen Huang's high-profile resignation on Nov. 12.

In a recent development, how Kress communicates has drawn attention and, in some circles, criticism. On Wednesday, The Wall Street Journal described her public speaking style as "slow, steady, almost monotone." That doesn't necessarily make it ineffective. Her situation, however, highlights a broader challenge leaders face when they need to inform, reassure and persuade large groups of people at once.

Recent findings suggest that generally speaking, anyone can stand a better chance at persuading others to follow them by honing an engaging speaking style, says Abrahams. Some people may be naturally expressive and engaging. For others, it's a skill that requires time and practice.

Abrahams suggests a three-step playbook for anyone learning to speak more persuasively: "repetition, reflection and feedback.".

Abrahams' simplest advice for making yourself a more engaging speaker: "When you're using emotive words, reflect the emotion in them." That means adding some emphasis to words like "exciting," "huge" or "challenging," and so on. If you tend to speak monotonously, leaning into obviously emotive words can help you vary your tone and inflection enough to keep your audience's attention.

In a recent development, there's a catch, of course: People find inauthenticity off-putting and disingenuous, notes Abrahams. Don't alter your voice entirely, he suggests — just add some variety to your intonation and speaking volume. Learning to do so while still sounding natural takes practice, he notes.

According to recent reports, "Your voice is like a muscle," says Abrahams. "You can train it. You can grow it [and] strengthen it, just like you go to the gym to lift weights.".

Industry observers note that practicing repetition starts with reading aloud from a chosen text, which Abrahams recommends so you don't have to overthink what you're saying. And the same way you might lift heavy weights to get stronger, Abrahams suggests reading overly expressive material to start getting out of your comfort zone and practice being energetic and engaging. He instructs his Stanford students to try reading Dr. Seuss books, as their playful words and bouncy rhymes can naturally make for a lively performance.

Industry observers note that while practicing reading out loud, record yourself to watch and listen to later, Abrahams recommends. Then, use the recording to reflect on what works and what doesn't, and incorporate your self-feedback into your next reading. You can also play the recording for a few trusted peers to get their thoughts, Abrahams notes.

It has been revealed that if a line reading doesn't sound natural in your voice, feels flat or even rushed, keep trying it until you're satisfied, he notes. Leaders are often asked to deliver lengthy speeches or talk for long periods in interviews and panels, so it's key to build your vocal stamina, he adds.

"We have to calibrate ourselves," he says.
