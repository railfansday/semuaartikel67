Bitcoin miners found a way to profit without mining BTC, but the advantage may not last

Surging Texas electricity prices made shutting down more profitable than mining Bitcoin for some operators in early November.

The disruption contributed to Bitcoin's 9.13% mining difficulty reduction on Nov. 14, its largest since June, as expensive electricity forced miners to reconsider whether keeping their machines running was worthwhile.

In a recent development, in a Nov. 17 analysis, mining services firm Luxor found that West Texas electricity prices exceeded the mining revenue of a machine operating at 25 joules per terahash during 45% of the hours between Oct. 29 and Nov. 14. That compares with just 9% during the preceding difficulty period.

It has been revealed that the pressure intensified between Nov. 7 and Nov. 11, when average daily electricity prices ranged from $64 to $122 per megawatt-hour. Meanwhile, the modeled mining equipment generated approximately $54 in revenue for every megawatt-hour consumed, before electricity and other operating costs.

Luxor estimated that higher electricity prices alone accounted for roughly four-fifths of the increase in unprofitable mining hours, suggesting energy costs drove the disruption rather than Bitcoin's declining price.

The resulting shutdowns reduced the computing power securing Bitcoin's network, contributing to the difficulty adjustment that lowered the measure to 134.82 trillion from 148.36 trillion.

While miners exposed to wholesale electricity prices could avoid losses by shutting down, companies with favorable power contracts had another opportunity.

Bitcoin mining company Riot Platforms, for example, can temporarily suspend operations and return contracted electricity to the grid in exchange for credits when market prices exceed its fixed electricity rates.

According to its third-quarter SEC filing, Riot earned $24.7 million in power curtailment credits during the three months ended Sept. 30, including $22.9 million from voluntarily reducing electricity consumption.

Those earnings preceded November's electricity surge but demonstrate how the arrangement works.

Consider a miner generating $54 in Bitcoin revenue per megawatt-hour while paying a fixed electricity rate of $35.

In a recent development, continuing operations would produce $19 after electricity costs. However, if wholesale electricity prices reached $100 and the operator could return its contracted power, it could receive a $65 credit instead.

Under those assumptions, switching off would generate $46 more per megawatt-hour than continuing to mine.

The same power price can make a wholesale miner stop to avoid a loss and give a miner with resale rights a reason to collect a credit.

With a fixed electricity price and resale rights, giving up $19 in mining margin can earn a $65 credit in this example.

That difference helps explain why electricity markets can influence Bitcoin mining activity independently of cryptocurrency prices.

However, the relief from November's shutdowns may prove short-lived.

Luxor estimated that Bitcoin's computing power recovered to approximately 1,100 exahashes per second as West Texas electricity prices eased between Nov. 13 and Nov. 15, even as Bitcoin's price continued falling.

The recovery accelerated block production after the difficulty reduction, raising the prospect of another substantial adjustment.

Through Nov. 17, Bitcoin blocks had averaged approximately 9.11 minutes since the reset, faster than the network's targeted 10-minute interval.

Recent findings suggest that if that pace persists throughout the current difficulty period, the next adjustment could increase difficulty by approximately 9.85%, reversing nearly all the previous reduction. Even if block production slows to 10 minutes for the remaining period, the early acceleration would still produce an estimated 2.72% increase.

The extent of that rebound will depend partly on whether Texas electricity prices remain low enough to keep miners operating.
