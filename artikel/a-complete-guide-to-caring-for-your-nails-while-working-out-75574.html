The one area where a sweat session can fall short, however, is when it comes to the health of our nails.

If you're someone who loves a fresh manicure, especially one with some length, you know the struggle of keeping your tips intact at the gym. Between chipped polish, cracks and peeling, and the mental gymnastics of trying to avoid both, it can sometimes feel like your nails (and your sanity) are getting a workout of their own. “When I’m lifting plates or handling weights, I’m always conscious of how I’m gripping,” shares celebrity manicurist and KISS ambassador Gina Edwards, who's a frequent gym-goer herself. “I use my finger pads rather than the tips of my fingers to help avoid putting unnecessary pressure on my nails.”.

It has been revealed that not into manicures? Unfortunately, that doesn't mean you're completely off the hook. Even bare nails can take a beating: “The nail is a thousand times more absorptive of water than the skin,” states New York dermatologist Dana Stern, MD, who specializes in nail disorders. And because frequent handwashing and exposure to moisture often go hand in hand with working out, nails are subjected to expansion as they absorb water and contraction as they dry. “Repeated wet-dry cycles can contribute to splitting and peeling, particularly in people already prone to brittle nails,” Dr Stern adds.

But not to worry—you don’t have to choose between having polished digits and getting your fitness on. With a few smart habits, you can avoid all the pitfalls. Here’s how.

No matter how you choose to work out, there are plenty of ways your nails can suffer. And most of us don't even realize we're putting them in harm's way until it's too late. Maybe you're in a workout class, engaging in some friendly competition with your neighbor to see who can knock out their burpees the fastest, only to jam a nail as your hands hit the ground. Or perhaps the damage happens before you even break a sweat. Picture it: You're pulling on your most compressive pair of leggings when a nail snags on the fabric or, worse, bends backward and breaks.

Of course, some activities pose a more obvious risk than others. Exercises that involve “repetitive gripping, pressure, or friction, such as weightlifting, rock climbing, and certain types of strength training” can be especially hard on your nails, says Marisa Garshick, MD, a dermatologist based in Latest York City.

Here are a few of the ways your workout routine might be doing a number on them:.

Handling dumbbells, weight plates, and other equipment can put pressure on nails, especially if they're long, dry, or already weak, says celebrity manicurist Jin Soon Choi.

Jamming a nail against a hard surface or repeatedly putting pressure on it can cause the nail plate to separate from the nail bed, a condition known as onycholysis. This can happen to natural nails, too, although extensions may create additional opportunities for snagging.

When a falsie gets caught on something, it can even cause the underlying natural nail to lift from the nail bed, explains Dr. Stern. Depending on the extent of the damage, it can take months for a healthy new nail to grow in.

According to recent reports, frequent handwashing and exposure to harsh cleansers (including those used to wipe down gym equipment and alcohol-based hand sanitizers) can contribute to splitting and peeling, Dr. Stern adds.

While running and cycling may be gentler on your hands, repeated pressure inside athletic shoes can cause bruising beneath the nail (known as a subungual hematoma), thickening, or even nail loss, explains Dr. Garshick.

The good news? Most workout-related nail damage is temporary, provided the nail matrix, which is responsible for nail growth, hasn't been damaged. But more serious injuries can have lasting consequences. A crush injury—heavy weight falling on a nail, for example—could cause permanent damage to the nail matrix, Dr. Stern says. Even when the damage isn't permanent, patience is key. According to Dr. Garshick, fingernails typically take three to six months to grow out, while toenails can take twelve to eighteen months.

The goal isn't to give up your favorite workouts or your favorite manicures. A few tweaks to your nail-care routine and how you handle equipment can make all the difference.

Persistent discoloration, thickening, or lifting can sometimes indicate a fungal infection, psoriasis, or another nail condition. A new or changing dark streak, particularly one that doesn't grow out, should also be evaluated by a board-certified dermatologist.

Sources indicate that at the end of the day, you don't have to choose between crushing your fitness goals and keeping your nails in good shape. In fact, Choi points out that exercise itself can actually be beneficial for nail health, as physical activity promotes healthy circulation, helping deliver oxygen and nutrients to the tissues responsible for nail growth.

According to recent reports, it's the accidental injuries and repeated pressure that can cause problems, not the workouts themselves. With a few simple adjustments to your nail-care routine and a bit more awareness of how you're handling equipment, you can keep breaking a sweat without breaking a nail.
