Why Good Voice Acting Doesn’t Have to Happen in Isolation

In a recent development, film is always a collaborative medium, but one of the special things about animation is that you can craft incredibly nuanced performances with people who never get to meet. Voice acting in animation is a discipline in its own right — as legends like Mel Blanc and Eric Bauza show — where actors impart a kind of electric energy that carries through into the art style of what they’re voicing. So is voice directing. And the secret to both is in providing foundations for animators to build something bigger.

“Wildwood” director Travis Knight did a lot of the directing of his voice cast in lockdown Zooms, and hodge-podge recording setups, where it can be hard to remember that you’re developing something that needs to spiritually connect to the animation, whether existing animatics already exist or not. There isn’t the same kind of immersiveness for actors that live-action sets, costumes, and the basic pleasure of getting to read lines against other actors all provide. They have to build it from scratch. “It asks something very different of an actor, which is challenging because an actor can bring so much to performance before they even utter a word,” Knight told IndieWire on an episode of the Filmmaker Toolkit podcast. “So when you cast these things, it’s really interesting because sometimes the things we love about actors are not their voice; it’s everything else. So we will always listen, we’ll take clips from actors’ other films and their interviews, and we’ll put them against a design of a character.”.

According to recent reports, hearing a voice against a design can be revelatory, Knight believes, because it can teach a director and an animation team about who a character really is, whether a performer is really going to work or not, even before they step into the booth. It’s also an key way to help sort the whole of an ensemble so that actors occupy different aural frequencies and accents, and don’t sound too similar. You want to really be able to feel the difference between Carey Mulligan’s powerfully sinister Alexandra and Angela Bassett’s regal General.

And then, once the voice cast is in the booth, it’s also on the director and the team to help each voice actor sketch out all the different permutations of emotion the animators will then use as inspiration, and as tools.

“You’ll get the scene 30, 40, 50 different ways,” Knight remarked. “It takes forever. But, in the end, if you can do it properly, it feels like these characters are living in the same scene at the same time. And then it comes down to the animator to try to figure out how they can match the beauty of the vocal performance with the physical performance.”.

That’s the normal process for feature animation: finding compelling voices, matching them with a design that feels spiritually connected to that voice, covering the performance many different ways, and then assembling a template for animators to build off of. And it’s the director, really, who has to hold in their head the many ways that the disparate parts will all come together.

“An animator is an actor, but they’re just acting through their hands and through a puppet, and it’s two actors collaborating to create a complete performance. But oftentimes it happens across space, across time. And when it comes together in a harmonious way, it’s really beautiful because this thing seems like it’s completely alive,” Knight remarked.

In a recent development, but sometimes, voice actors’ performances do get to be alive together. That was a key creative choice that director Leah Nelson made for “Tangles” — it’s hard to argue with the logic of putting Abbi Jacobson, Julia Louis-Dreyfus, and Bryan Cranston all in a room together to act out an earnest family drama, even if there’s a bit of mic bleed.

“I’m so glad we [put them together]. And it wasn’t that hard. It took a little bit of scheduling Tetris to get it done, but it was so worthwhile,” Nelson told IndieWire. “Abbi does a ton of voice work, and often they’re alone and not even able to have someone to read against them. Sometimes they’re just doing the lines and sort of jumping around. And what I ultimately wanted was to have this really naturalistic dialogue.”.

“Tangles” boasts naturalism when it comes to sound, from bespoke foley to overlapping banter that isn’t meant to be precisely understood but to convey the chaos of one’s own (infuriating, loving) family. So Nelson and her team embraced the ways actors can build on each other, and even on improv, in recording the dialogue.

“A lot of humor came out of giving them time to not only play with the dialogue but to play with each other. But also, those really intimate and difficult moments of the film, they could look in each other’s eyes and work through those moments. That was key to me, and it was important to them as well. So it was an experience we all went through together.”.

Recent findings suggest that the one downside of treating recording sessions more like the rehearsals for a play is the risk of wrecking takes. “We spent a lot of time at the table trying to hold [our reactions] in or like, ‘Don’t sneeze! Nobody move!’” Nelson joked. “But I didn’t want it to sound like there was so much suturing; the mastery of the dialogue editing to make it all sound naturalistic, so we also did a lot of work to make that happen. But what we had to work with felt very naturalistic to begin with.”.

The ultimate example of naturalistic voice acting, however, might be on display in “Wildwood.” Knight’s own son was a newborn while the team was laying down track for the film, which made him the perfect actor for the role of kidnapped Baby Mac.

In a recent development, “I essentially chased him around the house with a digital recorder. Sometimes with my iPhone. I was just trying to coax things out of him, make him laugh and giggle and verbalize. And the thing about babies is that they’re constantly trying to make sense of the world,” Knight mentioned. “They’re always babbling and trying to make shapes with their mouths. So we have a lot of that babbling in the movie, and it helped make it feel naturalistic.”.

It speaks to the wide-ranging power of voice acting in animation: you can get to naturalism through a grounded collaboration between actors, an actor in complete isolation, or hitting “record” on an iPhone while you’re changing a diaper.

“Wildwood” is now in theaters. “Tangles” releases in theaters on January 15, 2027.
