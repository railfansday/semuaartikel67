Latest Update: Artificial Sweeteners May Be Linked to Faster Cognitive Decline, Latest Study Finds

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that sugar can be quite a controversial ingredient. Added sugars and refined versions have been linked to diabetes, obesity, and heart problems. But attempts at going the healthier route with artificial sweeteners have mixed reviews over the years because of both taste and possible long-term side effects. Now, a study by the American Academy of Neurology suggests that out of the 13,000 adults analyzed, those who consumed the most artificial sweeteners over the eight-year study period had a 62 percent faster decline in memory and thinking skills than those who consumed the least. The data means the brain aged by just over a year and a half. Experts explain what artificial sweetener use means for long-term health and whether it’s time to get rid of the sugar substitutes for good.

Rachel Brief, RD, a registered dietitian nutritionist based in NYC, explains to ELLE that glucose is the brain’s primary energy source and essential for normal and healthy brain function. However, she warns that excessive consumption of added sugars can have negative implications for long-term brain health. “Diets high in added sugar have been associated with insulin resistance, inflammation, and metabolic dysfunction, all of which may influence cognitive health over time,” she shares. “Sugar also activates the brain’s reward pathways, including dopamine signaling, which reinforces pleasurable eating experiences.”.

Overall, sugar isn’t bad; however, there are several ways both regular sugar and artificial sweeteners affect brain function. Jessica L. Zwerling, MD, director of the Montefiore Hudson Valley Center of Excellence for Alzheimer’s Disease (CEAD), shares a few of the main ones:.

Industry observers note that brief further explains that artificial sweeteners, specifically, interact with sweet-taste receptors, “Allowing us to experience sweetness without the same glucose load or caloric contribution as conventional sugar. However, their effects may extend beyond simply providing sweetness,” she notes. “Emerging research suggests that certain sweeteners may influence metabolic signaling, appetite regulation, and the gut microbiome, which plays an important role in communication between the gut and brain. We’re still learning how these interactions may influence cognitive health over time,” claims Brief.

Sources indicate that researchers from the American Academy of Neurology examined seven common artificial sweeteners used in foods and beverages: aspartame, saccharin, acesulfame-K, erythritol, xylitol, sorbitol, and tagatose. While the study results show an association between higher consumption and brain function, particularly memory and verbal fluency, as noted, it doesn’t prove a cause-and-effect relationship between the two.

Sources indicate that since there is insufficient evidence to establish a causal link between artificial sweeteners and deteriorating brain function, both Brief and Zwerling caution against directly making that claim. However, Zwerling indicates that other research has connected artificial sweeteners to dementia and ischemic stroke.

Industry observers note that as for the decline in brain function, Brief points out that emerging research has identified associations between higher consumption of certain sweeteners and faster declines in cognitive performance, particularly in areas such as memory and processing speed. “Potential mechanisms being explored include changes in metabolic health, insulin signaling, inflammation, and gut microbiome composition. More research is needed to understand whether these sweeteners directly contribute to cognitive changes,” she adds.

Zwerling elaborates on these potential mechanisms that may be at the root of it:.

According to recent reports, artificial sweeteners may alter the composition and function of the gut microbiome. These changes may influence inflammation. “In other words, there is a change in the communication between the gut and brain,” Zwerling explains.

In a recent development, some sweeteners may influence insulin secretion, glucose metabolism, and hormonal responses. “Insulin signaling is crucial for neuronal function, synaptic plasticity, and memory. Truly, what we eat affects how we think,” Zwerling cautions. Additionally, she notes that empirical studies have shown that consuming aspartame can lead to increased cytokine IL1β—a signaling protein in the body that regulates immune and inflammatory responses—thereby inducing systemic inflammation as well as suppression of tumor necrosis factor (TNF) alpha, another naturally occurring protein that helps coordinate immune responses, regulate cell survival or cell death, and increase inflammation. Zwerling notes that further research needs to be done to see how artificial sweeteners impair signaling and promote inflammation in humans, specifically related to the biomarker pathways associated with Alzheimer’s disease.

In a recent development, the 2025 study raises concern that regular consumption of certain low- and no-calorie sweeteners may be associated with accelerated cognitive decline. However, it does not establish that artificial sweeteners cause neurodegeneration or dementia. Not all artificial sweeteners affect neurotransmitters the same way. “The study highlighted this risk in the younger cohort. Given that artificial sweeteners limit sugary beverages and risk for obesity, further studies are needed as midlife obesity has been associated with risk of cognitive decline,” Zwerling clarifies.

Recent findings suggest that preclinical and computational research has proposed potential inflammatory and molecular pathways through which aspartame could influence biological processes relevant to Alzheimer’s disease. However, these mechanisms have not been established in human studies, and their relevance at typical dietary exposure levels remains uncertain.

The answer depends on several factors, mainly a person’s health history and potential risks. Zwerling says that some sweeteners may influence gut microbiome composition. Others may affect glucose metabolism and insulin responses. She also notes that observational studies have raised questions about cardiovascular and cognitive health. Replacing sugar with non-nutritive sweeteners can reduce caloric and sugar intake, but Zweling cautions that this does not necessarily translate into long-term health benefits.

In a recent development, brief agrees, adding that we shouldn’t automatically label artificial sweeteners as bad, but we also shouldn’t assume that replacing sugar with a zero-calorie alternative automatically makes a product healthier. “Different sweeteners have different properties and potential physiological effects. For someone who regularly consumes sugar-sweetened beverages, switching to an artificially sweetened, lower-calorie, lower-sugar option may be a practical way to reduce added sugar intake,” Brief explains. “Just like everything in nutrition and wellness, this is relative to a person’s individual diet.” She encourages people to gradually reduce their reliance on intensely sweetened foods and beverages and prioritize minimally processed foods. “The goal isn’t to eliminate carbohydrates or naturally occurring sugars, but to be mindful of excessive added sugar intake,” says Brief.

According to Zwerling, no established amount of artificial sweetener has been demonstrated to be optimal for long-term brain health. According to Zweling, certain regulatory agencies have established acceptable daily intake (ADI) levels for individual sweeteners. “These represent amounts considered safe for daily lifetime consumption, not thresholds at which cognitive effects have been ruled out.” She reiterates that excessive added sugar consumption is associated with adverse metabolic health and may indirectly contribute to cognitive decline through insulin resistance, inflammation, and vascular disease.

It has been revealed that she adds that neither excessive sugar nor heavy reliance on artificial sweeteners should substitute for a nutritionally adequate dietary pattern. For brain health, Zweling states a reasonable approach is to minimize excess added sugar and avoid relying heavily on artificial sweeteners as a long-term substitute. A dietary pattern emphasizing whole foods, fruits, vegetables, fiber, and minimally processed foods is preferable. Brief co-signs the advice: “I generally encourage people to avoid excessive reliance on sweeteners and focus on building a diet that doesn’t require everything to taste sweet.” She recommends exploring ways to add natural sweetness without relying heavily on sugar substitutes. “Fresh or frozen fruit, mashed banana, unsweetened applesauce, cinnamon, and vanilla extract are all great options. Honey, maple syrup, coconut sugar, and date syrup are better-for-you, less-processed options that are still sources of added sugar,” says Brief.

Recent findings suggest that beyond the research pool, Zweling adds that work is needed, especially in food desert areas, to ensure these nutritious foods are available. “This emphasizes the need for health providers and patients/caregivers to work with community-based organizations to ensure access to healthy foods,” Zweling stresses. “Brain health is not simply a matter of telling people what to eat; it is also about ensuring they have access to the foods that support healthy aging,” she concludes.
