I’m standing in front of the fish counter at Market Basket, the grocery store chain that serves much of eastern Massachusetts. I scan the cooler for oysters. Shucked oysters, to be specific. It’s the day before Thanksgiving. I’m trying to recreate the menu of my youth. Those offerings included scalloped oysters, a recipe that my mother made every Thanksgiving and Christmas. Mom passed away in 2023. She never wrote down the recipe. She always followed the "handful of this" and a "pinch of that" school of cooking. She was masterful. I, on the other hand, celebrate the occasional culinary win, but more often than not, my successes are hit or miss—especially when it comes to the infrequent concocting of scalloped oysters.

Why do we try so hard, at this time of the year, to recreate the recipes from our past?

We are attempting to do more than recreate those dishes; we are trying to conjure the ghosts of those friends and family members who are no longer with us.

Roast the perfect turkey. Prepare the perfect pumpkin pie. Arrange an artistic nut tray. Make the perfect mashed potatoes with just enough lumps to know they’re homemade, but not so many that we ask, “Who cooked the potatoes?”.

Some families carefully divvy the work among invited dinner guests. Auntie will handle the fresh cranberry relish, while Cousin brings the almond green bean casserole. Somehow, two people end up making the candied sweet potatoes, but one looks a little gray while the other is adorned with perfectly toasted mini-marshmallows. We will struggle to consume both. We don’t want to hurt anyone’s feelings.

Recent findings suggest that are we attempting to make Instagram-worthy dishes for the social media kudos? Or are we endeavoring to improve upon the butter-laden, sugary dishes of our youth? Remember that Uncle Robbie needs to watch his caloric intake after suffering a minor heart attack last spring. And Cousin Joanne is supposed to be watching her sugar since she started taking insulin to control her diabetes.

“It’s just one day,” we counter. “How bad could it be?”.

In a recent development, but there are ethical considerations, too. Shannon’s new friend is vegan. Out of respect, we throw together a dairy-free lasagna. Is that really enough?

In-laws bring new traditions. My husband’s grandmother’s family favorite of orange ice befuddles our daughter-in-law’s family, but they agree to serve it every year. “We eat this first?” one asks. “But won’t the turkey get cold?” A question I’ve asked myself for almost fifty years. “Maybe this would be better served for the 4th of July!” someone says. And we all nod. And we never actually follow through.

In a recent development, newly introduced Orleanian author Amy Cyrex Sins mourned the absence of a recipe for Doberge cake after losing her treasured recipe file and her home after Hurricane Katrina ravaged Louisiana in August of 2005. While she suffered the obvious loss of possessions, it was the inability to recreate a favored family treat that continued to jab at her with the sharp barbs of loss, both physical and emotional. I remember sharing her essay with an honors college class many years ago. One student took it upon herself to make the dish. We New Englanders had no context to judge her success, but I know that I enjoyed a slice of cake that day. And I’ll never forget how she cut the cake by carving out a center circle and then cutting slices from the outside rim. A new tradition? Probably not. A new taste memory? Definitely.

But tasting our past and our memories encompasses so much more than carefully measuring and mixing ingredients.

When we attempt to recreate recipes from our childhoods and our pasts, we honor those cooks and chefs of our ancestors.

Vera, my maternal grandmother, was a terrible cook. I remember her making hamburgers and boiled potatoes. The patties were overcooked, the potatoes fork-mashed with butter and pepper. That was a staple meal served almost every night of the week! Grammy was a single mother, having been widowed at the age of nineteen. At the time, she was pregnant and had a two-year-old. Survival was critical. Tastes were secondary. And this was during the Great Depression.

One forgives a lack of herbs and spices when cheap ground beef and farm-raised spuds were often rationed between the extended family members of Canadian immigrants.

In a recent development, psychotherapist and Relationship Expert Mary Pender Greene writes, “Remember that there are NO perfect holidays” (2009, p. 26). She suggests that the malaise of the holidays stems from “unrealistic expectations, which is often inspired by holiday marketing” (Greene, 2009, p. 29). Thank you, Martha Stewart.

One year, my husband received a 24-pound turkey as a bonus from his organization. I calculated that the bird would take about 8 hours to roast. Just after 6 a.m., I popped the pale, featherless carcass into the oven. But at about 1 p.m., the fire alarm started to beep. Smoke filled the kitchen. I opened the oven door and orange flames licked at the stove front. My husband doused the fire with the white foam from a trusty fire extinguisher.

We didn’t have turkey that day. We had hamburgers. I think my elderly grandmother actually preferred the Thanksgiving dinner that day.

Sources indicate that this year, I will once again attempt to make my mother’s scalloped oysters. I will be the only one eating them, as my husband and daughter eschew eating shellfish. But that just means more for me.

I hope that I swerve closer to the perfect scalloped oysters of my memories, but even if I miss that mark, I know that I continue to honor a long-held family tradition.

According to recent reports, we strive to taste the memories of the past, but tastes do change. Ingredients become hard to find and obsolete. And that’s absolutely okay.
