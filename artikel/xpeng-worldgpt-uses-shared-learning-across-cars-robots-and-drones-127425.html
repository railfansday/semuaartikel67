According to recent reports, XPENG unveiled WorldGPT on November 24, 2026, a world action model that predicts physical scenes and prepares machine actions. It was trained across six embodiments, or machine types: cars, boats, drones, single-arm robots, dual-arm robots and humanoids. The system belongs to XPENG’s Turing World Model family.

Sources indicate that the WorldGPT project site indicates the model starts with about one second of video from one or several cameras and a text instruction. It can then generate multi-camera video of the next few seconds, actions such as steering, joint motion or thrust, depth and segmentation outputs, and a plain-language explanation of its decisions. The same weights handle every supported machine, replacing separate perception, simulation and action pipelines with one model.

In a recent development, XPENG’s argument is that these machines share physical problems despite having different controls. A car must judge distances and anticipate surrounding vehicles, while a robotic arm must locate an object and adjust its movements to grasp it. Developing a separate model for each machine splits the available data and repeats development work.

Sources indicate that worldGPT instead learns in a shared pixel space. Steering angles cannot transfer directly to robot arms, and joint angles cannot transfer directly to drones, but camera images provide a common input through which each embodiment’s data can teach the others. The model then produces actions appropriate to the machine it is controlling.

In a recent development, worldGPT’s architecture separates the large model from deployment on individual machines. The cloud-based teacher learns across embodiments and is designed for distillation, the transfer of its knowledge into smaller, specialized student models that run on individual machines. A latest embodiment adds its data to the teacher rather than requiring another complete model stack.

XPENG claims this arrangement could reduce development costs, improve data efficiency and simplify extensions to new machines. It places WorldGPT within a broader physical AI strategy spanning intelligent vehicles, humanoid robotics and autonomous mobility.

XPENG reports 12 first-place results on public benchmarks across autonomous driving, robotic manipulation, visual understanding and video generation as of November 12, 2026. Four are in driving, including NAVSIM v2 results for standard and challenging scenarios. The reported average success rate for robotic manipulation is 95.2% across 50 single-arm tasks. Its other first-place results cover CV-Bench and two BLINK subtasks; WorldGPT also has the highest overall VBench 2.0 score and a tie for first on WorldOlympiad.

It has been revealed that the project site qualifies those comparisons: they do not control for training data, and VBench 2.0 is marked as self-evaluated with prompt rewriting. A technical report is listed as coming soon, so the current technical disclosures are the announcement and project page.

The site also hosts 32 demonstration clips across the supported machines. Each begins with real camera footage, while the following video is WorldGPT’s prediction; a real recording plays alongside for comparison. Separate demonstrations start from the same recorded moment and change the instruction, showing how the generated future and planned action change with it.

In a recent development, worldGPT’s broader implication is a shift from building separate AI stacks for each machine toward training a shared foundation and adapting it for deployment. The benchmark results describe its reported breadth; the teacher-to-student handoff is the mechanism on which XPENG’s projected gains in development cost and data efficiency depend.
