The study shows that listening and speaking neurons sit side by side, opening latest targets for neurosurgical mapping, autism research, and brain-computer interfaces that restore speech.

For more than a century, neuroscience textbooks have divided human language into two separate anatomical compartments: Wernicke’s area in the temporal lobe to understand heard speech, and frontal motor circuits (such as Broca’s area and the motor cortex) to produce spoken words.

Yet that neat division never fully explained a persistent clinical paradox: why patients who suffer strokes or injuries in Wernicke’s area frequently struggle not only to comprehend language, but also to retrieve and speak the words they intend to say.

Now, in a landmark study published in Science, researchers at UC San Francisco (UCSF) led by neurosurgeon Dr. Edward Chang have resolved that 150-year-old puzzle.

Drawing on 15 years of high-resolution intracranial brain recordings alongside targeted neural experiments, the team demonstrated that a specialized hub within Wernicke’s area actively generates a cognitive “blueprint” for each word roughly a quarter-second before a person begins to speak.

According to recent reports, in the 1870s, German neurologist Carl Wernicke originally speculated that the posterior temporal lobe housed an “auditory image of words”, an internal template that could guide spoken output. Over subsequent decades, however, because the region sits nestled inside auditory cortex, neuroscientists largely pigeonholed it as purely a passive receiver for incoming speech sounds.

Recent findings suggest that “What’s different now is that we finally have the tools and the data to see the brain at the speed and scale at which speech actually happens — millisecond by millisecond, down to individual neurons,” explained co-first author Dr. Matthew Leonard, associate professor of neurological surgery at UCSF.

Combining longitudinal intracranial recordings gathered in Chang’s laboratory with new experiments designed to separate listening from speaking, Leonard and co-first author Dr. Alex Silva, a student in UCSF’s Medical Scientist Training Program, pinpointed a crucial cortical hub: the left posterior superior temporal gyrus (lpSTG).

Tracking neural activity at millisecond resolution revealed that specific populations of neurons inside the lpSTG surged into action approximately 250 milliseconds (a quarter-second) before vocalization began.

Crucially, this pre-speech firing did not represent raw acoustic sounds or physical movements of the lips and tongue. Instead, lpSTG neurons constructed an abstract linguistic blueprint encoding:.

It has been revealed that once assembled in the lpSTG, this structural blueprint traveled forward to speech-planning networks in the frontal lobe, and subsequently to the motor cortex, which translated the plan into precise articulatory muscle commands. Furthermore, high-resolution mapping showed that neurons dedicated to hearing speech sit directly beside, and sometimes overlap with, the neurons generating outgoing word blueprints.

“This changes the textbook view of how the brain perceives and produces speech,” mentioned Chang, chair of the UCSF Department of Neurological Surgery and member of the UCSF Weill Institute for Neurosciences. “Instead of being separate systems, the processes of hearing speech and actually speaking are very intertwined.”.

Housing incoming auditory processing and outgoing word planning inside the same microcircuit provides a compelling neural mechanism for how infants acquire language, converting heard sounds directly into motor-ready speech blueprints. It also offers a targeted anatomical region to investigate why roughly one-quarter to one-third of autistic children remain minimally verbal despite demonstrating strong language comprehension.

“It gives us a specific place in the brain to look, and a specific question to ask,” Chang noted.

Equally transformative are the implications for neurotechnology. Today’s speech brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) typically decode intended words from the motor cortex, a strategy that fails if a patient’s motor pathways are severely damaged. Because the lpSTG operates upstream of motor execution, it offers an alternative decoding gateway.

In proof-of-concept testing with two participants living with severe paralysis and anarthria (inability to speak), the UCSF team successfully decoded both the words participants attempted to say and the words they heard directly from lpSTG signals, pointing toward future conversational BCIs capable of capturing both sides of human dialogue.

How does this discovery change what we know about how the brain produces speech?

For over 100 years, textbooks taught that speech comprehension (Wernicke’s area) and speech production were handled by separate brain systems. UCSF researchers proved that a region inside Wernicke’s area (the lpSTG) actually activates a quarter-second before we speak to build an abstract “blueprint” of each intended word, including its length and syllable count, before sending it to motor areas.

Why can damage to Wernicke’s area make it hard to speak words, not just understand them?

Because the neurons that process heard speech sit directly alongside the neurons that build the structural blueprint for spoken words in the left posterior superior temporal gyrus, a stroke or injury to that region disrupts both language comprehension and word planning.

How could this finding help paralyzed patients communicate?

Sources indicate that current speech brain-computer interfaces read signals from the brain’s motor cortex, which can be damaged in some paralyzed patients. Because the lpSTG creates word blueprints earlier in the speech pipeline, decoding signals from this region could restore communication for a wider range of paralyzed individuals—and potentially capture both heard and spoken words for natural conversation.

A neural code for generative speech production in human Wernicke’s area.

In a recent development, wernicke’s area, located in the posterior superior temporal gyrus of the human brain, has long been considered essential for understanding speech. However, damage to this region also disrupts the ability to produce speech, creating a long-standing puzzle in neuroscience. Traditional explanations propose that this area primarily supports speech production indirectly by monitoring sounds during speaking. However, it remains unclear whether this region plays a more direct role in generating speech itself, and if so, what kind of information it represents: sounds, movements, or even properties of whole words. Resolving this question is critical for understanding how the brain constructs language and for developing technologies to restore speech in individuals who have lost it.

Addressing this question requires probing the human brain at multiple scales with both correlational and causal methods. We combined several complementary approaches, including high-resolution direct recordings of neural populations and single neurons during speaking and listening, electrical stimulation of specific brain regions to test causality, and analysis of patients with localized brain lesions. Across these methods, participants performed a variety of speaking and listening tasks ranging from naturalistic speech to single word repetition, providing a detailed and holistic view of the role of the posterior temporal lobe in generating upcoming speech.

In a recent development, we identified a specific subregion within individual participants’ Wernicke’s area—the left posterior superior temporal gyrus—that becomes active before speech begins. This early activity precedes word onsets in continuous speech rather than reflecting feedback from hearing one’s own voice. Brain stimulation and lesion analyses showed that disrupting this region causes errors in speech production, confirming its causal role.

Detailed analyses revealed that this region encodes properties of entire words, such as their length and frequency, rather than individual sounds or motor movements. This is distinct from neighboring brain regions that encode auditory feedback and do so by representing lower-level features such as articulation or sound.

Timing analyses indicated that activity in this region occurs earlier than in motor areas responsible for controlling speech muscles, placing it at the start of the speech production process. Recordings from individual neurons further confirmed that cells in this region respond before speech production, respond selectively to specific words, and are active during both speaking and listening.

Finally, in people unable to speak due to paralysis, signals from this region could be used to decode both intended and perceived speech, demonstrating its potential for multimodal brain-computer interface technologies aimed at restoring communication.

According to recent reports, this work identifies a specialized region within Wernicke’s area that generates word-level representations before speech is produced. Rather than serving primarily as a center for speech perception, this region acts as an early stage in the production process, organizing upcoming words before they are translated into motor commands. These findings resolve a long-standing paradox about Wernicke’s area and provide a unified framework for understanding how the brain links language comprehension and production. They also highlight a promising target for future neuroprosthetic devices aimed at restoring speech in individuals with communication impairments.
