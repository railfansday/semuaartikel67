The Business Council of Australia is lobbying for gas expansion policies that clash with the climate concerns of the majority of its own members, according to latest research that raises questions about the sway of a handful of major companies.

The business association also does not publicly disclose who is on its energy and climate committee, prompting transparency concerns among researchers over how its policies are shaped and whose interests they represent.

Analysis by London-headquartered InfluenceMap found the BCA’s positions aligned more closely with energy companies Woodside, Santos and ConocoPhillips than with the majority of its corporate base, particularly on the energy transition where it has advocated for upcoming gas supplies over phasing down.

InfluenceMap’s Australia lead, Jack Herring, said major associations such as the BCA are an influential voice in government policymaking.

“These findings suggest the BCA’s climate policy engagement better reflects the narrow interests of members connected to the fossil fuel value chain than its wider membership,” Herring said.

“Advocacy for fossil gas expansion risks undermining Australia’s climate targets and also threatens to create increased health risks and energy affordability and security concerns for everyday households, so this is an concern that affects us all.”.

InfluenceMap’s analysis of organization positions shows more than two-thirds of the members assessed, including Qantas, Woolworths, Coles, Atlassian, Fortescue and major banks CBA and NAB, advocate for stronger climate policies than the business council itself.

That support typically includes backing faster renewable energy rollout and advocating for more ambitious emission reduction targets.

For the study, the thinktank assessed 58 companies – a representative sample of the BCA’s more than 120 members – drawing on thousands of public statements, government submissions and regulatory filings.

Of those members assessed, 69% backed stronger climate policies than the BCA, a gap that widened to 78% on energy transition challenges.

Herring said the BCA does not disclose who sits on its internal climate and energy committees, obscuring how its policy positions are formed.

“We obviously do know who’s on the association board, but beyond that, there’s no transparency around those internal committees,” he remarked.

A spokesperson for the BCA said the association supported the “national goal of achieving net zero by 2050 and the setting of interim targets, while also delivering reliable and affordable energy and ensuring we don’t undermine our international competitiveness along the way”.

“The BCA has long advocated for gas as an essential part of the net zero transition – ensuring grid stability – while we move to greater reliance on renewable energy,” the spokesperson noted.

The BCA declined to comment on the makeup of its climate and energy committee.

The analysis comes as industry groups ramp up lobbying over the “safeguard mechanism”, Australia’s main policy for curbing emissions from its largest industrial facilities, which is under federal review.

While the BCA and most significant resources companies publicly support the Paris agreement and Australia’s net zero 2050 carbon emissions target, they interpret what those commitments require in radically different ways to what is widely regarded as climate best practice.

Several oil and gas producer members, including Woodside, argue that increased gas supply is required to support the shift to renewables and enhance energy security in language that has been adopted by the BCA.

This rubs against those BCA members aligned with the findings of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, which found greenhouse gas emissions from existing fossil fuel infrastructure were more than enough to push the world beyond its climate goals.

InfluenceMap also found that the business council frequently lobbies for a “technology-neutral” or “technology agnostic” energy transition, which is the same language used by the fossil fuel sector designed to steer renewable energy funding toward gas.

Persephone Fraser, the ethical research and climate policy lead at fund manager Australian Ethical, mentioned misaligned lobbying was a significant risk for companies and their shareholders, and a major risk to the economy.

“Companies must be scrutinising what is being advocated for in their name and actively correcting the record when it is inconsistent with their own positions, and interests,” Fraser said.

“Membership of an industry association cannot mean outsourcing responsibility for climate lobbying.”.
