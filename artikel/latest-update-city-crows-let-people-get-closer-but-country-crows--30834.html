Latest Update: City crows let people get closer, but country crows can be bolder in their own way

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

City crows tolerate people better than rural crows, but unfamiliar objects near food can make them more cautious.

Sources indicate that city crows let people get closer than rural crows do. But when researchers put a lamp base in a pile of peanuts, city crows were less likely than rural crows to take a peanut.

The study of American crows around Ithaca, Fresh York, suggests that a crow’s fears are shaped by its past experience.

According to recent reports, two experiments were run by Dave Colucci and Anne Clark, biologists at Binghamton University. In the first experiment, objects were left next to food on crow territories.

In the second, Colucci walked toward crows and measured how close he got before they flew.

The evidence for greater wariness in city crows comes from a single object.

Sources indicate that it also comes from only one of the three measures used by the team. In addition, the city sites were closer to suburbs than to a downtown.

In crows, Colucci said, fear of anything upcoming exists side by side with a great deal of curiosity. The fear does not always prevail, he added, so a new opportunity is sometimes seized.

Ten crow families were chosen from parking lots, parks, and dead-end streets near Ithaca.

Another 10 families lived on farmland and natural areas in the nearby town of Dryden.

American crows live in family groups that share a territory.

On most days of the week, Colucci scattered two cups of unsalted peanuts in the shell on each territory.

Recent findings suggest that the same spot was used each time. He continued until the family had fed there three times in a row.

It has been revealed that A single object was then set in the middle of the peanuts on four separate mornings. The crows were filmed by a hidden camcorder. Colucci watched from at least 164 feet (50 meters) away.

Recent findings suggest that the trials were run in July and August of 2019 and 2020. Four objects were used: a rock, an ear of corn in its husk, a takeout box, and a lamp base.

The box was paperboard, and the lamp base was made to look like wood.

Every crow was expected to find the lamp base new. Bright colors, shiny surfaces and sharp angles are typical of human-made things.

These features are probably better known to crows living in cities. None of them was present on the lamp base.

When only peanuts were offered, nearly every crow that came within about 10 feet (3 meters) took one.

The team estimated the probability of eating at 99% for such city crows and 97% for rural crows.

With the lamp base in place, the estimated probability dropped to 76% among rural crows and 53% among city crows.

Sources indicate that wariness of upcoming things is called neophobia by biologists. By this measure, the city crows had more of it.

Sources indicate that the other two measures concerned only the first crow of each family to arrive and to eat. Their timing was compared with that on days when no object was present.

With the lamp base in place, the first crow to arrive and the first to eat were about as quick in city families as in rural ones.

To Colucci and Clark, that result suggests both places have some bold crows.

Results from earlier experiments on city and country birds have not agreed with one another. Lower wariness in cities has been reported in some of them, higher wariness or no difference in others.

Colucci and Clark wrote that some of those experiments used human-made objects, which city animals may know better.

Sources indicate that A second idea was tested with the corn and the takeout box. An ear of corn should be ordinary to a crow that lives beside farm fields.

Industry observers note that that ear of corn should seem strange to a crow whose home is beside a parking lot. For a takeout box, the reverse should be true.

More rural crows were put off by the takeout box, and more city crows were put off by the corn.

The team could be confident of that pattern only in the share of crows that ate. In how promptly the first crow arrived or ate, the pattern was too weak to be sure of.

In a recent development, A one-mile (1.6 km) stretch of road was also walked in each setting once a week for three weeks. Every human-made object was recorded.

A total of 341 objects was counted, with 169 along the city road and 172 along the rural road. A typical object nevertheless remained in place for a shorter time along the city road.

Objects turned over faster along the city road, but city crows were still more reluctant to eat beside the lamp base.

City crows are thus exposed to a constant turnover of objects, yet their wariness of newly introduced things hasn’t been reduced.

The authors suggested that most litter may be treated by city crows as one category.

In the second experiment, crows were approached on foot by Colucci 145 times over three summers. These approaches took place across upstate Fresh York and Quebec.

It has been revealed that during some approaches his gaze was fixed directly on the crow. During others his whole face was tilted toward the ground. A camera on his chest streamed video to a phone in his hand so that his direction could be maintained.

The average crow took flight while he was still about 150 feet (45 meters) away. A distance of 33 feet (10 meters) or less was reached in only eight approaches.

Closer approaches were tolerated by city crows than by rural crows, as is usual for city animals. Rural crows fled from far away regardless of the direction of his gaze.

In a recent development, in the most built-up areas, crows let him get about 82 feet (25 meters) closer when his face was down. The authors wrote that the true effect of his gaze could be either small or large.

According to recent reports, A similar but smaller effect was found in a 2013 study of crows in Seattle. To the authors’ knowledge, a difference between two populations in the use of human gaze had not been shown before.

Hunting is one possible explanation for the behavior of the rural crows.

Within city limits in New York, crows may not be hunted. Hunting them in rural areas is legal from September 1 to March 31.

The rural response was described by Clark, an associate professor emerita of biological sciences, as a blunt defense against predators. “Don’t take any chances, don’t stop to assess; let’s just be safe,” she noted.

It has been revealed that clark added that such a habit would not work for city crows. People pass by too often in a city for a crow to take flight every time.

The peanut experiment also provided evidence of greater caution among rural crows. With no object present, the first city crow reached the peanuts at about four times the rural rate.

In a recent development, the number of people living within about half a mile (800 meters) gave the best prediction of a crow’s reaction. When smaller or larger circles were used, the predictions were poorer.

A circle of that size is larger than the territory held by a crow family.

It was suggested by the authors that crows average their experience of people over a wide area. They then adjust to the person approaching them.

Individual people who have threatened them are also remembered by crows.

It has been revealed that the study leaves open whether city crows gain any advantage from being more cautious around an unfamiliar object. Two possible explanations have been put forward by the authors.

One is that cities contain dangers which cannot be predicted even by a crow that knows its neighborhood well.

According to recent reports, the other concerns the crows that first settled in towns about 70 years ago. Those birds may have had little fear of latest things, with caution proving useful only later.

Recent findings suggest that it is also uncertain whether the lamp base was equally new to both groups. Its shape is perfectly symmetrical, and such symmetry is rare in natural objects.

City crows might therefore have found it more familiar than the team had intended.

Were that the case, the authors wrote, the difference between city and rural crows could be underestimated.

Future research could test whether wariness slows crows down when new problems have to be solved. It could also test whether cities hold more unfamiliar threats.

Sources indicate that for comparisons of urban wildlife with rural animals, a first step was recommended by Colucci and Clark. They wrote that the objects present in each environment ought to be identified first.

The full study was published in the journal Behavioral Ecology.
