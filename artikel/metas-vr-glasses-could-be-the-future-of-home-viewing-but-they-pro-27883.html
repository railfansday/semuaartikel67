Meta's VR Glasses Could Be The Future Of Home Viewing (But They Probably Won't)

Recent findings suggest that in stark contrast to the dire straits that the movie theater industry was in just a few years ago, 2026 has been especially encouraging to those of us who adore going to the movies. A big part of why that's changed lies in the industry's embrace of unique Premium Large Formats, with numerous releases this year having been shown in formats like IMAX, 4DX, Screen X, Dolby Cinema, and so on. Along the way, screening on celluloid (70mm in particular) have become a draw unto themselves, and some cities are home to even more specialized formats, among them Las Vegas' immersive Sphere and the Cosm experience. And while the biggest draw to movie theaters will always be good films, it's clear that audiences also enjoy an experience they can't get elsewhere.

As usual, however, the tech industry isn't far behind to try and stoke the competition between theaters and home viewing. While most consumers have been content with things like big screen 4K TV's or even their handheld devices for watching at home or on the go, the latest attempt to leap forward from these is just around the corner, in the form of Meta's VR Glasses. It's crucial to note that these are not the latest version of the smart glasses that have existed on the market since 2021, as they're explicitly not designed to be used while engaged in other activity. Instead, they're a cutting edge virtual/augmented reality system intended to bring together multiple work and entertainment options while idle.

Recent findings suggest that I recently had the opportunity to try these glasses for myself, and their potential, especially for home viewing, is amazing. That said, there are some restrictions to them which, unless changed, mean they won't be replacing our existing personal devices anytime soon.

Although virtual reality, or VR, has existed in some form for decades, it's mostly been utilized as a gimmick more than a viable first choice for media. Fortunately, the folks behind Meta VR Glasses seem to understand this. I got to see a clip from the recent concert doc "Billie Eilish — Hit Me Hard and Soft: The Tour (Live in 3D)" on the glasses, and the experience was like I was watching it on a movie theater screen. That is to say that the aspect ratio was exactly the same as its theatrical exhibition, and the 3D display was pitch perfect. Turning my head in any direction didn't move the "screen," but kept it fixed in front of me, as if it was a physical object in the space, which is a very smart design.

While the James Cameron documentary acted as a demo of how watching most films and shows on the glasses would go, the Meta employees next indicated me what they're calling "Enhanced Cinema." Using a clip from "Avengers: Infinity War" in which Thor (Chris Hemsworth) constructs Stormbreaker, the presentation was almost exactly akin to the Cosm experience. This meant that the film was playing on a similar fixed "display" in front of me, while all around me (literally 360 degrees — I checked!) was the environment of the forge on Nidavellir. It's an effect that's totally immersive but not distracting, nor does it betray the original intent of the film itself. After being told that Meta has an exclusive license with the IMAX corporation to present movies filmed for that format on the glasses, I have no doubt that the device could brilliantly replicate those screens for home viewing, and that's incredibly exciting.

While the Meta VR Glasses are not intended to replace a smartphone or similar device, they do seem to do a lot of what those devices do, only bigger and better. From working on VR video games and multiple computer monitors at once to allowing you to watch sports games as they broadcast while seeing the view from courtside, the glasses seem almost too good to be true as a personal media device.

Recent findings suggest that this is where the catches come in. For one thing, watching something with other people at the same time can only occur depending on the content. For another, though they're engineered to be used while stationary, the temptation for people to wear them in public while walking (or, god forbid, driving) exists, and it's quite troubling. Then there's the fact that the glasses seem designed to only work with whatever services and content Meta allows. This means that us physical media fans are SOL, and as with any all-digital service, the content available on the glasses could be subject to change (or disappear) at any time. That's a lot to ask for a device priced at $1,299.99 USD, which is about the same as a fresh iPhone.

Right now, Meta's killer app beyond the tech itself are its exclusive licenses, such as with IMAX and James Cameron's Lightstorm Entertainment. The only way it'll compete with devices already on the market is if it can consistently deliver the quality experiences I got to have with it while being as user friendly as possible. If the glasses prove they can be used at home and on the go as smoothly as an iPhone can now, then they could be a game changer. Until then, they're just a gimmick, albeit a really nice one.
