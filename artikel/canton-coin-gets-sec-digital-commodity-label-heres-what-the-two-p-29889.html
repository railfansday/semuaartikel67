Canton Coin Gets SEC "Digital Commodity" Label: Here's What the Two Prongs Actually Mean

AI-assisted summary, reviewed by NullTX editors.

The SEC's Division of Corporation Finance called Canton Coin a digital commodity on November 3. The ruling rests on two representations, including the absence of a "central party.".

Recent findings suggest that the most notable words in the SEC's Canton Coin letter aren't "digital commodity." They are the ones the staff put in front of that label: the conclusion holds "in reliance on the representations" Canton's lawyers made. The SEC didn't independently prove the network has no central party. It accepted a description and attached a conclusion to it.

On November 3, the Division of Corporation Finance's Office of Chief Counsel answered Canton Network's October 30 request. The response came from Deputy Chief Counsel Jonathan A. Ingram and was addressed to the staff team of Ted Yu, Andrew Schoeffler and Mark Vilardo. The Division remarked it views Canton Coin as a "digital commodity" as the term is described in the SEC's March interpretation.

It also noted it will not recommend enforcement action if Canton Coin is minted programmatically by Super Validators, Validators and application providers, as described in the request. That holds without registration under Section 5 of the Securities Act or Section 12(g) of the Exchange Act, and it applies if the issuer relies on an opinion of counsel. The facts the staff relied on are laid out in the Canton Network's incoming letter.

Early coverage focused on the classification label. The more useful story, though, is how the staff got there.

Sources indicate that the staff pointed to two representations. The first is that Canton Coin is intrinsically linked to, and derives its value from, the programmatic operation of the Canton Network, along with supply and demand dynamics. Its value does not come from an expectation of profits from the essential managerial efforts of others.

Industry observers note that that phrase echoes the Howey test, the long-standing U.S. framework for investment contracts. The prong asks a simple question: when someone holds this token, are they betting on a team's work, or on a network that runs by itself? On the facts presented, the staff's answer was the network.

This is the more familiar half of the analysis, and most token projects argue some version of it. Because almost every issuer makes the same claim, the first prong carries less weight on its own, which is why the second one deserves a closer look.

The second representation is that the Canton Network has no "central party," as the March interpretation defines that term. In the SEC and CFTC's framework, a digital commodity belongs to a functional crypto system that has no central party overseeing participation or distributing rewards to users.

In a recent development, this prong is the one to watch, because Canton Coin is minted by Super Validators, Validators and application providers. Those are named roles in a structured network, so a skeptic could fairly ask whether the Super Validator tier looks like a central party in practice. The letter's answer rests on the word "programmatically": the minting follows protocol rules rather than anyone's discretion.

Legal commentators reading the March interpretation have also pointed out that it doesn't fully explain how the "central party" concept relates to its separate definition of decentralization. The Canton letter doesn't settle that either. It applies the term to one set of facts and stops there.

A no-action letter is narrower than it sounds. The Division itself says the response states its position on enforcement action only and expresses no legal conclusion on the concerns presented. It also says the positions rest on the representations in the request, and that different facts or conditions might lead to different conclusions.

In a recent development, in practice, that comes down to three points. First, the letter is staff-level guidance rather than a Commission vote. Second, it protects reliance on the specific arrangement described, not the token in every future circumstance. Third, if the representations change, the comfort can disappear.

It has been revealed that for a manufacturer, the letter is still valuable, since a written statement that staff won't recommend enforcement is a strong signal. Even so, it shouldn't be mistaken for a permanent legal classification. It is a snapshot of staff views based on facts someone else supplied.

The framework behind the letter is the Commission's March 17 interpretation, which the CFTC joined. It sorts crypto assets into five categories: digital commodities, digital collectibles, digital tools, stablecoins and digital securities. Four of the five are generally treated as non-securities, and the securities analysis stays fact-specific for the rest.

The two documents do different jobs. The interpretation is a general map: it tells the market how the Commission thinks about categories and which features push an asset one way or the other. The Canton letter is an application. It takes a named token, tests it against the map's digital commodity category, and gives a case-specific enforcement answer.

Sources indicate that that sequence matters. The interpretation states what a digital commodity looks like, and the letter says that, if the facts are as represented, Canton Coin looks like one.

It has been revealed that the practical lesson is about preparation. Canton's request framed its facts to fit the two prongs, and the staff responded in those terms. Projects hoping for similar treatment will likely follow the same template: document how value arises from network operation, and document why no single party oversees participation or distributes rewards.

The harder test will come from tokens with more visible governance or a more active operating team. Those projects will have to explain why their structure doesn't amount to a central party, and the Canton letter doesn't say where that line sits.

For now, the letter works best as an early data point rather than a final answer. The classification label drew the attention, but the representations behind it are what other projects will study. How the staff handles the next request, especially one with a messier governance story, will show whether the "no central party" prong is a narrow gate or a wide one.
