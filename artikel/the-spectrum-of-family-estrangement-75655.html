The Spectrum of Family Estrangement

They say to write what you know. For better or worse, I know this topic from both sides of the couch.

A 2025 YouGov survey found that 38 percent of American adults reported being estranged from at least one immediate family member (Orth, 2025). When you hear the word estrangement, you may think of Hollywood-style relational pyrotechnics, with family members storming out of the house, never to speak to one another again.

In a recent development, to be fair, that does happen. But estrangement isn’t always about screaming fights and punishing silence. Researcher Kristina Scharp (2019) describes family estrangement as a continuum involving multiple dimensions, including emotional closeness, communication, physical distance, and the desire for reconciliation.

Here are four ways family estrangement can show up.

It has been revealed that proximity is a factor that can contribute to closeness, but is insufficient by itself. You can live near your family, see them every week, or even reside under the same roof, but physical proximity doesn’t guarantee emotional intimacy. More simply put, seeing your family regularly doesn’t mean that you share emotional closeness with them.

According to recent reports, with structured contact, the relationship remains intact, but you have a non-negotiable list of rules that need to be in place in order to avoid conflict either interpersonally or intrapsychically. You still maintain contact, but you’ve established limits around when, where, and how interactions happen. Structured contact may look like time limits when attending family gatherings, staying in a hotel instead of with your family, or limiting conversations to topics that don’t escalate. Structured contact can provide a way to maintain a connection while reducing exposure to harmful dynamics.

According to recent reports, with low contact, you may have rules about how and when to engage, or you may find yourself stuck in the same conversational loops on repeat. With low contact, you limit your exposure to family to only the minimum amount necessary to “check the box” of spending time with family. Low contact can often be a healthy way to preserve what remains available in a family, or it can be a way to mitigate the guilt and shame that come when you go full no contact.

No contact is exactly what it sounds like. There are no phone calls, emails, texts, or visits. No contact with family can run counter to deeply held beliefs about family connection, and many people agonize for years or even decades before they do it. Contrary to public opinion, going no-contact with family is not simply a trend, and ethical psychotherapy involves carefully exploring the reasons for considering estrangement rather than encouraging it without thoughtful consideration.

In a recent development, research by Reczek et al. (2023) found that periods of parent-child estrangement can be followed by renewed connection. When all parties involved are committed to the change process, even long-standing periods of estrangement may find their way toward repair. Often, when repair fails to be an option, it’s because one or more parties may be unwilling or unable to participate in the process.

It has been revealed that the decision to estrange yourself from family, regardless of which level of estrangement is appropriate, is one that is best made with informed supporters. Sometimes, estrangement isn’t even a choice, but the consequence of another family member’s rejection or withdrawal. While it can be a grief-laden experience, and one that should never be taken lightly, estrangement can also open up possibilities for loving and reciprocal relationships that do not cost you your energy or well-being.
