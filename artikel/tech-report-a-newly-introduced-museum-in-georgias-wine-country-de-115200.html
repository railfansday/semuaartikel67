Tech Report: A Newly introduced Museum in Georgia’s Wine Country Demonstrates Another Side of the Country’s Art History

Industry observers note that A long journey from Tokyo to the island of Naoshima to see art gave Jemal Inaishvili, a shipping executive and collector, an idea for how he might convince people to make a similar trek within his home country of Georgia in the South Caucasus. Unlike visiting Naoshima, which typically involves a full day of travel, the drive from the capital city of Tbilisi to Kakheti, Georgia’s eastern wine region, is around two hours, three in heavy traffic. “Of course, I cannot copy them,” he told me recently, “but I will do something similar, but based on Georgian art.”.

Sources indicate that the result is the Kvareli Foundation for Contemporary Art, which opened to the public earlier this month on land Inaishvili bought a decade ago for a small winery. The site now also holds a hotel, a sculpture park, and a red-slatted museum with a rooftop for performances and film screenings by the Tbilisi practice Midamo. This admission-free venue will stage four exhibitions a year, drawing from Inaishvili’s collection of about 1,000 works.

It has been revealed that to lead the programming, Inaishvili has hired 28-year-old Nino Sulkhanishvili. “Sometimes one’s experience will be the barrier,” he noted of the creative director, who is in her first institutional role. “Together with the foundation, she is also growing.”.

In heading up a private museum, Sulkhanishvili sees her role as helping to give Georgian artists the infrastructure to exhibit at home, like Elene Chantladze, whose solo show will open at the Kvareli Foundation in February. Such a setting, Sulkhanishvili mentioned, “is extremely crucial, especially in your own country. … I feel Georgian artists deserve much more.” Location, too, matters: “contemporary art needs to be decentralized,” she said, noting the foundation’s aim to create an art ecosystem by bringing schoolchildren to visit and training local guides. A residency for both local and international artists will launch next year.

According to recent reports, but Georgian art won’t be the foundation’s only focus after the first two years. The collection also includes work by Ukrainian, Turkish, and Armenian artists, which will be shown in the future. In the years to come, Sulkhanishvili hopes to build exchanges with neighboring and post-Soviet countries before looking further afield. “We really want to be a beacon within the region,” she remarked.

The inaugural exhibition, “Georgian Contemporary Art: Origins and Continuities” (on view through January 15), came together only six months before opening. Instead of just focusing solely on Inaishvili’s collection, Sulkhanishvili proposed a historical survey of the artists who worked outside the Soviet era’s state-sanctioned socialist realism. The show gathers 57 artists from the 1960s to the present, with roughly half the works from his collection and the rest on loan. “Even in the national museums, you don’t see everything together,” Sulkhanishvili stated of the artists on view, many of whom are still often “excluded from the [official] narrative” of this moment in Georgian history. This time around, socialist realist artists are intentionally left out.

In a recent development, irena Popiashvili, the dean of the Visual Arts, Architecture and Design School at Tbilisi’s Free University, remarked the importance of a show like this could not be understated when state museums often only focus a handful of national artists, like Georgia’s most celebrated painter Niko Pirosmani. “As an educator, it’s notable for me, for example, to refer my students [to this show] and for them to be able to see the continuation of the Georgian art history, 20th century throughout and the beginning of the 21st,” she said.

Recent findings suggest that the survey begins with the Thaw that followed Stalin’s death in 1953, when socialist realism remained doctrine but foreign magazines, like ARTnews and Art in America, trickled in. A pioneering figure of Abstract Expressionism in Georgia, Shura Bandzeladze saw a black-and-white reproduction of a Jackson Pollock in Art in America and took it as proof he was “on the right track,” according to Sulkhanishvili.

Industry observers note that the risks of deviating from socialist realism were real, though. Ushangi Khumarashvili, a set designer from Kakheti’s largest city, Telavi, clashed with his director and was sent to a psychiatric hospital. On his release, he burned his earlier work and turned to abstraction. In 1974, non-conformist painter and graphic artist Otar Chkhartishvili took part in the unapproved avant-garde Bulldozer Exhibition in Moscow, which the authorities violently broke up. In Levan Chogoshvili’s painting 1924 (ca. 1980s), a dog carries a severed human hand, an image drawn from his grandmother’s account of Bolshevik executions, an episode that official Soviet histories left out. In an officially atheist country, even a return to earlier Western art traditions and the Christian themes they drew on could be equally dangerous. In 1978, Irakli Parjiani began writing out the Gospels by hand and illustrating them in the manner of medieval manuscripts, which Popiashvili described as “a revolutionary act.”.

In a recent development, upstairs, with perestroika in the years before the collapse of the USSR, the mood turns more hopeful. “Even though there was still censorship, even though there was the Soviet Union, there was the sense of optimism,” Sulkhanishvili stated, “that something was about to happen, something was about to change.” Artists no longer worked only against the system but began building their own scene, and their canvases began to grow in scale, too. The 10th Floor group, named for its studio at the Tbilisi State Academy of Arts, later moved into a theater workshop large enough for them to realize monumental paintings. Its intellectual leader, artist and art historian Karlo Kacharava, introduced his peers to Western writers: one work here pays tribute to Allen Ginsberg. Keti Kapanadze, whom Sulkhanishvili calls Georgia’s first woman conceptual artist, plays with gender in her “Probable Need” series (1990), for which she drew over photographic self-portraits, representing herself as “Native,” “Mystery,” and “Gentleman.”.

Industry observers note that by the 1990s, war and chaos in the wake of the collapse of the USSR are answered with bitter humor. Kote Sulaberidze’s video Playing War (1994) restages the 1992–93 war in Abkhazia, a breakaway region under Russian-backed control that Georgia and most of the world regard as occupied Georgian territory, as a children’s game through a tragic yet absurd lens. Nearby, his painted portrait of former Georgian president and Soviet foreign minister Eduard Shevardnadze takes its title, A Face of Caucasian Nationality (1996), from a Russian slur.

Recent findings suggest that the exhibition’s final rooms turn inward, where no common movement or aesthetic prevails, though many of the artists are connected via their drawing on their personal histories. Vajiko Chachkhiani’s 2026 installation one wind after another (2026), for example, comprises recycled Georgian calfskin sewn around everyday objects, like the kind of ice cream sold during in his childhood. A metaphor for a period now gone, only the outer forms of these ice cream boxes remain.

Recent findings suggest that some artists try to contend with the unease of the recent past. Andro Eradze’s Nightvision, Limited Access (2021) is a short, poetic video of domesticated dogs abandoned during the pandemic and left to run loose, while his digital print Flow State (2025), of a taxidermy monkey in a hat, alludes to humans being locked in. In a sign of Georgian artists’ increasing international visibility, another of Eradze’s videos is now in the collection of the Museum of Modern Art in New York.

Sources indicate that the Kvareli Foundation opens at a tense moment in Georgia. A significant portion of Georgia’s art community joined the mass protests in 2023–24 against the “foreign influence” law, which requires NGOs and media groups that receive foreign funding to register as serving foreign interests. (Critics, who call it the “Russian law,” say it mirrors legislation Moscow has used to silence dissent and that it threatens Georgia’s bid to join the EU. The international watchdog group Human Rights Watch warned it would “marginalize and stifle critical voices.”).

Further protests followed the parliamentary elections in October 2024, which some claimed were fraudulent. International watchdogs describe a coordinated crackdown on dissent under the ruling Georgian Dream party, with activists and journalists facing detention, fines, and prosecution, and an ongoing lawsuit before the Constitutional Court that aims to ban five opposition parties, including the United National Movement, founded by former Georgian President Mikheil Saakashvili and of which Inaishvili was a member.

Sources indicate that this tense political situation is one many young artists now live abroad, where they also have better opportunities to show their work and sustain themselves as artists. That is partly why Sulkhanishvili did not want to shy away from including political work from the Soviet era with contemporary works that are less explicitly political. Still, she hopes visitors will see Georgia as a country still in flux. “I will be happy here if someone comes in and can see the line, how things were changing,” she stated.

According to recent reports, on the current state of the country, Sulkhanishvili strikes a different tone to the foundation’s founder; Inaishvili’s tenure as deputy chair of parliament from 2004 to 2008 is central to Georgian Dream’s ongoing lawsuit, which alleges that Saakashvili’s government is the root of the opposition parties’ wrongdoing that the ruling party hopes to root out. Inaishvili insists that people “don’t know me as a politician.”.

During the opening, he reported no pressure from the government to show a certain kind of art or mount exhibitions from a specific perspective. “Every government likes when this kind of project is happening,” he said, particularly when it is privately funded. He called the overall situation “quite okay” and said artists have always been in opposition.

Sulkhanishvili is warier of the political realities of state interference, saying that while overt censorship may no longer be mandated, there is still a chilling effect that impacts the kind of art that can be made and shown in Georgia. As such, she does not strategy to collaborate with state museums in order to protect the foundation’s independence. That independence rests largely on Inaishvili’s money; he has already sunk more than €25 million ($27.4 million) into the project, though funding for its continuation will be partly helped by the hotel income.

In a recent development, “We’re not attacking anyone. We’re just not asking anything from them,” Sulkhanishvili remarked, adding, “We’re not neutral in any way.” The inaugural group show’s aim to change the story of what Georgian contemporary art has been, she said, “is our statement.”.
