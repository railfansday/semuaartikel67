Curiosity rover discoveries since 2012 include an ancient lake bed, rounded stream pebbles, organic molecules and seasonal methane — 10 findings from NASA's Mars Science Laboratory mission.

It has been revealed that could Mars ever have supported microbial life? NASA built its Mars Science Laboratory mission around that single question. The mission's rover, Curiosity, was never designed to detect living organisms. Its task is narrower and more testable: judge whether the planet once offered conditions in which microbes could have survived.

Curiosity landed in Gale Crater in August 2012. The crater holds a layered mountain that NASA calls Mount Sharp, officially named Aeolis Mons. Each band of rock in that mountain preserves a slice of the environment that existed when it formed. Lower layers are older, and higher layers are younger. A rover that crosses the crater floor and climbs the mountain's lower slopes can read that record in sequence, one rock unit at a time.

Industry observers note that the first major answer came swiftly. In early 2013, the rover drilled into a mudstone that turned out to record an ancient freshwater lake. Other results added evidence of flowing streams and a lake system that lasted a long time. They also pointed to groundwater that persisted after the lakes were gone. Together they describe a planet where liquid water appeared in more than one form.

Sources indicate that the rover's chemistry lab also found organic molecules preserved in mudstone about 3 billion years old. Organic compounds are carbon-based molecules that living things use, but they also form through ordinary chemistry. Their presence shows that Martian rock can hold such material for billions of years. It does not show that life existed. A separate set of measurements tracked methane in the air above Gale Crater. The gas rises and falls with the seasons, and its source remains unexplained.

According to recent reports, curiosity also studied the thin Martian air and the radiation that reaches the planet. Measurements of argon point to an atmosphere that was once thicker and escaped to space. A radiation detector recorded conditions both on the cruise to Mars and on the surface. That data matters for planning crewed missions.

The 10 Curiosity rover discoveries below are a selection, not an official ranking. They cover water, chemistry, atmosphere and radiation. Each slide explains where the evidence came from and what it suggests about Mars' past.

Curiosity's drilling at Yellowknife Bay produced the mission's first direct evidence that ancient Mars could have supported microbial life. In March 2013, NASA announced that the Sheepbed mudstone there records a freshwater lake bed that once held mild, near-neutral water.

Recent findings suggest that the rover drilled the rock in early 2013 at a spot the team named John Klein. Yellowknife Bay lies on the floor of Gale Crater, not far from Curiosity's landing site. The rover studied the area before heading toward Mount Sharp, the mountain at the crater's center.

According to recent reports, the drill reached past the weathered outer surface of the rock. It ground material from inside the mudstone into powder. That powder went to laboratory instruments inside the rover's body. CheMin identifies minerals by passing X $TWTR-rays through powdered rock. SAM heats samples and measures the gases they release.

According to recent reports, the rock type carries its own clue. Mudstone forms from fine sediment that settles out of still or slow-moving water. On Earth, it often marks the floor of an old lake.

It has been revealed that the chemistry of the sample delivered the central result. It contained carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur and phosphorus. Those six elements are the main chemical ingredients of life as scientists know it on Earth. Carbon forms the backbone of organic molecules. Phosphorus is part of DNA and of ATP, the molecule cells use to carry energy. Sulfur appears in some amino acids.

It has been revealed that the type of water also matters for habitability. Near-neutral water is neither strongly acidic nor strongly alkaline. Fresh, mild water poses fewer chemical obstacles to life than highly acidic or very salty water.

In a recent development, that contrast sets Yellowknife Bay apart from earlier rover results. NASA's Opportunity rover had found evidence at Meridiani Planum of past water that was acidic and salty. Water like that is a harsher setting for microbes.

In a recent development, the Yellowknife Bay result does not show that life existed on Mars. It shows that this lake offered conditions in which microbes could have lived. Scientists call such an environment habitable, which is different from inhabited. The finding came less than a year after the rover landed.

Recent findings suggest that rounded pebbles cemented into an outcrop called Hottah show that water once flowed across Gale Crater on Mars. Curiosity's images of this conglomerate rock gave the mission physical evidence of an ancient streambed. The science team estimated that the water was roughly ankle-deep to hip-deep.

A conglomerate is a sedimentary rock made of gravel and smaller grains bound together by finer material. On Earth, this type of rock commonly forms in riverbeds. Currents carry stones along and drop them when the flow slows. Once cemented, the mix preserves a record of the stream that laid it down.

The shape of the stones holds the key information. Pebbles start out angular when they break off a larger rock. As water pushes them downstream, they knock against each other and against the channel bed. Over distance, that grinding wears away corners and edges. A rounded pebble has therefore traveled some way in moving water.

Recent findings suggest that the Hottah pebbles had that worn, rounded profile. The team read it as a sign of long transport by flowing water rather than a short drop down a slope. Stones of this size are also too heavy for Martian wind to move and shape. That leaves running water as the explanation.

In a recent development, the depth estimate turns the rock into a picture of an actual stream. A current roughly ankle-deep to hip-deep can roll and bounce gravel along its bed. That points to moving water. Pebbles dropped into a still pond would sink and stay put.

In a recent development, spacecraft in orbit had already photographed channels and fan-shaped deposits on Mars. Those features suggested past water when seen from above. Hottah supplied evidence at ground level. Curiosity could look at individual stones and see how water had shaped them.

According to recent reports, the result matters for the rover's broader task of judging past habitability. Liquid water is a basic requirement for life as scientists understand it. A stream that moved gravel over a distance shows liquid water once ran across the Martian surface. That made the floor of Gale Crater a credible place to search for other signs of a once-habitable environment.

Curiosity's air measurements show that Mars has lost a large share of the atmosphere it once had. The rover's SAM instrument found light argon-36 depleted relative to heavier argon-38, a pattern that fits a thicker early atmosphere escaping to space.

The result was published in 2013. SAM stands for Sample Analysis at Mars. It is the largest instrument on the rover and sits inside the rover's body. The suite combines a mass spectrometer, a gas chromatograph and a tunable laser spectrometer. The three instruments can analyze gas drawn straight from the Martian air, as well as vapors released when rock and soil samples are heated.

Industry observers note that argon is a useful tracer for this question. It is a noble gas. It does not bond with rocks, ice or other gases. Chemistry cannot pull it out of the air or lock it into minerals. A shift in the balance between its isotopes therefore points to physical loss rather than chemical change.

In a recent development, isotopes of the same element behave alike chemically but differ in mass. Argon-36 has two fewer neutrons than argon-38. That makes it the lighter of the two. Processes that strip gas from the top of an atmosphere remove lighter atoms more easily than heavier ones. Over long periods, that preference leaves the remaining air relatively richer in the heavy form.

That is the signature SAM recorded. The shortage of argon-36 indicates that a substantial amount of gas left the planet. Gas that had stayed in place would have kept its original isotope balance.

Sources indicate that the finding bears on the planet's early climate. Liquid water is hard to keep stable at the surface under the thin, cold atmosphere Mars has today. A thicker envelope of gas would have made a wetter climate easier to sustain.

According to recent reports, the argon data also frame the present atmosphere as a remnant of a larger one. An isotope ratio records the cumulative result of escape. On its own, it does not show how fast that escape happened.

It has been revealed that curiosity's Radiation Assessment Detector, known as RAD, recorded radiation levels during the cruise to Mars and again on the Martian surface. Its cruise results appeared in Science in May 2013.

Sources indicate that those cruise readings came from inside the Mars Science Laboratory spacecraft as it crossed interplanetary space. That placement matters for human spaceflight. Astronauts bound for Mars would also make the journey inside a spacecraft, not exposed in open space.

In a recent development, RAD is a compact particle detector. It identifies charged particles such as protons and heavier atomic nuclei. It also registers neutral radiation, including neutrons and gamma rays. Sorting particles by type and energy lets scientists estimate how much radiation human tissue would absorb.

Recent findings suggest that two main sources produce the radiation RAD tracks. Galactic cosmic rays are high-energy particles that originate outside the solar system and arrive at a fairly steady rate. Solar energetic particles come from the sun, often in bursts tied to flares and coronal mass ejections.

On Earth, a thick atmosphere and a global magnetic field absorb or deflect much of this radiation before it reaches the ground. Mars lacks that protection on the same scale. Its atmosphere is far thinner than Earth's, and the planet has no global magnetic field. Particles reach the Martian surface with less filtering. Some strike the soil and air and generate secondary radiation.

Industry observers note that after Curiosity landed in Gale Crater, RAD kept operating on the surface. Those readings describe the conditions a surface crew or long-lived equipment at the crater would encounter.

The measurements feed into planning for crewed missions. Mission designers need measured values to judge how much shielding a spacecraft requires and how long a crew can remain in deep space. The same data inform how surface habitats on Mars should be built. Direct measurements also give engineers a check on the computer models used to estimate exposure.

RAD supports Curiosity's science goals as well. Radiation reaching the surface can break down organic molecules in rock and soil over long periods. Knowing the radiation level helps scientists judge where preserved organic material is most likely to survive. It also helps them decide which drill samples are most worth analyzing.

Recent findings suggest that curiosity's analysis of the Cumberland drill sample at Yellowknife Bay revealed that wind erosion exposed the rock about 78 million years ago. That estimate carries an uncertainty of about 30 million years. The result appeared in Science in 2014.

According to recent reports, that margin places the exposure somewhere between roughly 48 million and 108 million years ago. Mars formed about 4.5 billion years ago. Measured against that span, the Cumberland surface was uncovered recently in geological terms.

Cosmic-ray exposure dating relies on a steady physical process. High-energy particles from space strike rock and split some of the atoms near its surface. These collisions produce particular isotopes that accumulate in the stone over time. The longer a rock sits close to the surface, the more of those isotopes it holds.

The particles reach only the upper few meters of rock. Material buried deeper stays largely shielded. As a result, the exposure clock effectively starts when erosion strips away the layers above.

Mars offers little protection from these particles. The planet has a thin atmosphere and no global magnetic field. On Earth, a thick atmosphere and a magnetic field deflect or absorb much of the incoming flux before it reaches the ground.

The Cumberland result identifies wind as the force doing the uncovering at Gale Crater. Liquid water does not flow across the Martian surface today. Wind-driven sand wears down exposed outcrops over long periods. It removes overlying rock and leaves deeper layers open to the sky.

Industry observers note that that process matters for the search for ancient chemistry. Radiation breaks down organic molecules over time. Rock that has sat at the surface longest has absorbed the most of that damage. A surface uncovered more recently has had less time to accumulate it.

Sources indicate that the 2014 finding suggests that erosion on Mars can expose fresh rock for study. That gives scientists a way to choose where to drill. Surfaces where wind has removed material recently may hold chemistry less altered by radiation.

The Cumberland measurement also highlighted that a rover could date a surface with instruments carried to Mars. Scientists did not need to return a sample to a laboratory on Earth to get the age.

Mineral veins in the Kimberley area of Gale Crater contain high levels of manganese, according to measurements from Curiosity's ChemCam instrument. Manganese oxides generally form in strongly oxidizing conditions. That points to more oxygen in the ancient Martian atmosphere or water than scientists had expected.

In a recent development, the result was reported around 2014. It came from a part of the rover's route where fractures in the bedrock had filled with minerals left behind by fluids. Those veins formed after the surrounding rock did. They record water that moved through the ground later in the crater's history.

ChemCam sits on the rover's mast. It fires laser pulses at a rock target several meters away and vaporizes a tiny spot on its surface. A spectrometer then reads the light from the resulting plasma to identify the elements present. This method, called laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy, lets the team survey many targets without driving up to each one or drilling.

Manganese is useful as a clue because of its chemistry. The element dissolves readily in water when little oxygen is available. It tends to settle out as solid oxide minerals when a strong oxidant strips electrons from it. Concentrated manganese in vein material suggests the fluids that carried it met conditions capable of driving that reaction.

Sources indicate that on Earth, manganese oxide deposits became common in the rock record after oxygen built up in the atmosphere. That history is one reason geologists treat these minerals as markers of oxidizing environments.

According to recent reports, the Kimberley result does not pin down where the oxidizing power came from. It could have come from oxygen in the early atmosphere or from oxidants dissolved in the water. Either source would mean early Mars was less oxygen-poor than many models had assumed.

The degree of oxidation also matters for habitability studies. It shapes which chemical reactions were possible in the water that once moved through Gale Crater's rocks.

The layered mudstones at Pahrump Hills, at the base of Mount Sharp, are deposits left by an ancient lake. A 2015 Science paper led by John Grotzinger concluded that the lake likely persisted on the scale of millions of years.

Pahrump Hills is an outcrop at the foot of Mount Sharp, the layered mountain inside Gale Crater on Mars. NASA's Curiosity rover studied it as the rover began working on the mountain's lowest slopes.

Industry observers note that mudstone is rock built from very fine particles of sediment. Grains that small settle only where water is calm. In a fast-moving current, they stay suspended and are carried away. Fine-grained mudstone therefore points to standing water, such as a lake.

Industry observers note that the layering at Pahrump Hills adds a second clue. Each layer marks a separate episode of sediment settling to the bottom of the water. A thick stack of such layers cannot form all at once. Grotzinger's team described the rocks there as lake deposits, laid down one on top of another.

According to recent reports, duration is what separates this result from other signs of water at Gale Crater. A single wet layer could record a brief flood or a pond that dried up quickly. A thick stack of lake sediments points to water that stayed in place. The team concluded that this lake was not a passing event.

That timescale bears directly on the mission's central question: whether Mars once had conditions that could support microbial life. A brief wet spell offers only a short window for stable, watery conditions. A lake lasting on the order of millions of years keeps that window open far longer. It describes standing water present over a long stretch of Martian history, not a fleeting puddle.

Recent findings suggest that the finding does not show that anything lived in the lake. It establishes that a body of standing water existed at the foot of Mount Sharp and that it endured.

Curiosity found boron in calcium sulfate mineral veins at Gale Crater, a result the rover's science team reported in September 2017. The discovery suggests that water continued to circulate underground after the crater's lakes dried up, and that this groundwater could have been habitable.

The veins appear as pale streaks that cut through the surrounding bedrock. They form when water seeps into cracks in rock that has already hardened. Minerals dissolved in that water settle out and fill the fractures. Because the veins slice across older layers, they record a later chapter in the crater's history.

Recent findings suggest that calcium sulfate is the mineral group that includes gypsum and anhydrite. On Earth, it commonly forms where water evaporates or where groundwater carries dissolved salts through rock. Boron locked inside these deposits on Mars links the element to water circulating underground, not to open water at the surface.

ChemCam, the rover's laser instrument, made the detection. It fires a laser at rock targets from several meters away and vaporizes a tiny spot. A spectrometer reads the light from that flash to identify the elements present. The technique lets the rover check thin features such as veins without drilling into them.

Industry observers note that boron draws attention because of where it tends to collect on Earth. It is often tied to evaporated lake beds, where standing water dried out and left concentrated minerals behind. Its presence in Martian veins that formed after the lake sediments hardened places that kind of water chemistry in a later, subsurface setting.

Recent findings suggest that the timing bears on habitability at the site. Lakes at the surface offer one window in which microbes might have lived. Groundwater that persisted afterward would extend that window into a later period, beneath the surface rather than on it.

Industry observers note that boron also appears in research on the origin of life. Borate minerals are one proposed way to stabilize ribose, a sugar that forms part of RNA. The Gale Crater result does not show that such chemistry took place on Mars. It does not show that life existed there either. It shows that an element relevant to that question was present in the planet's ancient groundwater.

Recent findings suggest that curiosity found organic molecules preserved in 3-billion-year-old mudstone from Gale Crater's Murray formation. A June 2018 paper in Science, with Jennifer Eigenbrode as lead author, reported the compounds, which include thiophenes, benzene and toluene.

Organic molecules are carbon-based compounds, typically built from carbon bonded to hydrogen and often to other elements. They are the chemical building blocks of every known living thing. They also form without any biology at all. Meteorites carry them, and nonbiological chemical reactions can produce them too.

Sources indicate that that dual origin matters when reading the result. The paper reports organic compounds in the rock. It does not report life. The molecules on their own do not show whether biology had any role in making them.

In a recent development, each named compound has a distinct structure. Thiophenes are ring-shaped molecules that contain sulfur. Benzene and toluene are aromatic hydrocarbons, each built around a ring of six carbon atoms. Toluene is benzene with a methyl group attached.

Mudstone is a fine-grained sedimentary rock. It forms when silt and clay particles settle out of calm water and later harden into stone. On Earth, fine-grained sediments of this kind are a common place to find preserved organic matter.

Curiosity detected the compounds with its Sample Analysis at Mars instrument suite, known as SAM. The rover drills into rock and delivers the powder to SAM, which heats it in an oven. The released gases are then separated and identified by their mass.

Industry observers note that survival over time is a central part of the result. Mars lacks a global magnetic field and has only a thin atmosphere. Radiation that reaches the surface can break apart organic molecules over long periods. The Murray formation samples show that carbon-bearing material in rock this old can still be detected.

For later missions, the finding identifies ancient Martian mudstone as rock that can hold organic material within reach of a rover's onboard laboratory.

Methane in the air above Gale Crater rises and falls with the Martian seasons. A June 2018 paper in Science, led by Chris Webster, reported that the gas peaks in late northern summer and drops to lower levels in winter.

It has been revealed that the measurements came from SAM, the Sample Analysis at Mars instrument suite inside the rover. One of its components, the Tunable Laser Spectrometer, draws in Martian air. It then measures how strongly the gas absorbs laser light at specific wavelengths. Methane absorbs light in a distinctive pattern. That pattern lets the instrument pick it out from the far more abundant gases around it.

It has been revealed that the Martian atmosphere is thin and made mostly of carbon dioxide. Methane is only a trace component. The spectrometer is tuned to the methane molecule so it can register such small amounts.

According to recent reports, A repeating seasonal cycle is a different kind of result from a one-off reading. A single detection can leave open questions about contamination or instrument error. A pattern that tracks the Martian calendar suggests that some process on Mars adds or removes the gas on a regular schedule.

According to recent reports, methane does not last indefinitely in a planetary atmosphere. Ultraviolet sunlight and other chemical reactions break the molecule apart over time. For the gas to remain detectable, something has to keep supplying it.

In a recent development, the source of the Gale Crater methane remains unexplained. It could be geological or biological. The seasonal pattern alone does not decide between those possibilities.

On Earth, most methane comes from living things, including microbes. That link is one reason the gas draws attention in the search for life on Mars. Methane can also form without any life at all. Water reacting with iron-bearing rock, a process called serpentinization, releases hydrogen that can combine with carbon to make methane. Gas formed long ago could also be trapped underground and seep out later.

In a recent development, the methane cycle extends Curiosity's work from ancient rock into the present-day air. Most of the rover's results describe conditions on Mars billions of years ago. A gas that changes with the seasons today gives scientists a current process to track alongside the planet's older geological record.
