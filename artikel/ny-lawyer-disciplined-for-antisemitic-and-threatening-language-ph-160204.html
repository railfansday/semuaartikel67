Industry observers note that from a N.Y. appellate decision Thursday in Att'y Grievance Comm. v. Fernich (Judges Sallie Manzanet-Daniels, Tanya R. Kennedy, Barbara R. Kapnick, Kelly O'Neill Levy, and Matthew Parker-Raso); the respondent, Marc Fernich, is a prominent Latest York criminal defense lawyer, who represented, among others, Jeffrey Epstein, John Gotti, and Joaquin Guzman (drug cartel leader El Chapo):.

Sources indicate that the motion arises from an altercation between respondent and participants in a demonstration outside of the federal courthouse for the Southern District of Latest York on September 29, 2025. On May 27, 2026, the AGC served respondent with a notice and petition of charges alleging one charge of professional misconduct under Rules of Professional Misconduct (22 NYCRR 1200.0) rule 8.4(h), which prohibits conduct that adversely reflects on the lawyer's fitness as a lawyer. The parties have submitted a stipulation of facts, respondent's affidavit, and materials addressing aggravation and mitigation. Respondent admits to the stipulated misconduct, acknowledges the consequences of consenting to discipline, and agrees to a public censure.

Sources indicate that on September 29, 2025, respondent, then 61 years old, was leaving a meeting at the Latest York County District Attorney's Office and was walking toward the Brooklyn Bridge. On Centre Street, he came upon a group of demonstrators in support of Palestine outside of the federal courthouse. Some demonstrators were singing and chanting, one played a guitar, and others held a banner reading "Jewish Artists for Palestine.".

Respondent attempted to make his way through a circle of demonstrators, and admits that physical contact occurred as he attempted to proceed. Respondent grabbed a demonstrator's arm, pushed and grabbed others while moving through the circle, and engaged in heated verbal confrontation. The cellphone recordings of the incident also show respondent holding a demonstrator from behind with his arms around the person's waist, with the demonstrator shouting at him to remove his arms, respondent pushing and grabbing others, and respondent holding another demonstrator from behind with his arms around the individual's neck.

Among other things, respondent's threatening and antisemitic remarks included "Back off me, Kapo, Kike, Jew Boy!," "You fucking animal, you're worse than a fucking Muslim!," "You should have been aborted," and "You should have been thrown into an oven, I'd throw you in if I could!" He also said that the demonstrators' grandparents should have died in the Holocaust, as well as other inflammatory remarks invoking violence. Respondent remained at the demonstration for approximately one hour, until it ended.

At his deposition, respondent maintained that the video depiction of the incident was a compilation of cellphone recordings made by several individuals and that they were out of sequence and out of context. He asserted that the confrontation had not been one-sided; demonstrators had been linking arms, blocking his way, pushing back, and squeezing his genitals.

He also maintained that the recordings had purposefully omitted much of the physical contact and extreme anti-Israel and anti-Zionist rhetoric directed at him. Nonetheless, he recognized that the demonstrators had been exercising their constitutional rights to assemble and engage in free speech, and that his words and actions could not be justified. He was deeply ashamed of his conduct and expressed remorse.

Sources indicate that the parties have stipulated that respondent's misconduct is aggravated by his substantial experience in the practice of law. They have also stipulated to considerable mitigation. Respondent has no prior disciplinary history. The incident was unrelated to his representation of a client or the practice of law, and did not disrupt a court proceeding. Respondent self-reported his misconduct to the AGC through counsel, cooperated with the AGC investigation, voluntarily appeared for examination, accepted responsibility, and expressed remorse and embarrassment for his actions. Fifteen character references described respondent as being well-regarded in the community and having character traits of honesty, integrity, and trustworthiness.

The record further reflects that respondent was experiencing significant medical concerns at the time of the incident. He was recovering from a recent back surgery and experiencing significant pain, and had undergone emergency surgery for retinal tears shortly beforehand. He was also undergoing psychiatric treatment and taking medication for depression, anxiety, and ADHD. A report submitted by respondent's treating psychiatrist explains how the pain, emotional distress, and psychiatric symptoms respondent was experiencing may have contributed to his impaired judgment and agitation and, while respondent had been under his care for 13 years, he would never have expected him to engage in the kind of "angry and out-of-control conduct that he engaged in outside the Courthouse." Respondent, who has family in Israel, also maintains that the October 7, 2023 attacks in Israel affected him deeply and that his memory of those attacks caused his anger to boil over.

Under 22 NYCRR 1240.8(a)(5), the Court may resolve a disciplinary matter by consent where the submissions establish an admitted violation and an appropriate agreed-upon sanction. In support of the proposal for a public censure, the parties cite decisions imposing a censure for similarly offensive, abusive, and discriminatory remarks and other isolated misconduct (see e.g. Matter of Melogy, 233 AD3d 135 [1st Dept 2024]; Matter of Mukambetova, 204 AD3d 158 [1st Dept 2022]; Matter of Schlossberg, 192 AD3d 8 [1st Dept 2020]).

Respondent's use of antisemitic and threatening language, together with his physical aggression towards demonstrators, warrants public discipline. At the same time, the absence of a disciplinary history, the isolated nature of the incident, the medical and psychological circumstances, his cooperation, and his acceptance of responsibility supports the sanction jointly proposed. Public censure appropriately expresses the profession's condemnation of the relevant conduct while also recognizing the substantial mitigation.

Accordingly, the parties' joint motion for discipline by consent pursuant to 22 NYCRR 1240.8(a)(5) should be granted, … and respondent publicly censured….
