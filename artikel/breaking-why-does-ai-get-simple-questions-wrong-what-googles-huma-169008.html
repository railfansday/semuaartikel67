Breaking: Why Does AI Get Simple Questions Wrong? What Google's Humanity's First Exam Shows

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that you ask an AI model to name the color of the third letter in each word on a neon sign. It gets it right. You ask again, and it slips by one letter. Nothing broke.

The model could do the task. It just did not do it every time. Google DeepMind built a test for exactly that gap, and the results show how far "usually right" can sit from "reliably right.".

Most AI benchmarks ask a model to solve a hard problem once. If it passes, it scores. Google DeepMind's Humanity's First Exam (HFE), released on November 24, 2026, asks something different. Can a model solve an easy task, and slight rewrites of it, every single time?

It has been revealed that the prompts are elementary on purpose. They cover counting objects, naming colors, tracking where something moved, basic arithmetic, strict formatting rules and spotting false premises. Humans solve them in about a minute.

One public example: "List the color of the third letter of each word in the neon sign in order, separated by a semicolon." The answer is green; yellow; white; blue; blue.

The paper calls the difference the Family Reliability Gap. One slip anywhere in the 16 answers fails the whole family.

Recent findings suggest that the top five models score 96% to 98% on a single try and 100% on family pass@4. Their family pass⁴ averages only 85.8%. Across all 18 models, the gap runs from 7.6 to 80.1 percentage points.

Recent findings suggest that the bottom row is the sharpest case. That model gets 68.7% of single tries right, yet clears only 18.6% of question families every time. Its family pass@4 is 98.8%, so it could solve nearly every family at least once.

The math is simple. Say a model is right 90% of the time on each answer, and the answers are independent. Getting 16 in a row right happens about 18.5% of the time (0.9 to the 16th power). That is a rough illustration, not the paper's calculation, but it highlights how small error rates compound.

Sources indicate that the paper's own example makes the same point. One model trails the leader by 6.6 points on single tries and by 29.4 points on family pass⁴. On average, about half the shortfall comes from prompts a model always fails. The other half comes from flakiness: right on some runs, wrong on others.

Sources indicate that humans and models also struggle in opposite places. People were least accurate on math and logic seeds. Models were strongest there and weakest on counting and reading text inside images.

Traders now hand AI tools real jobs: reading a contract, summarizing a token page, checking a wallet address in a screenshot, or running a bot. In those jobs, one wrong character or one miscounted number is the whole failure. A model that is right 19 times out of 20 will still be wrong on the 20th, and a signed transaction cannot be undone.

Sources indicate that 1. Ask twice. Run the same question more than once. If the answers differ, treat both as unconfirmed.

Recent findings suggest that 2. Check the visual parts yourself. Counts, colors, positions and text read from an image are where models slip most. Compare addresses and numbers character by character against the original.

Recent findings suggest that 3. Match the effort to the task. More reasoning helps with text and math. It helps little with images, so do not assume a longer "thinking" mode makes a screenshot read safe.

According to recent reports, 4. Keep a human on irreversible steps. Sending funds, signing transactions and changing settings should not rest on a single AI answer.
