Surveillance pricing uses our personal data to make us pay more

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

“It costs what it costs” is a mindset I’ve found useful when encountering a sizable, but highly necessary or desired, expense. The sentiment encourages me to accept the price because that’s the accepted value of the gadget, app, or service that I really need or want.

Recent findings suggest that but with surveillance pricing, that comfort is no longer a given. I could end up paying more for my TV because of where I am or more for a software subscription because of my age.

Surveillance pricing leverages data collected from our web history, smart devices, and apps with the goal of getting us to pay more. That obfuscates the value of products, challenges smart shopping and budgets, and makes it harder for people to get products and services at a fair price.

Surveillance pricing, according to the nonprofit Electronic Privacy Information Center (EPIC), is an algorithmic pricing practice that charges individuals or groups of individuals different prices “for the same goods or services.”.

“The goal of surveillance pricing is to infer the highest price that the customer is willing to pay, based on consumers’ personal data and other market data,” EPIC explains.

Instacart previously practiced surveillance pricing, also called personalized pricing. In 2025, Consumer Reports found that, “based on how much Instacart states the typical household of four spends on groceries, the average price variations observed could translate into a cost swing of about $1,200 per year.” Instacart has since removed its surveillance pricing tools.

Dynamic pricing, or surge pricing, differs from surveillance pricing in that, instead of relying on consumer data, it uses changing market trends, like supply and demand, inventory levels, and competitor pricing, to set prices.

While dynamic pricing may charge you more for an Uber ride when it’s very busy, surveillance pricing can feel less reasonable.

For instance, Target’s app was previously accused of using location data to charge customers $100 more for a TV if they bought it while standing inside a Target store. At the time, Target said that it would match lower prices presented by customers, but many customers wouldn’t think to look for pricing discrepancies. In another instance from 2012, Orbitz was found to more prominently feature expensive hotels to people using macOS. Companies often dispute whether their practices truly qualify as surveillance pricing. But these examples all reflect companies using data about their customers to influence the prices they show them.

It has been revealed that zipRecruiter was one of the first companies to leverage surveillance pricing as we think of it today. In 2018, its profits increased 84 percent by using surveillance pricing versus charging every customer $99. Surveillance pricing is still somewhat nascent, but its potential lucrativeness and the growing availability of user data suggest it may become more common.

Other technological advancements, including advanced machine-learning methods, have further facilitated the growth of personalized pricing.

In 2025, the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) noted at least 250 companies used surveillance pricing.

According to a 2025 statement from then-FTC Chair Lina M. Khan, “retailers frequently use people’s personal information to set targeted, tailored prices for goods and services—from a person’s location and demographics, down to their mouse movements on a webpage.”.

It has been revealed that surveillance pricing has become more widespread as food delivery platforms, rideshare services, rental housing providers, and other businesses have gained access to the technology and data needed to implement it. Third-party data brokers, advertising and analytics companies, and credit providers make it easy for organizations to get the information needed to build customer profiles. The growth of web browsing, the Internet of Things, and user tracking have created data-rich digital lives that are simple for retailers to analyze.

Additionally, the rise of e-commerce means even more customer data is available, which makes it easier to charge customers different prices than a physical store could.

Recent findings suggest that surveillance pricing makes it hard for shoppers to get the best, or even fair, prices and makes it difficult to assess the true value of products. There are also broader implications that go beyond how consumers feel about it.

A 2025 report from the American Economic Liberties Project, “Prohibiting Surveillance Prices and Wages” (PDF), says surveillance pricing relies on biased algorithms:.

Recent findings suggest that algorithms often rely on data that reflect historical and ongoing biases, embedding these prejudices into wage- and price-setting systems and perpetuating them. For example, as studies of rideshare firm platforms have shown, riders traveling to predominantly non-white neighborhoods are often charged higher prices,a pattern that mirrors historical residential segregation. And … college test-prep services have sometimes been priced over $1,000 more in zip codes with large Asian populations.

According to recent reports, dating app Tinder, for example, was previously reported to use surveillance pricing to charge older users more money for subscriptions. Tinder denied that it used social or demographic data for pricing, agreed to pay $60.5 million to settle a class-action lawsuit about the charge, and notes it stopped charging older users more.

Surveillance pricing also incentivizes data collectors to gather increasingly detailed, potentially intrusive, consumer data, which could be used in harmful or exploitative ways.

Industry observers note that as EPIC states: “Consumers are categorized by data brokers that identify or infer characteristics such as age, demographics, shopping habits, lifestyle, or economic status. Such personal data collected for commercial purposes also often ends up in the hands of third parties, including law enforcement agencies, and is used for unrelated purposes.”.

In a recent development, some critics of surveillance pricing argue for legislation that bans the practice or sets parameters about how and when it’s used. However, some argue that legislation could hurt consumers, since surveillance pricing can also show customers lower prices.

Barring legislation, there are things that companies can do to better serve shoppers in this regard. One approach is to disclose when they are using personalized pricing, similar to when Uber alerts consumers that surge pricing is in effect.

Speaking of surge pricing, prioritizing dynamic pricing over surveillance pricing would allow organizations to continue leveraging data and real-time market conditions to maximize revenue while reducing the risk of biased or unfair pricing.

Sources indicate that surveillance pricing often occurs surreptitiously, so consumers, lawmakers, and retailers will likely continue to be challenged by the practice. Consumers can strive to protect themselves somewhat by setting their own standards for what they pay when possible. For instance, when I buy a new TV, I have an idea of how much I am willing or able to spend before I start shopping. If the prices don’t come close to aligning with my needs, I reassess my options, which may include using an aging gadget longer.

Shoppers should no longer assume that “it costs what it costs.” By setting personal spending limits and deciding in advance what you’re willing to pay, you can avoid paying more for a gadget, app, or service than is fair.
