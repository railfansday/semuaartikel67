What does the Anthropic IPO mean for your 401(k)?

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

You may have no interest in investing in AI companies, and you may even suspect that the AI boom is a bubble about to pop. But you could already have a stake in one of the industry’s biggest players anyway: Anthropic, which went public today, may be in your retirement account or about to show up there. Some mutual funds, including American Funds Growth Fund of America (AGTHX) and Fidelity Select Technology (FSPTX), hold substantial equity stakes in Anthropic. And some index funds will also soon acquire shares, after recently changing their rules to make it easier for massive companies to join shortly after going public. Previously, companies often had to wait months or a year to be included in major indexes, in part because of concerns about stock volatility. Earlier this year, ahead of the SpaceX IPO, Nasdaq shortened its waiting period from three months to 15 trading days for qualified mega-IPOs. Nasdaq argued that companies as large as SpaceX and Anthropic shouldn’t have to wait so long. FTSE Russell has similarly shortened its waiting period to just five trading days, though inclusion depends on how many shares are available for public trading. Morningstar already had a short five-day waiting period, but relaxed its rules about available shares. The S&P 500, meanwhile, still requires companies to wait 12 months and turn a profit before joining—two hurdles Anthropic has yet to clear. When Anthropic is included in a retirement portfolio, it won’t have a large weight initially, Rodney Comegys, chief investment officer at Vanguard Capital Management and head of global equity at Vanguard, tells Fast Manufacturer. That’s because a relatively small portion of the company’s shares will be available to public investors to begin with. Vanguard has estimated that Anthropic will represent 0.05%–0.15% of its total market index fund. If you have $500,000 in that fund, that might mean $250 to $750 worth of indirect exposure to Anthropic. How to find Anthropic in your 401(k) To know if your 401(k) or an index fund you own will include Anthropic, you’ll have to dig into the details. “Most people don’t look at their index funds, or anything that their 401(k) is invested in,” says John Polonis, a former Wall Street lawyer who now writes about finance. Even if you’ve chosen not to invest in the Nasdaq-100, for example, the stock could still be hiding somewhere else. Other funds, including those that invest in the largest publicly traded companies, could also hold Anthropic shares. “There may be ways that you are getting exposure,” Polonis says, “and if you don’t want any exposure at all, you really have to do a lot of diligence to kind of screen that.” To check your own portfolio, log into your 401(k) account. If you have actively managed mutual funds, look up each fund’s portfolio holdings and search for Anthropic. If you have index funds, check to see which indexes they track. The indexes that will soon acquire Anthropic stock include Russell 1000, Russell 3000, Nasdaq-100, Morningstar U.S., FTSE Global All Cap, and MSCI USA. Because companies like Alphabet and Amazon also own large chunks of Anthropic stock, index funds also have exposure through those companies. The risk for passive investors is low at this point because exposure is limited. But exposure is likely to grow over time. And some critics argue that because Anthropic is still at an early stage, and not yet profitable, it shouldn’t be part of retirement accounts. Jonathan Westin, executive director of Climate Organizing Hub and part of a group called Stop Funding Billionaires that protested in front of Anthropic’s offices in advance of the IPO, argues that the company’s early investors stand to reap enormous profits while ordinary investors shoulder the risk if the AI boom collapses. “They’re all just trying to get paid out and get paid out now before the collapse,” he says. “And then the real people that are left picking up the pieces are our 401(k)s and pension funds that are going to drop.” There’s a broader issue: major indexes are supposed to reflect the market, not shape it. But fast-tracking stocks like Anthropic arguably influences the market, says Michael Green, an investor and financial market researcher who is CEO of Tier1 Alpha Asset Management. When a fresh company is added to a major index and index funds automatically have to buy it, that drives up the company’s stock price. A recent study calculated that fast-tracked IPOs have a 5% price premium over those added to indexes more slowly. Index funds are stuck paying prices that are inflated by their own demand. Fast-tracked companies can also raise 6.2% more capital. “The far more vital question is whether or not is it appropriate to use a platform like an index fund to facilitate a particular class of IPO that largely serves to enrich a subset of the population on the back of a government-mandated retirement system,” Green says. And therein lies the broader problem: Green argues that the system could give the biggest companies a disproportionate advantage over smaller competitors, reinforcing an economy increasingly dominated by massive business.
