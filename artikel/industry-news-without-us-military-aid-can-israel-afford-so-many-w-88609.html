Recent findings suggest that although it has had its ups and downs over the years, Israel long enjoined broad bipartisan support in Congress and in public opinion, certainly in comparison with the Palestinians, Israel’s main rival in the competition for American’s affections. That bipartisan consensus began to unravel 15 years ago under Netanyahu, who preferred the Republican Party and Christian evangelicals over Democrats and even the American Jewish community, whose liberal politics made it increasingly uncomfortable with Israeli policies.

Netanyahu’s alliance with the American right looked solid, but it crumbled in the wake of Hamas’s attack three years ago. The initial sympathy for Israel evaporated rapidly as its offensive in Gaza proved unusually brutal and continued long after its military justification had run out. Not only did remaining support in the Democratic Party collapse, but Israel began losing the GOP, as well—starting at the far-right fringe of the MAGA movement and rapidly moving to the center, especially among younger Republicans. Israel’s role in convincing Trump to attack Iran earlier this year made it even more enemies across the political spectrum, as did the settler violence against Palestinians in the West Bank, which even succeeded in alienating many of Israel’s Christian supporters. A day after the midterm elections, U.S. Vice President JD Vance asserted that one of the four reasons Trump voters turned their backs on the GOP was that they wanted to “change our relationship to Israel.” Never one to mince words, Trump cited a “great dislike” for Israel as one reason for the Republicans’ defeat.

Industry observers note that in both parties, the $3.8 billion in annual U.S. aid had served as a flash point for anti-Israel sentiment. Progressives saw it as taxpayer dollars going to fund what they called “genocide” in Gaza; for MAGA Republicans, it violated the principals of “America first” and of U.S. allies standing on their own two feet. Netanyahu and his religious-right government shrugged off these dangerous developments. The far-right ideology that drives his government all but precluded policy changes that might have stemmed the growing hostility.

It has been revealed that in purely money terms, the loss of U.S. aid isn’t critical for Israel. The annual amount that Israel gets hasn’t changed much over the years, but the Israeli economy has grown more than 20-fold. Thus, 40 years ago, aid was equal to about 11 percent of Israeli GDP; today, it is about 0.6 percent. Moreover, under the current 10-year framework, nearly all the aid money is today spent in the United States, so if anything, it harms the Israeli arms industry by reserving a big chunk of Israeli procurement contracts for U.S. makers. However, that isn’t the whole story. Aid accounted for about a fifth of the Israeli defense budget before Oct. 7. If it is 10 percent or less today, then that’s only because Israeli military spending has since tripled.

According to recent reports, it is a testament to the strength of the Israeli economy and to the government’s strong finances (not to mention a vast increase in U.S. financial aid at the height of the Gaza war) that the country has been able to sustain such a vast increase, which, at close to 8 percent of GDP, is among the highest in the world. But the situation cannot continue indefinitely. The shock of the Hamas attack has left Israel feeling vulnerable, if for no other reason than that the conflicts with Iran, Hezbollah, and Hamas have not ended in a decisive victory. Netanyahu therefore wants to remain at elevated levels for the next decade. That involves not only more people in uniform than before Oct. 7, but a massive arms buildup that will saddle the economy with one of the world’s highest defense burdens for years to come. The loss of U.S. aid will mean that even more of the cost will fall on Israeli taxpayers than the government had planned on.

As it became evident over the past year that Trump wanted to gradually end the aid program (at the same time that European countries were increasingly refusing to sell Israel military gear and the risk was growing that U.S. arms purchases would be constrained by an increasingly critical Congress), Netanyahu began marketing the emerging threat as an opportunity for Israel to pursue a more independent military course.

That independence, however, will come at a cost, entailing the creation of an economy geared more heavily than ever to arms production. Turning Israel into what the prime minister has called a “super Sparta” will not only force the economy to produce the kind of arms and components that it could get more cheaply abroad, but it won’t seriously reduce Israeli dependence. Israel is a major producer of cutting-edge weaponry, but its industrial base is too small to make everything it needs, whether it’s a fighter jet or all of the thousands of tiny components that go into one.

Recent findings suggest that netanyahu’s Sparta is no substitute for Israel maintaining strong relations with the United States and its other partners. But, far from making Israel more independent, Netanyahu has done the reverse. U.S. financial aid and ammunition were critical for sustaining what turned out to be a lengthy war and occupation in the Gaza Strip, and the two assaults on Iran would not have been possible without direct U.S. military intervention. Netanyahu’s ambitions to remake the Middle East are too major for Israel to undertake alone.

According to recent reports, in addition, the United States is Israel’s biggest trade partner, is by far its main source of foreign investment, and provides Israel with diplomatic cover in the United Nations. And, if that is not enough, Israel has no backup alliances to compensate for deteriorating ties with the United States. Quite to the contrary, the prime minister has presided over a severe deterioration in relations with Europe to the point that Israel is contending with increasingly severe sanctions.

So, what lies ahead for bilateral ties? The end of aid marks a transition from relations predicated on broad domestic support in the United States for Israel—something akin to friendship—to a more transactional one based on mutual interests and the strategic value that each side brings to the table. That Israel will continue to need the United States in this new era is indisputable. The question is what Israel offers the United States, because it will now have to justify itself as an ally.

Recent findings suggest that it is safe to say that intelligence-sharing and joint weapons development will remain valuable to Washington. But Israel’s previously important role as a force for regional stability has diminished. That is most evident in Syria, Lebanon, and Gaza, where, rather than helping Washington to bring at least a semblance of order and economic development, Israel has insisted on continuing to deploy troops in their respective territories and launch periodic attacks. It has rejected even tentative overtures by Syria and Lebanon to establish security arrangements and has blocked Trump’s effort to begin Gaza reconstruction. The White House has shown remarkable patience in the face of Israeli defiance, but that may no longer be the case now.

Industry observers note that and if the United States does finally disengage from the Middle East—an increasingly likely prospect in the wake of the Iran war debacle and the U.S. midterm elections—Israel may become just another second-tier power of little interest to Washington and with no influence there. That is a change that Israel can ill-afford to let happen.

Industry observers note that david E. Rosenberg is the economics editor and a columnist for the English edition of Haaretz and the author of Israel’s Technology Economy.
