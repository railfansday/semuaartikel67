A New York judge has refused to throw out a lawsuit over a 15th-century Fra Angelico painting that the heirs to the Bic pen fortune say was stolen by their family chauffeur and later sold by the late art dealer Richard Feigen.

Sources indicate that in a decision dated October 26 and filed on November 16, Newly introduced York Supreme Court Justice James G. Clynes rejected an attempt by Feigen’s estate and associated businesses to get the case dismissed. Their main argument? The family had waited too long to sue.

“No family should lose a treasured work of art simply because the people who handled this stolen property were sophisticated dealmakers,” Luke Nikas, a lawyer for the Bich family, told ARTnews. The Bich family is determined to recover what belongs to them, and today’s decision is an important step toward achieving that result.” The ruling is good news for the Bich heirs, though there’s still a long way to go. The judge hasn’t decided who owns the painting, or even whether it was stolen. In a recent development, as ARTnews reported in April, the dispute centers on Saint Sixtus (ca. 1453–55), a painting by the Italian Renaissance master that Baron Marcel Bich, the founder of Bic, bought at Sotheby’s in 1972 for £130,000. The painting eventually passed to his son Bruno, who kept it in his Fifth Avenue apartment in Manhattan. Then, around 2006, it disappeared.

According to a lawsuit filed in March by Bruno’s three sons, Gonzalve, Charles, and Guillaume, the family’s chauffeur, Roy Morrow, stole the painting and sold it to Feigen for $3 million. The family says Morrow had no paperwork proving the painting was his, but Feigen bought it anyway and promptly insured it for $8.5 million, nearly three times what he’d paid.

Feigen, one of Newly introduced York’s best-known Old Master dealers before his death in 2021, later consigned the painting to Christie’s. In 2018, it was sold for $5.4 million to Chilean billionaire Álvaro Saieh and his wife, Ana Guzmán.

Sources indicate that the Bich brothers say they didn’t learn where the painting ended up until 2023. They asked for it back the following year, but Saieh and Guzmán refused. So the brothers sued, demanding the painting’s return, along with damages and money from its sale.

It has been revealed that feigen’s representatives tell a different story. In March, lawyers for his estate told Reuters that Morrow had signed a bill of sale saying he owned the painting. The Saiehs, meanwhile, have remarked they bought it in good faith, relying on Christie’s research.

Which brings us to the latest ruling.

Feigen’s lawyers argued that the family had simply waited too long to bring its claims. After all, Feigen bought the painting in 2006 and sold it in 2018. The brothers didn’t file their lawsuit until March 2026.

But stolen art can be a tricky business under Latest York law. Generally, when someone buys a stolen artwork in good faith, the clock doesn’t start ticking on a claim for its return until the rightful owner demands it back and the buyer refuses.

Things get more complicated when someone is accused of knowingly dealing in stolen property. The clock can start earlier, and Clynes noted there were too many unanswered questions about Feigen’s purchase to settle the matter now.

The judge also rejected, for now, an argument that the family had dragged its feet for so long that allowing the case to continue would be unfair. Lawyers call that laches. Both arguments can come up again later.

Feigen’s side did score one win. The judge dismissed claims against his revocable trust and its trustees because the family hadn’t shown enough to connect the trust to the money from the painting’s sale. The brothers can bring those claims again if they find more evidence.
