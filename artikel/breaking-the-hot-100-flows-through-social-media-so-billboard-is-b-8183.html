Breaking: The Hot 100 flows through social media, so Billboard is bringing YouTube data back into its charts

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that billboard and YouTube have kissed and made up. A disagreement between the two companies kept the latter out of the former’s chart calculus for about a year, but Billboard is adjusting its weights to give more recognition to ad-supported streams that occur on YouTube. As a result, YouTube data will be incorporated into the Billboard charts (again) in 2027 and beyond.

Lyor Cohen, YouTube’s Global Head of Music, declared the new policy in a blog post. “We are returning to the Billboard charts starting in 2027,” Cohen wrote. “Billboard’s new methodology better reflects how fans engage with music today across both subscription and ad-supported services.”.

In a recent development, youTube views first became part of Billboard‘s rankings in 2013, when the music magazine recognized the growing influence of digital streams across its charts. As soon as the single-based Hot 100 and album-based 200 began factoring in YouTube-based plays, the platform’s impact became abundantly clear. At the time, Baauer’s “Harlem Shake” was the soundtrack to an inescapable viral trend, and the EDM banger shot to the top of the Hot 200 as a result.

In a recent development, soon, the Hot 100 was filled with songs that were significant on YouTube, and that led detractors to argue that Billboard was overemphasizing ad-supported streams. Record companies and fans could use repetitive videos to push their favorite songs to the top of the charts — and that’s exactly what they did.

In a recent development, in a 2017 adjustment to its chart policies, Billboard decided to weigh paid streams more heavily than ad-supported streams. As of 2025, it took 2.5 streams on an ad-supported service like YouTube to equal one stream on a subscription-based service like Spotify Premium.

According to recent reports, cohen protested that policy, and the squabbles eventually compelled YouTube to pull its data from the Billboard charts. “Every play should count equally,” Cohen wrote at the time.

Sources indicate that notably, Billboard‘s updated policy does not count all streams equally. Paid streams are still given more weight, though the gap has shrunk. Now, one paid stream will equal 1.5 ad-supported streams. That breakdown, according to Billboard, “better reflects the varied user activity occurring on these services.”.

Billboard wants to do right by all its partners, but its charts couldn’t ignore YouTube forever. The platform’s impact on music consumption is undeniable, even if some of its streams only last for a few seconds. So when the Billboard charts come out on January 16, they will better reflect the pop culture landscape as it at the moment looks.
