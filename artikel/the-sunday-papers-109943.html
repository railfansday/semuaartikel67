According to recent reports, while following a peculiar trail of silver and saffron leaves around the largest roots of the Treehouse, you come across a hexagonal wooden door lying half-hidden among ivy and moss. Scraping away the dirt, you discover a faint, absurd engraving of a newspaper. What mystery is here? You turn the brass knob – strange, it is warm! – and the engraving of the newspaper flutters. The pages turn faster and faster, emitting a howling gale that bowls you head over foot. When you pick yourself up, you are inside a vast attic full of books and bricabrac, lit from one side by windows that offer no view of the outside world – just light and mist.

Tarnation! You have been lured into the eternal prison of Sunday Afternoon. Fortunately, there are several ways to escape. Each of the arcane objects before you is secretly a portal, transporting you to another site of arcane activity, in a bizarre web of diversions that cannot possibly reflect the hand of a human curator. Which will you choose?

As you step forward, your toe nudges an ornamental scroll. It growls and wriggles like a panicking caterpillar, scooting away under a table. Before you lose sight of it, you discern the title and subject of the text – it's an essay by Kyle Tam about the yokai of Promise Mascot Agency.

Sources indicate that deep in a drawer, you discover a sour-smelling binder full of extracts from an ancient and all but indecipherable dispute over the discovery of "generative AI" in something called "Big Walk". The stakes are long-buried, but many of the individual pieces of writing are lively. Here is Bruno Dias noting in passing that "one of the harms of the 'AI' political project is the propaganda machine dedicated to making you feel stupid and helpless." Here is a professorial interjection from Brendan Keogh calling on the warring parties to recognise when "critiques of generative AI are actually just deflected critiques of the capitalist relations of technology and labour". Here is Mike Cook arguing that "what's happening is not unusual, it's not extreme, it's not unfair and it's not a 'public flogging'." Here is a ticker tape message from Hannah Nicklin observing that "if your radical position doesn’t have room for transformation it will not be safe until it contains just you".

A footnote in the same dusty sheaf of papers points you towards another work from Mike Cook in a different drawer. This one consists of neon slides, slotted into an antique View-Master, and explores a comparison between procedural generation and street photography.

Sources indicate that peering at the weirdly white and opaque windowpanes, you realise there are intricate equations traced in the condensation. It appears to be a post-mortem for a "post-cosy" game called Danchi Days, in which developer Melos Han-Tani discusses wishlist conversion rates and the problem of not fitting neatly into any recognisable genre.

Sources indicate that A snowglobe stands on a mantelpiece, the flakes still orbiting a miniature washtub as though the object had been recently shaken, even though the chamber has clearly been abandoned for decades. You put it to your ear, anticipating the faint jingle of Xmas bells. Instead, you are almost deafened by a screeching guitar chord. Here is Erin Keane to explain.

Next to the snowglobe on the mantelpiece, there's a souvenir shop conch shell, polished to a high sheen. You put that to your ear, hoping to catch the soothing shuffle of surf on shingle, and a disembodied voice tells you to piss off. Tim Sniffen has some thoughts about gentrification, and why only he should be allowed to do it.

It has been revealed that again, one of these mystic artefacts hides the key to your release from the suffocating stillness of Sunday Afternoon. Which will you choose?
