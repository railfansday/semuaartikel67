What are we so afraid of?

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

This year, I discovered I absolutely love horror movies. Through their twisted stories, we can play with our fears, project them onto the screen and laugh at them collectively. I can’t watch them alone, but I started a Scary Movie Club.

Our fears say a lot about us and our society, and I think financial fear shapes more buyer decisions than anyone realizes.

In a recent development, financial fears run throughout Chapman University’s latest survey of American fears, from economic collapse to medical bills and making rent. Not having enough money for the future ranks above public speaking, sharks and even death.

It has been revealed that firstHome IQ’s latest NextGen Homebuyer Report, “How You’re Built to Buy” found similar levels of anxiety. Two in three respondents worry their finances won’t last, and 86% report some form of financial shame.

According to recent reports, last week I hosted a financial workshop at my gym, and with all of my Scary Movie Club members, we faced our fears together. While I’ve studied the data, that night I met the ghosts and the monsters, and they explained a lot about what’s standing between buyers and their financial goals.

Industry observers note that I pitched my financial workshop at run club with promises of pizza and games. But money is still a significant scary shark in an ocean we don’t understand.

In a recent development, “Are you a psychopath?!” One run club member laughed as I excitedly shared the proposal for collectively discussing our finances. “Are you also going to tell me we’ll be public speaking after?” (He attended, and now that I think about it, I did make him public speak a little).

I discovered the level of my own horror in finance a couple of years ago when I hired a financial advisor. I smiled and nodded, with a tightened chest and shallow breath. I later noticed my fitness tracker thought my meeting was a nap. It confused my frozen, horrified body as shutting down for sleep. (The last time it recorded a nap was during The Conjuring, while we waited for the demon to inhabit the mother and I was too scared to breathe).

Since I started researching financial psychology, I have been pleasantly surprised to find out how unexceptional that financial horror is. In keynotes for housing professionals and in our financial fitness workshop, everyone has their own financial ghost story.

In a recent development, someone who grew up with very little noted that when money comes in, it still looks like a way to plug a hole. “Even though my life’s changed so much now from when I was a kid, I don’t think my brain ever stopped thinking, like, okay, plug this up, plug this up.”.

In our research, earning more and saving consistently didn’t impact the anxiety, avoidance or shame numbers by much, likely because they’re old stories. Researchers at Cambridge found that many of the habits we bring to money are largely in place by the time we’re seven, absorbed from our parents’ relationship with money.

According to recent reports, if you haven’t seen Poltergeist, the family’s dream house turns out to be built on top of a cemetery with some very active ghosts. We can make more money, save more money, and move into the nicer house, but the bodies are still down there.

For one person in the room, that history had shaped whether owning a home ever seemed like an option. “When you grow up with so little, you just really don’t think you can have those things.” By the time someone asks what they can afford, they may have already decided what kind of life is available to them.

My favorite form of financial madness is avoidance. There’s always at least one medical bill sitting on my counter, taunting me, reminding me of all the ways I fall short. Fortunately, I can afford those bills now (and usually I discover I’ve already paid them). But this is the kind of sick game we play.

Industry observers note that in our survey, over a third of people are avoiding the bill on their counter, too. One woman said of her financial anxiety, “My brain just keeps cycling through the fear instead of realizing that it’s not rational.”.

Last week, I interviewed Todd Ballenger, a professor and loan officer, on financial psychology, and he told me the fear of being judged is the number one reason people don’t call. People are afraid of being judged, of being taken advantage of, of not knowing something they should.

A business owner in our workshop mentioned of investing in property, “It’s a game I don’t fully understand…so I defer it, and I defer it.” Over half in our survey said they avoided talking with a professional because of how they might look.

Industry observers note that that fear reminded me of Brené Brown’s viral TED Talk, where she described shame as something that grows in secrecy, silence and judgment. It is the creaking staircase to the dark basement, the unknown around the corner.

Todd said we can’t focus our attention on the hardest things. “Think about where you feel the opposite of shame in your finances,” and he said to start building there.

Industry observers note that I held the workshop at my gym because it’s where we already do hard things together. I still can’t watch a horror movie by myself, and I don’t think most people can face their finances by themselves either.

Everyone has a ghost in their basement, and it’s your job to make sure they don’t go down there alone.
