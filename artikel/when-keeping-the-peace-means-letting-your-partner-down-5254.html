When Keeping the Peace Means Letting Your Partner Down

According to recent reports, imagine you and your partner have just finished having dinner with your parents. The drive home seems eerily quiet, even though you feel like the evening went pretty well, and you can’t shake the general impression that your partner is upset.

Eventually, they sigh right before asking, “Why didn’t you say anything when your mother made that comment?”.

You try to explain that you didn’t want to start an argument. After all, your mother has always been outspoken, and you know from experience that challenging her would have started “a thing” and made everyone uncomfortable. You had assumed your partner understood this and would instinctively know you didn’t agree with what your mother noted.

Industry observers note that your partner sees things differently. They were embarrassed and went on to spend the entire evening feeling unsupported and increasingly resentful. You’re also beginning to realize that while you might have avoided one argument over the dinner table, you set yourself up to face another one at home.

Recent findings suggest that situations like these are common for couples managing complicated relationships with extended family. Doing your best to keep everyone happy seems like the responsible adult choice, especially when family gatherings can already be tense or stressful. However, avoiding uncomfortable conversations with relatives can easily come at the expense of your partner.

According to a 2013 longitudinal study, early dynamics with in-laws often correlate with later marital stability. This connection can vary according to factors like race and gender, so complicated family relationships don’t necessarily affect every couple the same way. However, wanting to maintain family harmony is second nature to most people. This can especially be the case for those who grew up in households in which challenging a parent was unacceptable or disagreements almost always escalated into ugly arguments.

Industry observers note that it’s easy to go into adulthood with the general idea that keeping quiet is the cleanest way to deal with uncomfortable situations. That doesn’t necessarily stop once you’re in a committed relationship of your own.

Naturally, you love your partner and feel responsible for protecting their feelings, but that doesn’t necessarily mean you’re comfortable confronting your parents, siblings, or other relatives.

Picture a situation in which your sibling cracks a joke at your partner’s expense during a family get-together. Everyone laughs, so you join in, even though you also know your partner finds such remarks hurtful.

Recent findings suggest that from your point of view, laughing along was the easiest way to avoid a potential scene, especially if that’s your family’s usual routine when this particular sibling makes off-color comments or gives someone a hard time. Meanwhile, all your partner knows is that you joined in while someone else embarrassed them in front of others.

In a recent development, disconnects like these can become huge sticking points in a relationship when they happen repeatedly. Your partner may start to associate getting together with your family with having to endure uncomfortable comments and potential embarrassment. If they also feel you expect them to tolerate this, they may eventually wonder whether they can count on you at all when life gets difficult.

A 2019 study published in Social Work Research surveyed 351 women about their relationships with their mothers-in-law. Findings suggested factors like family-triangle dynamics or unclear relationship boundaries directly impacted relationship quality.

Having a partner who stays silent during disagreements can further aggravate relationships that may already be complicated.

Recent findings suggest that when one partner makes a habit of not addressing comments or behaviors that are inappropriate, the other can feel responsible for managing everyone’s discomfort. They’re forced to choose between speaking up and potentially causing a scene or staying silent and dealing with feelings of resentment or abandonment.

Properly supporting your partner doesn’t necessarily mean agreeing with them every time they complain about your family. However, you can (and should) acknowledge your partner’s feelings and talk things out when conflicts come up. There’s a world of difference between saying “You’re right, my family is awful” no matter what and saying “I get why that was hurtful, so let’s talk about it” instead.

Couples can minimize recurring disagreements further by working through expectations before people butt heads and things get tense.

Sources indicate that 1. Discuss potential sticking points beforehand. Some relatives may be notorious for disregarding other people’s boundaries or making intrusive comments. If this is the case in your family, prep for upcoming gatherings by talking to your partner beforehand about how you’ll respond to any possible incidents.

Recent findings suggest that avoid assuming you already know what your partner will find helpful in the moment. Instead, ask them what would truly help them feel supported and go from there.

In a recent development, 2. Address inappropriate behavior calmly. Intervening if things do get tense doesn’t have to mean starting an argument. Try shutting down hurtful comments, intrusive questions, and so forth by simply saying, “We’d rather not discuss that” or “Please don’t make fun of my partner.”.

Statements like these help establish boundaries with relatives and set expectations without necessarily escalating the situation.

It has been revealed that 3. Avoid volunteering your partner to keep others happy. Instead of instantly making commitments or agreeing to family plans that affect both of you, talk to your partner about it first. Being considerate to your family is important, but it shouldn’t mean making decisions and promises on someone else’s behalf.

It has been revealed that 4. Check in privately. If something happens at a family get-together that feels uncomfortable, pull your partner aside and ask how they’re doing. If necessary, talk about it for a moment or offer to leave early. Even a brief check-in is often enough to keep your partner feeling supported and stop resentment from building up through an evening.

5. Take responsibility when you get it wrong. You’re only human, so there may be times when you only realize after the fact that staying silent may have hurt your partner. Acknowledge it and explain what happened. Then apologize and express a willingness to respond differently next time.

Conversations like these should go both ways, so be sure to give your partner a chance to explain their own family histories or talk about genuine misunderstandings as well.

According to recent reports, ultimately, it’s more than possible to keep your family relationships strong while also helping your partner feel properly loved and supported. But doing so might mean learning to manage occasional discomfort instead of simply expecting your partner to absorb the impact and understand.
