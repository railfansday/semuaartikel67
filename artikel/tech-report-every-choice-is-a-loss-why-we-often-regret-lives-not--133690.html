Nurses often recount stories of patients near the end of their lives who express regrets about experiences they never had and paths they never took. They sometimes wonder whether choosing differently might have made them more satisfied with their lives. Regret is, to some extent, part of almost everyone's life. If we marry, we may eventually regret it; if we do not, we may remain alone and regret that too. If we emigrate, we may regret leaving; if we stay and endure our circumstances, we may regret that as well. Whether we remain in a tedious job or choose another, whether we speak our minds to those around us or keep silent, we may still end up regretting our choices.

Sources indicate that in the first part of Either/Or, Søren Kierkegaard—writing through the persona of a young man, "A"—posits that life is inherently characterized by boredom and that the choices we make are attempts to escape this state. We choose things that promise pleasure, yet such choices never lead to lasting joy or satisfaction; what is thrilling today may become mundane and tedious by tomorrow. Boredom inevitably returns, leaving us to lament the choices we made and wish we had chosen differently. To break this cycle of regret and boredom, "A" offers an intriguing proposal: much like farmers who rotate crops to prevent soil erosion, individuals should constantly alter their approach to life—avoiding fixation on any single experience—in order to mitigate boredom. Praising the character of Don Juan—the seductive, womanizing figure from Mozart’s opera—"A" champions the rejection of all commitments as a means to banish boredom and regret. In the same volume, Kierkegaard introduces another character, Johannes, who employs psychological tactics to seduce a young woman named Cordelia, gradually cultivating her affection. He uses strategies such as maintaining distance, employing silence, sowing doubt, and stirring the other person’s emotions—all of which he finds deeply pleasurable.

In the second part, Kierkegaard writes through another persona, Judge Wilhelm. He recommends that “A” choose an ethical and committed life. A significant portion of this section addresses marriage. Wilhelm argues that only a stable, committed relationship can prevent the initial fire of love and passion from rapidly burning out, allowing it instead to endure. Through Wilhelm's voice, Kierkegaard writes that our choices shape our ultimate selves—an idea later further developed within existentialist philosophy. By employing pseudonymous characters, Kierkegaard deliberately avoids taking sides, leaving the conclusion to the reader. Ultimately, he presents choice and regret as fundamental components of human existence [1].

It has been revealed that now, we explore the neurological mechanisms underlying the persistent human experience of regret. In their book The Molecule of More, Lieberman and Long discuss two brain circuits—the mesolimbic and the mesocortical—both of which utilize dopamine as a neurotransmitter. The first circuit primarily drives "wanting," motivation, and the anticipation of a reward; it is not about the pleasure itself but rather the anticipated pleasure expected from a given stimulus. The second circuit activates top-down pathways for impulse control, enabling consideration of the future consequences of one's decisions and desires. From an evolutionary perspective, this capacity has allowed humans to transcend immediate, relatively simple responses and proposal for the attainment of long-term goals. However, this simplified model of the reward system’s neurology suggests that repetition of a stimulus leads to a diminished reward—driven by a decline in dopamine levels and function within the mesolimbic system—thereby shifting attention and motivation toward new rewards [2].

Although the prefrontal cortex is primarily responsible for logical reasoning, evaluation, and behavioral regulation, certain regions within it also play a role in generating feelings of regret. A 2004 study revealed that individuals with damage to the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), unlike healthy controls, did not report experiencing regret or anticipating negative outcomes during a gambling task. Additionally, other brain areas, such as the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and the hippocampus, contribute to the experience of regret by enabling the mental simulation of alternative, unlived scenarios [3].

In an intriguing paper published in Science Advances in 2023, researchers examined the distinct roles of the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), the ventral striatum, and dopaminergic neurons in counterfactual thinking in monkeys. Their findings indicated that the brain can represent not only the reward actually received but also the value of a reward that could have been obtained through a different choice [4]. This suggests that, at times, the source of our regret is not an event that actually occurred in real life, but rather an event that never took place, yet one whose possibility the mind has retained. In effect, we may find ourselves mourning a life we ​​never lived.

In a recent development, in conclusion, the human brain is designed to anticipate the future outcomes of decisions we might have made and to compare those potential results with our current situation. While this capacity can aid in making better future choices, it can also foster a chronic sense of regret. This trait leads us to constantly evaluate our present circumstances against hypothetical alternatives—lamenting the paths we did not take. In the modern era, this inclination has been exacerbated by the rise of social media, which offers numerous opportunities for social comparison and exposes us to constant reminders of "alternative" lives. Observing the lives, homes, cities, and joys of others can trigger anxiety that we are perpetually falling behind.

Here, it helps to distinguish this phenomenon into two types: 1) Constructive Regret, which helps us recognize past mistakes and make better decisions moving forward; and 2) Ruminative Regret, which traps individuals in a cycle of constantly comparing their actual lives with hypothetical scenarios or the lives of others—without necessarily leading to any constructive action or decision. Since it is impossible to experience every life possibility or to know for certain whether a different choice would have been better, we can take the message of the second part of Either/Or to heart: By committing fully to our own lives, rather than constantly dwelling on lost possibilities, we can liberate ourselves from the anxiety and the wearying melancholy of regret.
