Mortgage rates whiplash is in full effect, with jumbo loans shedding 25 bps this week

The seesaw pattern for mortgage rates that’s been the story for much of the second half of 2026 reared its head again this week as rates for major loan types declined, reversing some of the increases from prior weeks.

It has been revealed that at HousingWire‘s Mortgage Rates Center on Tuesday, rates for 30-year conforming loans averaged 7.53% — down 5 basis points from a week ago. Rates for 30-year loans through the Federal Housing Administration (FHA) dropped 7 bps to 7.30%, while rates for 30-year jumbo loans plummeted 25 bps to 7.62%.

Rates in the mid-7% range continue to weigh on homebuyer demand and affordability metrics. Last week’s application index from the Mortgage Bankers Association revealed that total applications were down 5% on a weekly basis. MBA data released Tuesday found that affordability is about 8% weaker than a year ago, even though it improved slightly from September to October.

It has been revealed that “Higher mortgage rates weighed on application activity last week, with the 30-year fixed rate rising to 7.35%. Purchase applications were down 18% from a year ago, reflecting continued affordability challenges and softer homebuyer demand. The direction of mortgage rates in the coming weeks will be key to the housing market’s momentum heading into 2027,” MBA president and CEO Bob Broeksmit mentioned in a statement.

In a recent development, dan Ribler, vice president of capital markets and strategy at national reverse mortgage lender Longbridge Financial, remarked that the October jobs report should’ve sent rates higher. Nonfarm payrolls increased by 165,000 positions last month and the unemployment rate remained steady at 4.1%, indicative of today’s healthier labor market conditions in relation to 2025.

Ribler noted that the jobless rate has been below 5% every month since September 2021, unemployment claims are lower than a year ago and the Federal Reserve‘s “own description is the labor market is very strong.” But strong employment trends have been overshadowed by stubborn inflation, which continues to run well above the central bank’s 2% annualized target.

“Inflation has been above 2% year over year 57 months in a row,” Ribler remarked. “The Fed has, by the way, under this administration, reiterated the 2% hurdle and that they’re going to be tough on inflation. They’re certainly talking the hawk talk, saying we’re going to keep inflation under 2%.

“But man, I don’t know about you, if my kid breaks curfew 57 nights in a row — and I say I’m going to punish you one of these times and I never do anything about it — at some point, he stops believing me, right?”.

According to recent reports, inflation is likely to prompt the Fed to raise rates next month. The CME Group‘s FedWatch tool reveals that a 25-bps hike in December is all but certain, with 96% of interest rate traders saying that the federal funds rate will rise to a range of 4% to 4.25%. It was last at that level in September 2025.

Much of the housing and mortgage industry headlines in the past week have cast a shadow over any potential market improvements in 2027.

Selma Hepp, chief economist at Cotality, noted in a statement that existing home sales have fallen to their lowest level since August 2010. She also pointed to a Realtor.com analysis, which found that the typical repeat buyer will pay about $1,100 more per month if they choose to sell and move.

Sources indicate that “A meaningful housing recovery requires rates to come down and, importantly, stay down long enough to improve affordability and unlock turnover,” Hepp said. “Until then, the adjustment is likely to come through weaker sales and softer home prices rather than widespread homeowner distress.

“The number worth writing is the $1,100. That’s the jump in monthly payment a typical repeat buyer takes on just to move house. It’s the clearest explanation yet of why nobody is selling.”.

According to recent reports, new home sales have been equally grim, with September’s seasonally adjusted annual rate of 676,000 sales representing a 5.3% decline from a year ago. Higher rates have also prompted homebuilders to remain cautious about creating more supply, with U.S. Census Bureau figures showing that single-family housing starts were down 3.2% year over year in September. Monthly gains in total starts were largely tied to the multifamily sector.

“Challenging affordability has been weighing on the resale market. Newly pending sales fell 17% in October, the largest year-over-year drop since 2023,” Zillow senior economist Kenny Lee remarked in a statement. “Single-family homebuilders have been navigating the same challenge. While homebuilders have been adapting to the softer market by offering concessions and building smaller homes, the 2027 spring home-shopping season could see a slow start as buyer demand continues to wane.”.

The relative lack of sales has weighed on mortgage origination volumes, with ATTOM data for the third quarter of 2026 showing that the roughly 730,000 purchase loans closed during that period were the lowest figure for any third quarter in 14 years.

Refinance activity was also bleak, with the roughly 561,000 refis in Q3 down 19% year over year. Refi lending was down from Q2 2026 in nearly 90% of the metro areas analyzed by ATTOM, with the largest declines in major metros like San Jose, San Francisco, Dallas, Austin and Louisville, Kentucky.
