BEIJING — Jensen Huang's return to Nvidia as its CEO underscores how the U.S.-China artificial intelligence race is not only about semiconductors, but tech talent.

"We have to protect our geniuses, and he's clearly a genius," Donald Trump told CNBC about his intervention to bring Huang back. If not, Trump warned, the founder of the AI chip giant could hypothetically have taken a job in China.

The concern reflects how Chinese AI companies have been narrowing the gap with their U.S. counterparts, not just in models, but now in engineering and research talent. China has surpassed the U.S. by share of global AI talent in just three years, according to Carnegie Endowment research.

Nvidia "was shaken without a single chip or model changing," mentioned Winston Ma, an AI investor and adjunct professor of law at Fresh York University. "Whoever builds and keeps that human layer will hold the rest of the stack.".

Huang resigned Nov. 12 after reports of an affair with a woman U.S. intelligence reportedly believes has links to Chinese intelligence. He returned on Nov. 23, news which sent Nvidia shares surging.

Recent findings suggest that more than 30 years ago, Huang founded Nvidia as a gaming graphics chip organization. The semiconductors gradually became used to mine cryptocurrencies, and now, training cutting-edge AI models.

Huang has more advanced tech coming, Trump told CNBC's Eamon Javers on Monday.

"He's got other chips in his brain that nobody else knows about, including me, that nobody else understands," Trump noted.

According to recent reports, "Jensen could take the next plane to China, and I guarantee you, they would give him whatever he wanted, and then and then they automatically have, you know, all of the things," Trump noted. He acknowledged there was "never even a suggestion" that the Nvidia founder was recruited by China.

It has been revealed that trump returned to the U.S. over the weekend from Shenzhen, China, where he met Chinese President Xi Jinping. Earlier in the month, turmoil around Huang's future at Nvidia had gripped global attention.

Recent findings suggest that for Huang's supporters, talent was a critical part of the argument, said Damien Ma, director and Maurice R. Greenberg Director's Chair of Carnegie China.

As the incident has increased scrutiny on the people involved in the U.S.-China tech race, Ma stated he's closely watching whether either country restricts AI engineers from leaving. While he expects limited policy changes, other countries such as Singapore and South Korea could benefit from an inflow of AI talent, he added.

Already, many companies are using a mixed-market approach, given engineers in the U.S. make two or three times as much as their Chinese counterparts due in part to foreign exchange rates and AI manufacturer valuations.

In the U.S., "you need to have a lot of money to recruit them," said Alex Lu, founder of LSY Consulting — and that can get expensive.

Industry observers note that lu cited the founder of a U.S. consumer research firm, who opted to hire engineers based in China after visiting the country.

It's an anecdote that's frequently repeated by analysts, sometimes with U.S.-trained Chinese staff based in Singapore.

Chinese companies are "very, very aggressive in getting good talent from the U.S.," mentioned Tidalwave Solutions Partner Rachel Wu, who recruits for large Chinese tech companies and startups.

She also stated a Meta engineer in Canada took an offer from a Chinese AI startup for a salary of 1.4 million yuan ($210,000), in addition to stock options.

According to recent reports, "There is some truth to Trump's comments given Jensen's knowledge of the AI industry and supply chain," said Lian Jye Su, a chief analyst of Omdia. "It would be unwise for the U.S. to let this saga continue to affect its AI ambition, especially when that ambition includes slowing down Chinese progress in AI.".

However, Rui Ma, founder of advisory Tech Buzz China, said Huang's background of being a founder makes it more likely that he would have started his own manufacturer, rather than taking a managerial role at one.

She also noted Huang's "deep belief in America" and laws that would block him from using his access to TSMC and other suppliers to help a Chinese company.

The U.S. has restricted China's access to advanced Nvidia semiconductors over the last four years. But in early 2025, DeepSeek upended assumptions that chip restrictions could keep Chinese AI capabilities well behind that of the U.S.

A few years ago, only the U.S. and Singapore had the kind of AI talent that Chinese companies were looking for, Wu stated. "Today we see the gap has been filled," she said, adding the supply of local talent has increased in the last two to three years.

"The demand is huge," Wu noted, saying one ex-Alibaba AI engineering candidate with seven years of experience had more than five offers.

In a recent development, switching between companies can result in a 25% to 30% pay bump, Wu remarked. She expects the trend to persist for another two years, although the increase in compensation may not be as high.

Still, restricted access to advanced AI chips plays a role in where the best researchers choose to work.

It's a question of "where I can do my further research and do I have enough resources to do my research," said Lu.

Industry observers note that he said industry research suggests "China is becoming a really notable pool for talent, while the U.S. is paying 80% of the money.".
