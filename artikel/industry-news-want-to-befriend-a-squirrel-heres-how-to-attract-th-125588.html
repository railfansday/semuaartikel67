Industry News: Want To Befriend a Squirrel? Here's How To Attract These Cute Critters To Your Yard and Earn Their Trust

You don't have to be a Disney princess to befriend a squirrel, which is good news for anyone who's long been keen to woo one of these cute critters themselves (ideally without a Snow White-level singsong).

Look, I'll level with you; I know that squirrels might not be everyone's favorite garden visitors. After all, they're notorious for raiding bird feeders, digging up freshly planted bulbs and burying nuts in flower pots. Before you declare war on these bushy-tailed garden acrobats, though, there's another side to consider... and not just the fact that they're the birth month animal for May babies everywhere.

Sources indicate that yes, squirrels can actually play a surprisingly useful role in the garden and wider ecosystem. From helping disperse tree seeds to spreading beneficial fungi, these busy little mammals contribute more to the natural world than you might realise. And they're also wonderfully entertaining to watch, with their energetic antics, impressive climbing skills and habit of darting between trees (so much so that it's well worth adding this charmingly rustic squirrel house to your backyard).

Recent findings suggest that it's easy to focus on the damage squirrels can sometimes cause, but they also provide several ecological benefits. By digging and burying seeds, for example, they can help new trees and plants establish themselves, not to mention turn over small patches of soil.

Sources indicate that squirrels eat mushrooms and other fungi, helping disperse fungal spores which go on to form important relationships with tree roots, helping trees access nutrients and water. And let's not forget, either, that they are – sorry – an important food source for predators such as hawks, owls, foxes and other wildlife.

Rather than trying to keep squirrels out of your yard, why not consider making them feel welcome? With the right food, shelter and a little patience, you can encourage them to visit regularly and gradually become more comfortable with your presence.

Here's how to befriend a squirrel, then, along with some top tips on what to feed them and how to enjoy their firm without sacrificing your bird feeders or flowers.

It has been revealed that squirrels are naturally drawn to gardens with plenty of trees, shrubs and places to hide. The easiest way to befriend a squirrel, then, is to offer up mature trees (which provide opportunities for climbing, nesting and foraging,) and dense shrubs that offer shelter from predators and bad weather.

In a recent development, oak, hickory, beech and walnut trees are particularly valuable to many North American squirrel species because they produce nuts and seeds that provide natural food. So, if you have space, consider planting native trees and shrubs that offer food and shelter throughout the year.

One of the easiest ways to make your yard appealing to squirrels is to let nature do some of the work. Rather than removing every fallen nut, seed head or piece of natural debris, then, consider leaving a few areas where wildlife can forage.

Trees that produce acorns, walnuts and hickory nuts can provide valuable food, while berry-producing native shrubs offer additional opportunities for wildlife to feed. And you could also leave some fallen leaves in quieter areas of the garden, as these support insects and other small creatures that contribute to the wider ecosystem.

Anyone who's ever managed to befriend a squirrel before will tell you, in no uncertain terms, that offering small quantities of food always goes a long way!

Unsalted hazelnuts, walnuts and pecans in their shells provides a natural foraging activity. Sunflower seeds, too, are a familiar food source for many squirrel species, although they should only be offered in modest quantities. And plain pumpkin seeds can provide another occasional treat, too.

Natural food from your garden should always be the first port of call, of course; acorns, tree seeds and other naturally available foods are preferable to making squirrels dependent on a regular supply from you (especially as healthy wild squirrels don't really need supplementary feeding).

Sources indicate that on that note, remember not to overfeed; the trick here is to only offer a small amount, remove uneaten food and avoid creating a feeding station that attracts large numbers of animals or unwanted pests. And take care to keep any feeding equipment clean, too, as communal feeding areas can allow diseases to spread between squirrels.

Like other wildlife, squirrels need access to fresh water, and a shallow birdbath or a wildlife water dish like this one can provide a convenient drinking spot, particularly during hotter, drier weather.

It has been revealed that just as you would for your beloved birds, make sure the water is kept clean and refreshed regularly. Adding a few stones can also provide a helpful foothold for smaller creatures, too, and don't forget to position the water somewhere squirrels can reach safely (ideally near trees or shrubs that offer a quick escape route).

Perhaps the most rewarding part of welcoming squirrels into your garden is watching them gradually become more comfortable with your presence. Start by simply observing them from a distance, and then try to spend a little time in the garden without making sudden movements or approaching them.

According to recent reports, if you provide a small amount of food, use the same designated feeding area and let squirrels discover it independently. Over time, they may become accustomed to seeing you in the garden and feel more comfortable foraging while you're nearby.

Sources indicate that even if you are attempting to befriend a squirrel, please don't attempt to chase, touch or hand-feed them. Even seemingly friendly squirrels can bite or scratch if they feel threatened, and encouraging wildlife to approach people can create problems for both animals and humans.

If you want to welcome squirrels without losing your bird feeders, consider using squirrel-resistant feeders, particularly models with weight-activated mechanisms that close feeding ports when a heavier animal climbs aboard.

You can also install a squirrel baffle on feeder poles to prevent squirrels from climbing up to reach the food. And don't forget to position feeders away from nearby trees, fences and other launching points wherever possible, as squirrels are surprisingly good at jumping.

Recent findings suggest that if you'd like to provide food for both birds and squirrels, consider giving them separate feeding areas. This can help reduce competition, although it won't guarantee that squirrels won't investigate the bird feeders.

If you want to stop squirrels digging up your flower bulbs, be sure to protect the plants you want to keep safe. This might mean covering newly planted bulbs with wire mesh or use protective bulb baskets, as these allow plants to grow while making it much harder for squirrels to reach the bulbs.

Alternatively, you can also cover the soil in containers with suitable mesh or another physical barrier, particularly during the autumn planting season. This approach allows squirrels to continue visiting the garden while protecting the areas where their digging is less welcome.

Squirrels might occasionally test a gardener's patience, but they're also fascinating little creatures that play a role in supporting healthy ecosystems.

If you want to befriend a squirrel, you may find that simply providing natural food sources, shelter and fresh water does the trick! And if you do decide to put out the occasional treat, remember that moderation and good hygiene are important; these are wild animals, and the aim should be to encourage natural visits rather than turn them into pets.

Oh sure, you might not be able to stop every squirrel from investigating your bird feeders or digging in your flower beds, but learning to accommodate these lively visitors? Well, that could make your garden a much more interesting place to spend time in the long run...
