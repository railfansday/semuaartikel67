So, say you want to be one half of a power couple—you know, a romantic pairing where both of you are successful and influential like Beyoncé and Jay-Z or Mr. and Mrs. Incredible. But the problem is you are still early on in your own personal power trip—meaning that you yourself have not become successful yet. What then can you do?

Well, how about trying to be part of a "possibility couple" now? That's a pairing that's all about the P's—namely "potential," "possibilities", "prediction," "potent," and, yes, maybe "power" eventually. A "possibility couple" is one that doesn't have power per se at the present. But it's got the potential to become quite potent in the future. In other words, based on what you are seeing now, you predict that together you'll have lots of potent possibilities down the road.

Recent findings suggest that it shouldn't be super-surprising that the "possibility couple" is central to Hinge's third annual Gen Z D.A.T.E. (Data, Advice, Trends, and Expertise) Report titled: “Dating While Dreaming Big.” Since Gen Zers are those born between 1997 and 2012, they are now no older than 29 and thus are mostly still in the fruitcake batter stages of their lives—still forming their identities and possibilities.

The Hinge report hinged on surveys administered to the over 13,000 users of the dating app. The survey results did suggest that many Gen Z respondents are looking into the future when evaluating their present dating prospects. For example, 81 percent of Gen Z respondents felt that "ending up with their ideal partner would make achieving their ideal life more likely," and 67 percent "would prefer a partner who makes them feel hopeful and inspired over someone who simply fits the life they’re already building." This forward-oriented view seemed to be more present among the Gen Zers, with 76 percent of them vs. 65 percent of millennials saying that "when they imagine their ideal romantic partner, they’re also imagining the person they want to become.".

According to recent reports, none of these are terrible things. Thinking more about your future and playing more of a long game when dating can be good approaches. The opposite would be just considering the here and now and trying to satisfy your immediate urges, such as, "Sure, that person is a bit crazy but so, so hot, and I'm so, so horny." Focusing too much on the present when looking for a partner can be like buying a pair of low-riding skinny jeans and thinking, "I'm going to wear these for life.".

When you think only about who you are right now, rather than who you might become, you could be destined for some D's—meaning dispute, despair, and potentially divorce. You could end up outgrowing the other person. Or the person could end up holding you back. So, yes, it does make sense to find a person who is more open to possibilities for both of you.

A seemingly big challenge, though, of finding a possibility pairing is having to P a lot—meaning predicting, projecting, and prognosticating. It's not like a potential power pairing, where you can simply say, "I got the power. Do you?" and find the answer to be "yes" in both of your eyes. Whether the two of you might last without an eventual power outage is a different story.

It has been revealed that with a "possibility couple, any potential projections are further complicated by having to prognosticate for not just one but both of you when you don't know each other very well. In fact, neither of you may even be that self-aware yet. Plus, as stock market investors, weather forecasters, and the makers of Crystal Pepsi, Segways, and Joker: Folie à Deux will tell you, predicting what will happen in the future is not easy.

It has been revealed that sure, there are present-day signs. For example, someone right now in law school is very likely to become a lawyer in a few years. But that lawyer could easily one day say, "Forget the law. Bring on yarn bombing." And who knows what twists and turns life may bring, such as health challenges, unexpected people entering or exiting, workplace and industry overhauls, or encountering a life-changing piece of tiramisu. I have previously warned in "A Funny Bone to Pick" about the dangers of dating someone's potential future self rather than who the person currently is because that person may end up never changing.

Therefore, the best possibility might be to balance the present with the future. You can ask yourself the question that I've already posed for "A Funny Bone to Pick"—namely, are you stronger with or without the person? Look for qualities that potential partners already have that would project well into the future.

According to recent reports, one such quality is open-mindedness—being open to different possibilities and people. After all, you may become a very different person at least outwardly. So, beware of anyone who stereotypes, focuses too much on superficial things, and holds strong biases.

A second quality is adaptability—the ability to accommodate future changes, such as the two of you growing in different ways. Or is your potential partner more like a starched denim thong—rigid and inflexible in a way that's surely going to drive a wedge between you in the future?

Thirdly, do they even want you to succeed and have a true teammate mindset where they want their partner to be as potent as possible? Or must they outshine everyone, including you? Beware of the people who make themselves feel high by keeping those around them low.

Finally, are they a match for what's inside you—your core, your core personality and values, what won't change over time? If the two of you are attracted to each other's hot legs, abs, job, wallet, memberships, pick-up lines, car, or cast-iron cookware, remember those things definitely change over time—with the possible exception of the cast-iron cookware.

By contrast, what allows for growth is having strong roots beneath the surface. If the two of you can connect those roots—those things that go beyond the superficial—then the resulting possibilities could be endless.
