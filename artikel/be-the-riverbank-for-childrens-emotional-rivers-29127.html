Be the Riverbank for Children’s Emotional Rivers

“I used to yell at my daughter when she cried, to make her tougher. Even after I learned that crying is how she expresses herself when she has big emotions, I still yelled. I couldn’t deal with feeling helpless to make her happy again. Slowly, I learned that I have to take care of my own emotions and let my own tears out. Now when my daughter cries I cuddle with her, she lets out her tears, and we both feel better afterward. We call it cuddling the cry away.”.

Industry observers note that children’s emotions need to flow freely, like a river. That means parents need to be the riverbank. A riverbank safely contains the flow of feelings—preventing a flood—but doesn’t dam the flow by ignoring feelings, judging them, or forcing them to change. After all, a riverbank doesn’t decide how warm or cool the water in the river should be, or how fast or slow it should flow.

Parents who can express their own emotions in a healthy way can listen to children’s feelings without becoming overwhelmed by them. As a dad mentioned to me recently, “I often tell my children something that I only learned recently for myself: If you are happy, you can laugh; if you are sad, you can cry.”.

The riverbank is the river’s home. Children need a safe physical home, and they also need a safe emotional home, often called the secure home base. This isn’t always easy for adults. It’s hard to sit with intense feelings in children, especially difficult ones like anger or grief.

Imagine two children who fight at a playground, and each goes running to their parent. One parent claims, “Don’t come crying to me.” The other parent says, “Oh, poor baby, we will never come to this playground again. I’ll never let that mean child near you.” I see many instances of each extreme—dismissal of the child’s emotions or joining the child in emotional flooding.

In between is a good level of emotional support: “That looked like it hurt your body and your feelings. How did you try to solve the problem? How did that work? What else might you try next?”.

In a recent development, if you can talk with both children, you might add: “Looks like you two are having some trouble. It’s hard when friends fight. Does anyone have any ideas on how to play together again?”.

It has been revealed that children need a safe place for the free flow of all their feelings, but a safe home base is especially relevant for sadness. When children feel sadness, loneliness, or loss, they need a place of warmth and love where they can heal from that pain.

Recent findings suggest that mabel was almost 3 when her parents began to talk to her about starting preschool. It would be her first time away from both parents for any extended period. She enjoyed her visit to the school, but asked her mother many times, “Will you be here with me?” When she understood that she would not be with her parents or grandparents at school, she lost her enthusiasm and was a bit sad.

Industry observers note that mabel’s family understood that their job was to offer comfort and security, not to convince her it would be fun: “I understand that you’re sad. It’s hard to imagine having fun when you’ll miss your family.”.

A few days before school started, while her grandma gave her a bath, Mabel said to the little bath-toy animals, “You’re going to school.” Grandma picked up a toy and gave it a voice: “Will you be with me?” Mabel answered, taking on the role of the mother, “You’ll be by yourself.” Grandma had her animal say with worry, “Oh no!” Mabel responded gently, “Oh, it will be OK.” Then they made all the animals splash around in the water, with lots of laughter.

It has been revealed that this game provided Mabel with the ability to work through her sad feelings, and she made an excellent adjustment to preschool. Of course, she was sad sometimes when saying goodbye, but she knew she had a safe place at home to feel her sadness.

It is inevitable that children will be sad at times. Children need to know that their parents can handle their heartbreak, without becoming so overwhelmed that they respond with anger or dismissal, such as: “Stop crying or I’ll leave you here all alone.” Sad children need to hear instead, “I’m here with you. This is very painful. Together we can hold this major feeling.” This may not make the sadness go away immediately. That’s not the goal. A mother confronted me at a workshop: “I listened to my son, like you said, and he was still sad.” Yes, it’s heartbreaking and true. Fortunately, it’s not a parent’s job to take away the feeling, but to sit with the child while the tears flow. The sadness will pass.

A mother recently shared with me an example of this upcoming way of thinking about sadness:.

Sources indicate that “The other day my daughter came back from the neighbor’s house crying. The other children did not play with her, and she felt very sad. I squatted down and hugged her. At the same time, my brain was thinking fast. How should I solve the problem for her? For a moment, I had nothing to say, so I repeated what she told me and looked at her. I gave her a hug and sympathized with her. She finished her tears and had good ideas for resolving these friendship problems the next time. The trouble was solved easily. Our emotional connection worked much better than my usual approach, which was to tell her what she should do. In this case, it was a very good thing that I couldn’t think of any so-called good ideas!”.

It is time to stop seeing tears as a sign of weakness, especially in boys, who are expected to be tough from the very start of their lives. Many boys learn the “boy rules” and try to block all their tears from an early age. If they are “successful,” they are on the road to being emotionally constricted men. If they “fail,” they may be teased or bullied by other boys, or even by adults. This puts boys in an impossible position. The solution is for them to have safety at home, with parents, to let down their guard and let out their tears, even if they choose to hold them in sometimes with their peers. Boys are lucky if they know that someone sees them as OK no matter what they feel or how they show it. Boys are really lucky if they have adult men in their lives who are willing to accept tears, talk about emotions, and even cry themselves sometimes.

According to recent reports, children’s sadness needs to flow freely, with a loving adult sitting nearby or holding them close. Even the toughest kids (and grownups) have tears inside that need to come out.
