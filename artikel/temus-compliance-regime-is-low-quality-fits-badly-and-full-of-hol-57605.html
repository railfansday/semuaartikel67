Temu's compliance regime is low-quality, fits badly, and full of holes, indicates Europe

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Prepares to unleash full wrath of Digital Services Act over Chinese e-tailer's alleged sales of illegal products.

Chinese e-tailer Temu has a reputation for selling low-quality knockoff products at low prices made possible through use of forced labor. Now, the European Commission has made a preliminary finding that the firm also runs a low-quality compliance regime that means it’s not meeting its regulatory obligations.

The Commission issued that finding yesterday, accusing Temu of breaching its Digital Services Act (DSA) obligation to operate serious measures that mitigate the risk of illegal products being offered on its platform.

Temu is a marketplace that allows third parties to sell on its platform, a model that doesn’t relieve it of responsibility for the goods available on its site and apps.

Eurocrats therefore have beef with Temu over several matters, including:.

Requiring sellers who use the platform to test the quality of “only a very small share of products,” and then often not bothering to check the reports;.

Not doing enough to prioritize checks of products in known high risk categories, like toys and cosmetics, then not following up on those checks;.

Running a moderation system that mostly considers whether products comply with Temu’s terms and conditions, rather than product safety;.

Not hiring enough human moderators to keep pace with growth in listings on Temu;.

When it finds stores selling non-compliant goods, Temu acts against that store – but not the trader who may sell the same products in multiple stores;.

Allowing stores to commit many serious infringements, including selling products that fail safety checks, before suspending their stores.

A Temu spokesperson told The Register the manufacturer respectfully disagrees with the EC's findings.

Recent findings suggest that "Temu invests significantly in compliance infrastructure and human expertise and implements a suite of control measures that effectively mitigate illegal product risks with a consistently low incidence of problematic products," the spokesperson mentioned. "We will review and respond to the preliminary findings carefully and will continue to engage constructively with the Commission. We are confident that we will reach an amicable resolution of the matter that fully addresses the Commission's remaining concerns.".

Industry observers note that the EC has already tangled with Temu. In 2024 Eurocrats investigated its risk assessment processes. In May 2026, Eurocrats found Temu’s processes inadequate and levied a €200 million fine.

Brussels also sent a €550 million fine to another Chinese e-tailer, Alibaba, for not doing enough to stop the sale of dangerous goods.

This announcement of fresh action against Temu invoked Article 35 of the Digital Services Act, the sweeping regulation of online businesses under which the EU could fine Temu up to six percent of its global revenue and order corrective actions.

Industry observers note that europe is concerned that the low prices Chinese e-tailers charge are enabled by forced labor and ignoring dangerous products. EC officials believe those practices mean the likes of Temu don’t compete on a level playing field with continental retailers. The bloc has tried to make life harder for Chinese e-commerce outfits by charging a temporary €3 customs duty for every shipment sent from outside the EU and valued at under €150. Google recently revealed that newly introduced charge saw its advertising revenue from Asian customers fall.

Beijing sees its e-tail giants Temu, Shein, and Alibaba as crucial actors in its strategy to grow China’s exports and overall economy, which hasn’t performed strongly in recent years. Indeed, Chinese consumers have slowed their discretionary spending to the extent that Beijing last year subsidized purchases of consumer electronics – including Apple products –  to keep cash registers ticking over. ®.
