The journalist and broadcaster who died last week aged 69 knew the enduring power of radio, even as the landscape changed so promptly.

It’s injury time at the Emirates on the 29 October 2008, Arsenal are 4-3 up against Spurs. Tom Huddlestone cushions a ball to Luka Modric, whose strike takes a deflection. It loops over Manuel Almunia and on to the post into the path of Aaron Lennon, who prods it home and starts skipping towards the Spurs fans before diving on to the ground and being enveloped by Modric, Benoît Assou-Ekotto, Darren Bent and a couple of pitch-invading fans. Pandemonium, for once, seems the apt way to describe it.

In a recent development, but the real entertainment is happening in a radio studio at 18 Hatfields, London SE1; the old home of TalkSport. As the goal goes in, a guttural voice makes an indecipherable but very loud noise. It’s hard to transcribe – perhaps “ERRRRRAAAARRRGGHH”, or maybe “OOEEERRAAGGH”.

According to recent reports, it’s just a noise, but it’s unmistakingly Danny Kelly. He regains the power of speech and follows it up with: “Gooooaaaal … Goalll Stan Goalll at the Emirates.” Reporter Ian Abrahams and Stan Collymore join the excitement, before Danny comes back: “I LOVE FOOTBALL, STAN, I LOVE IT.” It is such a brilliant piece of radio. From one of the absolute greats.

According to recent reports, danny died last week. In a beautiful tribute on the Spurs podcast he hosted – A View From The Lane – Charlie Eccleshare pointed out the irony that for someone so articulate and erudite and able to do so much more than just “the football”, his most iconic moment is so simple. Even the opening noise – not even a real word – is perfect in that context.

Words such as pioneer and trailblazer often get thrown out in moments like this. But Danny really was. I first met him at BBC London more than 20 years ago. As someone who’d listened to him and Danny Baker as a teenager, I couldn’t really believe I was in the same office as them.

His career was ridiculous, editing the NME and Q magazine, setting up Football365, but it was radio where I knew him. He understood the absurdity of life and never forgot the absurdity of earning a living by talking about football.

I remember at TalkSport one World Cup – 2018 perhaps – wandering in and he was hosting a live match, watching a game on the TV just marvelling at the fact that this was a job, and that this was his job.

Industry observers note that and he was funny. Blimey he was funny – what an ability to be blessed with. I listened to a best of Baker and Kelly Upfront from the old Radio 5 on YouTube the other day; amazing calls about Arthur Cox locking a disgruntled fan in his office for two and a half hours, a man playing football with a pig’s head in a sausage factory in Peckham: “I used to like the volleys when the eyes used to pop out.” Anyone who has tried to make funny football content, of any kind – radio, TV, podcasts, social media – owes a debt of gratitude to him.

Last Wednesday afternoon Charlie Baker and I had to try to do him justice on TalkSport. Afternoon UK time, midnight til 3am for me in Australia. I was asleep when my phone rang and instantly presumed I’d slept through my alarm and missed the show. A millisecond of internal panic surged through me as I jumped up and cracked a shin on the bedside table. Danny would appreciate the ridiculous scene of me hopping around in the dark trying not to wake the kids while one of the bosses gave me the incredibly sad news.

If you’ve broadcast for any amount of time, you will be confronted with having to host demonstrates just after someone has died. Jack Charlton, Terry Venables, Jimmy Greaves – but never before have I had to pay tribute to a friend.

Industry observers note that paul Hawksbee’s voice cracks and we all go. There are West Ham and Chelsea fans calling admitting they listen to his Spurs podcast because it’s him. There are all manner of texts recounting hilarious stories they heard on his shows over the last 30 years: the man who took a TV to a game in Northern Ireland so they could watch the FA Cup final, having to move around holding the aerial to keep the signal.

There’s a moment I think Danny would have found so funny, when I’m being sad – because it is sad, and I am sad – and into the break I read the sponsor’s lines: “This show is brought to you by Toolstation, first stop Toolstation” in such a maudlin way it’s as if I’m delivering a eulogy, just one sponsored by a hardware store. I catch myself just in time.

Recent findings suggest that but most importantly there are so many messages from people who never met Danny, who never knew him, but who felt as if they knew him. We believed he would have wanted a bit of fuss made of him.

It has been revealed that he definitely knew the enduring power of radio, even as the broadcasting landscape changes so quickly. It connects in a way nothing else can. And nothing mentioned that more than messages we received from listeners who at one time or another were struggling and going through incredibly hard times and Danny’s energy, wit and joy were an escape and a tonic.

There will be a million people more important in Danny’s life than me. Our paths crossed a great deal, but never for very long. I was a minuscule cog in his world – but Danny had so much love to give. Voice notes about how to broadcast remotely or celebrating Spurs’ win in Bilbao; the tributes highlighted he had almost infinite time for others.

Recent findings suggest that it always feels such a bland cliche to say it’s impossible to consider someone so full of life not being there – we all know that we have a sell-by date. At the start of October he had been given a choice: to ebb away slowly over a couple of years or have a big but very risky operation. Danny never ebbed.

He sent a major group WhatsApp voice note to many friends and colleagues, and I don’t think he would mind me writing what it said.

“I want you to know that I’ve enjoyed being around you, I’ve learnt from you, I’ve enjoyed working with you, you know how much I love my work – the fact I’m pretty good at it is secondary to how much I enjoy it, but look I’m very much hopeful that the boffins and my own determination will get me through this and we can all laugh about this message. But know how I care about you, how much you have meant in my life and on Thursday night or Friday morning when you look at the sky or feel the wind against your face, think about me and what’s happening and hopefully we’ll all have a pint and a laugh about this somewhere down the line.”.

From “ERRRRRAAAARRRGGHH” to that – his words just hit the right tone every time.
