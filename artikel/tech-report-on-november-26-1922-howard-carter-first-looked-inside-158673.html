In his journal entry for that day, Carter recorded his reply to Lord Carnarvon as 'Yes, it is wonderful.' The famous line comes from his 1923 book.

On November 26, 1922, in Egypt’s Valley of the Kings, Howard Carter held a candle up to a small hole in a sealed doorway and looked into the tomb of Tutankhamun for the first time.

Sources indicate that what he said next is one of the most quoted lines in archaeology. In his published account, Carter, asked whether he could see anything, answered, “Yes, wonderful things.”.

But in the journal he kept during the excavation, Carter wrote down a different reply.

Recent findings suggest that carter’s handwritten journal for the first season of work on the tomb is held by the Griffith Institute at the University of Oxford, which has published a transcript online. Its entry for November 26, 1922, describes the moment step by step.

That afternoon, after clearing 9 meters (about 30 feet) of a descending passage, the team reached a second sealed doorway.

Carter made “a tiny breach in the top left hand corner to see what was beyond.” Candles were brought, he wrote, as a test for foul gases.

Carter’s patron, Lord Carnarvon, his daughter Lady Evelyn Herbert and Carter’s colleague Arthur Callender waited “in anxious expectation,” Carter wrote. “It was sometime before one could see, the hot air escaping caused the candle to flicker,” he added.

Then the room appeared, “with its strange and wonderful medley of extraordinary and beautiful objects heaped upon one another.”.

According to recent reports, “There was naturally short suspense for those present who could not see, when Lord Carnarvon mentioned to me ‘Can you see anything’. I replied to him Yes, it is wonderful,” the entry reads.

Recent findings suggest that the more famous version appeared in 1923 in the first volume of “The Tomb of Tut.Ankh.Amen,” credited to Carter and A. C. Mace, an associate curator at the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York.

In the book, Carter describes seeing “strange animals, statues, and gold” and “everywhere the glint of gold.”.

He writes that he was “struck dumb with amazement,” and that when Carnarvon asked, “Can you see anything?” it was all he could do “to get out the words, ‘Yes, wonderful things.'”.

The journal does not settle exactly what Carter noted out loud in that passageway. But it shows that the line most people know comes from the book, not from the words Carter wrote in his journal.

Sources indicate that the journal entry goes on to list what the light revealed. Carter wrote of “two strange ebony-black effigies of a King, gold sandalled, bearing staff and mace,” gilded couches, painted caskets, alabaster vases, “a golden inlaid throne” and “a confusion of overturned parts of chariots glinting with gold.”.

His first impression, he wrote, was of “the property-room of an opera-house of a vanished civilization.”.

Industry observers note that the team was not even sure it had found a royal burial. Even that afternoon, Carter wrote, the passage had seemed to support “our first conjecture that we had found a cache.”.

Then they saw a sealed doorway between two guardian statues and the name of Tutankhamun on most of the objects.

Recent findings suggest that “Was it a tomb or merely a cache?” Carter asked in the journal. With the evidence in front of them, he concluded, “there was little doubt that there behind was the grave of that Pharaoh.”.

The next day, Callender set up electric lights, and the group made a careful inspection of the room, which came to be called the Antechamber.

Industry observers note that the look inside came 22 days after the first sign of the tomb. According to the Griffith Institute, Carter’s journal covers the discovery of the tomb’s stairway on November 4, 1922, and the uncovering of the first sealed doorway on November 5.

On November 4, Carter wrote, “At about 10am I discovered beneath almost the first hut attacked the first traces of the entrance of the tomb.” By sunset the next day, the workers had cleared down to the 12th step and exposed the top of a plastered, sealed doorway.

It has been revealed that carter then sent a cable to Carnarvon, who was in England. “At last have made wonderful discovery in Valley a magnificent tomb with seals intact recovered same for your arrival congratulations,” it read, as copied into the journal.

More than a century later, the tomb still needs protecting. Earth.com has reported on cracks from water damage that threaten King Tut’s painted tomb and on another long-lost royal tomb that has emerged in Egypt.

Sources indicate that the information was obtained from Howard Carter’s journal for the 1922-23 excavation season, as transcribed by the Griffith Institute, University of Oxford, and from the first volume of The Tomb of Tut.Ankh.Amen by Howard Carter and A. C. Mace (1923).
