Homo erectus is already one of humans’ most extraordinary ancient relatives. The substantial-brained, tool-wielding species emerged two million years ago in Africa and expanded across Eurasia, before vanishing around 100,000 years ago in Java, Indonesia.

According to recent reports, researchers have now added another feather to the hominin’s cap. A pair of studies analysing fossil teeth1,2, which were published in Nature on 18 November, suggest that H. erectus thrived in dense rainforests in southern China as recently as 180,000 years ago.

Researchers had thought that H. erectus was limited to savannahs and open woodlands, states Kira Westaway, a geochronologist at Macquarie University in Sydney, Australia, and a co-author of both studies.

It has been revealed that “Never, ever have we seen them in rainforests,” she adds. “This is a really substantial deal, because we thought Homo sapiens were the only species that had the skill set to survive in really challenging rainforest environments.”.

The earliest H. erectus fossils, from eastern Africa, are 1.9 million years old. But the svelte, upright-walking and running hominins wasted little time, reaching western Asia and present-day China some 200,000 years later and modern Java, using land bridges, not long after. The last-known H. erectus remains, from central Java, date to roughly 110,000 years ago3.

Limestone caves in Guangxi province, southern China, contained more than 10,000 fossil teeth.Credit: Kira Westaway (Macquarie University).

According to recent reports, while looking for fossils from a three-metre-tall great ape called Gigantopithecus, Westaway and her colleagues identified a pair of limestone caves in Guangxi province in southern China. There, they discovered more than 10,000 fossil teeth from animals such as orangutans (Pongo spp.), monkeys and wild boar (Sus spp.), as well as 9 from hominins. The researchers estimate that the hominins lived between 184,000 and 324,000 years ago, according to dating of both fossils and the surrounding sediments using various methods.

Eight of the hominin teeth resembled those of H. erectus in their shape, a designation that was confirmed by analysing the sequences of proteins preserved in the dental enamel of four specimens, says co-author Frido Welker, a molecular palaeoanthropologist at the University of Copenhagen.

The tooth enamel was analysed and its proteins sequenced.Credit: Kira Westaway (Macquarie University).

The ninth lacked the shape and protein signature of H. erectus teeth, indicates Welker, and it also didn’t resemble those of H. sapiens or Neanderthals (Homo neanderthalensis). Denisovans — enigmatic ancient humans first discovered in southern Siberia and now linked to diverse fossils from China and elsewhere in Asia — are a possibility, but the evidence is circumstantial, says Welker. “Every label we put on that ninth tooth is a little bit of speculation.”.

The multitude of animal teeth was probably accumulated by a scavenger such as a porcupine. The presence of fossils from species such as muntjac deer (Muntiacus muntjac), leopards and orangutans hints at a rainforest environment.
