It has been revealed that “This can go one of two ways,” I think as I slide behind the wheel. “Either this makes city driving a lot less intimidating, or I regret ever giving it a try.”.

Recent findings suggest that as someone who rarely drives – and doesn’t even own a car – in San Francisco, I was eager to see whether Mercedes-Benz’s newly introduced point-to-point driving assistance system could ease my anxiety as I navigated the city’s notoriously challenging streets. How would it handle the many four-way stops, steep inclines, pedestrians, cyclists and double-parked robotaxis?

In a recent development, after an hour of weaving through these complex environments, I walked away feeling more at ease than when I started. That’s largely because I barely had to lift a finger – or a foot.

Industry observers note that I got an early opportunity to try Mercedes-Benz’s fresh point-to-point driver assistance system, MB.Drive Assist Pro, which will roll out to select California customers in December before expanding to the rest of the US next year. The Level 2 system handles functions like steering, braking and acceleration, but the driver has to be attentive and ready to take over at any moment. However, I hardly ever needed to.

In a recent development, as I made my way through San Francisco’s Financial District, Marina and North Beach in a Mercedes-Benz GLC 400, I repeatedly had to resist the urge to tap the brakes, press the accelerator or grab the steering wheel out of instinct. But within minutes, my apprehension had given way to awe at just how much the system could handle and how little I needed to do. With MB.Drive Assist Pro engaged, I watched as the vehicle obeyed traffic signals, yielded the right of way and even maneuvered around stalled robotaxis. Occasionally, I had to briefly place my hands on the wheel so it would know I was paying attention, but I didn’t find it bothersome at all.

Recent findings suggest that there were moments when I had to take control. At one intersection, a pedestrian was preparing to cross, but the system persisted in making a right turn. (The death glare I got was a sign I needed to take over and tap the brakes.) Further down the road, I grabbed the wheel to steer around a parked truck unloading cargo. Other times, my patience was tested as the car came to a complete stop at every stop sign (rolling stops are my forte, sorry not sorry). To its credit, though, the vehicle subtly asserted itself at four-way stops by inching forward as we waited for our turn.

Unlike Level 4 robotaxi services like Waymo and Zoox, Level 2 systems like MB.Drive Assist Pro aren’t fully autonomous, but can take some of the stress out of everyday driving, particularly in dense urban environments.

Recent findings suggest that tesla’s so-called Full Self-Driving (Supervised) offers similar capabilities, navigating city streets, maneuvering around obstacles, making turns, changing lanes and responding to traffic signals, while still requiring driver supervision. Other systems like GM’s Super Cruise, Ford BlueCruise and BMW Highway Assistant offer hands-free driving on compatible roads, but are primarily designed for use on highways rather than complex city streets.

A feature like MB.Drive Assist Pro could not only bring increasingly advanced driver assistance technology to more car owners but also lay the groundwork for a future in which autonomous driving becomes more common in personally owned vehicles.

“We’ve clearly shown that the autonomous driving sector is an area that we will continue to develop and [be] involved in, and being one of the first companies to offer a system like you guys experienced today reveals that we’re committed to be in that space and continue to grow,” Jason Hoff, CEO of Mercedes-Benz North America, noted during a reporter roundtable following the demos.

MB.Drive Assist Pro uses 27 sensing components, including 10 cameras, five radar sensors and 12 ultrasonic sensors scattered throughout the vehicle. This hardware is at the moment available on certain 2027 Mercedes vehicles, including CLA Electric, GLE, GLS and S-Class cars. Starting with 2028 models, the manufacturer says more of its US passenger vehicles will be equipped to support the upcoming feature.

Nvidia’s Alpamayo Stack powers the vehicle’s autonomous driving capabilities, using AI to interpret complex road scenarios and reason through driving decisions. It works alongside Nvidia’s Halos safety tech, including Halos OS, which provides safety guardrails to help ensure the vehicle follows the rules of the road, like stopping at a red light or waiting for an intersection to clear before crossing.

Industry observers note that with Level 2 driving, there’s a mental hurdle that drivers like me will need to overcome and a certain degree of control we’ll have to relinquish to let the system do its thing. For instance, I sometimes found myself getting nervous as the car confidently moved into the oncoming lane to pass a stalled vehicle, even with approaching traffic. I was assured by the Mercedes team that the system can determine whether there’s enough room to safely squeeze through and otherwise wouldn’t attempt the move. Still, it’s not something I’d personally feel comfortable doing if I were in full control.

According to recent reports, some of this simply comes down to a learning curve. Over the hour I spent behind the wheel, I found myself feeling less anxious and more trusting of the system. I’d imagine that confidence would only grow with repeated use of MB.Drive Assist Pro, so drivers can feel less apprehensive about the car taking the lead, even if some decisions still occasionally give them pause.

Sources indicate that MB.Drive Assist Pro will be available nationwide starting next year, with one-month, one-year and three-year subscription options. It’ll come to eligible Mercedes-Benz models including the 2026 CLA electric sedan, 2027 CLA sedan, 2027 GLC electric SUV, 2027 GLE SUV and coupe, 2027 GLS SUV and 2027 S-Class sedan. The system introduced in China alongside the new CLA in late 2025. The upcoming rollout will bring it to the US and Germany as well.

Industry observers note that customers can factory-order MB.Drive Assist Pro, or purchase it digitally through the Mercedes-Benz store; it will be delivered as an over-the-air update. Mercedes hasn’t yet shared pricing.

It has been revealed that mercedes is also bringing hands-free highway driving to the US in spring 2027. I had the chance to try out that tech early as well, which proved to be an even bigger test of my trust.

As I drove from Alameda, across the Bay from San Francisco, onto Interstate 880 in a GLS 580, I took a deep breath and prepared to hand over some control to the car. After pressing the Resume button on the steering wheel (or adjusting the cruise control speed), I could activate Hands-Free Highway Assist. The feature comes with a few restrictions: you have to be on a freeway, not in an exit lane and away from construction zones or particularly sharp curves. You also can’t be driving faster than 85 mph.

Hands-Free Highway Assist lets you kick back a bit as the vehicle drives, slowing down and speeding up with traffic. A capability called Proactive Lane Change Assist can automatically switch lanes to overtake a slower vehicle or approach an upcoming exit based on the navigation route.

It has been revealed that as with MB.Drive Assist Pro, you have to watch the road and be ready to take over at any time while using Hands-Free Highway Assist. Given the high stakes and high speeds, I wasn’t as hands-off with the highway driving feature as I was in the urban environment. Driving on the highway already feels nerve-wracking to me even when I’m in full control, so I couldn’t quite overcome the mental hurdle of letting go, physically and metaphorically, for more than about a minute at a time. I’d activate the feature, panic, then reactivate it. (Clearly, I’m a huge thrill seeker.).

Industry observers note that one key hurdle is that it can be difficult to know when Hands-Free Highway Assist has been activated. An icon appears on the dashboard showing a green steering wheel with gray hands and two As on each side when the feature is available. Those hands will disappear when the feature is active, leaving only the green steering wheel and As. The icon turns red when you need to take over. Still, it was a little tricky to tell when the feature had correctly been enabled and when I hadn’t yet met all the requirements. Again, part of this is the learning curve, but it’s a major lesson to learn at 65 mph.

Still, when I was brave enough to activate the feature, it was impressive to witness the vehicle smoothly weaving across lanes on its own and following the flow of traffic, especially when I had the chance to try it again later in the afternoon on the S 580E. It seems like a handy capability that can make long-haul journeys and daily commutes a little less miserable.

Hands-Free Highway Assist will be available in the US in the spring, and pricing has yet to be determined. An early access program will give select California customers the chance to access the feature next month, alongside MB.Drive Assist Pro. Hands-Free Highway Assist will be on vehicles that support MB.OS (Mercedes’ in-house operating system) as part of the MB.Drive Assist offering, which already includes Steering Assist, Lane Change Assist and Proactive Lane Change Assist. The latest feature will arrive via an over-the-air upgrade.

After a day of stepping outside my comfort zone in more ways than one, it was fascinating to get a glimpse of where automotive technology is headed – and how it could make everyday drives, even the more stressful ones, feel a little less daunting. But for now, I hopped into an Uber and returned to my usual routine of avoiding the driver’s seat.
