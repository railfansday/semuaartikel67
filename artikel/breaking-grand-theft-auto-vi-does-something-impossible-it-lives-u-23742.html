Thirteen years later, Rockstar has crafted a worthy successor to GTA V and the newly introduced standard-bearer for open-world games.

How do you follow a game like Grand Theft Auto V? Thirteen years ago, Rockstar’s crime-fueled epic went on to redefine what an open-world game could be and became an enormous success, selling more than 230 million copies across three generations of game consoles. It sits just behind Tetris and Minecraft on the list of the best-selling games of all time, and its lucrative online mode has helped it remain popular for well over a decade.

That level of success has allowed Rockstar to take its time with a sequel, which has further increased the hype around the next GTA. A series of major leaks and delays only exacerbated this. At a time when much of the video game industry is struggling, the next edition of GTA stands alone, a cultural moment so major (almost) everybody has been forced to get out of the way. The buildup to Grand Theft Auto VI’s launch is unlike anything the game industry has ever seen.

Even more remarkable? The game was actually worth the wait.

According to recent reports, at this point I’ve spent nearly 80 hours in Leonida, GTA VI’s fictional take on Florida. And what I’ve experienced is a game that combines the wide-open freedom offered in GTA V with the remarkable level of immersion from the studio’s 2018 release Red Dead Redemption 2, while also improving on both qualities. At the same time, it’s also the most mature GTA game Rockstar has ever made, merging the criminal antics and absurdist humor the series is known for with a love story that gives you fresh ways to interact with the world and new reasons for wanting to push forward. This is the GTA game the studio has been building toward for its entire existence.

Let’s start with that upcoming premise. GTA V introduced the idea of multiple protagonists to the series, and VI takes that concept in a different direction. It’s a Bonnie and Clyde-inspired story about Jason Duval and Lucia Caminos, two small-time criminals who meet while, of course, stealing a car. It’s the most GTA-possible meet-cute. The idea is that you can play as both characters individually and also build their relationship over the course of the game. Things start out relatively slow. At the outset, Lucia is in prison, and you play solely as Jason, getting things ready for when she gets out. You can fix up your ramshackle house — that involves actually nailing down new stairs and rolling out paint on the walls — and also start building up a nest egg through means that are not exactly legal.

Jason and Lucia’s relationship grows naturally over the course of the game, but it’s also a slow buildup to becoming the inseparable pair they were by the end of my playthrough. GTA VI approaches most of the experience with confident and deliberate pacing. Even when you control them individually, the characters are often together, either just hanging out or partnering for a crime. And being able to play as both is one of the game’s standout features. But I didn’t get to that point in the game until almost 17 hours in. Before that, you play as both leads mostly solo, learning who they are as people, before you’re given free rein to switch between the two.

What’s great about the premise is how seamlessly it fits with what you’re actually doing in the game. There’s some bigger-picture stuff that I won’t spoil, but generally speaking, the main goal in GTA VI is to make a lot of money so that Jason and Lucia can get out of the criminal life for good and retire somewhere comfortably. And outside of the substantial story missions, you’re largely free to choose how you go about that. You can rob jewelry stores, steal cars, raid drug dens, or even search for hidden treasure at the bottom of the sea. Over the course of the game you’ll also meet different criminals who serve as fences, so you can sell them whatever you’ve got to build up your savings account. Sometimes they’ll even text you targets that are particularly lucrative.

Sources indicate that the structure feels natural and gives you a lot of freedom in how you play. I’m not a big fan of armed robbery, for instance. There are just so many things that can go wrong, and I almost always end up killing some innocent bystander when I try to escape. But I love getaway chases, and so stealing cars became my main form of income. Most importantly, though, this structure gives you a lot of reasons to go out in the world and explore. The missions take you all over the map — which is actually entirely accessible from the very beginning, if you choose to play that way — including not only the bright lights of the Miami-like Vice City, but also tourist beaches, swamps, trailer parks, and a wild variety of other locations, each with its own landscape to explore and characters to meet.

In a recent development, to be clear: Despite being larger, more mature, and more lifelike, this is still very much GTA. This is a game about chaos and crime. This comes across in a few ways. First, there are those smaller missions where you never really know how things will play out. The cashier at the pawn shop you’re robbing might have a shotgun, or you might accidentally smash into a massive rig during a high-speed getaway. The world itself is full of unpredictable moments that force you to think on your feet. Several times I stopped my car to talk to an NPC whose vehicle broke down on the side of the road. In one instance the driver just swore at me to leave her alone. In another, I got pulled into an entire storyline involving a delivery driver who needed help to keep her AI chatbot manager off her back. I delivered her packages and ended up dealing with everything from porch pirates to tigers.

Sources indicate that there are also the multistep heists, which return from GTA V more intricate and complex. These are the big scores and involve several different missions: You spend time getting the right gear and gathering all of the information you need to actually pull them off. Even some of the sub-missions can be gigantic, including one where you have to go undercover for weeks of in-game time to get everything you need in order. But the payoff is incredible. The heists play out like over-the-top action set-pieces, as if they were pulled out of a Christopher Nolan movie like Tenet or Inception (but without all of the sci-fi twists). Even better, all of the missions in the game feel streamlined with a new checkpoint system that allows you to spend less time replaying sequences after failing, and if you fail too many times, you can just skip that mission altogether. There are still a few too many prolonged shootouts, but the checkpoints mean they’re much less tedious than in past GTA games.

According to recent reports, all of this action is crammed into a world that isn’t just huge, but also feels more alive than ever before. Like in Red Dead Redemption 2, you can talk with any NPC, but here you have more options, letting you either greet them or, if you feel like causing trouble, taunt them. Most of the time you’ll get a simple “hey” or someone begging for a few dollars. Other times you’ll get pulled into a fistfight or an unexpected storyline. Once, on my way to buy upcoming clothes, I accidentally bumped into some gang members who followed me inside the store, and I had no choice but to fight them. The owner of the shop called the cops, and so I figured if I was in this much trouble, I might as well empty the register. What started out as a quest to find a cool T-shirt turned into a high-speed escape from the cops.

Another notable thing to note: While the game retains the series’ satirical tone in many ways, the humor doesn’t punch down like in past GTAs, at least from what I’ve played. Instead it makes fun of stuff that deserves to be made fun of: men’s rights activists, NFT bros, conspiracy theorists, Bryan Johnson.

But what really makes the world so special are all the small, meaningful details. Changing your clothes is a lot of fun, but it also helps keep the cops off your back when you’re wanted. Eating and drinking helps improve your physical abilities. And, this being GTA, you can use just about every kind of vehicle imaginable. That includes taking the bus, which runs on a predetermined route, making stops along the way. You can rent electric scooters, and just like in real life, they’re a menace clogging up the streets of Vice City. Your cars even need to be gassed up from time to time.

In a recent development, it’s a careful balancing act. These details make the world feel more nuanced and textured, but they’re also minor enough that they don’t feel tedious. This isn’t The Sims where you’re constantly monitoring your characters’ bodily functions. Usually, I only remembered to eat when the fitness app on my phone told me I needed more protein, and I didn’t once have a car break down because I forgot to fill up the tank. These smaller mechanisms are just enough to add a sense of realism without getting in the way of the action. You can also ignore a lot of them; at no point do you need to shower, but you might hear some snide remarks from your partner if you don’t.

Perhaps the best example of this is the in-game smartphone, which is integrated into the lives of both characters in a thoughtful, plausible way. Crime bosses might text you a location to meet up, for instance, and you can add it to your GPS and head over. Or Lucia might text Jason a link to a funny video, which you can then open in a social networking app, and you can even read the comments. Smartphones have become a common user interface in games, but in GTA VI it’s a part of the world; the game doesn’t pause when you pull out your phone, meaning that if you get a text while you’re driving, you can either pull over and read it or try to text and drive.

In a recent development, I also found these details, like the phone calls and texts between the couple, pulled me further into the story of Jason and Lucia and made me feel closer to both of them. When the two are together, you can press one of the shoulder buttons to make them hold hands. As long as you keep pressing it, they stay together. At first I used this strategically, imagining that if a couple holding hands was casing a jewelry store, it would be much less conspicuous. But eventually, as I grew to know the couple, it just became a thing I did without thinking about it. These two are crazy in love; of course they’re going to hold hands all the time. You can even do little things like make a coffee and choose to hand it to your partner instead of drinking it yourself, and all of these acts improve your relationship, which in turn impacts how the characters relate to each other and how some of the narrative elements play out.

Sources indicate that you don’t have to do any of that. There are just so many ways to interact with the world that different players will find their own things to fixate on. For me, it was a lot of holding hands and drinking coffee every morning. Someone else might get really into fashion or hunting or customizing cars. I barely touched on any of those elements and still felt like I had a substantial experience.

Jason and Lucia are two very different people, each with their own kinds of stories to explore within the game. The major missions are cinematic heists that usually involve some combination of the two. But there are large, branching narratives that are only playable as one or the other character. Jason, for instance, has an old friend who is trying to start a record label in the back room of a strip club. As with the best GTA stories, the tale steadily escalates until you’re feuding with a famous rapper and facing down a number of life-or-death situations. Lucia, meanwhile, has to deal with her mom’s quest to become a real estate agent that gets her caught up with a violent gang, and her best friend pulling her into a pyramid scheme that seems very much like a cult but turns out to be something even more ridiculous. These storylines are so big and absorbing that I would often forget about the main campaign for long periods of time.

According to recent reports, it’s not that there are zero concerns with the game. The combat is still clunky, and the weapon wheel is still frustrating; there were multiple times when I accidentally equipped a flashlight instead of a shotgun when in the heat of battle. I also ran into a handful of small bugs, like dead bodies comically flopping around or a coffee mug that was stuck floating in a hotel room. But given how ambitious GTA VI is, and how badly things can go with large open games, the relative lack of technical issues during my playthrough is impressive. Even better: I played the game on a launch-version PS5, and it ran just fine.

Back in 2018, I called Red Dead Redemption 2 “the most convincing open-world game ever made,” and over the last eight years that has remained true. There have been great examples of the form since then — Elden Ring, Tears of the Kingdom, Control Resonant, Cyberpunk 2077 (eventually) — but none that approach the scale and detail of Rockstar’s works. Certainly none that attempted to do it in anything like the contemporary, real-world setting that has made GTA so pervasive. Now that mantle has been passed to GTA VI.

To give you a sense of how substantial this game is, despite spending more than 70 hours in the world and completing the main story, I’ve only managed to get through less than 60 percent of the total experience. And I just spent 2,500 words explaining how good GTA VI is without even touching on some of the best parts: the incredible selection of music, the hilarious talk radio stations, the pitch-perfect casting, the lifelike graphics. There are full-on TV reveals, books, and video games that you can experience inside of the game. The most pretentious indie game of 2026? It exists in GTA VI.

For the last decade the idea of GTA VI has been inseparable from the hype around it. All of the noise caused by leaks, delays, and the protracted development time has turned the game into something larger than life, an experience hailed as the biggest game of all time before it even launched. It really seemed like no project could live up to the level of scrutiny that GTA VI has been and will be subjected to. But now we can forget all of that, because GTA VI is actually here. And the game is so good that the outsize hype is the least interesting thing about it.

Grand Theft Auto VI releases on the PS5 and Xbox on November 19th.
