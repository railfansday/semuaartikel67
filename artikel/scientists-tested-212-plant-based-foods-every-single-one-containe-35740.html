Scientists tested 212 plant-based foods. Every single one contained fungal toxins

Every one of 212 plant-based foods and drinks tested in a newly introduced study contained at least one naturally occurring fungal toxin, raising questions about the potential health effects of regularly consuming these products.

Sources indicate that researchers examined a wide selection of vegetarian and vegan products sold in the UK, including meat alternatives and dairy substitutes. They detected at least one of 19 mycotoxins in every product, and many contained multiple toxins.

According to recent reports, mycotoxins are poisonous substances produced by certain fungi, including molds. They can enter the food supply when agricultural crops become contaminated during growing, harvesting, or storage.

The investigation was led by scientists at the University of Parma in Italy, with contributions from Cranfield University. The researchers analyzed 212 products, including plant-based meat alternatives (PMBAs) and plant-based beverages (PBBs). The selection covered foods commonly found on supermarket shelves, such as vegan burgers, vegetarian chicken pieces, vegan sausages, and oat, almond, and soy milk.

According to recent reports, although the findings highlight how widespread these naturally occurring contaminants can be, the researchers also identified an important reassuring detail. Every product contained mycotoxin concentrations below recommended European Union guideline levels, which the team remarked reflected the high food quality standards maintained by the UK food industry.

Why Plant-Based Foods Can Contain Fungal Toxins.

Recent findings suggest that many of the ingredients used to manufacture plant-based foods are particularly susceptible to fungal contamination. Grains, legumes, and seeds can encounter mold in fields or storage facilities, creating opportunities for mycotoxins to develop before the ingredients reach food manufacturers.

Unlike bacteria or viruses, mycotoxins are chemical substances produced by certain fungi. Their presence in food does not necessarily mean the product contains living mold, and detecting a toxin does not automatically mean the food is unsafe. The potential health risk depends on factors such as the type of toxin, its concentration, and how much a person consumes over time.

Sources indicate that this distinction is especially important for people who eat plant-based products frequently. Someone whose diet regularly includes meat substitutes, dairy alternatives, grains, and legumes may encounter these substances through several different foods rather than a single source.

Could Repeated Exposure Pose Health Risks?

While the concentrations detected in the UK products were below recommended EU guidelines, researchers say the potential effects of repeated exposure deserve closer attention.

Earlier studies have suggested that consuming small amounts of mycotoxins regularly may contribute to cumulative exposure and potentially increase health risks. In other words, assessing a single product may not provide a complete picture of a person's overall dietary exposure.

Eating one of these products occasionally is unlikely to cause problems based on the levels reported. However, a diet that relies heavily or exclusively on plant-based foods could involve repeated exposure to mycotoxins from numerous ingredients and products. Researchers say understanding the combined effects is important for evaluating possible long-term health consequences.

The severity of mycotoxin exposure varies depending on the compounds involved and the amount consumed. At sufficiently high or harmful exposure levels, certain mycotoxins can damage the liver and kidneys, interfere with normal immune system function, and increase the risk of cancer.

The new findings do not establish that the tested products cause these conditions. Instead, they provide information that scientists can use to estimate how much exposure consumers may receive through their diets and develop more accurate food safety risk assessments for the UK population.

Scientists Call for Better Monitoring of Plant-Based Ingredients.

Andrea Patriarca, Senior Lecturer in Mycology at Cranfield University, emphasized that mycotoxins are a natural part of the food environment and should not discourage people from eating a varied diet. Mycology is the branch of biology devoted to studying fungi, including molds and yeasts.

According to recent reports, "Mycotoxins occur naturally in foods and cannot be completely avoided. As consumers, we should not be frightened or deterred from enjoying a variety of products.

However, a significant concern arises when new foods enter the market, as there are at the moment no established regulations to monitor mycotoxins. We collaborate closely with various sectors in the food industry, from farmers to food companies, to help implement effective mycotoxin management integrated within food safety standards. The data from our research helps food safety organizations in assessing risks, particularly in complex multi-ingredient products.

We are presently collaborating with the University of Parma to evaluate the risks faced by the population based on different dietary habits. Our aim is to advise policymakers and raise awareness among vulnerable consumers.".

It has been revealed that the researchers are particularly interested in how food safety monitoring can keep pace with the growing range of plant-based products. Foods made from multiple ingredients present additional challenges because each component may introduce different contaminants, making overall exposure more difficult to assess.

It has been revealed that their work also underscores the importance of monitoring raw materials throughout the food production process. Identifying and controlling contamination before ingredients are combined into finished products could help manufacturers manage potential risks more effectively.

What the Findings Mean for Plant-Based Diets.

The discovery of fungal toxins in all 212 products highlights the importance of studying dietary exposure across multiple foods, rather than evaluating each product in isolation.

For consumers, the results are not a reason to abandon vegan or vegetarian foods. The concentrations measured were below recommended EU guidelines, and the researchers stress that mycotoxins cannot be completely eliminated from the food supply.

Recent findings suggest that instead, the study points to the need for continued research into how different eating habits influence exposure, particularly among people who depend heavily on plant-based products. The University of Parma and Cranfield University are continuing their collaboration to investigate these dietary patterns, identify potentially vulnerable groups, and provide evidence that can inform future food safety policies.

The research, titled "Mycotoxin contamination in plant-based beverages and meat alternatives: A survey of the UK market," was published in the journal Food Control.

The study was conducted as part of the European Union-funded Horizon Europe FunShield4Med project (HORIZON-WIDERA-2021-ACCESS-03), Grant Agreement No 101079173.
