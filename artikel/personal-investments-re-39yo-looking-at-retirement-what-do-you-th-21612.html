Sources indicate that the bond aren't reducing your risk (you can't withdraw from the account) and for a 20 year timeline, any rebalancing bonus is irrelevant. If you want bonds, they should be in taxable - this wouldn't actually be a big issue given your relatively low income.https://www.bogleheads.org/wiki/Tax-eff ... _placementWould disagree with you about placement (though it does also say there are some criticisms of that strategy).Just because you can't draw from pre-tax without penalty doesn't mean the bonds aren't reducing risk. One could always sell some equities in taxable (potentially for a loss) and buy something equivalent enough (but not substantially similar to avoid wash sale rules) in pre-tax (after selling the bonds held there). The AA stays the same. Longer term tax consequences might make this not so beneficial (the gain on the equities would be taxed as ordinary income instead of LTCG).This is early retirement. The "Boglehead basics" don't work here. If OP has $1m in stocks in taxable and $500k in bonds in IRA (67/33 for ease of math) and market drops 30%, he could sell $100k of bonds for equities in IRA. He'll then have $700k in stocks in taxable and $100k of stocks and $400k in bonds in IRA (67/33). OP draws down $100k from equities over two years, and in each instance sells some bonds to rebuy equities in IRA. Same AA - after 2 years OP has $600k in stocks in taxable and $130k in equities and $370k in bonds in IRA (67/33). Stocks then rise 35%, OP sells some of his equities in IRA for stocks, and now has $800k in stocks (in taxable) and $150k of stocks and $450k bonds in IRA. OP now has a $1.4 million portfolio at 67/33. Easy.The difference is in doing this, OP has also transferred $100k of assets from taxable to tax-sheltered. That's the issue. OP will have to now pay ordinary income tax on that $100k when withdrawn, and if withdrawn early, a withdraw penalty. That is substantially less tax-efficient than keeping bonds in taxable. Traditional 401ks are tax-efficient in the sense that they allow tax arbitrage when one is working, but in retirement, moving assets from taxable to tax-deferred is a horrible move. Consider that at OP's tax rate, 67/33 in stocks/bonds in taxable, using historical averages and tax drag on the bonds, would have grown by 345% over 15 years; he'd owe capital gains on 206% of that (about 60% of the total). If that money were instead put in trad IRA, he'd get a 349% return instead, which is a difference so small he wouldn't notice (it's 1% bigger), but would owe ordinary income rates on the entire amount. And that's assuming he doesn't withdraw early.That's the difference. Using a balanced AA reduces the risk of the total portfolio, but it primarily reduces the risk of money locked away in tax-deferred accounts, which have no immediate risk because they're untouchable (they can be withdrawn in an emergency, but the early withdraw penalty is steep). This strategy may work for traditional retirees who are going to have to withdraw from trad IRA anyway, but for OP, he'd be better served with some safe assets in taxable.Statistics: Posted by breakfastinbed — Tue Nov 17, 2026 8:55 am.
