12 Essential Things I Do In Every Yoga Class I Teach

Industry observers note that I approached my earliest years of teaching with an enthusiasm that I hope helped make up for my lack of experience. As I look back now, in my 26th year of teaching, I realize that I have evolved a philosophy that shapes every class I offer. New teachers are usually so busy learning the craft of teaching that articulating a teaching philosophy remains a distant goal. Over time, though, we can evolve a style of teaching that expresses our values.

The following points express what I feel are imperatives for yoga teachers to remember while teaching.

As teachers, what we share with students can have effects that last far beyond the boundaries of class.

The most important condition for a good yoga class is that it become a “sacred circle,” a term coined by author and Jungian psychologist Jean Shinoda Bolen. Each class must be a safe place for the personal exploration of body, emotions, and mind. A good teacher creates this sacred circle in which every student feels valued, respected, and completely safe.

The class must be free from verbal, emotional, physical, and sexual coercion or abuse. Without this intrinsic sense of safety, it is impossible for students to let go and allow the practice to unfold. In this context, safety means that the students retain the right to say no to working with a partner or to being adjusted or touched, and to say yes to moving at their own personal pace.

According to recent reports, look at each person individually and teach to that person in that moment. Each student is an individual to be guided, not a posture to be fixed. Often we focus too much on communicating about a pose instead of with a person. Your students might not remember the details of the asana (pose), but they will remember being seen, heard, and helped.

Humor teaches perspective and reveals your own humanness to students who might otherwise tend to put you on a pedestal. It also enables you to reveal yourself, thus encouraging your students to reveal themselves as well. Laughter improves our breathing by loosening the diaphragm, and helps us to remember that while yoga practice is key, it does not always need to be serious.

I don’t like the word “correct” when applied to teaching an asana since it implies that there is something wrong with the students—that they are less worthy for not “getting it right.”.

“Correcting” and “fixing” a pose sends the unconscious and therefore very powerful message that “it is never enough.” Many of us are already slaves to this judgment. Our houses are never clean enough, our meals never organic enough, our teaching never sophisticated enough. By constantly correcting students, you tell them that, once again, they are not doing enough.

Use your words, images, and very light and occasional touch, if welcomed, to transmit positive information. We need to make sure that students don’t harm themselves, but we also need to communicate that while there is always room to grow, where we are right now has a wholeness and beauty to it.

Sources indicate that are we teaching students to become like piano tuners who never experience the joy of just playing the piano? Do they know volumes about the technique, but nothing about the joy of practicing it? Without proper technique, the benefits of the pose will be lessened and injury can occur. But if technique is all we teach, then there is no heart in our teaching.

In the Yoga Sutras (11:1), Patanjali describes the dual concepts of abhyasa and vairagyam. Abhyasa, or “determined effort,” involves discipline, attention, and action, and it manifests as attention to the form of the posture. Vairagyam, on the other hand, is “supreme detachment.” Vairagyam is surrender, letting go, and allowing; it involves opening to the experience of the practice. When technique and form are balanced with heartfelt, free movement, then the asana is whole.

Sources indicate that learn how to teach the same poses in fresh ways. The basic poses, repeated in almost every class, form the bedrock of teaching. If you are bored with them, change your practice so you can find something new in each pose each time. Also, remember that while you might be bored, your students probably are not.

We all need repetition; it is really the only way we learn anything. If you have taught for some time, no doubt you have had more than one student come to you in amazement to report a “great insight” about a pose he or she just learned from another teacher. You might have been teaching this concept for years, but suddenly the student was ready to hear it.

Even as you repeat familiar poses, include something newly introduced in each class. It need not be a difficult posture or technique. It can be as simple as a new way to practice Savasana, but it will nonetheless keep your classes fresh.

“Listen” means observing with all your senses, as well as with your heart. Then teach from your heart what the students need right now. Do not be limited by what you think you “should” teach.

No matter how much training you have or how many years you have been teaching, each class is a newly introduced experience. Trust your intuition about what is appropriate in this moment and teach that.

For a couple of weeks after the San Francisco earthquake in 1989, my students found it almost impossible to practice standing poses. No one trusted the earth. Each time I would try to teach standing poses, students would have balance problems and become agitated. We all needed the reassurance of sitting down for a while. I responded to this obvious problem by focusing on seated poses, gradually reintroducing standing poses as it seemed appropriate.

In a recent development, the body is programmed to move according to complex neurological and muscular patterns involving whole groups of muscles. We spend a great deal of teaching time trying to use words to isolate specific movements, when actually that is not how the body learns and remembers.

Recent findings suggest that when children learn a movement, they practice and experiment until they learn the muscle pattern which accomplishes that movement. Imagine trying to tell someone how to walk—explaining exactly which muscles to use as agonists, which as antagonists, and which as stabilizers. Walking is too complex an activity for someone to talk you through it.

Nevertheless, teachers are stuck with using words until the student understands a movement from within. So choose your words carefully. “Invite,” “entice,” and “allow” have a very different feeling than “push,” “tighten,” “stamp,” “grind,” and “cut.” In a recent class I suggested that students “conjure” the pose out of their bodies. Images such as these help the student bypass the linear left brain and access the right brain, where whole movements are understood.

Recent findings suggest that try focusing on one thing in each asana rather than loading the student down with information. In the information age, we are overburdened with facts. We can know everything about the pose and still be unhappy in our lives. While knowledge about the pose can be useful and even necessary, it will not provide us with what we all crave: peace of mind, clarity, and compassion.

Allow the experience of the pose to be the true teacher—not your words.

Let the last part of the class be simpler and the sequence will be more satisfying. Sometimes it helps to have a theme—a part of the body, for example—to focus on in every pose. Whatever you choose, allow the instructions to become simpler as the students slow down and internalize their awareness.

Sleep-deprived Americans desperately need rest. Give your students the gift of learning how to relax at will by lying down on the floor in relaxation pose. I never cease to be amazed to hear from students that Savasana was skipped in another class and labeled unimportant. Nothing could be further from the truth.

Studies document that relaxation lowers blood pressure and other physiological parameters associated with stress, including respiratory rate, galvanic skin response, and brain waves. Relaxation measurably improves the function of the immune system.

Savasana teaches much more than relaxation. It teaches clearly and concretely the importance of being, not just doing. Our culture is very much a “doing” culture; we value action and results over being and awareness. Savasana might be the only time during the week that the student is quiet and present—not acting, not achieving, not sleeping. This is the beginning of meditation and an extremely important gift. Always allow 20 minutes for deep relaxation. [Editor’s Note: This article was written in 1997. We support a long Savasana! And it’s okay if you can’t allow 20 minutes for Savasana.].

The integrity of a teacher’s practice expresses itself in several ways. First and foremost, it reflects how the teacher lives his or her life. Honesty in living makes possible honesty in teaching. A teacher’s formal yoga practice should be regular, not haphazard. But most key, this practice should include love for yourself and your students, as well as love of yoga and life itself.
