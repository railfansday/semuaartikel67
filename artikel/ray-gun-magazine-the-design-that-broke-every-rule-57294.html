Ray Gun Magazine: The Design That Broke Every Rule

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Ray Gun magazine launched in November 1992 and tore up the rulebook of editorial design, with David Carson turning type into image.

The premiere issue sets the tone on the cover alone. The masthead reads rAY GUn in oversized red letters, off-kilter and cropped by the page edge. A photocopied portrait of Henry Rollins is torn across the cover, and the contents run down the left side in a typewriter face. Publisher Marvin Scott Jarrett started the magazine with three people in the dining room of his one-bedroom apartment, and the design matches that beginning. It looks assembled by hand, not laid out on a grid. Blue duotone, scratched film, hand-scrawled headlines. The magazine looks the way the music sounds.

Carson was a former professional surfer with no formal design training, and he designed like it. His most famous move came in 1994, when he set an entire Bryan Ferry interview in Zapf Dingbats because he found the interview boring. The symbols fill the spread, with a green portrait of Ferry slumped in a chair and one giant red dingbat floating above it. His line on the work was simple: don't mistake legibility for communication. The same instinct runs through the first issue. The word RAVE is split across a spread in letters that fill the page. The headline for the rock star piece scatters its letters down the page. One article is piled into a solid block of black type. The page is a single expressive surface, and the reader has to work to read it. That friction was the design.

In a recent development, the texture comes from the making: photocopied collage, contact sheets, torn edges, duotone photography, type cut and pasted by hand. It reads as a zine printed at magazine scale, and that roughness carried the magazine past Carson too. He left after matter 30, the October 1995 David Bowie cover. Robert Hales took over, then Chris Ashworth took the design from 1997, later sharing the role with Amanda Sissons, with a harder, distressed look they called Swiss Grit.

In a recent development, the masthead kept changing color and weight, green on problem 12, white on black on the Perry Farrell cover. Ray Gun ran over 70 issues and closed in January 2000. Its influence outlived it. A generation of designers learned the grid was a choice, not a law, and Carson's book The End of Print carried the argument into design schools everywhere.
