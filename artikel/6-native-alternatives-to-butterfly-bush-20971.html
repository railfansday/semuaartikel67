6 Native Alternatives to Butterfly Bush

Butterfly bush (Buddleja davidii, Zones 5–9) might seem like an easy choice for a pollinator bed, given the name, but is it the right shrub for your garden? There are a couple of reasons you might want to think twice and consider native butterfly bush alternatives instead.

In a recent development, originally from Asia, butterfly bush is a prolific bloomer that attracts pollinators like no tomorrow. Unfortunately, it is also a prolific seeder, easily spreading past its intended area and potentially outcompeting other plants. Even after the shrub is removed, its seeds can remain viable in the soil for years until conditions are favorable for germination.

Recent findings suggest that butterfly bush is listed as an invasive plant by several state and regional invasive-plant organizations across the Southeast. Although some cultivars on the market are advertised as sterile or seedless, their fertility varies, and some can still produce small amounts of viable seed. While low-fertility cultivars can reduce the risk of spreading, butterfly bush also lacks an vital benefit of many native plants: It does not serve as a host plant for native butterfly caterpillars.

The good news is that if you’re open to planting butterfly bush alternatives, there are plenty of beautiful flowering shrubs native to the Southeast that have both high visual appeal and benefits for native pollinators, including butterflies. Many also provide food and habitat beyond their flowering season. Here are just a few to get started:.

According to recent reports, blooming in late spring to early summer, false indigo bush produces distinctive spikes of purple flowers that attract bees, butterflies, and other pollinators. A wonderful nectar source for long-tongued pollinators and a host plant for several butterflies, including the silver-spotted skipper and gray hairstreak, it offers benefits besides its gorgeous blooms. Its tolerance of a range of moisture conditions makes it a versatile option for sunny garden spaces.

Virginia sweetspire produces fragrant white flower spikes in late spring, followed by colorful foliage in fall. Its adaptability to moist soils makes it an especially useful shrub for rain gardens and other sites where drainage can be a challenge. A nectar source for many bees and butterflies, it is also reported as a host plant for the American holly azure butterfly in the coastal Southeast.

In a recent development, its fragrant flowers attract butterflies, hummingbirds, and long-tongued bees. Blooming in late summer, when some other shrubs have finished flowering, summersweet offers an additional nectar source later in the growing season. Summersweet is also worth thinking about for gardens with consistently moist soil. Although it tolerates full sun, it generally needs more moisture in sunnier locations.

This deciduous native azalea brings soft pink to white flowers to woodland gardens in mid-spring, often before its foliage has fully emerged. An excellent nectar source for many pollinators, including hummingbirds and butterflies, pinxterbloom azalea is particularly well suited to partially shaded garden beds with acidic soil.

Spicebush blooms in early spring, producing small yellow flowers before its leaves emerge. Its early flowers also provide nectar for pollinators, while the bright red fall fruits on female plants, when a male plant is nearby for pollination, offer food for birds. A host plant for the spicebush swallowtail, this native shrub also brings pretty yellow foliage to the fall landscape.

Sources indicate that flame azalea earns its name from its showy flowers, which bloom in shades of yellow, orange, and red from late spring to early summer. A terrific general nectar source for many pollinators, particularly butterflies, this deciduous shrub adds striking seasonal color to woodland gardens and other partially shaded spaces.

Recent findings suggest that if you’re only interested in the “butterfly” aspect of butterfly bush and not the “bush” aspect, choosing a great plant becomes even easier. There is an entire world of pollinator plants to explore, from host plants for the Southeast to nectar-rich native perennials and annuals. By incorporating both nectar plants for adults and host plants for caterpillars, you can create a garden that supports butterflies throughout their life cycle.
