At Jaguar Land Rover, the word Sport essentially means “fun size.” Full-size Range Rover? That’s just a Range Rover. The wee version that’s a bit nippier through the bends? That’ll be the Range Rover Sport. Now, with the Defender getting its own scaled-down Sport version, it’s going to be electric.

Now, there’s still a chance that this smaller Defender model could be named something other than Sport, as JLR head PB Balaji has been a little cagey about explicitly calling the upcoming car Defender Sport. Speaking on JLR’s second earnings call, he confirmed that the factory that presently builds the Discovery Sport and Evoque will be manufacturing the upcoming Defender and the Range Rover GT starting next year.

It has been revealed that the two cars will share JLR’s fresh Electrified Modular Architecture platform, which is designed to support 800-volt electric vehicles and also hybrids. However, Balaji indicated that only the battery-electric small Defender would be ready to go by the end of next year.

Pretty much everything in JLR’s range (leaving out the rebooting Jaguar brand) has pretty impressive off-road chops, but a Defender Sport has to be particularly special. It’s one thing to offer a smaller, more agile Range Rover Sport that has most of the luxury of the full-size vehicle with better agility. This newly introduced model will be the first in a planned series of vehicles that will establish Defender not just as a single product but as a brand.

JLR probably can’t get away with how Ford positions the Bronco and the Bronco Sport as a Jeep Wrangler competitor and an Escape crossover, respectively, with more personality. The Defender Sport might be smaller than its substantial brother, but it’s going to need to offer up most of the same off-road chops.

Sources indicate that as distinct from the electric version of the Mercedes-Benz G-class, which tucked its batteries onboard in such a way as to offer up all of the same ability as the combustion-powered versions, the Defender Sport’s EMA setup utilizes underfloor batteries. JLR’s Mark Cameron, who heads up the Discovery and Defender brands, admitted that there may be a slight reduction in wheel travel and articulation versus a full-size Defender.

Then again, few owners are using the full capabilities of their Defenders on the regular, even if they really are landed English gentry roving around their untamed huge tracts of land. A lot of the Defender’s ability isn’t just mechanical doggedness, like an old Series Land Rover, but clever terrain-management software. All of that suite of abilities should port over.

A smaller Defender, even one that’s perhaps not quite ready to take on the Dakar Rally, does sound like plenty of fun, especially on narrower trails. Given how successful the current Defender model has been for JLR as a whole, it probably can’t get here fast enough.
