It has been revealed that here at Backpacker, we respect old-school cool. It’s not that we’re Luddites or avoid latest ways of doing things—from lightweight internal-frame backpacks to gourmet freeze-dried meals, there’s no denying that most modern backpacking developments have made our experiences better. But there’s something about seeing a hiking buddy solve a problem with a retro skill that we admire. It reveals knowledge, competence, and the kind of self-reliance that every backpacker secretly aspires to.

According to recent reports, calling the following six low-tech, high-technique skills “lost” might be a stretch—they definitely still have their fans, and every Backpacker editor has used them at least a few times. But they aren’t part of the average beginner hiker’s repertoire anymore, and many hikers go their whole careers without using them. Start practicing them now to level up your skills for next season.

According to recent reports, whether they’re tying their tent’s guylines or their shoelaces, too many backpackers reach for the same overhand knot; it mostly works, but it lacks a certain… elegance? While you don’t need a Boy Scout’s skill at knotcraft to go backpacking, learning to use the right knot for each job will help you finish camp setup promptly and break it down without spending ten minutes coaxing apart the granny knot you’ve welded into your cord. Here are a couple to get you started (check out our guide to the 5 Essential Backpacking Knots for videos and in-depth descriptions):.

Slippery Backpacker’s Hitch: Good for hanging bear bags or rigging ridgelines for tarps, and comes undone with a pull. Wrap the rope at least three times around a tree or post, then tuck a bight of the loose end under the last loop.

It has been revealed that bowline Knot: A bombproof method for creating a strong loop. Make a loop in the rope; take the loose end and bring it up through the loop, around the rope, and back down through the loop. (You may or may not catch those of us who learned in school muttering “rabbit comes out of the hole, goes around the tree, and goes back in the hole” while we tie it.) Finish with an overhand and a half-hitch.

It has been revealed that sheet Bend: Need to attach two lengths of cord of different diameters? Create a bight with the bigger cord; bring the end of the thinner cord up through, wrap it around the back of the bight, and run it through the loop it creates.

Lightweight, direct-from-manufacturer shelters and quilts mean that there’s little practical reason to sew your own kit nowadays. However, for those who do like to get handy, the proliferation of patterns for sale and online tutorials on places like YouTube means that it’s never been easier to join the MYOG community. Plus, much like growing your own vegetables, sewing a few pieces yourself will give you a fresh appreciation for the ingenuity and labor that goes into making your gear.

Instead of jumping straight into the deep end with a high-investment piece like a tent or quilt, start small: A “ditty bag” is perfect for holding small items or replacing a torn or lost stuff sack. See our step-by-step instructions from our Spring 1973 issue, which is easy to sew without a machine. All you’ll need is ¾-yard of ripstop nylon, 30 inches of cord, needles, and thread.

Sources indicate that camp baking is everything modern backpacking meals aren’t: messy, ingredient-heavy, and slow. But there’s a kind of magic in coaxing a loaf of bread or a pizza into existence in the middle of the woods. It may not be an efficient way to eat, but it’s a rewarding skill, and as a party trick, guaranteed to impress your backcountry partners.

The most traditional method is to bake in a Dutch oven using campfire. One easier and lighter way to start: Make a cupcake on your camp stove, which is satisfying and near-foolproof. You’ll need a single-wall steel or titanium mug, a pot significant enough to fit it inside, and a lid, plate, or aluminum foil to cover it. Empty a packet of microwaveable cake mix into the mug, add three tablespoons of water, and stir. Drop three small rocks into the bottom of your pot, balance the mug on top of them, and add enough water to the pot that it reaches the level of the cake mix. (If the mug starts to float, you’ve added too much.) Cover and simmer for 20 minutes, then enjoy straight out of the mug. Bonus: also works with pancake mix.

It has been revealed that even if most hikers have moved on to screened-in lightweight tents, flat tarps still have dedicated fans. Besides the unbeatably low weight, there’s a simplicity to making a shelter from a rectangle of fabric that we can still appreciate. (Looking for your first? Borah’s Silpoly Solo Tarps are solid, and at less than $90, eminently affordable.).

There are innumerable ways to pitch a tarp, but the simple plow point shelter is a good place to start. Stake out three corners of your tarp, then use one of your trekking poles to raise the fourth, tensioning it with a guyline. (Pro tip: The first time you do this will take some trial and error to get a good pitch, so give yourself extra time in camp.) For extra dirtbag points, use a 9 x 10 scrap of Tyvek instead; create the guy-out points by pinching a bit of the material around a smooth pebble and tying off the base with a cord.

Recent findings suggest that whittling is one of those camp pastimes that’s largely fallen by the wayside—but to be fair, today’s backpackers are less likely than their predecessors to spend a lot of time kicking around camp. Still, basic knife skills are good to have in your pocket—you never know when you may need to carve a replacement for a snapped stake or shave tinder to build a fire—and whittling is a good, low-stakes way to practice those skills.

Carving a spoon is an easy project (and a useful one, if you forget your own utensil). Split a piece of deadfall, preferably still green. Carve your spoon’s outline, then hollow out the bowl. (You may have an easier time carving a shovel-shaped bowl than a traditional round one with a typical straight pocketknife.).

According to recent reports, once upon a time, planning a route for our magazine meant taking a walk down to the small library of guidebooks and topo maps we kept in the office and poring through them until we found the trail we were looking for. The world has changed since then—today’s mapping tools are quick and reliable for assembling a plan—but sketching out a trip the old-school way is still a good exercise for your brain. Plus, it can help prevent the kind of careless line-following that regularly gets hikers into trouble.

Recent findings suggest that picking up an expert-written guidebook for an area you initiative on visiting regularly is always a good investment. Otherwise, the public library is your friend: Your local branch likely stocks an array of up-to-date guides, or can obtain them for you via interlibrary loan. Instead of carrying the whole thing, xerox the pages you need and jot points of interest and notes right on the copies. Pair with a compass and a current topo map (your library may stock those, too), and don’t forget to check with rangers or local hikers for the latest beta before you set out.
