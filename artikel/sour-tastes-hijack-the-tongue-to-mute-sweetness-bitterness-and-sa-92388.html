In a recent development, biting into a tart lemon bar or sipping a sweet-and-sour beverage delivers a complex blend of chemical stimuli to the tongue simultaneously. For decades, neuroscientists have worked to determine whether the brain mixes these individual flavor signals centrally, or whether taste buds themselves edit and shape flavor combinations before a single electrical impulse ever leaves the tongue.

In a recent development, in a study published in Journal of Neuroscience, neuroscientists led by Dr. Stephen Roper at the University of Miami Miller School of Medicine recorded gustatory nerve signaling in mice exposed to mixed taste solutions.

The findings reveal that sourness exerts a dominant, suppressive effect over other primary tastes right inside the taste bud, driven by local serotonin signaling.

To trace how mixed tastes travel from peripheral receptors to the central nervous system, Roper and colleagues measured neural responses along the gustatory pathway while exposing mice to single tastes and multi-taste mixtures.

To uncover how sourness mutes competing tastes so early in the sensory pipeline, the investigators zeroed in on serotonin, a neurotransmitter released inside each taste bud by specialized sour-responsive receptor cells (Type III taste cells).

Manipulating local serotonin levels directly controlled the strength of flavor suppression:.

These results demonstrate that taste buds function as miniature computational hubs, using paracrine serotonin signaling to inhibit neighboring receptor cells before gustatory information is handed off to cranial nerves.

“Taste buds not only respond to and transduce taste compounds, they also integrate and manipulate gustatory signals before transmitting them to the brain,” explained Roper. “The neurons that collect signals produced in taste buds are themselves organized into two large groups or clusters, neurons that convey gustatory signals associated with pleasure and neurons that convey unpleasant taste signals.”.

By establishing how peripheral serotonin signaling shapes flavor dominance and how downstream neurons sort palatability, the study lays groundwork for identifying the genetic signatures of pleasure- versus aversion-coding taste neurons, and exploring whether targeted molecular interventions could one day modulate taste perception in clinical nutrition or sensory disorders.

Why do sour flavors overpower sweet, salty, and bitter tastes?

Researchers at the University of Miami found that sour-responsive cells inside taste buds release the neurotransmitter serotonin. This local serotonin dampens the signals of neighboring sweet, bitter, and salty receptor cells before the taste information even reaches the nerves leading to the brain.

Do taste buds simply send raw flavor data to the brain?

In a recent development, no. The study highlights that taste buds act like miniature processing centers that actively mix, filter, and modify taste signals locally before transmitting them to sensory neurons.

How do taste nerves organize flavors on their way to the brain?

According to the researchers, the sensory neurons collecting signals from taste buds are broadly organized into two major functional groups: neurons that carry pleasant taste signals and neurons that carry unpleasant taste signals.

Sour Suppresses Sweet, Salty, and Bitter: Implications for Signal Processing in Taste Buds.

It has been revealed that taste is typically encountered as complex blends, yet how peripheral taste organs process heterogeneous mixtures remains poorly understood. Here we asked how sour stimuli influence the encoding of sweet, salty, and bitter tastes in primary gustatory sensory neurons.

It has been revealed that using in vivo calcium imaging in mice of either sex to record neurons in geniculate ganglia during oral delivery of tastants, we compared responses to naturally occurring taste stimuli presented alone versus in binary mixtures that activate acid (sour)-sensing (Type III) taste bud cells. Adding citric acid or HCl robustly suppressed sucrose-evoked responses in geniculate ganglion neurons, even though acid itself did not activate those neurons.

In a recent development, acid-induced suppression generalized to bitter and salty tastes, which, like sucrose, are believed to activate Type II taste cells. Additional stimuli that activate acid-sensing taste cells (NH4Cl, KCl) also reduced sucrose responses, supporting a common mechanism linked to Type III cells. Manipulating levels of serotonin (a paracrine transmitter released by Type III cells) in taste buds altered taste suppression: increasing serotonin with 5-hydroxytryptophan enhanced acid-induced suppression whereas depleting serotonin with para-chlorophenylalanine weakened it.

Recent findings suggest that finally, population analyses, including UMAP dimensionality reduction, indicate that taste suppression is not limited to a particular subset of gustatory sensory neurons. The findings are consistent with serotonin-dependent paracrine inhibition within taste buds as a contributor to sour-driven suppression in taste mixtures and reveal a compression of gustatory information into hedonic categories as early as at the level of primary sensory neurons.
