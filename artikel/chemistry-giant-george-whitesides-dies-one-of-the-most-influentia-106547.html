According to recent reports, george Whitesides, a towering figure in chemistry who pushed chemists to cross boundaries to solve global problems, died on 19 November. He was 87.

His son George, a US congressman representing California, confirmed Whitesides’s passing on the social media platform X, saying that the chemist died at home surrounded by family.

Sources indicate that whitesides made landmark contributions to many fields: he was a pioneer in patterning surfaces with molecules, a technique that enabled ‘lab on a chip’ diagnostic devices, advances in nanotechnology and more. He prided himself on switching research interests often, telling students, “If somebody else is working on something, don’t work on it.”.

In a recent development, following the news of the chemist’s passing, messages celebrating his impact flooded online platforms. “I am so saddened to hear of his passing,” Zhenan Bao, a chemical engineer at Stanford University in California, wrote on LinkedIn. “George showed us that the most key problems don’t respect the lines between disciplines. He moved freely across chemistry, materials, biology, and engineering.”.

It has been revealed that whitesides trained hundreds of scientists who came through his laboratory, first at Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) and then at Harvard University, both in Cambridge. But he also inspired many others, says Joseph DeSimone, a polymer scientist at Stanford. In traditional chemistry departments, “there’s often a lot of pressure to stay in your lane, and he made it okay not to. In fact, he made it exciting not to”, DeSimone says.

Whitesides was born in Louisville, Kentucky, in 1939. One of his first experiences with chemistry was as a teenager, cleaning glassware at his father’s chemical engineering firm in his hometown.

After a bachelor’s degree in chemistry at Harvard, he enroled for a PhD at California Institute of Technology in Pasadena, studying how the workhorse chemicals known as Grignard reagents form and behave.

In a recent development, by 1963, Whitesides became a faculty member at MIT, where he would switch to studying the fundamentals of self-assembled monolayers (SAMs)1. These are tightly packed coatings of organic compounds that are a single-molecule thick, formed spontaneously because of forces drawing the molecules to a surface and to one another.

In a recent development, george Whitesides and his research group pioneered low-cost, paper-based microfluidic 'lab-on-a-chip' devices. Credit: Volker Steger/Science Photo Library.

From there, Whitesides saw ways of using SAMs to easily and cheaply create patterned surfaces — which at the time were often made with complicated lithography techniques involving light and ‘stencils’. He would go on to invent soft lithography2, which instead uses polymer stamps to transfer micro- and nanoscopic patterns of molecules to surfaces.

Industry observers note that this became an “enabling” research tool, claims John Rogers, a materials scientist at Northwestern University in Evanston, Illinois, who was a postdoc with Whitesides at Harvard. The ability to easily pattern surfaces opened the door to creating 3D nanostructures, microfluidics and much more. It also “democratized” the ability to do this type of research, Rogers adds, because you no longer needed sophisticated cleanroom facilities and etching techniques to create such materials.
