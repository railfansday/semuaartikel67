Snowshoes are some of the oldest winter gear known to humankind—the tech literally has prehistoric origins, with rudimentary versions dating back nearly 6,000 years ago. Today, the top ‘shoes boost flotation, grip, and ease of use, all while shaving weight. Over the years, our testers have tried hundreds of models in waist-deep drifts, through wet, cement-like snow, and on steep, icy slopes, all in the name of narrowing down the top of the class. The Revo Explore snowshoes from MSR have been a tester favorite for over a decade: They’re lighter, more flexible, and grippier than most snowshoes on the market today. Credit a hybrid construction, which combines a tough, injection-molded plastic decking with a jagged-edged steel frame and pivoting toe crampon. The 2026 model keeps last year’s upcoming binding—the TPU rubber stretch mesh Paradigm—which continues to impress testers with its security and comfort. The ridged, steel frame boosts side traction, while the two front toe crampons and a small, tooth-lined crossbar provide enough bite to stick to icy hills and steep ravines. Our Oregon tester reached for them in everything from deep powder to spring crust in Eastern Oregon’s Wallowa and Blue Mountains—when other people weren’t using them. “My husband makes fun of snowshoeing (he’s a skier) and claims to hate it, but I caught him happily using this pair,” she mentioned.

The Highlander Instinct takes the snowshoe crampon and dials it up to 11. Most snowshoes have a basic underfoot crampon for some traction on slopes, and a few brands add teeth around the edges. The Highlander Instinct uses a stainless steel crampon under the toe that’s as long and sharp as some mountaineering crampons we’ve used, plus lateral grips around the edge of the chassis and mini serrated grip bars at strategic grip points across the platform. Over three days of steep, icy hills in Rocky Mountain National Park, our tester didn’t slip once, aided, in part, by the easy-to-use heel lifts. The rockered profile and wasp-waist shape allow a natural stride and help the crampons bite, no matter the incline. That narrow waist and tapered tail kept these easy to walk in, too, without the waddling common in most wider snowshoes. They floated well in a few inches of new snow, but drifts of more than a foot proved challenging. The BOA bindings were easy to adjust even with bulky gloves, and once dialed in proved extremely comfortable and secure.

For those who loathe fiddly snowshoe bindings, the Symbioz Hyperflex Instinct is a godsend. While several snowshoes incorporate BOA closures into their design, few are quite as simple as the Instinct’s. The design: a single knob that tightens the toe, sides, and heel using interlinked cables that run through the perimeter of the binding, eliminating any and all trailhead exasperation. It takes fewer than 10 seconds to step into and secure the snowshoe, but the Instinct stayed glued to our boots all day long, even while walking on bumpy, uneven terrain in Colorado’s Indian Peaks Wilderness. Another characteristic we loved: flexible decking made of injection-molded polypropylene. The pliable material gave us just enough torsional flexibility to enhance comfort and reduce ankle-twisting on uneven surfaces. While sidehilling through a labyrinth of postholes in Rocky Mountain National Park, eight lateral stainless steel teeth kept our Colorado tester upright and stable.

The Atlas Montane’s lightweight PVC decking and an aluminum tube frame combine for a classic deep-powder snowshoe. Our six-foot-two, 200-pound Colorado tester took the 30-inch version for a winter ascent of Longs Peak and floated easily over two to three feet of latest snow, even with a pack full of heavy climbing gear. Even the smaller decks (down to 23 inches) wowed our testers with easy traverses of powdery alpine meadows and serious forest drifts. Underfoot crampons and carbon steel lateral grips at the snowshoe’s midpoint kept footing sure and steady even on icy sidehills.

In a recent development, the Montane is wider than most of the other snowshoes we tested in both the men’s and women’s models, which gives this ‘shoe its superior flotation, but it does take a moment to adapt your gait and stop banging the snowshoes together. Our testers found the strap bindings fiddly with thick ski gloves on, but once the two straps were latched in place, they stayed there for the whole trip. On powder days, the flotation more than makes up for the simple, slightly inconvenient binding. If you’re planning off-trail travel in deep snow, especially if you’ll be carrying heavy overnight packs, these are the snowshoes for you.

Flat, uncomplicated terrain doesn’t require too much from a pair of snowshoes. Simple, comfortable bindings and a deck that provides adequate flotation are all you need. If icy slopes or deep snow aren’t in your future, save money with a budget pair made from heavier molded plastic and less aggressive traction.

It has been revealed that on the other end of the spectrum are snowshoes built for challenging terrain—slushy hills, narrow singletrack, or mixed rock and ice. A secure wrap around the heel and/or multiple straps along the foot are important for stability. Quick binding systems, like those that utilize a BOA twist lock, are helpful when taking snowshoes on and off in precarious terrain and a huge time-saver compared with fiddly three-strap setups. Snowshoes geared towards mountainous ascents will have a wider variety of spikes and crampons underfoot—and in more abundance—to offer grip both horizontally and laterally, and to bite through different types of ice. A heel lift that provides about 2 inches of lift (that’s easy to flick open and closed with either gloved hands or a trekking pole) is a must for giving your feet and calves an assist on the steeps.

In deep, fresh powder, you’ll want wider, longer snowshoes (or those that come with extensions) for superior flotation. On the flipside, if you’re headed out on a weight-conscious backpacking trip and/or proposal to strap the snowshoes to your pack for much of the trip, prioritize lightweight construction, like a pair made from aluminum and TPU-coated nylon, for reducing fatigue on-foot and outside your pack.
