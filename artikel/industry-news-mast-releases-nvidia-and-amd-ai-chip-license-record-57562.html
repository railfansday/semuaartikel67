It has been revealed that house Foreign Affairs Committee Chairman Brian Mast on November 19, 2026, released Commerce Department licensing records for NVIDIA H200 and AMD MI325X chips sought by Chinese companies. They show applications for 3,444,160 chips and approvals covering 879,868 chips across 21 applications as of May 11, 2026, with every approved entry still unshipped.

Sources indicate that mast acted under the Export Control Reform Act after the committee determined that withholding the information was contrary to the national interest. The records cover applications to the Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) for exports, re-exports and in-country transfers. Their company scope includes entities located or headquartered in China and entities whose ultimate parent is headquartered there.

Industry observers note that the committee also holds other BIS-provided applications not fully reflected in that summary, including a December 19, 2025, NVIDIA application to export 445,000 H200 chips to Tencent. Because the committee possesses additional material, the disclosed totals describe the summary’s contents rather than every application in its possession.

The four-page license-decisions table highlights that authorizations extended beyond those four groups. Other approved entries name ByteDance, JD, Didi, miHoYo, Li Auto, Tesla’s Shanghai operation, Great Wall Motor and Kuaishou, among others.

Not every application was approved. The table lists one rejection, AMD’s proposed 75,000-chip transfer to UCloud, while requests involving Kingsoft, High-Flyer, Nio, Geely and Meituan remained marked submitted. Separately, two BIS licenses authorize various chips, including the H200, for Lenovo but exclude locations in China. They permit server manufacturing and research and development for products such as data-center racks outside China.

All but two NVIDIA applications provided to the committee list Maginfra, Beijing Latest Matrix Information Technologies, ZTE Kangxun Telecom and Lenovo or its subsidiaries as entities that “may install the systems in the datacenter.” Other entities appearing in that role on multiple NVIDIA applications include Aivres Systems, XFusion Technologies International, Cloudnine Information Technologies and Changkuai Computing Information Industry (Beijing), also called SmoothCompute.

Industry observers note that some of these companies also appeared on multiple AMD applications as ultimate consignees, meaning the listed recipients of the chips. Every NVIDIA and AMD application stated that its proposed transfer posed a low risk of diversion.

Mast characterized the NVIDIA requests as seeking to depend on possible chip-smuggling fronts; the narrower documentary statement attributed diversion-risk questions about Maginfra, Beijing Newly introduced Matrix and Aivres to public reports.

For export-control oversight, the disclosure makes enforcement as notable as approval volume: applicants’ low-risk assessments can be examined against the named consignees and installation networks, while delivery and compliance with license terms remain separate from authorization.
