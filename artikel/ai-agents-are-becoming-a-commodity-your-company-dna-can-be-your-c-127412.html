AI Agents Are Becoming a Commodity. Your Company DNA Can Be Your Competitive Advantage

🌐 Source: Unite • 📅 25 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 6 min read (1144 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

  • Most organizations assume that the solution to this problem is a better AI model.
  • According to McKinsey, nearly nine in ten respondents say their organizations regularly use AI in at least one business function.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Imagine two banks using AI agents to handle customer inquiries. One has built its reputation on flexibility, waiving fees and showing empathy when customers face financial hardship. The other prides itself on transparency and consistency, applying the same clear rules to everyone. When a customer disputes an overdraft fee, both agents might provide the same generic response unless they’ve been trained to reflect their bank’s approach to customer service.

Most organizations assume that the solution to this problem is a better AI model. But the real issue is often a lack of organizational context.

According to McKinsey, nearly nine in ten respondents say their organizations regularly use AI in at least one business function. While widespread adoption promises efficiency and productivity gains, it also makes technology less of a differentiator. As AI agents take on more work, make decisions and interact with customers on a company’s behalf, an key question emerges: Do those agents truly understand the company they’re working for?

Sources indicate that microsoft’s 2026 Work Trend Index highlights the growing integration of AI agents into organizational workflows and the importance of human judgment in how that work is designed. Yet, many businesses are building on the same foundation models and AI platforms. Without the right organizational context, their agents risk looking and sounding alike, eroding the qualities that make each customer experience different. The difference often lies in the decisions employees make every day, drawing on experience, customer knowledge and an understanding of what their organization values. These are the nuances that generic AI systems may struggle to replicate without additional guidance.

What if a firm could encode its corporate identity into its AI systems? The challenge for organizations is ensuring these agents reflect what makes the company unique: its secret sauce and competitive edge.

AI outputs can be technically correct but still fall short of how a company expects work to be done. For example, an AI agent at a luxury hotel responds to a guest’s request for a restaurant recommendation. It books a table at a highly rated restaurant without asking about the guest’s dining preferences or dietary restrictions. While the reservation might be made rapidly, the guest could end up somewhere with few suitable options, an experience that reflects poorly on the hotel.

According to recent reports, agents need more than basic training. Like employees, they require ongoing guidance, performance monitoring and coaching to ensure their actions reflect the company’s approach to customer service.

Beyond workflows and policies, AI agents need to understand the tech giant’s DNA: the institutional knowledge, expertise, decision-making principles, culture and values that shape how work gets done.

With AI capabilities becoming increasingly commoditized, manufacturer DNA becomes more valuable. Used right, AI should amplify what differentiates a company, not dilute it. That means teaching agents how work actually gets done, including the informal practices and judgment calls that contribute to success.

AI agents can also help preserve this knowledge as organizations grow, ensuring it remains accessible even as teams change.

According to recent reports, embedding organization DNA into AI doesn’t necessarily require overhauling existing systems. Leaders can start with several practical steps.

According to recent reports, define success metrics a competitor couldn’t copy: If agents are measured only on resolution rates and handling time, organizations risk creating the same experience as everyone else. Leaders should define what success looks like for their company, whether that’s helping customers feel supported during financial hardship or delivering the consistency they expect. Those differences should shape how agent performance is measured.

Sources indicate that codify the judgment calls, not just the rules: Mission statements and standard operating procedures provide a starting point, but company DNA often demonstrates up in how employees handle exceptions. When do they make an exception to a policy? How do they verify information or draw on past experiences? Coaching notes, quality reviews and conversations handled by top performers can help capture this knowledge and make it accessible to AI agents. Organizations can then use these examples to show agents how experienced employees navigate situations that don’t have a straightforward answer.

Map how work actually gets done: Leaders can create a “work graph” that captures how work moves across a team, including how customer support teams prioritize issues or managers make decisions. This should include the informal workarounds employees use when established processes fall short. These workarounds can reveal gaps between formal procedures and the realities of day-to-day work. By documenting these practices, organizations can help agents understand not just what gets done, but how and why.

Write down how to break a tie: Rules can’t anticipate every situation. Agents need guidance on how to navigate trade-offs, such as balancing policy with customer goodwill or speed with empathy. These principles should also define when an agent needs to involve a human, so escalation becomes a designed decision, never a failure.

Onboard agents like new hires and let your best people grade them: Before wider deployment, organizations should put agents through the same kinds of scenarios used to train new employees, including role-play exercises, difficult customer conversations and situations requiring. Experienced employees can review these interactions and identify where an agent’s response falls short of how the firm would approach the situation.

According to recent reports, make performance reviews a two-way coaching process: Review AI agent performance regularly, and have your people update its guidance as products, policies and customer expectations change. In return, AI interactions reveal where your people need help. For example, if the AI keeps escalating billing disputes and those calls still go unresolved, that’s a clear coaching priority. The result is a continuous feedback loop: people make the AI better, and the AI makes people better.

In a recent development, in the coming years, powerful AI agents will be available to nearly every tech giant. Those that codify and embed their identity into AI will be better positioned to scale without sacrificing what sets them apart.

Embedding company DNA is only the first step. Organizations must continuously guide and improve how agents perform, so they reinforce what makes the company different. Just as organizations evolve, so must their AI agents.
