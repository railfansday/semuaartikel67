Latest Update: A Minimalist House in Italy Makes the Case for Less

Sources indicate that the friendship between John Pawson and Noona Smith-Petersen began in Latest York City in a very 1990s way: working for Calvin Klein. Pawson, then an architectural wunderkind from the United Kingdom, and Smith-Petersen, a young Danish-Italian communications manager for the fashion house, found themselves collaborating on the opening of the brand’s first flagship on Madison Avenue.

According to recent reports, pawson had already earned a reputation for restraint. His earliest residential works (a Mallorca summer house for collectors, an art dealer’s London apartment) provided a series of simple surfaces, plinths, and frames—for objects, for vistas, and for life. The same was true at the Calvin Klein store, where designer and client amplified each other’s design approaches in a meeting of minimalist minds.

Furnished with Hans Wegner chairs and a Pawson-designed table, the dining area opens onto the land thanks to a sliding window wall.

The living area features a table and chairs by Poul Kjærholm.

A sauna is clad in blackened wood.

It has been revealed that pawson’s recent residence for Smith-Petersen and her husband, Enrico Erba Springorum, an Armani executive, makes another case for life with less. “The imprint came from childhood: the minimalism, the Danish design,” explains Smith-Petersen, who grew up outside Copenhagen. “Poul Kjærholm was our neighbor,” she adds, name-checking the widely influential midcentury modernist. There were also formative summers spent at the pared-back house her father designed on the Turkish peninsula of Çeşme. About the location, she recalls, “People stated, ‘You guys are crazy!’ But it was a place close to nature and the sea. And then later it became a destination.”.

A single sculptural tree dots the courtyard.

An oak bed with integrated bedside tables anchors the primary bedroom.

Recent findings suggest that ninety minutes south of Milan, the patchwork hills of the Val Luretta are far less isolated. Its proximity to the city has attracted editors, artists, and other creatives in search of space and quiet. That tranquility drew Smith-Petersen to the area when she was seeking land on which to build a compound with her younger brother, Henrik, for their families. (She and her husband have two adult sons.) Over the years, she and Pawson had maintained a friendship. “So when Henrik and I found the property, I had this crazy idea: Why don’t I ask John?” indicates Smith-Petersen. “My brother noted, ‘Forget it!’ But we went to visit John and his team in London and it kicked off from there.”.

The primary bath’s bespoke vanity, topped in a Grassi Pietri surface.

Smith-Petersen stands in the living area.

A view across the dining table toward the minimalist hearth.

The resulting structure, realized with project architect Giulio Marchesi, is the irst of two planned homes on the site, one for each sibling. Sitting long and low on the land, like an ivory piano key dropped onto the hillside, the single-story house frames expansive views of the Val Luretta. “The area of the countryside is so dramatic,” reflects Pawson, whose first visit to the site was in winter fog. “In the summer, it’s very different—it’s so lush.”.

According to recent reports, at some 2,500 square feet, the house is spacious but intimate, with Pawson’s trademark spareness and remarkable warmth. He designed it along one axis, a long corridor connecting the foyer to the open-plan kitchen, dining area, and living area, then bedrooms beyond. White-painted plaster walls and floors of pale-gray stone, sourced nearby in Vicenza, radiate calm. As Pawson explains, understanding the way the body and the eye move through a space is paramount—and can take time. “You’ve got to learn the choreography,” he notes. Living in and feeling the house is key.

Smith-Petersen and Pawson on-site.

The yearslong friendship Pawson and Smith-Petersen had forged helped him understand how she would use the space. There would be mornings at the Pawson-designed table, afternoons on the terrace or under one of the century-old oaks, and evenings making dinner in a kitchen brimming with Scandinavian tableware. In solitude or surrounded by family, there’s complete peace on offer.

The open-plan dining area and kitchen.

“Most architects—contemporaries, colleagues—always say, ‘Oh, it’s impossible, a private house,’ ” says Pawson. The process can be “very emotional. And that’s what makes it good.” Luckily for Smith-Petersen, the primary feeling the house evokes is serenity. “I didn’t expect to be in such awe,” she says. “I was never a country person. Sometimes, I try to arrive a day before anyone else is here and do a silent retreat,” she adds. “It’s where I feel best now. It’s pure joy.”.
