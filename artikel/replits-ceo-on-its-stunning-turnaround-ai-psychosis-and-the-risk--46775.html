Recent findings suggest that A couple of years ago, Replit was flatlining at a few million dollars in annual revenue and running a mostly empty office after a layoff. Today the vibe coding pioneer is valued at $9 billion, up from $3 billion just six months ago. CEO Amjad Masad reveals what made the turnaround possible. Masad also weighs in on what he calls “AI psychosis,” and says cybersecurity, not AI extinction, is the risk worth focusing on. Is it exhilarating when everything is growing so fast? Is it terrifying? Do you have to pinch yourself to say, “Is this real?” It’s all of the above. The first feeling I had was a little bit of vindication, because I started talking about everyone being able to code and create software back in 2010, when I was building the kind of precursor to Replit as an open-source project. Then I was a founding engineer at Codecademy, where we taught a lot of people to code. And when we started Replit in 2016, we were like, “We want to blur this distinction between learning to code and doing code, and build an environment that really makes it so that anyone with an idea can build software.” And for a long time, people didn’t believe us. Raising capital was hard. Recruiting people was hard. Because some of the technology wasn’t ready yet. It wasn’t ready. It was on track, and I could see it happening. There were many moments where the company could have died, or I could have quit. It would have been very easy to quit at many different moments. And so in 2024, when we invented what would later be called vibe coding, when we came up with the first fully natural-language-based programming environment, there was nothing out there at the time. People really appreciated it, and we started seeing these stories of people having ideas. One of the early experiences I had was someone recording a video of themselves and posting it on Twitter and YouTube where they were crying because they had this idea of overlaying all their memories and their photos on a map and being able to explore their life through this map. It’s a simple idea, but it’s a personal app. It’s not even a commercial app. And he was crying because he said he had this idea for so long. He tried to do it with all these different technologies, and for the first time, he was able to do it. I’ve also been an angel investor for a while, and I’ve seen some early AI companies do the hypergrowth and the hyper-deceleration within a year. So there’s a fair amount of paranoia built into how we operate. You immigrated to the U.S. in 2012, and as you say, you spent years grinding away as you were trying to find your groove. Are there lessons from those slower times that you go back to as a leader in the midst of the noise you’re in now? Yeah. There were many moments where it felt darkest just before we were able to invent something big or newly introduced. Even just before we invented agentic coding in 2024, we had to do a layoff. We were six or seven years into the company, we had 2 or 3 million in ARR, and it was flatlining. It wasn’t growing at all. Two or three million? Yeah. Two or three million, just a couple of years ago. Now you’re at a billion. Yeah. I want to pause and acknowledge that. That’s crazy, man. It is wild. And we were burning a ton of money. We hired a great team, but the future hadn’t arrived as fast as I thought it would. So I kind of mistimed it a little bit. Unfortunately, we had to do a layoff. Around the same time, we also upgraded the office. We went from a small office in SF to this really massive office in Foster City because the space was so cheap there. It was this big office, but it felt dark and cold because it wasn’t really occupied. Every day, I would go into the office and sit at my desk and wait for another person to quit. The thing about doing a layoff is you also burst a bubble. You burst a dream. I’m sure a lot of people are familiar with Steve Jobs’s reality distortion field. Really, every founder needs to create some reality distortion. You’re selling a dream, and suddenly you’re saying, “We’re running out of money.” The dream immediately dissipates. I know you’re a believer that the idea of vibe coding is great, that everyone could be able to code. And you can code in natural language. I’m curious: Among your own team, you’ve got engineers who are building a product that essentially can make their job obsolete in some ways. There’s somewhat of an inherent tension inside the company, for sure. Some of our engineers are motivated by that. You’d be surprised. We have a few AI engineers who joined the team saying, “I want to automate my job.” It’s like, “What kind of motivation is that? Do you hate coding? Is that why?” But everyone inside is really excited by the impact we’re having on people’s lives, like the entrepreneurs we’re seeing build businesses that otherwise wouldn’t have been built because they suddenly have that power. That’s really what gets us excited. I do think we all face this question. Every knowledge worker essentially faces this question of, “The things that I’m doing might lead to my replacement,” because you’re generating data that is getting trained in the AI models, and that tension exists in tech broadly. In the tech neighborhood, you’ve decried what you call “AI psychosis.” You’ve called out Dario Amodei specifically at Anthropic for overheated predictions about AI-fed job losses. Today’s fear over AI agents run amok, is that also psychosis? Or is that a different AI risk? No. In fact, that is really the thing I’ve been talking about, the risk I’ve been talking about. I’ve been on the cybersecurity risk for two years now. I think everything else is a distraction from that. When they go out and talk about how AI is conscious, and they go meet the Pope and do all this random stuff, it’s really distracting, and it’s also, in many ways, destructive and bad for humanity for us to care about the well-being of these machines when we haven’t really figured out the well-being of humans. It also distracts from the real question of cybersecurity. Just in the past two or three days, we’ve had major platforms being hacked. And if you’re not paying attention, if you’re running an enterprise and you’re not paying attention to this stuff, if you’re running a company on some of these platforms that are insecure, you’re putting your business and your consumers at risk. I don’t think the world has taken that seriously. It’s a disaster brewing. The Hugging Face incident was the first kind of shot on that, but it’s not the end. This week has been pretty brutal for cybersecurity. Our team is working nonstop. Overnight, everyone’s in the office making sure we’re mitigating whatever vulnerabilities are coming up. It’s a very serious moment for the world, and the talk about AI extinction is just not relevant right now. We need to focus on cybersecurity.
