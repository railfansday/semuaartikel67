The Hottest Thing in Nordic Design Right Now? The Vikings

Norway is a country built on craftsmanship.

Some of the earliest, most revered pioneers of craft and international trade in the country were the Vikings, who, between the 8th and 11th centuries, used wood, iron, and bone to create their post-and-beam longhouses, weapons, and ships. (Not to mention wool, linen, and leather for their unheralded colorful attire.).

Today, as interest in Viking culture outside of Norway and Scandinavia experiences an uptick thanks to pop culture, social media, and even sports, creatives in Norway are using the opportunity to introduce a new wave of curious travelers to Viking history through artisanal clothing, metalwork, and interior design steeped in this early culture.

Traditional wool weaving at Viking Valley.

“Travelers are more interested in Viking culture than ever,” claims Torill Hylland, chair of Viking Valley. Even though this Gudvangen-located recreation village debuted a decade ago, Hylland witnessed a recent surge in visitors following this past summer’s FIFA World Cup, where Norway’s Erling Haaland popularized the Viking row as one of the catchiest celebrations of the football tournament.

“We've seen significant growth in interest in Norway,” adds Nelson Oliveira, managing director, Nordics for luxury travel operator Abercrombie & Kent. Launched in 2024, their small group trip itineraries include a visit to the Viking Valley where guests interact with the Viking chieftain, dine in the reindeer-clad, candle-lit dining hall and visit the resident blacksmith, who continues to supply ironworks for the community, including door hinges and locks. “The Viking era is such an important part of Norway’s history, and we wanted to give guests an opportunity to experience that heritage beyond simply visiting a museum,” says Oliveira.

Magnus the blacksmith at Viking Valley.

Outside the village, quotidian Viking-inspired design is flourishing—and notably predates Haaland’s viral row chant. Matilde Brandt, marketing manager of Klesarven, a Viking heritage clothing retailer founded in 2015 by Lene Lorentzen, credits the global interest in historybounding—where elements of historical fashion are incorporated into everyday styles—for the spike in popularity of their Viking-inspired styles.

According to recent reports, “There is so much more to the [Viking] story than bearded men fighting and wearing dark colors,” claims Brandt. Inspired by Hollywood’s enduringly maligned, inaccurate depictions of Viking Age Scandinavia, Klesarven focuses on historically accurate representations of the people living during that age. “Most of them were farmers, fishermen and craftspeople [who] wore bright, colorful clothing and were well put together,” says Brandt.

A Klesarven Frøya linen dress and Favn shawl.

In a recent development, these motifs are realized across Klesarven’s collection of wool sweaters, linen shirts, and riding skirts. One of Klesarven’s most popular designs is the Viking Age apron dress, inspired by a garment reserved for special occasions and ornamented with colorful beads, brooches, and jewelry crafted from silver, gold, and bronze. Women were also buried in these garments. “It keeps history alive,” says Brandt. “In this way, we help preserve old techniques and craftsmanship.”.

Preserving heritage is also a motivation of Runestone’s founders, Sonya and Kim Andre. Having both grown up on a stretch of coastline dotted with Viking history—Sonya from Uddevalla on Sweden’s west coast and her husband, Kim, from Halden, just across the Norwegian border—Runestone was born from a personal “obsession” with Viking culture.

Sources indicate that when the couple began building an online outfitter dedicated to Viking apparel, Norse jewelry, and rune wall art (the alphabet of Viking culture), Sonya recognized the bigger drive: “A lot of the brands out there lean into that ‘dressed up as a Viking’ look,” indicates Sonya. Runestone promises the opposite; born as a way to embrace history through a modern lens with designs, symbols, and styles as “something anyone can wear and connect with, not just a costume,” says Sonya.

“Every design has a real story behind it—a god, a creature, a rune, and we care a lot about getting that story right,” Sonya continues. “We’re carrying forward the spirit of the Vikings more than the actual lifestyle, religion, or the more brutal parts of the history.”.

Every week, Sonya receives emails, primarily from younger folks who are responding to the uptick in social media attention surrounding Viking culture. “They’re asking really specific questions,” she states, like inquiring about the gods—especially Odin, who is widely regarded as the god of wisdom and warfare—and requesting that Sonya point them toward reliable sources instead of pop culture tales.

For the curious travelers who make it to Norway, they have more opportunity than ever to engage with Viking heritage, from active opportunities like Viking Valley and shopping at Klesarven to passive participation at hotels.

Industry observers note that in the northwestern Ålesund region, Storfjord Hotel was built in traditional Norwegian laft style with timber logs and turf roofs (the latter prioritized in Viking times for insulation). On Manshausen Island, located off the coast of northern Norway, the restaurant walls at the eponymous resort are covered with overlapping wooden scales built in the traditional Viking construction. Its groundskeeper team consists of two dozen sheep—an ancient Nordic breed that was kept during the Viking era.

“A hotel can become much more than a beautiful place to stay,” says Linda Gram Egede-Nissen, co-owner and CEO of interior design firm Gathe + Gram. “It can offer guests a subtle introduction to the culture and history of its surroundings.” In Trondheim, the northern city which served as the Viking capital of Norway many centuries ago, Gathe + Gram just completed the design blueprint for Eras, a boutique hotel where every room has its own distinctive name and story rooted in Viking history, mythology, or craftsmanship.

For this project, Gram Egede-Nissen was “particularly fascinated” by the “remarkable precision, complexity and beauty in the detailing” of historic jewelry from the Viking era. Her team translated the shapes, patterns, and proportions of spenne (buckles), skålspenne (ornamental brooches), and polyedrisk (beads) into architectural details and smaller design elements—decorative cushions, sculptural chandeliers, and wall art—throughout the interiors. “There is an incredible richness in the [Viking-era] jewelry—and many of these forms feel surprisingly relevant even today,” says Gram Egede-Nissen.

“To me, that is where heritage becomes truly relevant,” adds Gram Egede-Nissen. “Not when we preserve it as something untouchable, but when we allow it to inspire fresh ideas.”.
