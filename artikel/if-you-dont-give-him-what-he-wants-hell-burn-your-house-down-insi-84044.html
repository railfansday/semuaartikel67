The flat rural acres of the Turweston Aerodrome, in the north-west corner of Buckinghamshire, are far enough from human habitation for anyone padlocked in a building out there to feel seriously threatened. And if the testimony of Sir Ben Ainslie is to be believed, that’s what can come of crossing swords with Sir Jim Ratcliffe and Ineos.

The ‘imprisonment’ of the four-time British Olympic champion in a building, with signs declaring ‘No Entry’ fixed to the external doors, was one of the more extraordinary allegations to emerge from the court case, settled this week, over a £180million yacht Ratcliffe and Ainslie built together for the 2024 America’s Cup.

Recent findings suggest that ratcliffe, who had withdrawn financial support, was demanding the team’s assets and intellectual property, and, according to court papers, Ineos representative Raymond Fellows, assisted by two security contractors, arrived at the offices of Ainslie's Athena Racing and applied his own chain and padlock. He’d been refused the keys by the building’s owner. Ainslie and his financial officer Matt Robinson managed to leave the building through the fire escape.

Ineos were applying petrochemicals business methods to sport, as usual. Fellows had also been employed by Ratcliffe to provide security assistance amid protests at Ineos’ controversial British fracking sites. The experienced security industry executive observed that ‘a small hard core group of activists’ was at work and that social media surveillance was useful, according to court papers from an earlier case.

Ineos denied the ‘false imprisonment’ of Ainslie, insisting that their employee had padlocked the building to prevent anyone entering, not knowing that someone was inside and that, in any case, there were ‘alternative exit routes’.

Industry observers note that but the firm did not entirely challenge Ainslie’s claim that Rob Nevin, chairman of Ratcliffe’s sports division and a Manchester United board member, had told him during a heated meeting about the disputed boat in Barcelona: ‘We have a phrase at Ineos: scorched earth. It means that if you don’t give Jim what he wants, he will burn your house down, or words to that effect.’.

Sir Jim Ratcliffe's relationship with his one-time friend Ben Ainslie ruptured after Ineos withdrew support for the latter's America’s Cup bid.

Ratcliffe's running of Manchester United is criticised by one anonymous source. ‘People like him see football on TV and just assume they can do it,’ they say.

That threat, which Ineos say was only made figuratively, illustrates how Ratcliffe runs his businesses with what several sources describe as a cult-like authority, full of such maxims and mantras laid down to staff who fear him.

That ‘cult of Sir Jim’ is born of the billionaire’s own immense air of superiority and is something he seems to have instilled in some of his most senior executives.

Nevin, 68, a long-standing Ratcliffe lieutenant, has informed some who have encountered him since he arrived at Old Trafford that he has completed 60 or more major ‘transactions’ – business deals – in the oil and gas industry. He is described as ‘not short on confidence’.

Nevin chairs Ineos Sport, the collection of acquisitions built up when Ratcliffe believed his firm could straddle multiple sports and succeed at all of them, in the style of Red Bull. Ratcliffe disastrously installed his brother, Bob, as chief executive at OGC Nice, the second of the football teams he bought, and that air of superiority and ‘always knowing’ has been manifest at United, too.

Ratcliffe demonstrated to some of the Manchester United players the stretches he was using for London Marathon training, after buying the club. He considered his friend Sir Dave Brailsford to be ‘best in class’ when it came to running the football side of the club, despite the former Team Sky cycling chief only having a loose connection of football contacts, mostly picked up from his appearances on the Leaders in Sport conference circuit for his friend Jimmy Worrall, the entrepreneur who set up that business.

Sources have told us that Brailsford was so enamoured with Manchester City, after arriving to ‘save United’, that he and other Ineos people were ‘skipping around’ when Omar Berrada, chief operations officer at the Etihad, was hired as chief executive.

Industry observers note that ‘There was no sense from Ratcliffe of needing people who understand the industry and its craft,’ says one source, speaking on condition of anonymity. ‘People like this see football on TV and just assume they can do it.’.

Ratcliffe (pictured next to former United boss Erik ten Hag) inexplicably considered Dave Brailsford (right) to be ‘best in class’ when it came to running the football side of the club.

Rob Nevin (right) chairs Ineos Sport, the collection of acquisitions built up when Ratcliffe wrongly believed his firm could straddle multiple sports and succeed at all of them.

Sources indicate that none of Ratcliffe’s executives will be wanting to tell him that. A further sense of what a formidable presence he is for his own staff came in a 30-minute ‘Ask Jim’ interview with him, conducted by his own director of corporate affairs, Tom Crotty, this summer.

Ratcliffe stares at the floor for much of the time as Crotty bowls him a series of long-hop questions and he seems profoundly miserable, revealing little more than his unhappiness with the British government’s management of North Sea oilfields and his dislike for the British sports press.

According to recent reports, the interview was conducted the day after the World Cup final between Spain and Argentina in July, which Ratcliffe attended. ‘It was fabulous. A great celebration, despite the whining British press who really didn’t like it,’ he states.

Asked how he switches off, he cites time on the golf driving range and fishing on his salmon farm in Iceland – to which Berrada and the Glazers have had to make their way for some executive meetings. ‘Biggest lessons learned in 2025?’ There don’t appear to have been any. At no stage do United feature.

Ratcliffe trots out his extremely well-worn mantra about the major three traits Ineos prize most – ‘Grit, rigour, humour’ – informing the relentlessly positive Crotty of the need to ‘do work properly before you make important decisions, because you need to spend capital well.’.

That’s not happened in sport. The extraordinary rupture with Ainslie, after Ineos withdrew support for a third America’s Cup cycle and he continued to use the boat, is part of a general, hugely acrimonious winding down of the Ineos Sport masterplan.

In a recent development, OGC Nice and FC Lausanne-Sport, Ratcliffe’s European football clubs, are up for sale amid fan protest, while it is widely expected that Ineos will eventually disappear from cycling. They have already diluted their involvement, with the team formerly known as the Ineos Grenadiers now co-sponsored by Danish technology firm Netcompany. The All Blacks and Tottenham Hotspur both sued Ineos for prematurely terminating commercial relationships. Both parties ultimately reached a confidential legal settlement with the tech giant.

What United need, as they look to Mauricio Pochettino for deliverance, is for Ratcliffe to climb down from his lofty peak, display some of the humility which has been missing and admit there have been faults.

What United need, as they look to Mauricio Pochettino for deliverance, is for Ratcliffe to display some humility.

The club's executives, including CEO Omar Berrada (centre) and director of football Jason Wilcox (right), have appeared out of their depth and in need of support.

It has been revealed that the view among some outsiders who interact at a high level with the club is, in the words of one, that United are ‘the biggest club in the world and yet no longer the biggest club in the world'. They carry huge expectation, yet are unable to compete for transfer fees and wages with Barcelona, Real Madrid, Bayern Munich, Liverpool, Arsenal and Manchester City, and have removed excellent staff without replacing them. To cite only one, Victoria Timpson secured the club’s lucrative Snapdragon sponsorship deal but still departed.

‘If United were an iPhone, someone would need to unfold the paper clip pretty quick, push it in the side of the phone and hard restart everything,’ says the aforementioned source. 'The club need a genuinely industry-leading chief executive, a sporting director of the calibre of Edwin van der Sar who, unlike Jason Wilcox, has the experience, pedigree and knowledge he accumulated as CEO at Ajax. They need a boardroom presence and link to the fans like Bryan Robson.'.

Ratcliffe does not even sit on the United board himself, leaving it to Nevin and John Reece, one of the Ineos co-founders.

It has been revealed that another of his Ineos appointees was the 63-year-old Frenchman Jean-Claude Blanc, an experienced statesman and marketeer with an Olympic, Tour de France and PSG background, who is described as pleasant and without ego – though he was supposedly in the room when the 'scorched' earth comment was made to Ainslie. Blanc was recruited for his gravitas, rather than to pull up trees. He has stepped down from United's board.

Industry observers note that ainslie and Ratcliffe are no longer thought to be friends. Shortly before the alleged episode in the locked aerodrome building, Ainslie observed, in relation to Ratcliffe, that there was a difference between business and sport, where teams were delicately balanced, nuanced groups of people.

Sources indicate that ‘You want to save money and be efficient, but you’ve got to make sure you look after people. You need that balance,’ he remarked. Under the terms of the court settlement, Ainslie gets to keep the boat and sail off into the sunset, leaving Ratcliffe to whatever storms come next.
