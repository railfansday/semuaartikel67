Google Gets Spirit’s Data, But Not Its 97MM Customers

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that spirit Airlines stopped flying its bright yellow planes earlier this year. The final flights landed on May 2, 2026 after 34 years, and the estate sale has been running ever since. The planes went, the LaGuardia slots went, and in July I wrote about the strange bid for Free Spirit, a loyalty program attached to an airline that no longer exists. The oddest asset of all turned out to be the paperwork.

It has been revealed that google won Spirit’s internal data at an August 14 bankruptcy auction for $10MM. The package covers about 500MM Microsoft Teams chats, 100MM emails from 80,000 accounts and roughly 30MM lines of code across 516 software projects. Google has noted the data “can be helpful in improving our products and AI models” and that it “will not receive any personal information from this dataset.”.

Small business owners have been inundated with emails and other unsolicited bids for their corporate data for training projects in what seems to be high amounts of free money that those training algorithms will turn into better models. There’s something incredibly valuable to companies in that data, which to me looks like very old “can you see my screen” messages on Teams.

But for Google to get what they have for just $10MM – which it earns in less than an hour every day – seems like a steal for Gemini.

According to recent reports, the sale had been stuck in bankruptcy court over privacy. In October, 121 members of Congress led by Sen. Elizabeth Warren wrote to Google CEO Sundar Pichai and called the deal “uncharted territory.” On November 10, the Association of Flight Attendants and other unions withdrew their objections after reaching a deal with Google.

In a recent development, under that deal, Google will not get employees’ medical and disability history, drug and alcohol testing records or payroll records. The unions also won a stricter de-identification process and a ban on Google trying to re-identify anyone. AFA president Sara Nelson put it plainly: “Worker data privacy in the age of AI has become a whole new worker’s rights glitch.” A bankruptcy judge still has to approve the sale.

Roughly 17,000 Spirit employees lost their jobs overnight in May, and their union was the only party in the room arguing for them while their HR files were being priced. Most companies would never have to face this question, because most companies do not die with their servers intact. It’s admirable, though, that labor reps continued to defend their members even after it was clear the airline would never be resurrected.

The court-appointed privacy ombudsman recommended approval after personal information on 97MM Spirit customers was excluded from the sale. That should be comforting to Free Spirit members as well as casual customers who might have approved of Spirit using their information for transportation and transactional purposes but not to feed an AI.

In a recent development, but the contract details are less comforting than the headlines. The August 14 agreement says the firm doing the scrubbing is one “acceptable to or designated by” Google, that Google pays for it, and that Google gets to review the work. The agreement also requires the de-identification to preserve “referential integrity” across the data set, meaning the links between one pseudonymous person’s emails, tickets and records stay intact. That is what makes the archive useful for training. It is also what makes anonymized data easier to unravel. Not to worry though, we haven’t seen any tech related data concerns of late (wink.).

According to recent reports, none of that is improper, and Google may handle it perfectly. My point is narrower. An airline’s emails are full of customers: complaint escalations, refund disputes, disability assistance requests, denied boarding cases. Excluding the customer database does not remove every customer from 100MM emails, which is why the scrubbing standard matters as much as the exclusion.

When I wrote about Free Spirit in July, the argument was that a dead airline’s loyalty program is less a currency than a customer file, and that buyers want the file without the liability of the points. The Google deal is the same lesson from a different angle. When an airline dies, the miles usually die with it. The data does not die. It gets sold. It also answers the question as to what value could be garnered from a defunct loyalty program.

Recent findings suggest that every airline and hotel program you belong to holds your travel history, your home airport, your spending pattern through the co-brand card and every complaint you have ever filed. Program terms reserve the right to change or end the program, and bankruptcy law lets an estate sell assets while leaving liabilities behind. The customer file is an asset and it may change the way both travel suppliers and its customers see each other. Had I known, I might have removed my details personally when the airline collapsed. I expect legislation at some point will learn from Spirit Airlines’ shut down and require a period for which consumers can remove or adjust their details.

According to recent reports, spirit was a low-fare airline with a loyalty program most frequent flyers ignored. If the data from that operation is worth $10MM to Google, the data inside a major US carrier’s program is worth vastly more to whoever is holding it if that carrier ever fails, or is sold in part or in whole.

The unions and the ombudsman did their jobs, and Google will most likely get a scrubbed archive it can use to teach software how airlines work. That is a reasonable outcome for a $10MM auction lot and make no mistake that Google will have to invest significant multiples of that amount to gain the end goal. The bigger takeaway is for travelers: the most valuable thing you give a loyalty program is not your spending, it is your information, and nobody is obligated to honor your points when the business behind them disappears. Use the miles while they are worth something, and assume your data will outlast them.
