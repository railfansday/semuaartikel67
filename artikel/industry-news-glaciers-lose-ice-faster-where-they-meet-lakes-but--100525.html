Industry News: Glaciers lose ice faster where they meet lakes, but there’s more to the story

🌐 Source: Earth • 📅 23 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 7 min read (1474 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Glaciers that end in lakes lost ice about 65% faster than those that end on land, but climate and terrain explained more of the variation than lakes did.

Meltwater is often collected in the hollow that a retreating glacier leaves behind.

Sources indicate that once such a lake has formed, the neighboring ice can be lost more quickly. The lake melts the glacier front below the waterline, and pieces break off more easily.

According to recent reports, the size of that effect has now been estimated across the world’s glaciers by an international team. Between 2000 and 2020, glaciers ending in lakes were found to lose ice about 65% faster than those on land.

It has been revealed that in the researchers’ model, lake characteristics accounted for about 11% of the differences in ice loss the model could explain. Climate and glacier terrain accounted for most of the rest.

It has been revealed that the work was led by Shaochun Zhao and Guoqing Zhang. Both are at the Institute of Tibetan Plateau Research at the Chinese Academy of Sciences.

Their 15 co-authors work in China, Europe, and the United States. One of them is Tobias Bolch of the Institute of Geodesy at Graz University of Technology.

In a recent development, the team worked from satellite images and a statistical model. No instruments were placed at the ice.

Glacial lakes were mapped everywhere outside Antarctica using images from the Landsat satellites. The images were taken around 2000 and again around 2020.

Recent findings suggest that software trained to recognize lakes drew the first outlines. Those outlines were then checked and corrected by eye.

A lake had to cover about two-thirds of an acre (0.0027 square kilometers) to count.

Industry observers note that glacial lakes of every kind increased 9% in number, to 160,332. Lakes touching a glacier increased 35%, a gain of 3,567 lakes.

Three-quarters of the water added to glacial lakes went into lakes touching ice.

According to recent reports, the team estimated that lakes in direct contact with glaciers held about 16 cubic miles (66 cubic kilometers) more water by 2020. Volume was worked out from surface area with the help of depth surveys of 299 lakes.

Bolch’s university put that volume in city terms. Poured over Vienna in an even layer, the water would stand about 525 feet (160 meters) deep.

Worldwide growth of glacial lakes since 1990 had already been documented in the same journal by a 2020 survey.

According to recent reports, in that survey, however, lakes were not sorted according to whether they touch ice. That detail was required for the present study.

Industry observers note that expansion of ice-touching lakes by more than 39 square miles (100 square kilometers) was recorded in only two regions. These were Alaska and the southern Andes. Fifteen of the 20 lakes with the largest growth are located in these two regions.

The largest single gain, roughly 20 square miles (53 square kilometers), was measured at a lake in Alaska. Within the southern Andes, growth was concentrated around the two Patagonian ice fields.

Most of the growth came from a small number of large lakes. Although 2,946 lakes expanded in total, 810 of them grew by more than 25 acres (0.1 square kilometers).

Those 810 lakes accounted for 92% of the total area gain.

A further 4,538 ice-touching lakes were formed between 2000 and 2020.

Contact with the ice was not maintained everywhere. During the same period, 1,645 lakes became detached from their glaciers. In Upcoming Zealand, eight lakes were detached while only three new ones were formed.

In a recent development, to compare glaciers, the team relied on the Randolph Glacier Inventory, a global catalog of glacier outlines. It was combined with an existing record of glacier ice loss.

Of roughly 214,000 glaciers, 7,121 terminate in a lake and 1,512 in the sea, while the remainder end on land.

Sources indicate that ice loss is expressed by glaciologists as a depth of water. This is the depth the lost ice would produce if spread across the whole glacier.

By that measure, lake-ending glaciers lost about 26 inches (0.66 meters) a year.

For land-ending glaciers the figure was about 16 inches (0.40 meters), and for sea-ending glaciers about 14 inches (0.36 meters).

Bolch was part of a team that found a similar gap among Himalayan glaciers in 2019.

It has been revealed that the fastest ice loss was not seen at these glaciers everywhere. In Iceland, Svalbard, the Russian Arctic, and northern Arctic Canada, they lost less ice than land-ending ones.

Industry observers note that A possible reason was suggested by the authors. Lake-ending glaciers in those regions are considerably larger and lie at higher elevations.

In a recent development, over those 20 years, about 1.5 trillion tons (1,362 billion metric tons) were lost by lake-ending glaciers. This amounts to roughly 28% of all glacier ice lost worldwide.

Recent findings suggest that studies of single glaciers had disagreed about how much a lake matters. At two glaciers in southeastern Tibet, the loss was dominated at certain stages by ice breaking off into a lake.

Studies in the western Himalaya and British Columbia suggested that lakes played a secondary role.

Industry observers note that to weigh the causes, machine learning was used. Here, a program learns which measurements best predict an outcome. Three such programs analyzed the lake-ending glaciers.

According to recent reports, for each glacier, the lake’s size and growth were recorded together with 20 years of temperature and precipitation data. Slope, elevation, and latitude were also included.

It has been revealed that of the ice loss the best program could explain, 46% was attributed to a glacier’s shape and position. Climate was assigned 43% and lakes 11%.

Each share carries an uncertainty of 7 to 10 percentage points.

Steeper and higher glaciers tended to lose less ice. Glaciers whose lakes grew more tended to lose more.

In a recent development, the lakes’ share was highest in regions where lakes had expanded substantially. It was about 14% to 15% in Scandinavia, Alaska, and the southern Andes, and 5% in northern Arctic Canada.

Bolch ranked the causes the same way, placing climate and terrain ahead of lakes, which “significantly exacerbate” the loss.

Recent findings suggest that deeper lake water pushes up harder on the front of a glacier, which helps floating ice detach. Lake water also melts ice below the surface.

Recent findings suggest that A glacier’s loss is measured by satellites that track the height of its surface. Ice lost underwater can’t be seen that way.

In a recent development, that uncounted loss was estimated at about 62 billion tons (56 billion metric tons) for 2000 to 2020. If it were counted, the total for lake-ending glaciers would be raised by roughly 4%.

With other volume estimates, that figure reaches as much as 8%.

In a recent development, water that would otherwise reach the ocean is also held back by lakes. Between 2000 and 2023, melting glaciers added about 0.7 inches (18 millimeters) to global sea level.

It has been revealed that by comparison, only a small amount was stored in glacial lakes from 2000 to 2020. The team calculated it as about one hundredth of an inch (0.24 millimeters) of sea level. That estimate assumes all the water in the lakes came from glaciers.

The results should make forecasts of glacier change more accurate, Bolch remarked.

Mountain regions and the lowlands below them have the most use for such forecasts, Bolch added. A significant share of their water comes from meltwater.

The best program accounted for a little more than half of the differences in ice loss among glaciers. Several of its lake measurements were stand-ins.

Depth matters most right where water meets ice, and direct measurements there are scarce. The team therefore relied on an average depth, which was estimated from each lake’s surface area.

The length of ice touching water came from a single date. The warmth of each lake could only be approximated, using air temperature and lake volume.

Sources indicate that ice thickness at the glacier front wasn’t in the analysis, the authors wrote. Neither was the shape of the ground beneath it.

Anyone assessing the danger of outburst floods from glacial lakes can use the findings too, Bolch said.

Industry observers note that future research could measure water depth and temperature at glacier fronts. How the line where ice meets water shifts over time could also be tracked.

Physical models built for lake-ending glaciers would give a more accurate answer than statistics, the authors wrote. Such models already exist for glaciers that end in the sea.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Climate Change.
