Why Newcastle United the football club is an entity which I find increasingly loathsome

Over nearly forty years of supporting Newcastle United, there are few things more certain than the knowledge that this club will let me down time and again.

In a recent development, in terms of what happens on the field, that is part and parcel of being a football supporter. After all, there are 92 league clubs and it stands to reason that the vast majority of those teams will only watch on, frustrated, while a small minority achieve success.

To that end, on the law of averages we are very fortunate to support a club that has spent its entire existence in the top two tiers of English football, most of my lifetime in the top division and even won a trophy a couple of years back.

So in that context, you can be pragmatic about the failures, enjoy the successes when they come and proudly tell the players in black and white that “we’ll support you ever more”.

In a recent development, yet it is the club’s activity off the field that really gets my goat. I hate to say it, but while the club has stabilised since the takeover, improved as a commercial entity and enjoyed a bit of silverware for the first time in forever, it has also lost its soul.

“Our fans are the beating heart of Newcastle United,” declared CEO David Hopkinson as he declared the latest season ticket price hike earlier this year.

In 2025, he told the Newcastle United Supporters Trust: “Supporters are at the heart of everything we do.” Meanwhile, chairman Yasir Al-Rumayyan claimed our support “will never be taken for granted” in an open letter to fans back in 2022.

None of this is true though, is it?

We are absolutely taken for granted. And if we are at the heart of what the club does, it is in the most transactional sense possible – as paying lemmings who are a guaranteed source of income.

Take my experience of trying to get tickets for my sons for the Sunderland game as a prime example.

To be clear, I fully understand that match tickets are at a premium and for this game more than any other, demand would have been huge.

Imagine my surprise, then, when my two sons were seemingly successful in the ballot.

Given the club claim it is a strike rate of one in seven yet this was my eldest son’s first successful ballot in 22 attempts and my youngest son’s first in 15, this was a particularly welcome stroke of luck.

Worse still, because we tried to have him accompany my elder son in the family area, neither were allowed to utilise their ballot success, as my eldest was automatically disqualified when he no longer had a youth to accompany him.

Now I totally understand why these rules exist. Our situation was an unfortunate quirk of when my youngest son’s birthday fell and was an error made in good faith, but it would be very easy for people to exploit this and fill the family section with adults if the club were not vigilant. I get that and I respect that.

That is why I accept when I phoned the Box Office to try to resolve this unforeseen anomaly they were unable to provide us with tickets in that area.

What was less easy to accept was the absolute wall of intransigence and unhelpfulness I was faced with.

Industry observers note that could we get tickets in a different area? “No, you’ve won the ballot in that area so that’s it”.

Why does the website block us from buying the tickets now but did not block us from entering a ballot that it turns out we could never win? “It is your responsibility to enter the ballot in the right area.”.

Industry observers note that can we not just have two tickets elsewhere given that the allocation that goes on general sale will be unchanged – we will not be using the two we have won so it will be the same net figure? “No, the policy does not allow that. It would not be fair on other supporters.”.

The ballot system is inherently unfair as our strike rate highlights – we have had a rare success and now we cannot use it because the system allowed us to enter a ballot we could not win. Is that fair? “I’m not prepared to depart from the policy.”.

As anyone who has encountered our Supporter Services will know, escalating the matter to them was about as effective as the experiment of playing Nick Woltemade as a number 8.

Some will say I should not base my view of the club on interactions with a handful of lower paid staff working in the Box Office and Supporter Services. I disagree.

According to recent reports, senior leaders in any organisation set the culture. If David Hopkinson truly believed supporters are the “beating heart” of Newcastle United, he and his executive team would have led the behaviours that set the tone for everyone else.

Interactions with the frontline staff are the very best test for what an organisation really values, and I can tell you now that Newcastle United does not value its supporters beyond the transactional purpose they serve.

Industry observers note that allow me to give another example. Last season, my sister won two tickets in the ballot to take her son to see Newcastle play Bournemouth.

Industry observers note that now my sister is a proper fan. Growing up in Hexham, she spent most of her teen years trying to hang around Causey Hill in Hexham long enough to wave at the passing cars of Mick Quinn, Ray Ranson, Kevin Brock and Mark McGhee, who lived at the top of the road. She even became Roy Aitken’s family babysitter!

She started going to games during the desperate 1988-89 season which ended in relegation and has been a regular at home and away games ever since.

Now living in the south east, getting to home games is an effort and the Bournemouth game last season proved to be a particular trial.

Train delay after train delay resulted in that trip becoming akin to the Griswalds’ trip to Wally World in National Lampoon’s Vacation: rather than it being a day out, it became a mission.

After the first delay they knew it would be tight for kick-off, by the second they knew they would miss some of the first half, by the next they were hoping to get in for the second half, and by the time they eventually arrived it was a case of: “We’ve come this far, we might as well watch the last 10 minutes!”.

Recent findings suggest that off they bombed sprinting up Pink Lane to get to the ground, only to arrive in around the 80th minute and be turned away by the stewards. They presented their tickets, they told them what they had been through, but it was all to no avail. Supporter Services on the day were actually very supportive; those based at the ground stated the stewards had made the wrong call and if they called up on Monday they would “see what they could do.”.

It turned out, what they could do was never tested: what they would do was absolutely nothing. Supporter Services denied any knowledge of the response they had been given on the Saturday, and virtually accused my sister of making the whole thing up.

“Supporters are at the heart of everything we do”, are they Mr Hopkinson? Well that is not the experience most of us face when interacting with the people you field to manage those relationships.

No, this is a club which has lost its heart.

Sources indicate that you need look no further than the utterly dreadful rendition of Blaydon Races which they have forced us to endure on matchdays to understand quite how much the decision-makers at Newcastle United do not understand what our club is. What used to set us apart in home games was the intensity and atmosphere that the crowd brought. Messrs Keegan, Robson and Howe all understood that.

When I was at the depressing Everton game recently, I could not help but think immediately before kick-off ahead of both the first and second halves how the people who make those decisions are strangling the life out of what we are.

If ever there was a way to sap the atmosphere out of the crowd when you need them most it would be by drowning them out with a funeral-march rendition of the Geordie anthem being played at full blast through the stadium speakers. It even killed the Strawberry Corner.

You look at other things – like that dreadful variation on our club badge – and wonder what on earth motivates these people to make the decisions that they do.

I mean, seriously, was it worth the upset and cost of changing a crest we have known and loved for decades just to introduce that abomination?

According to recent reports, I will always love Newcastle United, because it is part of me. That is why, despite Yasir Al-Rummayan’s protestations to the contrary, it is so easy for the powers that be to take us for granted, which they do unreservedly and indiscriminately. We are hooked in by the nostalgia, the memories, the stories, the glorious moments.

Maybe we are motivated by that truly united feeling of togetherness when you are packed into the concourse at any away ground, chanting for 20 minutes non-stop just for each other and the looks on the faces of the bemused home stewards because there’s not a player or opposition supporter in sight.

Perhaps we are driven by the hope, or the sheer unbridled joy of that last-minute winner or a hard-fought victory against one of the league’s elite.

Or for some of us it is just a way of life. Something we did with our mum or dad and now we do with our children, and one day when they are mums and dads they will do with their children too.

In more crass terms, a cynic might say we are victims of Stockholm Syndrome: a condition in which people develop an emotional attachment, affection or loyalty to their captor or abuser.

Sources indicate that however much we love Newcastle United, we should never be allow ourselves to forget that it is a love which is utterly unrequited. We are commodities to buy tickets, buy shirts and create an image of fervour that can be sold as a commercial package to the highest bidder.

Sources indicate that the members’ general sale tickets for Sunderland sold out within minutes. Yet there is still plenty of room if you are prepared to pay £800 for your ticket, along with  bit of food and the brief musings of one of Keegan’s Entertainers pre and post-match.

While Newcastle United the football team remains something I seem bound to unconditionally love despite all my senses telling me I should not, Newcastle United the football club is an entity which I find increasingly loathsome.
