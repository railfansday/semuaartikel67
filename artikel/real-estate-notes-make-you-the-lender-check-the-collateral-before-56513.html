Industry observers note that A real estate note lets you lend against someone else’s property and collect the interest. Before you put money in, confirm your loan is no more than 70% of the property’s appraised value and gets repaid first if the property sells. If you can’t check that yourself, spread small amounts across many loans.

The interest is the appeal. The property is your protection, but only if you can claim it and sell it for at least what you’re owed.

You can buy a performing note, one whose borrower is paying, or pay less than the loan’s balance for a non-performing note, one whose borrower has stopped paying. You can lend straight to a house flipper. Or you can buy slices of many loans through a real estate crowdfunding platform. The real estate bonds some apps sell are loans to the company itself and give you no claim on a particular property.

A default can end in foreclosure, and foreclosures are slow. Homes foreclosed in the second quarter of 2026 had spent an average of 563 days in the process, according to property data firm ATTOM. Texas averaged 155 days and Fresh York 2,007. You can’t cash out early, either. You get your money back as the borrower repays, or by finding someone to buy the note.

Say you lend $100,000 at 10% to a flipper on a house worth $160,000. You collect $10,000 a year until the project stalls and the payments stop. Then you pay a lawyer and keep up the property’s taxes and insurance until the foreclosure ends and the house sells. The $60,000 between the loan and the value has to cover those costs and any drop in price.

Unless you can run all five checks on every loan, spread the same $100,000 across 50 loans of $2,000 each, so one default puts a fiftieth of your money at risk instead of all of it.
