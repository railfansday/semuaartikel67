Can Buying Your Childhood Home Bring Back the Past?

According to recent reports, think of the home where you spent your childhood. You may still remember the room you slept in, the conversations you had there, the sound of someone calling you for dinner or the family and friends who came to visit on special occasions. Even years after leaving, that house may remain closely connected to your memories and sense of self.

Industry observers note that some people go beyond remembering their childhood homes and decide to buy them. But can recovering a childhood home also bring back the life once lived there?

Sources indicate that below, I explore that question through the experiences of three people who did exactly that. What made them want to reclaim these properties, and what did they discover when they returned? Their stories invite us to consider what it means to return to a place that remains so much a part of who we are.

Eddie Dilleen is one of Australia’s largest residential landlords; he owns more than 200 properties. But there was one property he could not buy. For more than 12 years, he waited for his childhood home to go on the market.

Industry observers note that as he has written, ‘You can set reminders and stuff like that, but when I was on my phone messing around, I would randomly check it literally every one or two weeks for the past 12 years.’ When the house finally came on the market, he remarked, ‘I just bought it sight unseen.’ The price was just under $1 million. The house had been sold 25 years earlier for $97,000 following his parents’ divorce.

Industry observers note that eddie’s childhood home remained important to him throughout his adult life, even after he achieved financial success. His constant checking on it suggests an enduring emotional attachment to a place associated with loss and family separation. His decision to purchase the house without viewing it indicates a desperation to reclaim something he felt he had lost.

His experience also may be understood in the context of cognitive immobility, which can occur when an individual is unable to stay put in an intended place due to the absence or presence of certain restrictions or circumstances beyond their control, which may originate in their present or desired location, inside themselves, or in the external world.

Circumstances beyond Dilleen’s control made him leave his childhood home but some part of him remained in it.

Recent findings suggest that jenaca Rogers lived with her grandma but was also raised from infancy by her family friends, Pat and Jerry. Their house held many of her childhood memories, from family celebrations to everyday moments spent with her grandparents. When Pat and Jerry died, the house was sold following probate proceedings. As Jenaca recalled, ‘We were all heartbroken. Your whole life you hope for something and then it’s just taken away’.

Years later, in 2025, Jenaca unexpectedly discovered that the house was for sale, and called the estate agent. When he failed to answer, she mentioned, ‘I just started crying because I was like, I’m not going to get it’.

Sources indicate that she then found the agent’s mobile number online. She recalls, ‘I called him and he got us out there, and I cried in the driveway, I cried touring the house’. During the visit, she discovered her toy car in the backyard shed, which overwhelmed her further. She eventually bought the house and surprised her grandma, Connie, with a viewing. It was a homecoming for them both.

Buying the house brought Jenaca peace and comfort, but it did not erase her grief. Her experience suggests that reclaiming a meaningful place may help us feel emotionally connected with loved ones who have died but does not necessarily help with the loss.

Recent findings suggest that moving from Bogotá to Miami brought Colombian singer-songwriter Andrés Cepeda latest opportunities and challenges, but he left behind something deeply personal. After years of searching for something he felt he had lost in Bogotá, he was able to recover it. Cepeda bought his childhood home.

Sources indicate that after returning to the house, Cepeda said, 'I returned to the house, the street, the garden. Everything was just as I remembered. And yet nothing was the same. My parents were gone. My childhood was gone. Time itself had left.'.

Cepeda may have experienced a sense of metaphorical homelessness, the idea that those who experience cognitive immobility may find out after returning home that ‘…no home is truly a home; even the previous home – the ancestral home – has lost its distinguishing features and allure’. Although Cepeda had recovered the property, his childhood experiences could not be recreated: 'I went on a personal tour of my life. The parks, the schools, the cafés, the houses of my friends, the places where my heart broke. All of it started turning into music.'.

In a recent development, he wrote the song ‘Bogotá’ to express his feelings about his childhood city and family. As he describes it, ‘It captures the eerie, bittersweet feeling of returning to a place that once defined you – only to realise you miss not the place, but the person you were there’. Cepeda recovered his childhood home but could not recover his childhood. However, he could use his music to acknowledge what cannot be recovered.

Of the three stages of cognitive immobility, his music could have helped him enter the third stage, stabilisation, in which people ‘...tend to aim to retain values and pursue goals to help them with the loss, alleviating the sense of entrapment’.

According to recent reports, our childhood home can remain important long after we have left it. It is a reminder of the people we knew, our relationships and the experiences we had. If your childhood home has been lost through the breakup of your family, bereavement or some other circumstance beyond your control, recovering it may become a deeply personal goal.

It has been revealed that the experiences of Dilleen, Rogers and Cepeda show that buying a childhood home can be a chance to reconnect with a familiar place, but it does not mean returning to the life once lived there. The building may remain but the people in it, the relationships, the life that existed there will have changed. We can return to a place without recovering the past and move forward without leaving its memories behind.
