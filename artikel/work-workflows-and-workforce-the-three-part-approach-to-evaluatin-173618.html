Work, Workflows and Workforce: The Three-Part Approach to Evaluating AI ROI

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, CFOs need to explain what the business is getting from its AI spending. That becomes difficult when the same conversation covers an employee using AI to prepare for a meeting and a multimillion-dollar effort to redesign customer support. Those investments have different purposes, and evaluating them against identical expectations can produce poor decisions.

In a recent development, I think about AI transformation in three categories: work, workflows and workforce. Work is how individuals do their jobs. Workflows are the broader business processes leadership must redesign. Workforce is how teams, roles and responsibilities must be structured to support those redesigned workflows.

Sources indicate that each category requires financial discipline, but the timing and measures of return will differ. CFOs should encourage learning where capabilities are still developing, demand measurable outcomes for major process investments and fund the workforce needed to deliver them.

Industry observers note that work starts with individuals figuring out how AI can improve their daily responsibilities. Someone in accounting might automate a recurring analysis. A marketer might use AI to prepare a campaign brief. These improvements develop from employees learning the tools and recognizing opportunities in their own jobs.

Spreadsheets are a useful comparison. Employees across departments learned to use them without relying on IT to build every calculation. AI requires a similar expansion of skills. Employees need enough knowledge to use models, build simple agents where appropriate and question manual tasks they have accepted for years.

Recent findings suggest that putting a precise ROI requirement on every interaction would make that learning harder. An employee may prepare for a meeting more effectively, but assigning a defensible financial return to that improvement is difficult. Early in adoption, finance should give employees room to learn and experiment.

Recent findings suggest that that still requires cost controls. CFOs can establish budgets, set individual usage caps and monitor consumption. Managers should track repeat usage, practical applications and whether employees are developing useful skills. User groups can help effective approaches spread across departments.

It has been revealed that as usage matures, finance can identify underused licenses and compare the cost of tools performing similar tasks. Trying to maximize adoption and minimize every dollar of spending simultaneously can stall progress before employees know what the tools can do.

In a recent development, the largest returns come from rethinking how an entire business process operates. Giving developers AI coding tools may help them complete individual tasks faster. Redesigning software delivery requires leadership to examine how code is written, reviewed, tested and deployed, including where work slows down between those steps.

Recent findings suggest that that is an executive responsibility. The leader running the function must define how the process should change and partner with AI and IT teams on the technology required. Individual experiments alone are unlikely to produce that coordinated redesign.

For CFOs, these initiatives should carry the same expectations as other major software investments. Before committing capital, establish how the process performs today, the outcome management expects, the cost of achieving it and the period over which returns will be measured. The executive sponsoring the initiative should commit to those results upfront.

It has been revealed that customer support provides a practical example. Finance could measure cost per resolved case, resolution time and escalation rates, while checking that customer experience remains acceptable. In product development, relevant measures could include release velocity, defects and engineering capacity. The business case should connect those operational changes to the function’s existing priorities.

It has been revealed that that connection requires care. Hours saved create capacity, but the financial benefit depends on what happens next. Does the team handle more volume without additional hiring? Does it avoid outside spending? Can it deliver valuable products sooner? Faster output may create substantial value without reducing current payroll.

Recent findings suggest that finance and the sponsoring executive should agree on these assumptions before deployment and review them as the initiative scales. They also need conditions for modifying or stopping an investment that fails to deliver. Measuring ROI after a major rollout leaves too much room for shifting expectations.

Recent findings suggest that as AI changes how work gets done, leaders need to reconsider how teams are organized. Responsibilities, reporting relationships and handoffs designed around manual processes may no longer fit. Workforce transformation means adjusting those structures so teams can deliver the outcomes promised by redesigned workflows.

Recent findings suggest that consider customer support. As AI handles more routine requests, leaders may need to shift people toward complex cases, oversight of automated responses and improvements to the support process. In engineering, faster code generation may require a different balance between writing code and reviewing, testing and deploying it.

Sources indicate that these changes should be planned alongside the workflow redesign. Leaders need to decide who owns the process, where human judgment is required and how teams will coordinate as responsibilities shift. Automating tasks while retaining every existing handoff can leave the broader process slow and expensive.

Industry observers note that CFOs should test whether the proposed team structure supports the investment case. If AI creates capacity in one part of a process, can another team absorb the additional volume? Will employees move into higher-value work, or will capacity sit unused? The financial return depends on how effectively leaders reorganize work around that capacity.

According to recent reports, hiring and upskilling should support the roles those workflows require. Finance should include training and transition costs in the investment blueprint, then assess whether the fresh structure improves throughput, service quality or cost per outcome. That connects workforce decisions directly to the returns expected from AI.

Recent findings suggest that the framework also helps CFOs evaluate where AI capabilities should come from. Start with functionality already available in existing software. If that cannot meet the need, assess other products built for the task. Custom development should follow a clear assessment of whether available solutions can deliver the required outcome.

In a recent development, building a replacement for an established system can introduce maintenance obligations and technical debt alongside the initial development expense. Finance should compare total ownership costs, including integration, model consumption, security, human oversight and ongoing support. The objective is to improve the process at a sustainable cost.

Sources indicate that AI belongs within the overall software investment discussion. Finance has long weighed the cost of technology against the people and resources required to perform the work. Variable charges tied to model usage or agent activity make it especially notable to understand how costs change as adoption and transaction volumes grow.

Boards should see that distinction in reporting. Everyday tools warrant a view of adoption, learning and spending controls. Major workflow investments need a view of operational outcomes and financial returns against commitments. Workforce investments need evidence that the skills and organizational changes required for those returns are taking hold.

Recent findings suggest that CFOs can use work, workflows and workforce to make better funding decisions across the AI portfolio. Give employees a controlled opportunity to learn, hold executives accountable for process improvements and invest in the people required to sustain them. That gives finance a clearer basis for deciding where AI spending should grow and where it should be reconsidered.
