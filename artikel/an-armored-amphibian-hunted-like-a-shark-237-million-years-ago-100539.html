An armored amphibian hunted like a shark 237 million years ago

Two fossils from southern China reveal an armored amphibian that lived entirely at sea 237 million years ago.

According to recent reports, no living amphibian is known to spend its entire life in the open ocean. For most, seawater draws moisture out through their permeable skin.

Yet in the Late Triassic, a flat, armored amphibian about 2½ feet (75 centimeters) long lived on the sea floor at least 19 miles (30 kilometers) from the nearest land.

Two nearly complete skeletons from southern China provide the first evidence of an amphibian that lived entirely at sea.

The researchers named the species Gerrothorax maritimus. The name maritimus means “of the sea.”.

Their case rests on where the bones lay and what they are made of, plus a suit of armor the animal’s freshwater ancestors already wore.

The fossils were studied by Lipin Diao of the Geological Museum of Guizhou, Ryosuke Motani of the University of California, Davis, and Da-yong Jiang of Peking University, with two colleagues.

“If you think about today’s amphibians, this animal is unusual because it’s substantial,” Motani noted.

“It’s probably, body mass-wise, about the same as the giant salamander, but what’s different is that giant salamanders are elongated whereas this animal is really chunky.”.

Local fossil collectors dug the skeletons out of a quarry in Guanling County, Guizhou Province, in 2004.

According to recent reports, the bones went to the Geological Museum of Guizhou in 2022, when it opened as the province’s official fossil repository. The rock around them is about 237 million years old.

According to recent reports, the same bed has produced large ocean-going ichthyosaurs, animals of the open sea. In the Late Triassic, this rock was the floor of a gulf about 125 miles (200 kilometers) from any large landmass.

The bones argue against a carcass that drifted out from a river. The larger skeleton still has its fingers in place, and fingers are among the first parts a dead four-legged animal loses at sea.

“Carcasses at sea, unless they sink to the bottom right away, tend to dry out and lose limbs prior to sinking to the bottom,” mentioned Motani, a professor in the UC Davis Department of Earth and Planetary Sciences.

“But these animals had their fingers preserved, so that means they probably died near where they lived.”.

It has been revealed that strontium is a metal that animals take in with their water and build into bone. The mix of its forms differs between the open ocean and water that drains off land.

The team measured that mix in dense bone from both skeletons and in the rock around them.

The bone values were close to the global seawater value of the time. The surrounding rock read higher, closer to the older rocks of the nearby land.

The two skeletons also agreed with each other far more closely than the rock samples did, which the authors read as a living body controlling its own chemistry.

In an interview with Earth.com, Motani said the strontium result needed little interpretation.

“I was surprised to see a clean result, which did not require a lot of interpretations to say A or B,” he said.

Amphibians breathe and drink through their skin, and that’s what fails them in the ocean, Motani said.

A few living species can tolerate brackish water, and some can even withstand brief exposure to seawater.

“This wonderful device, however, does not do its job in sea water and instead works against the animal,” Motani told Earth.com.

Gerrothorax had a way around that long before it reached the sea. Its back was covered in a pavement of bony plates about an inch across and less than a fifth of an inch thick.

It has been revealed that A second layer of bony rods and small plates protected the belly. The lineage also kept internal gills into adulthood, which most living amphibians lose.

In a recent development, the researchers propose that the armor limited water exchange through the skin. If so, the animal may have taken in water by drinking seawater and shed excess salt through its gills, much as marine fish do.

The armor evolved in fresh water as protection from predators, Motani noted, and happened to be the right equipment for a later move into the sea.

“There was a freshwater amphibian that happened to have the equipment needed to colonize the sea – the body armor,” he said.

Industry observers note that the larger skeleton is 29 inches (74 centimeters) long as preserved. The skull is only about 5 inches (13 centimeters) long but close to 10 inches (25 centimeters) wide. The armored back reaches about 11 inches (29 centimeters) across.

It has been revealed that the animal’s hind legs are longer than its front legs. The authors read that as a sign it breathed with gills rather than lungs, since lung-breathing sea animals usually need longer front legs to stay balanced in the water.

The throat bones are heavily built, as strong as those of sharks that feed by suction. So the team pictures a flat animal resting on the bottom, like a wobbegong carpet shark, and swallowing whatever swam too close.

“They would probably just wait at the bottom and then when prey comes around, they would just suck it in,” Motani mentioned.

The salt-handling system and the feeding style are inferences from bone, since no soft tissue survived.

Nobody knows whether these ancient amphibians had permeable skin like a frog’s. Nor do the fossils say whether Gerrothorax maritimus laid eggs or gave birth to live young, and amphibian eggs do poorly in salt water.

The species also lived in a greenhouse world, much warmer than today’s.

Even a return to those temperatures would probably not open the sea to modern amphibians, the authors wrote, because most of them lose their gills as adults.

Land vertebrates have colonized the ocean roughly 70 times, and this is the only amphibian lineage known to have gone all the way.

Asked by Earth.com where more marine amphibians might turn up, Motani pointed to Triassic marine shales laid down in the tropics of the time.

According to recent reports, the best bet, he said, is near where the ancient Panthalassa and Tethys oceans met. Those rocks are where this one came from, and where China’s Triassic marine reptiles keep appearing.

Whether Gerrothorax maritimus was alone in the sea, or the first of several armored relatives to make the crossing, is the question those shales will have to answer.

Until a second one turns up, the whole case for a marine amphibian rests on two skeletons from one quarry.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Ecology & Evolution.
