Never put the backup server in the same Windows domain as production.

In a recent development, welcome, once again, to PWNED, where we’re thankful every time someone leaves a password sticky note on a monitor or fails to delete a terminated employee’s admin account. But neither of those common occurrences is what we’ll be talking about today. Instead, our subject broke the number-one rule of backups: you gotta keep ’em separated.

It has been revealed that our story comes courtesy of Da Li, who serves as global alliance manager at Info2Soft, a company that provides data protection and recovery software. At one point, a partner called him into a case where a Chinese vehicle parts manufacturer fell victim to a ransomware gang. Unfortunately, its backup was compromised too.

The manufacturer had religiously backed up its manufacturing execution system (MES) and SAP orders, inventory, and finance systems every night. There was even a green dashboard that indicated the backup process had succeeded, and someone checked that every morning.

Recent findings suggest that however, one Friday evening, crims broke into the network and found out that the backup server was joined to the same Windows domain as the rest of the network. The hackers found a plaintext password for the backup service account in a maintenance script on a file share and discovered that someone had used the very same password for a domain admin account.

So the first thing the crooks did was delete every restore point on that backup server. Then they started encrypting the production servers to ransom them.

“Every backup job had succeeded for three years,” Li noted. “The failure was that the thing meant to save them could be reached with the same keys as the thing being attacked.”.

In a recent development, the results were devastating. With the MES and SAP data both gone, the plant couldn't schedule production, ship parts, or track them.

According to recent reports, by the time Li was called in, the plant had already resolved its ransomware situation. We don’t know whether it paid or not. The manufacturer did ask Li and his tech giant to make sure that this couldn’t happen again.

Li’s fix was not radical, but it was instructive. He made sure that the company now keeps its backup system outside the production domain, uses unique credentials, and requires MFA to reduce the risk of unauthorized access even if a password is compromised. He also made sure that the company keeps at least one offline backup and runs a test restore quarterly to ensure it’s prepared for emergencies.®.
