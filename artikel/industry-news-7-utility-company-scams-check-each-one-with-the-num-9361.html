Industry News: 7 Utility Company Scams – Check Each One With the Number on Your Bill

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that utility scammers rush you to pay or share account details before you can check. A real utility sends multiple notices before any shutoff and never asks for payment by gift card, prepaid debit card, or crypto. So hang up, shut the door, or skip the link, then call the number printed on your bill.

Recent findings suggest that utilities set out those two rules themselves, through the Edison Electric Institute and Utilities United Against Scams (UUAS), a group of more than 150 electric, water, and natural gas utilities. By November 2024, the group had helped shut down more than 14,830 toll-free numbers that scammers used against utility customers.

Every scam below asks you to pay, or to hand something over, in a way your utility doesn’t. Your utility already has your account number and payment history, so one call to the number printed on your bill tells you whether a phone call, visit, text, or ad came from it.

In states that let you buy electricity or gas from a tech giant other than your utility, some of those companies send salespeople door to door. Maryland’s attorney general has warned that they may wear badges that look like the utility’s, say the utility authorized them to review your bill or is changing how it bills, and then ask to see a copy.

That bill holds everything a supplier needs to switch you without your permission. Your utility already knows what’s on your bill, so anyone at the door who needs to see it isn’t from the utility.

It has been revealed that say no, and ask for written material you can read later, as the Illinois attorney general’s office advises. That office also notes that the supply section of your bill names the supplier you’re enrolled with. Check yours, and report a name you didn’t choose to your state’s utility commission or attorney general.

Industry observers note that A call, text, or email claims your bill is past due and your service will be cut off within the hour unless you pay right now. Caller ID may show your utility’s number, since scammers can fake it. Some send a payment link, or a QR code or barcode to pay at a store’s checkout or bill-pay kiosk, where the money goes straight to them.

Industry observers note that your utility mails disconnection notices before it cuts service, according to UUAS’s consumer guide to impostor scams, and a call, text, or visit is never its first and only warning. It also doesn’t tell you how you must pay. The Federal Trade Commission indicates only a scammer insists on a wire transfer, gift card, payment app, or crypto.

Recent findings suggest that hang up or delete the message without paying, and don’t call back any number the caller gives you. Check your balance through the number on your bill or your utility’s own app.

After a storm knocks out power, a caller claiming to be from your utility offers to move you up the repair list, or to reconnect you, for an upfront payment or a reconnection fee.

Utilities don’t charge to restore electricity, gas, or water service after an outage, the UUAS guide says, and any charge that does apply goes on your utility bill. If your own equipment needs repairs before power can come back, the utility will usually tell you to hire a licensed contractor.

Hang up, and follow restoration updates through your utility’s website, app, or the number on your bill.

According to recent reports, A caller, or someone at your door in a safety vest, says your meter or other equipment must be reset, repaired, or replaced and that you owe a fee for the work. Some threaten to come back and remove the meter if you don’t pay. Others claim there’s a gas or water leak nearby and ask to come inside, while a partner slips in to steal.

Sources indicate that A vest, a hard hat, and a badge prove nothing, since the UUAS guide notes they’re easy to buy online or print at home. A utility that needs to replace equipment contacts you ahead of time and doesn’t collect a separate payment for the work. A worker who needs to come inside will have an appointment and photo ID.

Keep the door locked, ask for ID through it, and call the number on your bill to confirm the visit before anyone comes in.

A robocall or caller says you overpaid your bill and promises a cash refund, sometimes with a discount on future bills, once you press a number or read off your bank account or card number.

Utility companies don’t usually give cash refunds, the FTC notes, and credit the extra to your account instead. The UUAS guide adds that some mail a check to the address on file. A caller who needs your bank or card number to send a refund is running the scam. At best, the FTC states, the call is a sales pitch from another energy provider, which could leave you paying two bills, your utility’s and the upcoming provider’s.

Hang up without pressing anything, then check for a credit in your online account or at the number on your bill.

The payment can seem to go through for a day or more, since a bank can take that long to reject it, but no money ever reaches the utility. The bill stays due, your utility may add a returned-payment fee, and the scammer keeps your Social Security number for identity theft.

The real federal help with energy bills doesn’t work this way. The Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program (LIHEAP) pays no grants directly to individuals and charges no fee, according to the Department of Health and Human Services. States, tribes, and territories run it, and you apply through a local office. The department asks you to report any message offering a LIHEAP grant or asking for a fee to its fraud hotline, 1-800-447-8477.

A caller posing as a federal agency is a government impostor, and the FTC says a real agency won’t call, text, or email you for money or personal information. If you’re behind on a bill, call the number on yours and ask about payment prepares and assistance, LIHEAP included.

Recent findings suggest that messages made to look like your utility’s, down to the logo, link to pages that ask for your login, card number, or a payment. Search ads work the same way, with sponsored results at the top of the page that lead to fake bill-payment sites.

In a recent development, utilities typically don’t text you unless you signed up for their alerts, the UUAS guide says. Assume any link, attachment, or QR code you didn’t ask for came from a scammer until your utility confirms it.

Type the web address printed on your bill, or open your utility’s own app, and never pay a bill through a search ad.

Sources indicate that call the number on your bill to report every attempt, even one you stopped. Tell your utility the name the scammer used, the number they called from or gave you, and how they wanted to be paid. Customer reports helped UUAS shut down those 14,830 numbers.

According to recent reports, if you already paid, call the gift card issuer, your bank, or the payment app right away and ask for your money back. Hold on to any gift card and its receipt.

Then report the scam to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, whether or not you lost money.
