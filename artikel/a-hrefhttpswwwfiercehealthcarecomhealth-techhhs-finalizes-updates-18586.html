The Trump administration finalized a major enhancement to the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) Privacy Rule designed to give patients easier, faster access to their health records and streamline care coordination.

The final rule, issued by the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services Office for Civil Rights (OCR), strengthens and clarifies the right of an individual to access their protected health information and reduces regulatory and administrative burdens, the agency said.

The proposed rule was issued more than five years ago, in January 2021. An unpublished version of the final rule is available here.

In a recent development, under the final rule, healthcare organizations must respond faster to patient requests for copies of their health records. HHS said covered entities will have no more than 15 calendar days to respond to a request, reduced from the current 30-day period. Organizations are allowed one extension of no more than 30 calendar days, HHS said in the final rule.

The rule also establishes that patients can access their protected health information through personal health apps when the covered entity has the technical capacity to deliver the records in that form and format.

The rule also expands circumstances when patients must receive copies of their health records for free, requires advance notice of fees and modifies identity verification requirements.

It has been revealed that to make it easier for healthcare organizations to share data for care coordination, the final rule clarifies that health plans may use and disclose protected health information for care coordination and case management for individuals. The rule also expressly permits covered entities to disclose PHI to social services agencies, community-based organizations and similar third parties for care coordination and case management for individuals.

It has been revealed that to reduce regulatory burden, OCR eliminated the requirement for providers with direct treatment relationships with individuals to make a good faith effort to obtain an individual’s written acknowledgment that they have received a copy of the health care provider’s Notice of Privacy Practices. The final rule also permits disclosures to Telecommunications Relay Services (TRS) communications assistants for persons who are deaf, hard of hearing, or deaf-blind, or who have a speech disability, and excludes TRS providers from the definition of business associate.

Sources indicate that “Americans should never have to fight for access to their own health records,” noted HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy, Jr in a statement. “Under President Trump’s leadership, we are putting patients in control, requiring faster access to their information, and cutting red tape that gets in the way of coordinated care. This rule modernizes HIPAA to serve patients and help providers spend more time delivering care and less time navigating bureaucracy.”.

Provisions in the final rule will become effective 60 days after publication in the Federal Register, and compliance will be required 180 days after that date, according to HHS in a fact sheet.

During a Monday fireside chat at the HLTH 2026 conference in Las Vegas, CMS Deputy Administrator and Chief Product Officer Amy Gleason said the updates to the privacy rule "remove friction from the patient experience.".

It has been revealed that gleason stated there are three main components of the rule: the designation of a "personal health app," identity verification and speed. "It should be almost instantaneous with electronic data, and it also states that this information is free to the patient," she said.

According to recent reports, “This final rule provides Americans with faster and easier access to their health information which is critical to them being able to take control of their own health and health care,” remarked Office for Civil Rights Director Paula M. Stannard in a statement. “Consistent with the Trump Administration’s deregulatory goals, the rule also reduces regulatory burdens on health care providers and other regulated entities and decreases costs for the health care system.

It has been revealed that while the Trump administration moved forward with changes to the HIPAA privacy rule, federal regulators delayed a major overhaul of the HIPAA Security Rule, pushing back final action on the rule by a year. HHS had proposed a May 2026 release for a final rule that makes significant changes to the HIPAA Security Rule and marks the first major revision to the 23-year-old rule in more than 10 years. The U.S. Office of Management and Budget (OMB) website was updated in July and indicates that the final rule was pushed back to July 2027.
