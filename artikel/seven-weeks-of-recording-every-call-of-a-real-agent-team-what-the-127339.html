Seven weeks of recording every call of a real agent team: what the log shows

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that A software tech giant runs a team of eight AI agents. They research and write articles, translate them, and prepare tasks for the developers. Since October 6, every model call these agents make goes through a local SealedRun recorder, now on the current release 0.10.0.

Nothing about the agents changed to make this happen. The recorder runs as a container on the same machine and the agents point their API base URL at it:.

From then on each call is forwarded to the provider and written into a hash-chained, signed record. Seven weeks later, here is what that record holds.

None of this is a dashboard someone filled in. It is what the recorder wrote, call by call, and what anyone with the exported bundle can check again offline.

According to recent reports, the 164 failed calls are the most useful part of the log. Most of them are rate limits (HTTP 429, about 65 of them), then access denied (403, twelve) and an overloaded provider (529, eight). On the worst day, November 9, the log holds 17 failed calls, the first at 07:56 UTC and the last at 16:53.

Sources indicate that every one of them sits in the chain between the successful calls before and after it. It has a time, a session, a model and an outcome. It cannot be dropped later without breaking the chain, and that is the point: the record highlights what actually happened, including the parts that went badly.

When an agent reports "done", the log says how many attempts it took to get there and which ones failed on the way.

28 sessions never closed. The largest started on November 22 at 12:29 UTC, made 213 calls and stopped at 19:15 with no closing record. Another one on November 15 stopped after 254 calls.

An agent that crashes, times out or is killed does not write a tidy summary. The record does not need one. The last sealed call is the exact step where the session stopped, with everything before it intact and verifiable. Whatever the agent claimed at the end, the chain demonstrates where its work really ended.

149 sessions carry anchors from a public RFC 3161 time-stamp authority run by Sigstore. An anchor binds the head of the chain to a time issued by a third party.

So the record cannot be rewritten after the fact by the agent, and not by the operator either. Changing an old call means changing every hash after it, and the anchored head would no longer match the time stamp that a third party signed.

According to recent reports, sealedRun is open source (Apache-2.0 for code, open specification). The recorder works as a proxy for LLM APIs, MCP servers and A2A calls, and one request exports all records for a time window as a report a lawyer or an insurer can read.

If your agents already run in production, the question is simple: if one of them makes a mistake or hides something, can you prove what it did?
