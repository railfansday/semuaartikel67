Has global jihadism failed, or is it simply changing shape?

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that rice, pasta, sugar, cooking oil and beans are hardly what you'd expect to see in jihadist propaganda. Yet these were among the supplies that al-Qaeda's Somali branch, al-Shabab, said its "charity" had handed out to hundreds of struggling families in August, as it publicised the aid through its social media accounts and radio station.

The group, which controls large parts of rural southern and central Somalia, has been keen to show off its so-called humanitarian work throughout 2026.

"May God reward them," stated a local woman in one of al-Shabab's videos.

It's all part of an effort to win over local communities and present itself as more than just a jihadist group.

Al-Shabab has been publicising its humanitarian work.

A quarter of a century after al-Qaeda's 9/11 attacks in the US, and 12 years after Islamic State group (IS) declared its so-called caliphate in Iraq and Syria, the jihadist movement looks very different.

In a recent development, rather than focusing on attacking the West or fighting for a global Islamic state, some - especially those linked to al-Qaeda - are putting more effort into winning power closer to home. And they seem increasingly willing to make compromises along the way.

Recent findings suggest that because of this, a debate is growing within the movement. While hardliners reject compromise, pragmatists see working with local groups and former enemies as a way to gain power. But do the pragmatists represent a genuine change or simply a shift in tactics to achieve the same ultimate goals: establishing a state that applies their strict interpretation of Islamic Sharia law both at home and in its dealings with the outside world?

Neither al-Qaeda nor IS has achieved its ultimate dream: taking over one or more countries and ruling them according to their strict interpretation of Islam.

Both have suffered major defeats, lost leaders and faced years of international pressure. IS, which once controlled vast areas of Iraq and Syria, lost its last territorial stronghold in 2019. But jihadism hasn't gone away.

Recent findings suggest that africa is now the main battleground for jihadism, with al-Qaeda's powerful branches, JNIM in the Sahel (Mali, Burkina Faso and Niger) and al-Shabab in Somalia, fighting long-running insurgencies. IS, its bitter rival, has active branches in Nigeria, the Democratic Republic of Congo, the Sahel, Mozambique and Somalia.

One major source of inspiration, and envy, whether jihadists admit it or not, is Syria's fresh president, Ahmed al-Sharaa. The former IS and then al-Qaeda commander was once wanted by the US but rose to power in Syria in December 2024 and was welcomed by Western leaders.

Once known by his militant nom de guerre Abu Mohammed al-Jolani, he led the jihadist group Nusra Front - initially linked to IS and then al-Qaeda - and later the group Hayat Tahrir al-Sham (HTS).

He gradually broke ties with both IS and al-Qaeda and distanced himself from their calls for global jihad.

Instead, he focused on Syria, building a local power base in the north-western Idlib province.

What surprised everyone was his growing willingness to work with non-Islamist groups and engage with foreign powers. As HTS advanced on Damascus, its leadership sought to reassure the international community, reaching out even to Russia, a key ally of then-president Bashar al-Assad, to allay fears of an Islamist takeover.

That pragmatism, combined with favourable geopolitical conditions - including a weaker Russia and Iran - helped him take power in Syria.

But the sight of him being welcomed by world leaders, including US President Donald Trump, was even more remarkable.

Trump joked with Sharaa when he visited the White House.

During Sharaa's visit to the White House in November 2025, Trump sprayed him with his own branded cologne, jokingly asked how many wives he had and praised him as a "very strong leader".

In a recent development, for some jihadists, Sharaa's success has been inspiring. Others, however, see his actions as a betrayal of everything they believe in.

IS has declared jihad against Syria's latest Islamist government, branding Sharaa and his officials "apostates", and has carried out attacks against government forces on that basis. Other hardline clerics have taken a similar stance, but have stopped short of openly calling for jihad against Sharaa.

The divide between hardline jihadists and their more pragmatic counterparts has fuelled a bitter online debate since 2025, with rival voices trading accusations of betrayal and even publishing competing books to defend their positions.

Sharaa, centre, in 2016, was once a jihadist leader wanted by the FBI.

It has been revealed that for hardliners, going too far can mean abandoning their core beliefs. This might include holding back from imposing their strict version of Islamic law, accepting "man-made" laws or a constitution, taking part in elections, working with non-Islamist groups, or building ties with governments they consider "infidel".

Industry observers note that another source of inspiration for jihadists was the Taliban's return to power in Afghanistan in 2021, as it demonstrated that an Islamist movement could eventually take control of a country after years of fighting Western-backed forces. It engaged local groups, slowly secured power across the country, used international diplomacy, and bided its time before taking the capital, Kabul, in a lightning offensive.

Two groups are especially worth watching: al-Qaeda's branches in Mali and Somalia.

It has been revealed that in late April, al-Qaeda's Sahel branch, known as JNIM, highlighted considerable pragmatism by joining forces with nationalist Tuareg separatists - whom it had occasionally fought - to launch a major offensive that threatened Mali's military rulers. It even went as far as to call the Tuaregs "brothers".

But it's not just JNIM's growing military strength that's attracting attention. It's also how the group is presenting itself.

Its propaganda once prominently featured al-Qaeda's leaders, slogans and calls for global jihad. Since late 2024, however, its videos have dropped the familiar al-Qaeda branding.

And instead of talking about global Muslim causes, JNIM has increasingly focused on local problems, portraying itself as a defender of ordinary people against corrupt and abusive rulers.

It has repeatedly accused Mali's military government and its Russian allies of killing civilians and making people's lives worse.

In April, it appealed to "all sincere patriots" to help build a "latest Mali", promising security, justice and prosperity alongside Islamic law.

It has even suggested it could have "balanced relations" with Russia, despite the fact they're fighting Russian forces backing Mali's government. Its wording echoed that of HTS to Russia as it advanced on Damascus.

There are parallels with Sharaa's approach in Syria: build local alliances, reassure potential enemies and avoid giving outside powers a reason to intervene.

Africa security specialist Aman Bezreh sees similarities between the two groups, but claims JNIM's shift may take time.

"JNIM has indeed shown signs of pragmatism similar to HTS", she indicates. "However, its rebranding has progressed more slowly because the group is simultaneously focused on expanding into new territories and recruiting from diverse pools.".

The debate over which path to follow is also taking place within JNIM's leadership, according to Wassim Nasr, a France 24 journalist and jihadism expert.

He says one camp favours the Taliban's approach of taking power through force with minimal concessions. The other, which he says at the moment has the upper hand, favours an approach closer to that of Syria's HTS - making compromises and building wider alliances to gain power more quickly.

Nasr believes JNIM may borrow from both models, but that a break with al-Qaeda is inevitable as a result of its political evolution.

Like JNIM, al-Shabab in Somalia appears willing to make deals with former enemies.

According to Abdullahi Guji, BBC Monitoring's Somalia specialist, al-Shabab reached a truce in October with a powerful Somali tribe, the Ma'awisley, whose fighters had been at the forefront of the fight against the group.

The tribe has since withdrawn its fighters from the front line, dealing a serious blow to the government's campaign against jihadists, says Guji.

Guji believes the group may soon have a chance to test whether it can seize power and establish itself as an alternative government across wider parts of the country.

African Union forces are withdrawing from Somalia amid funding cuts and the approaching end of their mandate in December. Meanwhile, the Somali government is facing political crises and rebellions, creating vulnerabilities al-Shabab could exploit, he adds.

There's another reason some jihadists may see an opportunity to expand: they believe Western governments have bigger fish to fry.

With the war in Ukraine, US-Iran conflict in the Middle East and growing rivalries with China and Russia, fighting jihadists is no longer the priority it was after 9/11.

The US withdrawal from Afghanistan in 2021 and the Taliban's return to power, followed by Sharaa's rise in Syria, have reinforced that belief.

The Taliban has inspired many jihadists.

Colin Clarke, executive director of the Soufan Center, a non-profit research group that focuses on security, indicates some jihadist groups have learnt that avoiding confrontation with the US can help them escape Western military action and "live to fight another day".

Industry observers note that that doesn't mean the West has stopped fighting terrorism. But if jihadists believe it is distracted or less willing to intervene, they may feel emboldened to pursue power.

Sources indicate that unlike its fiercely uncompromising rival IS, al-Qaeda has long shown some flexibility in pursuing its goals. But history offers a warning.

After the 2011 Arab uprisings, which largely sidelined al-Qaeda, affiliated jihadist groups in Yemen, Libya and Tunisia began using the name Ansar al-Sharia (Supporters of Sharia) to win local support without carrying the al-Qaeda brand.

It has been revealed that in Mali in 2012, al-Qaeda leaders advised jihadists who had seized the north alongside Tuareg separatists not to rush into imposing strict Islamic rules. Instead, they urged them to work with local allies, avoid alienating communities and play down their al-Qaeda links.

Industry observers note that the message wasn't to give up their ambitions. It was to show strategic patience until the time was right. That same thinking could be at work today.

So are these groups really changing their ambitions, or just their tactics? Even those advocating compromise aren't necessarily abandoning their dream of establishing a state ruled by their strict interpretation of Islamic law.

Clarke wonders whether this more pragmatic approach could be a "Trojan horse strategy" â a way for jihadist groups to gain power without giving up their longer-term goals.

"The major question is whether being in power actually moderates these groups, or whether they're simply changing their tactics to survive and strengthen their position," he claims.

But not everyone is changing tactics.

Sources indicate that IS remains firmly committed to its global ambitions and rejects compromise. Al-Qaeda's Yemen branch, AQAP, continues to call for attacks against the US and Israel, and urges "lone wolf" attacks against Western and Jewish targets.

And it's too early to say whether groups like JNIM and al-Shabab will follow the Taliban or Syrian examples, and whether they're willing to make compromises.

According to recent reports, some jihadist groups may become more focused on local politics as they face the challenges of governing. Others may simply be hiding their wider ambitions until they're strong enough to pursue them.

The global jihadist dream may have failed, but jihadism is far from finished.
