I have always been enamored with the different ways that games have been brought to life, and Mods in particular have always felt magical to me. Someone, somewhere, loves a game so much that they start tinkering with it in their free time. Every now and then, that little side project ends up taking on a life of its own. Some of the biggest games in the world, including two of the biggest esports I write about, started out exactly like that.

The same goes for bonus modes and custom maps that were never meant to be more than a fun extra. A few devs probably thought it would be fun to include a little bit of an extra to fans, or had an idea with limited resources that they wanted to give a try. Some of them went on to get their own releases, others created entire genres, and a few of them quite frankly outshone the game they were tucked away in. And in the spirit of that, let's take a look at ten side modes that became bigger than the game they came from.

Sources indicate that right, before you are all coming for me with pitchforks: Gwent never actually became bigger than The Witcher 3, and I have no qualms about admitting that straight away. Very few things could. The hype around it was something else, though: people happily ignored the end of the world to sit in taverns and play cards with every merchant, blacksmith and innkeeper they came across, and CD Projekt Red clearly noticed.

In a recent development, the standalone version, GWENT: The Witcher Card Game, is something fans eagerly awaited. It did, however, come with its own sets of issues. The version of Gwent it delivered displayed a few stark differences to the version people knew and fell in love with: It was free-to-play, quite competitive, and ultimately, a lot of fans simply never warmed to it. If you're after that original feeling, most people will point you towards Thronebreaker: The Witcher Tales instead.

It has been revealed that still, a card game from inside an RPG becoming popular enough to get its own release almost never happens. CD Projekt Red supported it until the end of 2023, and even then, it handed the game over to the community rather than switching it off. It didn't quite live up to the hype, but it absolutely earned its place on this list.

Sources indicate that the Stanley Parable really shouldn't work as well as it does. You're an office worker, a narrator tells you exactly what you're doing, and it's up to you whether you want to listen to him. It's one of the funniest games I know, and it started out as a free Half-Life 2 mod that Davey Wreden released in 2011.

It has been revealed that wreden wanted to play around with what storytelling in games could be, and it clearly struck a nerve: the mod was downloaded more than 90,000 times in just two weeks. William Pugh then reached out about turning it into something bigger, and the two of them spent the next two years working on it entirely online. They only met in person a month after it came out, which is a detail to the origin story that I personally find quite charming.

That remake released in 2013 and sold over 100,000 copies within a matter of days. Ultra Deluxe followed in 2022 and did the same within 24 hours.

In a recent development, team Fortress 2 is so closely tied to Valve that it's easy to forget it didn't start there at all. It began as a Quake mod in 1996, made by three Australians, Robin Walker, John Cook and Ian Caughley, who had been inspired by a little Doom map they came across at LAN parties. Two teams, a bunch of very different classes, and everyone has a job to do. Sound familiar?

Valve liked it so much that it bought the rights and offered its creators jobs. Caughley mentioned no, but Walker and Cook moved over, and Team Fortress Classic came out in 1999 as a mod for Half-Life. It then took until 2007 for Team Fortress 2 to finally arrive, with a cartoony art style that has aged better than most games from that year.

Once it went free-to-play in 2011, it became bigger and better. Somewhere between capturing the intelligence and pushing the payload, a huge part of the community became very, very invested in hats. I'm not judging, but I am a little bit scared of how much money has gone into them.

In a recent development, as someone who writes about esports, the lore behind Dota is one of my favorite tidbits of the gaming world. It all started with Aeon of Strife, a custom StarCraft map, which inspired a modder called Eul to make Defense of the Ancients for Warcraft III all the way back in 2003. Eventually, Eul made it open-source, and from there, it passed from hand to hand.

The most popular version became DotA Allstars, made by Steve "Guinsoo" Feak, and when he moved on, the mysterious IceFrog took over. Guinsoo went on to help create League of Legends, which means one Warcraft III map is responsible for two of the biggest esports on the planet. What a wild family tree!

Valve hired IceFrog in 2009, Dota 2 came out in July 2013, and the rest is happily ever after: it has been one of the most played games on Steam ever since. Truth be told, I love that the person who has shaped Dota more than anyone is still somewhat of a mystery. Somehow, that makes the whole thing even more feel like classic Dota.

Industry observers note that counter-Strike is one of the most influential FPS games ever created, but it doubled as probably the most impressive job application to exist. Minh "Gooseman" Le was in his last semester at university and wanted something that would help him get into game development, so he started working on a mod for Half-Life. Jess "Cliffe" Cliffe ran the website and the community, and the first beta came out in June 1999.

Industry observers note that every new beta brought in more players, and by 2000, Valve had bought the rights and hired them both. From there, it was Source, Global Offensive and, in 2023, Counter-Strike 2, with the series becoming one of the biggest esports in the world along the way. Its Majors are still some of the biggest events in esports.

It has been revealed that counter-Strike 2 still regularly sits at the very top of Steam's charts, with over 30 million monthly players. Gooseman, for the record, definitely got the job. I'd say it worked out.

In a recent development, there was a point where it felt like every other game had zombies in it, and DayZ was right at the heart of that. It started out in 2012 as a mod for Arma 2, made by Dean "Rocket" Hall, and threw you into a huge open world full of the undead. The zombies were never really the scary part, though: other players were, because you never knew whether the person waving at you wanted to team up or take your tinned food.

In a recent development, you needed Arma 2 to play the mod, so its popularity sent a three-year-old military sim right back to the top of Steam's charts. It reached a million players within four months, Hall joined Bohemia Interactive, and work began on a standalone version. Bohemia, quite understandably, was delighted.

In a recent development, that version released into Early Access in December 2013 and sold over two million copies in under six months, before its full release in 2018. I'll say this much: it hasn't moved all that far away from the mod, and that is a good thing. If you like survival games and don't mind a bit of betrayal, it's well worth your time.

Industry observers note that I’ll be honest, most of the Skyrim mods I personally perused have always been about silly things like turning every dragon into Thomas the Tank Engine. Those of you with more dignified taste, however, might be aware of The Forgotten City. It’s a time-travelling murder mystery set in an ancient underground city. Nick Pearce spent more than 1,700 hours on it, and it clearly was time well spent: the mod had over three million downloads, and an Australian Writers' Guild Award, the first time a video game had ever won one.

In a recent development, pearce quit his job as a lawyer and rebuilt it as a standalone game with two other developers. It moved from Skyrim to ancient Rome, but kept its central idea: there is one rule, and if anyone breaks it, everybody pays. Steal from someone or hurt them, and the entire city is turned into gold.

Luckily, you get sent back to the start of the day whenever that happens, with everything you've collected so far. It's almost roguelike in that way, and every loop teaches you a little more about the people around you. I think it's one of the best detective games out there, and it started its life as a Skyrim mod.

According to recent reports, call of Duty: World at War was a very serious entry about the Pacific front of World War II, which is exactly why it's so funny that most people remember it for zombies. Treyarch hid Nacht der Untoten at the end of the campaign as an Easter egg, with no marketing behind it at all. You finished the story, and suddenly you were boarding up windows in a bunker whilst zombies tried to get in.

It has been revealed that it was a hit straight away, so every map pack after that came with a newly introduced Zombies map. The first map didn't even have a story, but fans started building one anyway, and Treyarch eventually leaned all the way into it. The result is one of the most convoluted storylines in gaming, spread across multiple Black Ops games.

According to recent reports, zombies has been part of nearly every Call of Duty since, and these days, it's not unusual for it to be the main reason people pick up a Treyarch game at all. Quite the career for an Easter egg. Not even the developers saw that one coming.

It has been revealed that dota Auto Chess is the youngest entry on this list, and boy, did it blow up fast. Drodo Studio released it as a custom game inside Dota 2 in January 2019. You put Dota heroes on a chessboard, combine them to make them stronger and then sit back whilst they fight on their own, and the developers have actually mentioned that Mahjong was a massive inspiration.

Within months, it had over eight million players, and the substantial studios very rapidly wanted a piece of it. Valve made Dota Underlords, Riot added Teamfight Tactics to League of Legends, and Blizzard brought Battlegrounds to Hearthstone. Drodo Studio, meanwhile, released its own standalone Auto Chess.

The hype has calmed down since then, but the auto battler genre is still very much here, and all of it traces back to one custom game. I find it quite funny that one of the biggest gaming trends of 2019 started out as just another option in Dota 2's custom games menu. Never underestimate a menu.

In a recent development, the Binding of Isaac has a truly baffling amount of mods, and as someone that used to be utterly besotted with both the OG as well as The Binding of Isaac: Rebirth, I have tried a large amount of them. A lot of them were high quality, but Antibirth was something else. Released by a group of fans in December 2016, it was basically an entire expansion: thousands of newly introduced rooms, nearly 100 new items and even its own soundtrack. It felt so much like Isaac that it was hard to believe it wasn't official.

Industry observers note that edmund McMillen clearly shared the fans’ feelings. He loved how well it understood the themes and style of his game, and rather than ignoring it, he started working with the team behind it. In 2018, he confirmed The Binding of Isaac: Repentance, the final DLC for the game, built on Antibirth.

Sources indicate that repentance released in 2021, with the Antibirth team helping to make their mod official, alongside a whole lot of new content. I love that a fan project ended up becoming the final chapter of the game it was made for. Not many modders can say that.
