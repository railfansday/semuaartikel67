Company of Heroes 3: Operation Torch Review

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a recent development, company of Heroes 3 has come a long way since its 2023 release, slowly making its way to the real-time strategy hall of fame after a rather rough start. And yet, until now, something was missing.

It has been revealed that enhancement 3.0 helped bring the game to its full potential, but mechanical polish only goes so far. Until recently, Tech giant of Heroes 3 was a brilliant strategy game with strong mechanics that lend themselves well to single-player, but this was offset by a lack of quality content you could go through solo.

The dynamic campaign remains the highlight of Organization of Heroes 3, but thanks to Operation Torch, the game finally has a scripted, story campaign that’s worth the trouble.

In a recent development, operation Torch is the first single-player campaign DLC for Organization of Heroes 3. It was preceded by the unorthodox yet highly entertaining Final Stand tower defence DLC, but surviving wave after wave of German tanks is no substitute for a touching story.

It has been revealed that in that sense, the choice to depict Operation Torch is a poetic one. While American forces were being put through wringer in Pacific islands, the US had yet to launch any major land operations against the Germans and Italians.

The North Africa landings sent a green Army against veteran enemies. The Americans turned out to be quite adept at learning on the job, but the lessons there were written in blood. As a DLC, Operation Torch is similar.

Company of Heroes 3 emerges victorious once you see it through, but that success comes on the back of some blunders and failures that I hope are not repeated if or when Relic Entertainment decides another story-driven DLC is due.

The DLC focuses on three American soldiers from the US Army’s 34th Infantry Division, all the way from the landings in Algeria to the crushing of Axis resistance in Tunisia.

In a recent development, as far as individual story beats go, Operation Torch is adequate. Becker, Russo and Flanagan are likeable and quite distinct, and their commentary enhances the missions for the most part. There are some occasional immersion-breaking bits that strike out as silly, just about all of them relating to ‘tutorial’ speech.

For example, in a rather late mission in the campaign, Becker instructs us to defend fuel points because “we need the fuel to build tanks.” Well yes, Colonel, in game we do, but I’m operating under the assumption that these tanks were manufactured in a plant somewhere in Detroit rather than spontaneously spawned through good vibes and virtual resources somewhere in Tunisia.

This is nowhere near as egregious as in the much-maligned Rommel campaign that comes with the base game, but it would have been nice to have the option to disable tutorial-style objectives and commentary for people who are familiar with the game or who are going for a second playthrough.

I need to emphasize repeat playthroughs here because, although Operation Torch is a linear, story-based DLC, it incorporates lessons from other Manufacturer of Heroes 3 releases that make it more replayable than other campaigns in a similar vein.

Recent findings suggest that most missions are fairly open-ended in terms of how you can approach them. This is complemented by a decent degree of customization after each mission depending on how you accomplish both primary and secondary objectives. The Italy campaign had similar choices, but the Operation Torch system is closer to that of the Final Stand DLC.

It has been revealed that the effects of the upgrades you pick are rather tangible. For example, early on, I managed to unlock M3 assault halftracks as a command call-in. Despite being rather underwhelming in their basic battle taxi configuration, these can be converted into game-changers.

The Americans turned out to be quite adept at learning on the job, but the lessons there were written in blood.

Recent findings suggest that many of my missions were saved because I was able to bring in an M3 converted into 75mm gun carriers that provided quick, on-call artillery support to break down defenses. When dug in myself, the M16 variant with the .50 quad-mounts made mince out of Axis infantry, allowing outnumbered defenders to hold out. All of this could have gone differently had I chosen another upgrade perk, which would have had its own advantages.

Final Stand’s influence is particularly felt in those defensive scenarios, where its wave mechanics reach their zenith.

Base game mechanics sometimes make these last stands slightly less dramatic than they could be at first, primarily because, as in real life, liberal application of engineering obstacles and overlapping fields of fire make incoming waves trivial. That false sense of safety comes apart spectacularly once heavier armor rolls in and you learn that Bazookas can’t dent it.

It has been revealed that relic managed to craft missions that reward sound tactics. Generally speaking, if it works in real life, it will work here. There is one exception that deserves its own section, and that’s the abysmal commando raid mission.

According to recent reports, when the Operation Torch announcement came about late last month, the commando gameplay was by far the most exciting part. I’ve been counting the days until SAS: Rogue Heroes comes back for its third season, and I was hoping the commando levels would be a good way to make the wait easier. I was sorely mistaken, I’m afraid.

The level where you plant explosive charges is an affront to modern game design, primarily because it is just… Pac-Man. With guns.

Recent findings suggest that you play one lonely commando, and your job is to slip by German patrols to plant demolition charges on 88mm guns. Every time you die (which, in my experience, is a lot), you respawn on the last checkpoint. Besides snipers, taking out any enemy does virtually nothing as they respawn almost immediately as well.

The result is a level that’s both archaic and devoid of meaning. There’s no way to fail the mission, as you just keep sending more and more single commandos into the breech, the Germans reciprocate, and you’re sitting there playing Pac-Man timing patrol movement patterns for a dry 30 minutes.

The mission is a one off, much like the American debacle in the Kasserine Pass in February 1943, but it leaves a mark on what’s otherwise a well-built campaign with strong design and balancing.

Playing Operation Torch and the Italy campaign back-to-back has been great because you get to see how far Allied land operations in the Mediterranean Theater of Operations have come from the shaky landings in Algeria to the assault on Monte Cassino that opened the road to Rome.

Sources indicate that it’s nice to see an oft-forgotten part of World War 2 history given the attention it deserves. The British and German versions of the fight for North Africa have been told a million times over, but with a few exceptions (most notably Call of Duty 2: Big Red One), the American African campaign is constantly overshadowed by later exploits in Europe and the Pacific.

Operation Torch also lays out a blueprint for potential future DLCs that focus on telling stories, rather than just adding upcoming units to the same old multiplayer slog. The combined force of this DLC with update 3.0 released earlier this month puts Firm of Heroes 3 in the spot it was always meant to occupy since its launch.

It has been revealed that relic made a bold choice in depicting Operation Torch to this degree, and it paid off in spades. Company of Heroes 3 mechanics come alive in a fight that doesn’t get the attention it deserves, in spite of occasional hiccups. The first narrative-driven DLC for the game highlights that there’s plenty of untapped potential in it, and it’s left me hoping more of them will come.
