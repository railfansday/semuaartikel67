Part 1 ended with a unit of delivery the size of one person. This is the part where that person stops being a heroic exception and becomes a safe default.

Part 1 argued that the atomic unit of delivery has moved. When synthesis becomes nearly free, the scarce input is no longer the person-hour that writes the change but the judgment that decides whether it should exist. A structure whose every interface is priced by headcount cannot absorb a resource priced at zero, so the bottleneck moved downstream into verification and the org chart stayed where it was.

According to recent reports, that leaves an uncomfortable question, and it is not a motivational one. If the unit of delivery is now a single engineer carrying end-to-end responsibility, what has to be true for that to be safe? Not faster. Safe. Because a lone engineer with a model and a repository is not a smaller team; it is a different object with different failure modes, and you cannot make it safe by asking it to be brave.

Industry observers note that this part answers that question. The answer is three roles around a cell, and the property that makes them work is not their job descriptions. It is what each one is structurally forbidden from doing.

ANTcell — AI-Native Team Cell — starts from a different first principle than the incrementalists. It does not ask how to make a five-to-eleven-person team more productive with AI. It asks what the team was for, and whether the reason still holds.

The team was the atom because one engineer could not hold frontend craft, a distributed backend, quality engineering, and operations in the same head. Under an AI Capability Platform — standardized model APIs, agent sandboxes, and a retrieval layer over the organization's own context — that constraint softens. The platform can supply conventions, failure history, and candidate implementations on demand. The engineer's job shifts from producing the change to deciding among changes the platform makes cheap to produce.

Industry observers note that so the unit of delivery becomes one engineer with the authority to decide. Not a hero engineer selected for exceptional breadth — that would just be the old constraint with better recruiting. An ordinary engineer, made sufficient by a platform and made safe by a structure around them.

According to recent reports, this is the part skeptics usually stop at. One person, end to end, with real authority. That sounds like an accountability improvement until you remember why accountability was diluted in the first place: a single person cannot hold business intent, the whole technical context, and the discipline of working with a model that confidently hallucinates. Give one person all three and you have not created a cell. You have created a bottleneck with a name badge.

The three roles exist to solve that. They do not assist the engineer by taking work off their desk. They surround the engineer with the three things no individual can supply to themselves — and, critically, each is barred from becoming the thing the engineer is accountable for.

Recent findings suggest that part 1 separated three jobs that the word "team" had fused: the unit of delivery, the unit of accountability, and the unit of coordination. AI attacks the first, demands the second shrink, and turns the third into a platform problem. The three roles are the organizational answer to that separation.

Each role maps to one thing the engineer cannot provide for themselves:.

According to recent reports, the mistake to avoid is treating these as advisors. Advisors can be ignored, and an ignored advisor is accountability theater. These roles are owners: each holds one boundary that the engineer cannot cross alone, and none can cross into the engineer's decision.

In a recent development, the Supervisor owns business intent, the semantic boundary of the work, and the acceptance gate. In return for owning "what," the Supervisor gives up "how." They do not decompose the work into micro-tasks and they do not choose the technical approach.

It has been revealed that that prohibition is the whole design. In SAFe, the Product Manager and Product Owner functions have drifted toward managing the backlog at fine granularity — a queue of small, pre-digested items that the team pulls and executes. That made sense when the expensive act was producing the item and the scarce resource was the team's capacity to pull it. Once production is cheap, fine-grained decomposition becomes the bottleneck: you are paying a human to pre-chew work that a platform can explore three ways in an hour, and you are stripping the engineer of exactly the judgment you claimed the new unit was for.

The Supervisor sets the boundary and then is structurally unable to reach inside it. Acceptance is a gate, not a stream of instructions. If the Supervisor finds themselves specifying how the work gets done, the cell has reverted to a capacity queue with a faster generator attached — which is the failure Part 1 already described.

The Supporter is the interface between the engineer and the complexity of the organization. They hold the global technical context, the governance constraints, and the accumulated memory of why the system is shaped the way it is. The whitepaper's metaphor is a living dictionary: when the engineer needs to know which unwritten rule a change will violate, the Supporter is where that answer lives.

In a recent development, the structural constraint here is sharper than in the other two roles. The Supporter must not be the engineer's performance supervisor. Coaching and evaluation are decoupled by design.

It has been revealed that this is not a soft, cultural preference. It is an information-flow requirement. If the person who holds the technical context also writes your review, then asking "why is this service shaped this way?" is no longer a neutral query — it is an admission on a performance record. The engineer will stop asking, the platform will be fed incomplete context, and the whole advantage of a context-rich cell evaporates. You cannot get the benefit of a living dictionary from a person the engineer has learned not to consult.

SAFe's Team Lead or Architect often carries both technical guidance and administrative reporting. That concentration was tolerable when the lead was coordinating eleven people and the engineer's judgment was a small part of throughput. It is not tolerable when the engineer's judgment is the throughput. The same role that was a convenience becomes a tax on the only scarce input in the system.

In a recent development, the third role is the one with no clean SAFe ancestor. The AI Helper owns the human-AI practice itself: pairing with the engineer, tuning context and prompts, controlling hallucination, and — the part that compounds — converting each incident into a deterministic rule so the same failure cannot recur.

In a recent development, the closest SAFe role is the Scrum Master or Agile Coach, and the comparison is instructive. That role facilitates process in the abstract: ceremonies, retros, flow. It was designed for a world where the main risk was poor coordination between people. The AI Helper is designed for a world where the main risk is a confident, plausible, wrong artifact entering the codebase, and where the fix for today's hallucination should become tomorrow's automated check.

It has been revealed that where the Scrum Master asks "is the process healthy?", the AI Helper asks "which failures have we made impossible to repeat?" The output of the role is not a smoother meeting. It is a growing set of durable rules — a test, a schema constraint, a gate — that permanently removes a class of error. This is the engineering of the upcoming bottleneck, not facilitation of the old one.

It has been revealed that at this point it is tempting to read the three roles as a renaming exercise: call the PO a Supervisor, call the Architect a Supporter, call the Coach an AI Helper, and declare the transformation complete. That would produce exactly the structure Part 1 criticizes — the same container with new labels.

What makes it more than relabeling is that each role is defined by a prohibition as much as a duty:.

Remove the prohibitions and you have not decentralized anything. You have three managers where there was one. Keep them and you have a structure where the engineer holds authority, the three surrounding roles hold the three things no single person should hold alone, and none of them can quietly take the decision back.

Industry observers note that this is what the whitepaper calls cognitive protection. It is not protection from hard work. It is protection from the specific ways a judging mind gets corrupted: being told what to think, being afraid to ask, or being left alone with a tool that lies fluently. Each role neutralizes one of those. Together they make the cell a safe default instead of a heroic exception.

It has been revealed that A critique that concedes nothing is not worth reading. The PM, the Architect, and the Coach exist for real reasons: someone must own intent, someone must hold the system's memory, someone must coach the practice. None of those functions is imaginary, and an organization that simply deletes them will rediscover them within a quarter under different names.

Sources indicate that ANTcell does not delete them. It re-slices them along the boundary that now matters — intent versus context versus practice — and it separates evaluation from enablement, which the old structure fused. That is a genuine change in the topology of authority, not a vocabulary change.

But it is also easy to fake. Adopt the three titles, keep the reporting lines, leave the Supervisor decomposing work into tickets and the Supporter writing reviews, and you have changed the org chart's labels while preserving its behavior. The test is not what the roles are called. It is whether the three prohibitions survive contact with a deadline.

The claim is narrower than "teams are obsolete.".

According to recent reports, A cell needs a platform underneath it: a standardized model API, an agent sandbox, and a retrieval layer over the organization's real context. Without that substrate, the single engineer is not made sufficient — they are simply under-supported, and the arrangement fails as understaffing always does. It also needs an engineer who can actually exercise judgment; the model does not supply that, and no amount of tooling converts an engineer who needs pre-digested tasks into one who owns a boundary.

It has been revealed that and it does not abolish what a team buys beyond delivery: fault isolation, on-call redundancy, a hiring surface, psychological safety for junior engineers, the informal learning of two people looking at one problem. Part 1 conceded all of that and still holds it. The re-slicing touches the three jobs the old team fused; it does not claim to replace every reason humans work near each other.

Finally, this is not a reorganization you execute in a quarter. The platform prerequisites and the role separation have to mature together, and organizations that jump to the labels without the substrate get the worst of both structures. That sequencing is the subject of Part 4.

Accountability dilutes with size. A team of nine that owns an outcome owns it collectively, which at 3 a.m. means no single person owns the decision to ship or roll back. Adding a model to that team increases the supply of candidate decisions without changing who is permitted to make one — the exact configuration that produces a review queue.

According to recent reports, A cell reverses this. The engineer has the authority to decide because the three surrounding roles have taken the three things they could not safely hold alone, and the platform has made the verification cheap enough that one person can actually check the decision. The span of what one person can personally validate has expanded; the structure is what lets them use it.

That is the argument in one sentence: the cell is not a smaller team, it is a team whose three non-delivery functions have been pulled out and given their own owners, so that the remaining person can be accountable for something real.

Industry observers note that none of this works on a stopwatch unless the clock is designed. A cell that can produce three candidates an hour still sits inside an organization that looks in eight-to-twelve-week increments. Part 3 is about that mismatch — why the twelve-week PI and the sixty-minute pipeline are not in conflict but are two halves of one control system, and how standardized contracts replace cross-team waiting with platform verification.

Part 4 is the part enterprises will actually use: the four-level maturity path from Assisted to Integrated, and why skipping a level fails in a predictable way rather than an unpredictable one.

Industry observers note that the single engineer is not a staffing optimization. It is the consequence of taking the cost structure from Part 1 seriously: when synthesis is nearly free and judgment is scarce, you put authority where the judgment is, and you build a structure around that person that supplies what they cannot supply themselves and forbids anyone from taking the decision back.

It has been revealed that three roles. Three prohibitions. One engineer who can be held accountable for something a team of nine only owns in the abstract. That is the cell, and it is why the unit is not the team.
