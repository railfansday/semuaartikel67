Breaking: Congress passes bill to protect older investors from financial fraud

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Bipartisan legislation aimed at protecting older Americans and other vulnerable adults from financial fraud has cleared Congress, putting it on track to become law.

Called the Financial Exploitation Prevention Act of 2025 and passed by the House in June, H.R. 2478 was approved by unanimous consent in the Senate Wednesday night — meaning no senators objected to its passage.

Recent findings suggest that the measure will next head to President Donald Trump's desk. The White House did not respond to a CNBC request for comment on whether Trump plans to sign the bill into law.

The bill would generally allow mutual fund companies and their transfer agents, which handle shareholder records and transactions, to temporarily delay paying out redemption requests when they suspect an older or otherwise vulnerable investor is being financially exploited.

Recent findings suggest that "Financial exploitation can have devastating consequences for victims and their families," remarked Erin Koeppel, a managing director and government relations and public policy counsel at the CFP Board, the organization that oversees the certified financial planner certification. "This legislation fills an key regulatory gap that can help protect vulnerable investors before assets are lost.".

The measure's congressional approval comes amid growing financial fraud among older Americans. Those age 60 or older reported losing about $3 billion to fraud in 2025, a 25% increase from $2.4 billion in 2024, according to the Federal Trade Commission's latest report to Congress on fraud among older adults, which was released in October. That amount is 400% higher than the $600 million reported lost in 2020.

"Generally, we support all legislation that aims to prevent any form of financial elder abuse," remarked Bob Blancato, national coordinator for the Elder Justice Coalition, an advocacy group.

Sources indicate that while just 6% of older fraud victims reported losing $100,000 or more in 2025, those losses accounted for 70% of the age group's total reported losses. The number of people reporting losses of that magnitude rose nearly sixfold to 6,769 in 2025, from 1,136 in 2020.

However, because many scams go unreported, the FTC estimates actual losses in 2025 could have been anywhere from $13.3 billion to $119.5 billion. In 2024, the estimated range was from $10.1 billion to $81.5 billion.

It has been revealed that scammers are increasingly targeting Americans' life savings by impersonating banks, businesses and government agencies, according to the FTC. The schemes often involve fabricated emergencies that convince victims to transfer money to supposedly protect their accounts from being compromised.

Additionally, content created by artificial intelligence that can help criminals carry out schemes is becoming increasingly difficult to detect and easier to make, according to the FBI.

Under the Financial Exploitation Prevention Act, mutual fund companies and their transfer agents could temporarily postpone payments when they reasonably believe a redemption request involves financial exploitation.

This would apply to investors age 65 and older, as well as younger adults reasonably believed to have a mental or physical impairment that prevents them from protecting their own interests.

Sources indicate that companies could initially delay paying out redemption proceeds for up to 15 business days. They could extend the hold by another 10 business days if they still reasonably believe exploitation already occurred, is underway or was attempted. State regulators, certain government agencies or courts could also extend the hold.

The bill also addresses the use of a "trusted contact" — an individual whom financial firms can contact when concerns arise about an investor's account. That person may be able to intervene to prevent the fraud by reaching out to the account holder.

Mutual fund companies that opt into the new protections would be required to ask customers to provide the name and contact information of at least one trusted contact.

According to recent reports, the legislation would build on existing investor protections. Already, the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority, or FINRA, permits brokerage firms to temporarily place holds on certain transactions if they suspect financial exploitation of an older or otherwise vulnerable investor.

Recent findings suggest that additionally, FINRA requires brokerages to make a reasonable effort to add a trusted contact to accounts, although investors are not required to provide one. Many banks and other financial institutions also ask account holders for a trusted contact.

In a recent development, separately, another House-passed bipartisan bill that aims to provide some relief for certain scam victims is awaiting action in the Senate. That measure, the Tax Relief for Fraud Victims Act, is intended to prevent people who lose money to scams from suffering additional financial harm through the federal tax system.

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 temporarily limited deductions for personal theft losses, and Trump's "big beautiful bill" of 2025 made those limits permanent. Some investment scam losses may still be deductible, but losses from some romance and impersonation scams generally aren't, according to a 2025 IRS memo. Victims who withdraw money from a traditional 401(k) or IRA may also owe income tax on the withdrawal and, if they're under 59½, possibly a 10% early-withdrawal penalty.

Sources indicate that the Tax Relief for Fraud Victims Act, H.R. 9500, would repeal those deduction limits and waive the 10% penalty on withdrawals tied to deductible fraud losses. The House passed it 408-17 in September, and it was referred to the Senate Finance Committee. It's unclear whether the Senate will take it up before this Congress ends.

"We would hope ... the Senate will join the House in passing H.R. 9500 to end another form of elder financial abuse," Blancato said.
