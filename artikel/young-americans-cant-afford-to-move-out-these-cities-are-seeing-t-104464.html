One in three U.S. adults between age 18 and 34 live with their parents, according to a latest report from Realtor.com. That’s 25.2 million or 33% of young adults living at home in 2025, only a sliver less than the 33.6% during the pandemic-era peak in 2020. Living at home is one way that Americans who can’t afford to buy or rent on their own wrestle with the high cost of housing and other expenses. While the cost of housing is a nationwide problem, the share of adults living with their parents offers an interesting glimpse into regional affordability patterns around the country. “The pattern of young adults at home has spread beyond the most expensive markets, with the share rising most in parts of the South and West, including some that have traditionally supported an earlier launch,” Realtor.com’s chief economist, Danielle Hale, wrote in the report. “As young people adapt to high housing costs, they are delaying homeownership and the wealth creation that comes with it.” Two stories with the same ending Predictably, at least one of the country’s priciest cities is among those with the largest share of adults living with their parents—but the full story is more complicated. In Philadelphia, Miami, Baltimore, and Houston, more than 38% of adults in that age range lived with their parents. Across Los Angeles, Atlanta, and Chicago, the share of adults living at home was over 35%. Houston, Atlanta, and Baltimore aren’t known for their high cost of living, unlike costly urban hubs like New York and Los Angeles, but home prices have soared in recent years in those markets. That rapid change to local affordability is freezing many young adults out of a housing market they might have been able to afford during a previous era. The share of adults living at home has grown the quickest in Las Vegas, San Francisco, and San Jose, but those metros tell different stories. In San Francisco and San Jose, two expensive cities close to the AI boom, housing prices have always been high, even as they grow higher still. In Las Vegas, once known as a relatively affordable market in the West, home prices have skyrocketed by more than 60% since 2019. “Las Vegas and the Bay Area show two different paths to the same challenge,” wrote Hannah Jones, senior economist at Realtor.com. “In Las Vegas, the sharp rise in home prices has made it harder for young adults to buy. In San Francisco and San Jose, already-high housing costs and limited inventory leave many young adults facing steep barriers to living on their own.” Living at home on the rise Regionally, the Northeast still dominates with 36.9% of young adults living at home in 2025, though that number is trending down. The West is next with a 32.4% “co-residence” rate, followed by 32.3% in the South and 31.7% in the Midwest. That share is rising everywhere except the Northeast, demonstrating that the housing affordability crisis may look distinct in different corners of the country, but it’s something that a surprising share of Americans everywhere are struggling with. “For young adults who hope to live independently or buy a home, elevated costs and limited entry-level supply can delay household formation and, for would-be buyers, the opportunity to build home equity,” Jones said. “Until affordability improves and entry-level supply expands, the report finds that pent-up demand is likely to continue accumulating.”.
