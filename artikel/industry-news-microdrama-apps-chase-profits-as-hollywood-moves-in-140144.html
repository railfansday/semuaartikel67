Industry News: Microdrama apps chase profits as Hollywood moves into short-form, vertical video

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Bogdan Nesvit says his mission is simple: "We are essentially building Disney for the 21st century.".

In a recent development, nesvit is the co-founder of microseries app My Drama, built around the latest, bite-sized form of storytelling. Short, serialized "microdrama" videos are created specifically for mobile devices and have generated billions of views this year. They often feature romantic tropes and typically end in cliffhangers to keep viewers hooked.

The category has exploded at a time when traditional media faces waning interest and YouTube now claims more viewing hours than individual streaming platforms.

The microdrama industry is projected to reach $1.5 billion in revenue in the U.S. in 2026, according to research firm Media Partners Asia. In China, where the format originally gained popularity, microdrama revenue surpassed what was made at the box office in 2024.

"It has the potential to evolve into mainstream entertainment and basically become mobile-first video streaming of the size of Netflix," Nesvit said.

Tech companies and startups such as My Drama at the moment lead the category in metrics like downloads and time spent on the apps.

TikTok's PineDrama is the most popular microdrama app of 2026 so far with 13.2 million U.S. downloads through September, according to Sensor Tower data.

Startups DramaBox and ReelShort beat out entertainment giants Netflix and Disney in average mobile usage time in the U.S. in the third quarter, according to Sensor Tower.

As they see microdramas win over viewers, legacy media companies, Hollywood executives and actors increasingly want in.

Fox Entertainment was one of the earliest Hollywood investors in microdramas. The entertainment studio acquired an equity stake in Holywater Tech, the company behind My Drama, in October 2025.

Fox agreed to produce at least 200 vertical video titles over two years that would be available on the Holywater app. Thus far, hits include dark academia series "Secret Society: Till Blood Do Us Part" and "Kiss of the Pirate King." Individual series have reached tens of millions of views, according to the company.

Nesvit told CNBC in an interview that partnerships like the one between Fox and Holywater benefit studios by providing the infrastructure to publish their short-form videos and get them in front of an audience.

Beyond distributing the videos, Holywater offers advice for what types of content perform best in a short, mobile-first format, according to Nesvit.

"One of the exciting things that we learned when we started talking to studios in Hollywood around two years ago is that all of them are super hungry for consumer data," Nesvit said. "They really want to see how people are interacting with each second of the content.".

Nesvit mentioned the organization will provide studios with feedback on everything from the length of a camera shot to what color dress a character should wear.

"Our goal is how we can drive audiences from YouTube, from TikTok, from Instagram to our platform, and also create those kinds of synergies [with] legacy media," he mentioned.

Holywater recently adapted Season 3 of Fox's reality TV hit "Farmer Wants a Wife" into a microseries fit for the platform. The company also intends to create a scripted series featuring 30 three-minute episodes inspired by Fox's "Fear Factor: House of Fear.".

Industry observers note that holywater currently relies on a combination of subscription fees, which make up about 80% of its revenue, and money from advertisers. Nesvit said the firm is also exploring incorporating brand integrations. The company is already profitable, its co-founders said.

Co-founder Anatolii Kasianov noted creating the videos is "very cost efficient," with an estimated expense of between $150,000 and $200,000 for an average 70-minute series.

The manufacturer's partnerships with content studios, he stated, help to bring down customer acquisition costs.

"You attract organically the audience that loves that [intellectual property]," he noted.

While Fox saw collaboration as the way into the format, some longtime Hollywood executives are betting they can skip the partnerships and create microdrama platforms in-house.

Industry observers note that jana Winograde, Susan Rovner and Lloyd Braun, who have collectively held leadership roles at NBCUniversal, Showtime, Warner Bros. Discovery and ABC, have jointly founded a microdrama startup called aTwist.

The company, which officially introduced in September, has garnered tens of millions of views as of late November across TikTok, Instagram and YouTube, where content lives in addition to the app.

"One of the specialties that we have is really what it means to be a great storyteller," Rovner told CNBC in an interview. "I was president of Warner Bros. Television. I was there for 22 years. During my time there, we had over 18 series go over 100 episodes, so I definitely think we have an expertise in getting people to care about these highlights, care about the characters, invest.".

"A lot of our competitors' completion rates are very low, their churn rates are very high," Rovner mentioned. "They can often bring people into the store but they can't keep them.".

The average churn rate for the most downloaded microdrama apps is nearly 60% so far in 2026, according to Sensor Tower data. The market intelligence firm notes that the number of customers lost in each quarter this year has roughly equaled the number of users gained.

According to recent reports, ATwist seeks to keep viewers enticed through high-stakes, compelling characters and unique stories. The app has already ordered second seasons of reveals and puts a particular emphasis on talent, according to the co-founders.

Jesse Tyler Ferguson, known for his role as Mitchell on "Modern Family," and his husband, Justin Mikita, a Broadway producer, have collaborated on the musical series "Theater Kids vs. Zombies" for aTwist. It premiered on Oct. 15.

Other series that debuted last month included "Honey, I Think Our House Is Haunted" starring Mario Lopez and "Unboxing the Killer Doll" starring Paris Hilton.

In May, aTwist announced an exclusive partnership with Skydance's linear television channel BET to create horizontal microdramas that air first on BET and later stream in vertical format on aTwist's app.

"That brings a lot of awareness to our brand, a lot of marketing," Winograde mentioned. "But it also gives these linear networks the opportunity to get programming at an incredibly low cost that, from what we're seeing, the audience loves.".

Winograde suggested aTwist could strike similar partnerships in the future.

Large streaming companies, which have already faced competition from social media apps and YouTube, are exploring ways to incorporate microdramas inside their existing apps.

NBCUniversal's Peacock released its own vertical video channel within the app this summer that features both original unscripted series and more traditional vertical dramas. NBCUniversal is also eyeing international development by investing in an Indian microdrama platform, EloElo Group's Story TV, earlier this month.

"At NBCUniversal, we see significant opportunity in mobile-first storytelling and emerging formats like microdramas to engage audiences and meet evolving viewing habits," Deepak Arya, executive vice president for corporate strategy and development at NBCUniversal, said in a press release.

In a recent development, disney+, Paramount+ and Netflix similarly introduced vertical channels embedded in their services this year. Notably, the channels are meant to help viewers discover traditional content and so far haven't featured original, vertical-first series.

On top of those media companies, head of Prime Video and Amazon MGM Studios Mike Hopkins told CNBC's Julia Boorstin in an interview last month that the tech company was interested in producing its own microdramas.

Despite the flurry of investment, Hollywood still faces the challenge of how to translate viewing hours into profit, especially for shorter content.

According to recent reports, leading U.S. microdrama app ReelShort posted a net loss of $12 million in 2025 despite bringing in total revenue of $785 million, according to research compiled by Media Partners Asia, or MPA.

The app, which was founded in 2022 by California-based Crazy Maple Studio, presently reaches about 65 million customers every month, according to the tech giant's LinkedIn profile.

According to recent reports, however, high marketing and content costs have weighed on the company's bottom line. MPA expects ReelShort to reach profitability for the first time this year, with an estimated $40 million in net profit.

Media Partners Asia CEO Vivek Couto told CNBC that acquiring newly introduced people is the largest cost, with ReelShort spending an estimated 55% of its revenue in 2025 on marketing.

Couto also noted high content spend, especially since microdrama apps typically require a high volume of content.

Sources indicate that "The content is very hit-driven … it wears out fast," Couto said. "The churn rate for this sector is high, so you have to fill that slate all the time, and you have to win back those owners.".
