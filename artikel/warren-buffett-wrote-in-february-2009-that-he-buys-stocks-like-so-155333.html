By Dave Kovaleski – Nov 27, 2026 at 5:30AM EST.

The major indexes entered the Thanksgiving holiday in correction territory, with the S&P 500 down about 10% since its all-time closing high on Oct. 6 and the Nasdaq Composite off 17% since then.

Where it goes from here is unknown, but the cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings (CAPE) ratio, also known as the Shiller P/E ratio, is still elevated at about 38. Although that's down from more than 40 in October, it is still as high as it's been since the dot-com boom.

It is unsettling in the near term, but in the long-term view, it creates a buying opportunity for investors, as Warren Buffett summed up in Berkshire Hathaway's (BRKA -0.04%) (BRKB +0.12%) 2008 letter to shareholders.

In a recent development, the 2008 shareholder letter came out on Feb. 27, 2009, in the teeth of the financial crisis. The S&P 500 had plummeted 37% in 2008, and as of the day the investor letter was released, it had dropped another 19% year to date to 735.

The S&P 500 was not done falling at that point; it sank another 8% to bottom out at 676 on March 9.

According to recent reports, these were clearly difficult times, certainly the worst since the 2000-2002 dot-com bust and worse than anything since. But Buffett was energized by the opportunity before him.

According to recent reports, buffett was busy that quarter, adding shares of Wells Fargo (WFC +1.53%), U.S. Bancorp (USB +1.53%), Johnson & Johnson (JNJ +0.25%), and Union Pacific (UNP +0.04%), among others.

It proved to be a smart move, as one year after the letter was posted, Feb. 26, 2010, the S&P 500 closed at 1,104, up 50% in a year. As for Berkshire Hathaway's performance, its B shares jumped 56% from $51.28 on Feb. 27, 2009, to $80.13 on Feb. 26, 2010.

Recent findings suggest that the lesson for investors is to, like Buffett, take a long-term view and seize the opportunity to add good-quality merchandise, or in this case, stocks, to your portfolio. Given the fact that the S&P 500 has averaged about a 10% annual total return over its history, markets have always recovered from downturns and moved higher.

It's not always easy to determine which discounted stocks are likely to recover and which aren't. The best strategy is to look for stocks with valuation metrics, like the P/E ratio, that are below historical averages. Then check their earnings, outlook, industry, and market share to make sure their decline is not a function of longer-term problems. If you are unsure, invest in an exchange-traded fund that tracks the S&P 500 or Nasdaq.
