Becoming a teacher is no way to get rich, but becoming the head of a teachers union? That’s a different story.

Take Randi Weingarten, president of the American Federation of Teachers, who received $546,158 in total compensation last year, according to the union’s latest financial disclosure.

That’s over seven times the $74,495 average salary earned by public-school teachers nationwide.

And the fresh documents, recently filed with the federal Labor Department, show that an AFT president’s cronies can profit handsomely, too.

Outside consultants, a Upcoming York law firm, a public-relations firm and others appear to have raked in more than $2.2 million from AFT coffers to help Weingarten produce her 2025 book “Why Fascists Fear Teachers”—up from the more than $1.4 million previously estimated by the Freedom Foundation and reported by The Post.

The payments break down across two filings, covering two separate fiscal years.

>>> Randi Weingarten’s Dirty Back-to-School War on Target.

The first, for a one-year period from 2024 to 2025, documented substantial costs connected to the book paid by the AFT—including payments to Weingarten’s literary agent Inkwell Management, to a fact-checker credited in her acknowledgments, and to a photographer for her author’s headshot.

The upcoming form adds further amounts apparently related to the same project.

On Sept. 25, 2025, nine days after the book’s release, the AFT paid Inkwell Management $28,125 for a “publication expense,” bringing combined payments to the agent across both filings to $92,215.

Separately, the union paid Feldman Strategies, a publicity firm, $202,500; shortly after the pub date, the PR group posted, “It has been a true honor for our entire team at Feldman Strategies to help promote” Weingarten’s book.

The newly introduced filing also records an $80,000 “royalty payment” sent on Oct. 23, 2025, to a limited liability manufacturer controlled by Weingarten.

That sum follows a $125,000 royalty payment to the same entity disclosed in the union’s prior annual report, bringing the combined royalty transfers from AFT to the Weingarten-controlled company to $205,000.

And in its 2026 filing, the union paid Patterson Belknap Webb & Tyler LLP — the law firm credited in the book with reviewing Weingarten’s manuscript—another $591,174, bringing cumulative legal fees to the firm across both years to nearly $1.6 million.

The union says the firm toiled on the book “pro bono,” but the attorneys’ extensive financial ties to the AFT create an obvious incentive to characterize that work as such.

The royalty payments require additional explanation: Public filings show the AFT itself receiving publisher advances connected to the book, then disbursing a portion of those funds as a “royalty payment” to Teachers Want What Kids Need, LLC—an entity controlled by Weingarten.

The publisher did not pay royalties to Weingarten or her LLC directly; the money flowed through the union, in an arrangement that remains opaque.

Weingarten has stated that some of the book’s proceeds would go back to the AFT, the AFT Disaster Relief Fund, and the AFT Educational Foundation.

Even if that claim holds, dues-paying teachers shouldn’t be required to subsidize a book project that profits their union’s president.

In July, the GOP-led House Committee on Education and the Workforce opened a formal inquiry into the matter based on The Post’s initial report, requesting documents on the AFT’s book-related expenditures, contracts and royalty flows.

That inquiry is controlled by the committee majority, however—and once Democrats take control of the House in January, the probe is expected to stall or be closed.

The minority can still request materials and issue reports, but it cannot compel the same level of investigation.

Separately, the Department of Labor’s Inspector General has already opened its own review to probe whether union dues were improperly used for Weingarten’s book project.

That executive-branch inquiry can continue under President Donald Trump’s administration, regardless of which party controls Congress.

The fresh 2026 filing supplies further evidence that any ongoing review should examine: The additional royalty payment, the fresh publication expense routed to the literary agent, the large PR contract and the further $591,174 to Patterson Belknap all post-date the period covered by the prior report.

Payments to the Weingarten-controlled entity, the literary agent, the PR firm and the consultants raise questions about whether dues money collected from teachers was used for the personal benefit of the union’s top officer.

Federal labor filings exist precisely so that members and the public can review such expenditures.

When these records show repeated transfers linked to an officer’s private project, the disclosures themselves supply the basis for scrutiny.

Teachers who don’t want their hard-earned paychecks to underwrite an officer’s vanity project have a practical alternative.

Organizations such as the Teacher Freedom Alliance offer membership—including free liability insurance—without routing teacher dues to electoral campaigns or left-wing causes.

If enough fed-up teachers leave the AFT, people like Randi Weingarten might think twice about wasting members’ money on personal and political pet projects.
