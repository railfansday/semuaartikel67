Violent star collisions may have created the earliest giant black holes

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Crowded early star clusters could merge stars into one giant, and a fresh model shows how that giant may become a black hole seed.

Space is so empty that two stars almost never hit each other. In the first star clusters, though, stars were packed so tightly that they may have crashed into one another again and again.

A new model suggests those crashes could merge stars until one carried the mass of the whole cluster. A star that heavy may have no way to die except by collapsing into a black hole.

That matters because the James Webb Space Telescope keeps finding enormous black holes far earlier in cosmic history than astronomers expected.

The new work, led by Claire Williams, a doctoral student at UCLA, is a calculation rather than an observation. It maps where colliding stars could have made the seeds of those black holes.

Webb’s deep images show extremely compact star clusters in the early universe, and computer simulations of that era produce the same crowding.

Williams and her colleagues in the UCLA Division of Astronomy and Astrophysics built a model to ask what happens to the stars inside them.

Co-authors of the study came from Northwestern’s CIERA and seven other institutions.

The model clusters ranged from about two-thirds of a light-year in radius to roughly 300 light-years.

In a recent development, they held anywhere from ten thousand to a billion times the mass of the Sun. Some had a black hole at the center and some did not.

Industry observers note that for each one, the team worked out how often stars would run into each other before something stopped them. That could be the stars burning out or a neighboring clump of matter crashing in.

Industry observers note that even with the strictest assumptions, collisions should have been common in many of these systems. Test simulations tracking 1,000 individual stars near a cluster’s center agreed on where they happen.

Sources indicate that in an interview with Earth.com, Williams remarked star collisions matter today only in rare, extreme places, such as the cores of galaxies. So astronomers usually don’t treat them as central to how stars live.

“We were surprised to discover how ubiquitous these collisions might have been,” she said.

Industry observers note that what a collision does depends on how fast the two stars meet. If they meet at speeds below the escape speed at a star’s surface, they can merge into one bigger star.

At higher speeds, collisions can strip material from both stars or destroy them entirely, releasing gas from their interiors.

In a cluster with no black hole, the model found no system where stars moved fast enough to destroy each other. Every collision was a merger.

And because the merged stars are heavier than their neighbors, they sink toward the center swiftly, where they collide again.

“Perhaps the strangest outcome of our simulations is that stars can merge repeatedly within the cluster until they form one gigantic star with a mass equal to that of an entire cluster,” Williams noted.

“When a star this huge reaches the end of its life, models suggest that it may have no option but to collapse into itself and form a massive black hole.”.

How substantial that merged star gets depends mostly on how tightly the cluster is packed.

In the model’s standard setup, the central star passes 200 times the Sun’s mass only once a cluster holds roughly 100,000 suns’ worth of stars per cubic parsec. A parsec is about 3.3 light-years.

Above that line, nearly all of the lightest clusters in the model produced one. In the densest cases, the merged star passed 100,000 solar masses.

When the team filled the clusters with heavy stars instead of Sun-like ones, only 22 systems built a giant at all, and the largest reached 512 solar masses. Heavy stars burn out quickly, so the collisions ran out of time.

If each giant collapses into a black hole of the same mass, the result is an intermediate-mass black hole, a size astronomers have struggled to find.

Other groups have already proposed stellar mergers as a route to early black holes, and the team’s giant-star masses broadly agree with theirs.

The upcoming part is one framework that runs from an ordinary early cluster to a black hole star wrapped in gas.

Once a black hole is at the center, the outcome changes. Its gravity speeds up the stars closest to it, so collisions there happen too fast to merge.

The stars are torn apart instead, and the gas from their interiors piles up in a tiny region around the black hole.

According to recent reports, the Milky Way’s center is the nearby example. “One place in the nearby universe where collisions between stars are common is the galactic center,” Williams said.

“In our model, once these very massive stars turn into intermediate-mass black holes, the system resembles our own galactic center, which is a black hole surrounded by a dense star cluster.”.

That dense, gassy core is why the authors connect the model to Webb’s little red dots.

Astronomers believe these compact objects hold a rapidly growing black hole, and several recent studies describe that black hole as buried in dense gas.

In the model, a cluster as dense as a little red dot would build a central star of 10,000 to 100,000 solar masses. Once a black hole formed, collisions would keep feeding gas toward it.

Williams remarked other researchers had already proposed that very massive stars might be connected to the dots.

“Little Red Dots may represent the densest extreme of a continuum of stellar systems that formed naturally in the early Universe, rather than an entirely separate class of object,” she told Earth.com.

Smadar Naoz, a UCLA astrophysicist and co-author, compared the research on the dots to the tale of blind people each describing an elephant by the part they touch.

“Our research connects several of those perspectives together and may help us see more of the elephant,” Naoz remarked.

Asked by Earth.com what Webb could see to confirm the picture, Williams said there may not be a single “smoking gun.”.

The most direct test, she mentioned, is spectroscopy, which splits an object’s light into its component wavelengths.

The bright and dark lines in that spectrum reveal the temperature, chemical makeup and motion of the material giving off the light.

With better theoretical predictions, those lines could help tell light from very massive stars apart from light reworked by gas around a black hole that is actively feeding.

One clue would be unusual amounts of certain elements, thrown out when collisions expose the insides of stars. The gas from these collisions may be unusually rich in nitrogen, which has already turned up in some early clusters.

Sources indicate that A second test would look for leftovers. If collisions built very massive stars that later collapsed, some of the resulting black holes may now wander near the edges of galaxies.

In a newer preprint that has not been peer reviewed, Williams’s team reports that upcoming sky surveys could find them.

Surveys by the Rubin Observatory and the Roman Space Telescope may spot the black holes when they destroy stars that pass too close.

“Finding a larger population of these events would provide independent evidence that dense early clusters formed intermediate-mass black holes through runaway stellar collisions,” Williams noted.

The authors list what their simplified model leaves out. It ignores any gas left over from the cluster’s birth, which they expect would pull merged stars inward even faster.

It doesn’t enhancement the stars’ masses as mergers happen, so it may miss runaway growth.

It has been revealed that its estimate of how much material a fast collision strips from a star is also likely too high. So the gas densities around black holes may be overstated.

Sources indicate that the model also stops at the giant star. It doesn’t follow whether that star collapses, how much of its mass ends up inside a black hole, or how fast the black hole grows afterward.

Industry observers note that williams said the team’s next step is following the gas that fast collisions throw into the middle of a cluster. The current model estimates how much gas that makes, but not where it goes.

The gas could stay trapped, cool and feed the black hole, change the orbits of nearby stars, or be driven out. Each outcome would leave a different signature for telescopes to look for.

The full study was published in the journal The Astrophysical Journal.
