The hidden human intelligence of design

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a recent development, here’s a scenario you can likely relate to in our age of automated systems: Someone underestimates what you do to deliver a product or service. They are solely focused on the result and say, “I could do that with AI.” Often overlooked are the years of education and experience that shape what you do, along with the complex decisions you make with judgment, empathy, wisdom, and humility. We are increasingly seeing this in the architectural industry. And it’s more than just a feeling or a hunch. Recently, the U.S. Department of Education moved to alter the definition of professional degrees to exclude architecture. This demonstrates a serious misunderstanding of what architecture entails. The creative professionals delivering complex projects in the built environment are experts: systems thinkers choreographing designs that shape environments, cultures, and communities. DESIGN AND EMOTION Design decisions are interconnected, but that complexity is often underestimated. Changes made after work is underway can require significant rework and additional layers of coordination, impacting budget and schedule. Even seemingly small change requests can ripple through other systems and affect project value. Behind every built environment is a constellation of decisions and balancing acts that are invisible to the end user. A successful design blends noiselessly into daily life. When everything is working, and spaces are intuitive, comfortable, and almost anticipatory in supporting your needs, you rarely think about the creativity, imagination, and coordination behind each design and engineering decision. Architecture’s value is its ability to make us feel something as we enter a space before we even understand why we are experiencing those emotions. The reverence of a cathedral, the excitement of a stadium, the safety and security of a home—those emotional and physical reactions do not happen by chance. Each response is a specific result of a team’s design decisions. IMPACT VERSUS HOURS There’s an idea that generative AI can replace nearly any profession. This is a tough pill to swallow for those who’ve spent years or decades honing their craft. But this way of thinking raises a deeper question: Is worth measured by the visible impact you create or the hours you log? I know architects aren’t the only ones who experience this. Leaders in finance, medicine, tech, and other fields hear from people who see the tip of the iceberg and don’t realize how much is below the surface. When they don’t understand the extent of expertise, they don’t value it. They think it can be done quickly and simply. The final output is neat and clean. But the reality is that the design process is often complex. It involves researching, discussing, debating, ideating, innovating, finding common ground, and making decisions. One skill an architect learns over time is listening beyond the client’s initial brief, identifying added value to the design program. Early in your career, you treat a brief as a rulebook. Over time, you learn how to advocate for a vision often shaped by a process of listening, challenging, and refining. It’s important for us to embrace productive friction, in life and in this discipline. It’s the moments that must be experienced, including moments of friction when we are challenged and must overcome some adversity, that are more formative and leave a lasting impact. 6 DESIGN AREAS REQUIRING HUMAN SKILLS Consider these six challenging parts of any design process that require human expertise: 1. Consensus building: While AI can pull data from multiple sources, it cannot negotiate and compromise with diverse stakeholders. People disagree. An architect is a bridge between clients, project partners, and city officials. Architects have to balance vision with constraints, and to resolve disagreements and conflicts among all parties. 2. Constructability and feasibility: It’s impressive when AI can generate a beautiful rendering in seconds. But what if the rendering isn’t practical or realistic? Architects must make the design come alive in the real world. They need to know, through knowledge and experience, how a design will perform when built, and to exercise judgment based on wisdom. 3. Intelligence beyond pattern recognition: Despite the word “intelligence,” does AI think? Or does it recognize patterns, relying on existing data to predict future outcomes? As today’s problems grow increasingly complex, the synthesis of this complex data to anticipate outcomes is a powerful tool. Where AI recognizes patterns, architects bring discernment, interpreting data through a lens that is contextual and time-sensitive, creating solutions tailored to the histories and needs of specific sites and the communities that use them. 4. Risk management and resilience: Architects are tasked with thinking about future stressors on a building. Based on historic precedent, they consider what long-term resilience is required of buildings they are designing. AI can’t assume that human element of taking responsibility and accountability. IBM’s training manual in 1979 stated, “A computer can never be held accountable; therefore, a computer must never make a management decision.” AI doesn’t understand that lives will be seriously affected if its design isn’t resilient; it’s imperative to leverage the tool responsibly. 5. Challenge assumptions as a trusted advisor: We want to assume the best of those we work with, but trust isn’t easy to build. It’s built through real-world troubleshooting and problem-solving. Sometimes that means saying no. Anyone who ever had a chatbot agree with everything they noted understands that AI often tells you what you want to hear. 6. Center work around human experience and emotion: Joy Evans Therapeutic Recreation Center in Washington, D.C.—the only public recreation center in the region offering therapeutic recreational programming—exemplifies the importance of designing for a range of human experiences. To make the center a welcoming and inclusive place for people of all ages and abilities, our designers needed a thorough human understanding of what made people feel safe, seen, and inspired. AI can grasp key tenets of accessibility and belonging abstractly, but doesn’t understand human vulnerability. When we recognize and respect these messy parts of the process, we understand that human professionals can’t be replaced. It’s important we help our clients and customers understand this too, demonstrating how we can use the best of AI while emphasizing the value of human judgment and perspective.
