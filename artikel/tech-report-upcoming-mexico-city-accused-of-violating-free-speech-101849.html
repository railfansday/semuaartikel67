Four months later, she was arrested when displaying a similar sign at an intersection.

Two years after her arrest, she’s suing the northwestern Newly introduced Mexico city of Farmington over its sweeping panhandling ban, arguing that holding a cardboard sign requesting money is constitutionally protected speech.

It has been revealed that in her suit filed Monday in Upcoming Mexico federal court, Serrano-Himmel seeks damages against the city and its police officers for unreasonable seizure and deprivation of free speech, as well as a declaration that the ordinance violates the First and Fourteenth Amendments of the U.S. Constitution.

Industry observers note that farmington Mayor Nate Duckett, a defendant in the suit, signed the panhandling ordinance in February 2023. It prohibits the solicitation of money or goods on public and private property.

“Since the passing of the ordinance and still after the receipt of the letter alerting the mayor and the city attorney about the unconstitutionality of the ordinance, Duckett continued to enforce the panhandling ordinance in a manner that deprived multiple people of their first amendment right to free speech,” Serrano-Himmel writes in the complaint.

According to Serrano-Himmel, holding a sign requesting money in a public space is protected speech under the U.S. Constitution and the state constitution.

This ordinance allowed city police officers to cease her speech and retaliate against her as a result of her exercising her free speech, she says.

In her first run-in with city police in March 2024, Serrano-Himmel says Officer Aaron Favaloro seized her identification to check for outstanding warrants and issued a citation for panhandling and a trespass notice, despite having no ownership or control over the property.

Serrano-Himmel asserted throughout the interaction that she was engaging in lawful behavior. In response, Favaloro threatened to arrest her. She was found not guilty of panhandling in July 2024.

Two weeks later, city police officer Marcus Martinez approached Serrano-Himmel after spotting her walking in a parking lot with a sign stating she was homeless and requesting assistance.

It has been revealed that serrano-Himmel says Martinez told her she was panhandling, which was not allowed in city limits. She continued to state that she was acting lawfully. Martinez then arrested her for panhandling and criminal trespass and seized her sign.

The officers’ actions violate Serrano-Himmel’s First Amendment right, which protects her speech, and Fourth Amendment right, which protects her against unreasonable seizures, Serrano-Himmel writes in the complaint. Both officers are named as defendants in the suit, alongside Duckett and the city of Farmington.

According to recent reports, serrano-Himmel’s attorney, Joseph P. Kennedy of The Kennedy Law Firm, has challenged the ordinance before, according to Serrano-Himmel.

Kennedy gave notice to Farmington that non-party Jerry Sullivan was suing the city in August 2023 after Sullivan had been detained for panhandling.

Sherald Daniels, also represented by Kennedy, sued over the ordinance in another federal lawsuit earlier this year after receiving a citation for playing music in a public space.

“The city has enforced the ordinance repeatedly in a manner that has damaged and deprived the free-speech rights of residents and visitors,” Serrano-Himmel writes.

Farmington city attorneys declined to provide comment. Kennedy did not respond to a request for comment before publication.
