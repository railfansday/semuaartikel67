Glenn Kelman’s next act: Former Redfin CEO takes on health care with stealthy startup Sojourn

Glenn Kelman famously told “60 Minutes” in 2007, “Real estate, by far, is the most screwed up industry in America.” It looks like health care is next on his list.

The former Redfin CEO has co-founded Sojourn, a Seattle startup that aims to give working people “better healthcare at a lower cost,” according to its website.

“Since technology by itself can’t fix a broken system, we’re rebuilding the whole system to make it work for you — combining a software company, a group of doctors and nurses, and an insurance company,” reads the introductory message on the site.

His co-founder is Dr. Connie Chen, an internist who co-founded digital health company Vida Health and spent more than seven years at mental health benefits company Lyra Health, first as chief medical officer and then as chief operating officer, as Lyra grew to a $5.6 billion valuation.

Industry observers note that this is not a modest app for booking doctor’s appointments. Under the corporate name Silica Health Inc., the company filed a trademark application last month covering health maintenance organization services — HMOs are health prepares that pair insurance coverage with their own networks of doctors — along with insurance administration, medical billing and claims management.

Industry observers note that kelman, who is rarely at a loss for words, confirmed in response to our inquiry that he wasn’t ready to talk yet. He wrote in a GeekWire guest column this month that he and his business partner would announce their plans “after we’ve built our product.”.

It has been revealed that but after he posted about his team on LinkedIn last week, we got curious and couldn’t help but look around. Their resumes were a tip-off: In addition to Chen, the team includes an engineer who spent nearly seven years at tech-driven health insurer Oscar Health.

Searching Kelman’s name in state business licenses turned up Silica Health, with Kelman and Chen listed as officers. Plugging that name into the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office’s database produced exactly one result: the application for “Sojourn Health.”.

From there, it was a short trip to sojournhealth.com, which sketches out the blueprint.

Why health care: Kelman spent 20 years running Redfin, which stood out in the real estate industry for employing its agents rather than treating them as independent contractors. That turns out to be part of the origin story.

In a recent development, “Almost every year after its IPO, Redfin had to re-consider whether to keep employing its own agents, because of their rising healthcare costs,” Sojourn’s founders page explains. The options were to make agents contractors, ask employees to pay more, or cover less care.

“The choices were all terrible,” it says.

Chen’s bio on the site describes a career spent on one problem: how employers can give their employees better care at lower cost.

As a primary-care doctor, she saw her patients’ health and finances rise and fall together, the site says, and concluded that “a system taped together from broken parts couldn’t serve either.”.

Kelman, by his own account, is new to the field. In his guest column, he wrote that when he thought about the companies he would have been proud to start, they were all in one particular industry. He didn’t say which one, but it’s not hard to guess now.

“I had no experience in that industry, but a friend said I could offset that if I were willing to give it everything I have, which is what I’m now doing,” he wrote.

The team: In a LinkedIn post last week, Kelman noted the founding team also includes Jennifer Chao and Ben Baker, with Sasha Aickin “adding some part-time magic.” He promised that “the mission is good and the ambition is vast.”.

The lineup draws on Kelman’s past and the health insurance world:.

Chao and Aickin both worked with Kelman at Plumtree Software, the enterprise software company he co-founded in 1996, before following him to Redfin. Sojourn’s recruiting page nods to the pattern: “We’re honored by the old friends who have worked with us from company to company, but we’re also eager to bring in fresh blood.”.

The Sojourn site states the manufacturer is likely to have engineering hubs in Seattle for the member experience, San Francisco for the clinical platform, and Austin for the claims system.

AI all the way down: Sojourn is hiring software engineers and product managers “at every level of the organization.” The first thing on its list of qualifications: experience using frontier AI tools “to generate reliable, high-scale production code.”.

That fits with his guest column. A recruiter told him Seattle’s startup scene was “dead,” he wrote, and a friend described CTOs who were miserable trying to do “AI transformations” at companies with old code. Kelman wrote that “the logical response to all this has been to start an AI-native manufacturer in Seattle.”.

What we don’t know: The website doesn’t say when Sojourn intends to launch or where it will offer coverage first, although Chen’s bio suggests employers will be key customers, describing a care model “designed around the member and the employer who pays for it.”.

A upcoming health plan typically requires state insurance licenses and significant capital reserves, and the tech giant hasn’t disclosed any funding. Kelman wrote in his column that he “met amazing, generous investors who gave me a good idea,” but he didn’t name them, and a search of SEC filings for Sojourn Health and Silica Health found no record of a fundraise.

In June, he joined venture firm Greylock as an executive in residence, saying at the time that he was still looking to start a upcoming mission-driven enterprise, a process that involved “exploring ideas that are never going to work and howling at the moon.” It’s not clear whether Greylock is among his backers.

Kelman stepped down as Redfin CEO in January, six months after Rocket Companies completed its $1.75 billion acquisition of the Seattle brokerage. He noted at the time that he wanted to find “another mission-driven enterprise outside of real estate.”.

He appears to have found one. Whether health care is more or less screwed up than real estate is a question that will have to wait until he’s ready to chat.
