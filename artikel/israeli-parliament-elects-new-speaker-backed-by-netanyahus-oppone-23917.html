Knesset’s appointment of Matan Kahana marks step towards reversing policies of outgoing government.

The Israeli parliament elected a newly introduced speaker on Tuesday, a key step toward rolling back policies passed by the outgoing government led by the prime minister, Benjamin Netanyahu.

The upcoming speaker is Matan Kahana, an ally of Gadi Eisenkot, the party leader presently working to form the next government and replace Netanyahu.

Eisenkot’s bloc has pledged to undo some of Netanyahu’s most contentious policies following Kahana’s election.

Recent findings suggest that he was approved as speaker with 66 votes in the 120-member chamber, backed by the Jewish-led opposition parties and by the Arab-led parties. The candidate backed by Netanyahu’s bloc, Ofir Katz, received 52 votes following a stormy debate that stretched over seven hours.

“The appointment of Matan Kahana as Knesset speaker is a worthy democratic move that reflects the absolute majority in favour of change,” Eisenkot stated ahead of the vote.

Sources indicate that the vote took place weeks after the parliamentary elections on 27 October in which Netanyahu and his allies lost their parliamentary majority. Eisenkot was subsequently tasked by Israel’s president with trying to form a upcoming coalition government.

Eisenkot has up to five more weeks to secure enough alliances to form a coalition.

As negotiations continue on the sidelines, his bloc, along with unaffiliated Arab-led parties, can use their majority in the newly sworn-in Knesset to advance their legislative agenda.

They introduced several bills last week, including to set term limits on the role of prime minister, to form a government-led inquiry into the Hamas-led 7 October 2023 attacks and to reverse laws that limit the powers of the judiciary.

Kahana gives the opposition bloc a major legislative advantage, according to Assaf Shapira of the Israel Democracy Institute, a Jerusalem thinktank.

“It’s important because there is not much time, because the speaker of the Knesset can make the legislative process much, much faster or much slower,” Shapira said.

Kahana replaced Amir Ohana, a Netanyahu ally, as speaker.

Sources indicate that eisenkot and his allies are under pressure to swiftly advance their legislative priorities in the weeks ahead. Roy Folkman, a member of Eisenkot’s Yashar party, heads the temporary committee that decides much of the Knesset’s work until a government is formed.

If Eisenkot fails to form a government, the Israeli president could give Netanyahu an opportunity to do so. That would probably give Netanyahu’s Likud party control of the committee.

Even though Netanyahu’s opponents would still hold a majority, they would “feel that their momentum has gone”, Shapira remarked, and there would be a “real danger that some of the legislation will be delayed”.

Chamber procedures will pose a further challenge. A bill has to be introduced on the floor, considered in committee and voted on several times on the floor before it passes the Knesset. That winding process could make it hard to move bills across the finish line before time runs out.

Shapira noted Eisenkot and his allies are likely to prioritise rolling back controversial laws that gave the government a larger role in appointing judges and weakened the power of Israel’s attorney general, an independent role that is seen by many as central to Israel’s system of government checks and balances.

“It’s very easy to abolish these laws because no fresh institution has to be created,” he said.

Establishing a body to investigate the 7 October attacks would be easier, Shapira added, and carry greater legitimacy once a government is formed.

Netanyahu’s opponents argue he has blocked an independent probe into any military or intelligence failures around the day, the deadliest in Israel’s history, and evaded accountability.
