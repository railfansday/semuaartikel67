They take your privacy, seriously.

Privacy? What privacy? The 100 most-visited English-language sites are flouting European data protection laws on a massive scale, according to a privacy researcher's investigation.

Forensic data analysis, published by Alexander Hanff, an attorney, computer scientist, and occasional contributor to The Register, with the support of SOMI (Stichting Onderzoek Marktinformatie), a Netherlands-based privacy group, details the dubious data flow.

From October 1 to 3, 2026, Hanff took a clean Google Chrome browser profile and measured what happens when the browser loads the 100 most visited English-language websites for five minutes, without any consent banner interaction.

According to recent reports, all 100 sites produced what the report characterizes as Tier 1 events – interception, exfiltration, or high-entropy fingerprinting (a total of 14,675). Tier 2 consists of storage on the device, capturing device characteristics, and script loads without consent (284,463 events recorded). Tier 3 includes protocol mechanisms and passive reads used for tracking (219,632 events).

It has been revealed that the median website triggered 2,695 events, for a total of 28,695 events deemed high or critical. And 92.4 percent of the servers contacted during this process were on networks outside the European Economic Area (EEA). Most of these servers – 1,247 out of 1,400 – were in the US, and 1,034 belonged to third-parties.

The EU General Data Protection Regulation governs the transfer of personal data outside the EEA. The EU allows data transfers to the US for organizations participating in the EU–US Data Privacy Framework. Beyond that, according to Hanff and SOMI, a valid legal mechanism like a contract must be in place or the transfer may fail to meet GDPR or the ePrivacy Directive. In some cases, California Penal Code section 631 (interception) and section 502 (computer access) may also be relevant.

The report, released in conjunction with a presentation at the Field Talk conference, also calculates the estimated environmental impact to load all this third-party content on websites without consent: 1,271,444 kWh per year, or enough to power 353 homes in Europe at 3.6 MWh per home annually.

SOMI is hosting a data finder to help people request access to the personal data companies have collected about them.

Hanff in an email told The Register that he would be shocked if these findings fail to get the attention of lawmakers and regulators in the EU, UK, and US, given plans to file privacy complaints with relevant authorities.

"Complaints will be filed against all of them and the EU supervisory authorities have a legal obligation to investigate them all – so we will have to wait and see," he stated.

"One of the things we are discussing with SOMI is mass claims under the EU’s Representative Actions Directive but we are hoping that the industry and the regulators do the right thing so we don’t have to file lawsuits – again we will have to wait and see." ®.
