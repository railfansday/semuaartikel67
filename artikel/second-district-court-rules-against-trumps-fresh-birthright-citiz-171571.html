Sources indicate that yesterday, in the case of Nathan v. Trump, federal district Judge Christopher Cooper of the District of DC issued a ruling against Trump's fresh executive order restricting birthright citizenship. The ruling is very similar to an earlier September decision by Judge Boardman of the District of Maryland. The one difference is that the class action injunction in Nathan covers a class that literally consists of all children illegally deprived of birthright citizenship under the new order, whereas the class in the Maryland case is slightly narrower.

Industry observers note that I previously  wrote about Trump's latest executive order seeking to deny birthright citizenship to various classes of children born on US soil, and noted the first legal challenges to it. As I pointed out then, almost every part of the new order is at odds with the Supreme Court's recent ruling in Trump v. Barbara, which struck down Trump's executive order denying birthright citizenship to children of undocumented immigrants born in the United States, and those born to non-citizen parents here on temporary visas. The Fourteenth Amendment guarantees citizenship to all children "born or naturalized in the United States, and subject to the jurisdiction thereof." In Barbara, the Supreme Court majority made clear that "subject to the jurisdiction thereof" includes children of all persons who are subject to and required to obey US law.

Judge Cooper rightly concludes that nearly all the various classes of children barred from birthright citizenship by the newly introduced order are in fact protected by the reasoning of Barbara and other Supreme Court precedents, because they and their parents are clearly subject to US law, and required to obey it:.

It has been revealed that earlier this year, the Supreme Court struck down President Trump's Executive Order 14160, which purported to deny citizenship to children born to parents temporarily or unlawfully present in the country. Those children, like "nearly all children born in the United States," the Court explained, are "citizens at birth" because they are born "subject to American law." Trump v. Barbara, 609 U.S. 719, 734–37, 740–41 (2026). Barbara reaffirmed that birthright citizenship extends to all but three narrow categories of children, each long understood to be beyond the Nation's governing authority at birth: children of foreign officials enjoying full diplomatic immunity, children of enemy forces in hostile occupation of U.S. territory, and children born aboard foreign public ships.

According to recent reports, yet Barbara's ink had scarcely dried when the President issued a second executive order—Executive Order 14418, at glitch here—directing federal agencies to withhold recognition of citizenship from additional categories of U.S.-born children with noncitizen parents. Among those now targeted are children whose parents work for foreign governments or international organizations but lack diplomatic immunity, children born through commercial surrogacy arrangements, and children with a parent suspected of membership in certain gangs or terrorist organizations. As the Supreme Court has made clear, however, the President cannot carve out upcoming exceptions to birthright citizenship for children born within the United States and subject to its laws. The Court will therefore certify a class consisting of children who are or will be unlawfully denied citizenship under the Order. And it will preliminarily enjoin the Government from enforcing the Order against members of that class.

As explained in my original August post on the new executive order, that order covers four groups almost all of whose members are clearly subject to US law:.

Sources indicate that children of "alien enemies," defined as those where at least one of two non-citizen parents is a "designated Foreign Terrorist Organization under 8 U.S.C. 1189 or Specially Designated Global Terrorist, consistent with the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, 50 U.S.C. 1701 et seq., and Executive Order 13224 of September 23, 2001 (Blocking Property and Prohibiting Transactions With Persons Who Commit, Threaten to Commit, or Support Terrorism).".

Children born to at least one non-citizen parent who is a "foreign government employee" or employed by an "international organization that possess[es] international-organization immunity.".

Children where "either parent" has engaged in "birth tourist" transactions of various kinds (deliberately seeking to give birth in the US so the child would get birthright citizenship).

Children "born in a territory or territorial waters of the United States where citizenship is not conferred by Federal statute." …[T]his only applies to children born in American Samoa, the one populated overseas US territory to whose residents Congress has not conferred citizenship by statute….

Industry observers note that as I explained more fully in my August post, all of the children in these four categories are obviously subject to US law, with the exception of those born to foreign diplomats who have diplomatic immunity. This latter group is already excluded from birthright citizenship. In that post, I also explained why the analogy between "alien enemies" (as defined in the order) and children of members of an invading army occupying US territory fails.

It has been revealed that judge Cooper's opinion analyzes all four categories thoroughly and effectively. He's particularly strong on the case of children of "alien enemies," a category which - he explains - the Trump order defines far more broadly than is justified by legal precedent, and which in any case does not include anyone not "subject to the jurisdiction" of the United States, because all such people are under US control and required to obey US law when on American soil. That differentiates them from the historically recognized exception for children of enemy troops in "hostile occupation" of US territory during a war. The US does not have effective jurisdiction over territory occupied by a hostile enemy army. But the last time any such thing occurred was during World War II, when the Japanese occupied a number of US island possessions in the Pacific.

I noted some additional problems with Trump's "alien enemies" carveout in my August post about the second birthright citizenship order:.

Finally, the [order is wrong about] children of "alien enemies," defined…. as members of various designated "terrorist" entities. If they enter the United States, they are subject to US law. That's why they can be prosecuted for any crimes they commit on US soil - including acts of terrorism!

Recent findings suggest that members of these groups may be bad people. Some have committed horrendous crimes. But that is no reason to visit the sins of the parents upon the children. If a murderer or a rapist has a child born on US soil, the child is entitled to birthright citizenship, notwithstanding the reprehensible behavior of the parents. The same logic applies here.

It is also worth noting that the "foreign terrorist organization" (FTO) designation has been slapped onto various groups that are not actually terrorists in the ordinary meaning of the word, most notably groups engaged in smuggling illegal drugs.

Sources indicate that trump may be trying to use the "alien enemy" designation here to analogize these children to children of members of an invading army, born  at a time and place where the invaders have seized control of US territory, thereby preventing the exercise of US "jurisdiction." Trump v. Barbara reaffirms the longstanding view that such children are excluded from birthright citizenship, because born in an area where US jurisdiction does not (at the time) meaningfully apply.

Sources indicate that but FTOs have not, in fact, "invaded" or occupied any part of the United States. Thus, US jurisdiction still applies. Therefore, children of members these organizations are not analogous to children born to members of an occupying foreign army. No part of the United States has actually been invaded and occupied since the Japanese seized various US territories in the Pacific during World War II.
