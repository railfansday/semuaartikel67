Thanksgiving Outside the Echo Chamber

It has been revealed that “Polarized” has been one of the most overused words to describe America's political climate, but it is increasingly being applied to school boards, neighborhoods, and even families as well. Affective polarization, or the growing dislike and distrust of people on the opposing side of the political aisle, has more than doubled in the U.S. since the late 1970s (Boxell et al., 2024). Many of us now surround ourselves with like-minded friends and partners, creating a world in which we watch news, follow influencers, and make friends with people who reinforce what we already believe. This all gets thrown out the window at Thanksgiving, when we leave the safety of our echo chamber and spend time with the family we didn’t choose but love anyway.

Historically, the weird uncle at the Thanksgiving table could be counted on to tell a few off-color jokes or ask a child to pull his finger. These offenses seem almost quaint now that a passing comment about politics or social justice can leave half the table questioning the moral character of the other half. This annual exercise ends in one of two ways: The dinner crescendos into an actual altercation with one side accusing the other of crimes against humanity, or everyone smiles politely, and then unloads their thoughts and feelings to their significant other on the ride home. I’d like to propose a third option: intellectual curiosity.

It has been revealed that what if we approached a conversation with someone whose political beliefs we find baffling with a genuine interest in how they came to hold those beliefs in the first place? It is true that not all beliefs are equal (beliefs such as racial superiority are indefensible); however, many people come to believe something they consider logical based on their life experience. For example: free college for all. Imagine cutting into the turkey when one family member casually indicates free college could open doors for students whose families can’t afford an education, and another relative pipes up that that wouldn’t be fair. Eating slows, as everyone recognizes dinner just got awkward.

Rather than writing off the relative you disagree with, use this time to practice intellectual curiosity, or the desire to learn, explore new ideas, and better understand something, even when it challenges what we already believe. This requires a couple of steps. The first is to pause. Polarizing comments are often triggering, and people often get angry before anything else. Recognize the anger and then pause until the initial rush passes.

Next, commit to intellectual curiosity by asking instead of telling. In the free college example, this may mean replying with, “I’d like to hear more about that. What feels unfair to you?” And then sit back and actually listen. Make eye contact, don’t interrupt, and don’t prepare your next argument. Doing this requires the next step: using your Theory of Mind.

Theory of Mind is the ability to understand that others have thoughts, beliefs, desires, and intentions that differ from our own. Because we can’t open someone’s head and look inside, we don’t know what they're actually thinking, but we gather clues from what they say and how they act to develop a theory of their mind. Theory of Mind develops rapidly across childhood and adolescence and continues into adulthood. This doesn’t mean all adults are good at it; in fact, I’d argue that most aren’t. I We're already using Theory of Mind when we judge our relatives. We're just doing it with very little information and potentially a great deal of bias. When someone opposes free college, we might immediately assume they're selfish or indifferent to disadvantaged students. That assumption is itself a theory of their mind. Asking questions allows us to gather better information and revise that theory. At the Thanksgiving table, this means trying to put yourself in their shoes and take their perspective.

According to recent reports, finally, we can muster empathy. We’ve asked questions, revised our theory of their mind (why they believe what they do), and we still disagree with them. That’s okay; we can hold our beliefs and still find space in our heart to empathize with theirs. To return to the free college debate, imagine that a relative shares that they had to graduate in 5 years rather than 4 because they worked two jobs to pay for their education. Despite this, it still took them 17 years and a lot of low-budget vacations to pay off their loans, a milestone they just recently celebrated. Suddenly the word “fair” hits differently. You empathize with their situation, although you may still retain your initial position.

Recent findings suggest that by the end of the night, you may still find your uncle’s political beliefs baffling, but you’ll better understand how he came to hold them. Given how much time we spend speculating about the minds of people we disagree with, why not take advantage of having one sitting across the table from us? Ask a few questions, entertain the possibility that you’ve misjudged someone, and see where the conversation goes. You might even have a more pleasant ride home.
