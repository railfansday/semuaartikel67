A upcoming AI tool could help fight online wildlife trafficking

Researchers have developed a way to recognize elephant ivory, pangolin scales, and tiger claws in photographs to help investigators flag suspicious online listings.

A latest AI tool can identify elephant, pangolin, and tiger products in photographs, even among similar-looking everyday objects.

Tests suggest it could help investigators sift through online listings and decide which items deserve closer inspection.

The research was led by Enrico Di Minin at the University of Helsinki. Lead author Ritwik Kulkarni worked with the International Fund for Animal Welfare (IFAW) on the image library and prototype.

Wildlife products change appearance as they become jewelry, ornaments, or medicines. A photograph may show a carved bracelet, loose scales, or a claw, with few clues about its origin.

Sources indicate that illegal and unsustainable trade contributes to wildlife declines. Tracking it online means reviewing large numbers of photographs across shopping sites and social media, often with limited staff.

“Identifying illegal wildlife products, such as elephant ivory, using AI was one of the grand challenges we were facing,” Di Minin stated.

“These new tools offer novel opportunities for real-time monitoring of online illegal trade in ivory, pangolin scales, and tiger claws for enforcement purposes.”.

The researchers assembled 39,941 images collected through IFAW’s monitoring work in China between 2008 and 2023.

Sources indicate that sources included online advertisements, market inspections, publicly reported seizures, and image searches. This gave the team examples of products as they actually appeared in trade-related photographs.

The library also included objects that could fool a reviewer. Jade bracelets resembled ivory bracelets, while slices of herbal medicine looked like pangolin scales.

Two experienced IFAW analysts reviewed the labels and resolved disagreements. Objects were also marked with boxes, teaching the model where to look within each photograph.

That mattered for crowded product displays and collages. The system needed to locate a suspicious item even when other objects appeared beside it.

The team compared models trained separately for each target group with a model trained on all three. The combined approach performed best overall.

“The best model achieved an overall accuracy of 84% and exceeded 90% accuracy for tiger and pangolin products,” Kulkarni remarked.

The paper gives the figures as 93.6% for pangolin products and 90.3% for tiger products. Elephant products proved harder, with an accuracy of 71.1%.

Industry observers note that ivory carvings can resemble objects made from bone or resin. The researchers also noted that models may sometimes rely on surrounding visual clues instead of the object’s identifying features.

“When integrated into a research prototype smartphone application and evaluated independently, the tool reached 91.3% accuracy in practical screening tests, highlighting the potential of automated image analysis to help prioritise suspect content for human review,” Kulkarni explained.

The app was assessed using 138 additional images, separately from the initial model tests.

According to recent reports, the prototype lets customers choose an existing photograph or take a new one. It then returns a prediction, with boxes marking the objects it has detected.

According to recent reports, an investigator reviewing online advertisements could submit saved images. During a market inspection, an officer could photograph an item for an initial assessment.

“For these technologies to make a meaningful contribution to conservation, we need to make them accessible and usable by the people working on the ground,” Kulkarni noted.

According to recent reports, “Our prototype demonstrates how we can bridge the gap between AI research and practical conservation applications. An app built on it could help, for example, conservation organizations and authorities.”.

Owners can also give feedback on whether the labels and boxes are correct. That provides a way to record mistakes and improve later versions.

A photograph cannot settle whether a sale is illegal. A flagged image gives investigators something to examine, while qualified authorities remain responsible for legal decisions.

Sources indicate that errors create different problems. False alarms send reviewers toward ordinary goods, while missed detections can leave suspicious listings unnoticed.

Industry observers note that the aim is to help people decide where to spend their time. Specialists would still need to examine the item, the listing, and any other relevant evidence.

Industry observers note that the reported results measure image-recognition performance. They do not show how many illegal sales the tool would prevent or how much it would reduce pressure on wildlife populations.

The researchers initiative to improve accuracy, add more species and products, and explore pilots with conservation organizations and online platforms.

They also want secure, ethical procedures for referring suspected cases to authorities.

Industry observers note that continued training will matter because products and their presentation change. A system built from past photographs needs updating as latest examples enter the market.

The paper also suggests combining images with information from listing text. That could help when a photograph alone leaves too much uncertainty.

“We are excited to see this research translate into a practical screening tool for combating illegal online wildlife trade,” stated Lionel Hachemin, IFAW’s Wildlife Crime Program Director.

“This study demonstrates what’s possible when conservation organizations and researchers join forces. We see this as a practical step toward turning the tide on illegal wildlife trade online.”.

The study is published in the journal Conservation Biology.
