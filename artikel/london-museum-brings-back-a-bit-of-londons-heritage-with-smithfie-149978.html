London Museum brings "back a bit of London's heritage" with Smithfield Market opening

Architecture studios Stanton Williams, Asif Khan and Julian Harrap Architects have completed their long-awaited transformation of the 1880s Smithfield General Market into the London Museum.

Opening to the public on Saturday, the former Victorian market hall now functions as a museum that tells the story of London’s history through exhibitions designed by German studio Atelier Brückner.

According to the London Museum, it is the largest dedicated city museum in the world.

Ten years in the making, the opening of Smithfield General Market marks the first phase of the London Museum’s relocation from the Barbican Estate’s former Museum of London, which is set to be demolished.

Stanton Williams, Asif Khan and Julian Harrap Architects have overhauled the General Market, which fell into disrepair after it closed in the 1990s, and will also convert the adjacent 1960s Poultry Market, which is expected to open in 2028.

“There’s an opportunity to represent London as it is now and in the future, and really take city museums to another level,” noted London Museum’s Latest Museum Project director Alec Shaw.

“We’re bringing back a bit of London’s heritage – one building that has not been seen or encountered for at least 30 years, and the other one an amazing architectural gem that hasn’t been fully appreciated and will now be,” he told Dezeen.

The main entrance to the London Museum is marked by a covered street, West Poultry Avenue, which doubles as the Real Time gallery that stretches between the General Market and Poultry Market.

Entering the transformed Victorian market, visitors pass a museum shop and an auditorium before entering Our Time – an expansive gallery illuminated from above by an oculus.

Shops that once wrapped the perimeter of the market trading floor were removed to open up the space, but the restoration was designed to retain hints of the building fabric’s history.

“You can see pockets in the brickwork walls where joists of the first floor and little plaster columns were originally located,” said Julian Harrap Architects partner Lyall Thow.

“There was a second floor, represented by a darkened band where we didn’t overly clean the brickwork, and fireplaces in the upper offices were taken out, restored, and put back where they would have been originally.”.

Stanton Williams, Asif Khan and Julian Harrap Architects overhauled 42 individual shops that formerly encircled the General Market into 12 multi-storey units.

Six units were kept to contain a restaurant, galleries and learning spaces, while the other six were left for the museum to invite partner institutions to occupy.

“What the ‘museum of tomorrow’ will be is not just a single institute, but a coral reef of small institutes that can plug in – Dezeen could have an office here, a leather upholsterer might have a craft workshop here, restaurants and pop-ups…” stated Khan.

“This living ecosystem, which is reflective of the city, is possible because of this market building we’ve moved into,” he continued.

“What we’ve done is make openings, windows, and cuts through the floor and ceiling, which allow that energy to emanate out of the city and for the city’s energy to emanate inwards,” added Khan. “At the centre of it all is this enormous public space, the General Market ground floor, where anything can happen.”.

A staircase takes visitors down to London’s Roman street level, to the museum’s permanent Past Time exhibition, which displays precious artefacts.

The restored brickwork walls in the subterranean space are interrupted by a six-metre window, revealing passing Thameslink trains.

A series of underground vaults that were discovered during the museum’s construction will open to visitors in spring 2027.

“This museum epitomises the idea of the primordial reflex in us to dig to find various treasures,” noted Stanton Williams co-founder Paul Williams.

The architects aimed to make the qualities of the existing spaces work to the museum’s advantage, displaying delicate objects in the underground gallery and maintaining the communal feel of the market space on ground level.

“Decisions based on the building – like precious objects should be kept underground because it’s got a greater thermal inertia – loosen the straitjacket around the museum having to act like a museum in other spaces,” said Khan.

“It means some other spaces can be more market-like and have a more public-facing potential that a normal museum wouldn’t be able to do,” he continued. “The building has enabled the museum to become a different kind of museum.”.

Unisex toilets were designed in a Victorian style, with decorative tiles depicting wildflowers that grew in London’s bombed-out sites.

Elsewhere in the museum is a 24-hour cafe where original interior tiles and a timber staircase were restored.

Shaw hopes the London Museum will be an inviting place that residents and tourists want to return to time and again.

“We want to be super welcoming – we don’t want to be intimidating as a museum,” noted Shaw. “Some of the other museums are quite imposing buildings.”.

“We want to be a destination, not just a museum, but a place for people to keep coming back to.”.

Other museums recently completed in London include V&A East by O’Donnell + Tuomey and the V&A East Storehouse by Diller Scofidio + Renfro, which was given a Dezeen Award for its wayfinding design.
