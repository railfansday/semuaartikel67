It has been revealed that dubai's airline return hinges on a regulator's deadline. Riyadh's depends on weeks without strikes, and until then its hotels lose the regional traffic that fills them midweek.

Industry observers note that foreign airlines are trickling back to Dubai. They aren’t going back to Riyadh.

Dubai’s return has a deadline: Jan. 31, when the European Union Aviation Safety Agency’s (EASA) conflict-zone bulletins for the Middle East expire. Riyadh’s doesn’t. It depends on Iran-backed Houthi strikes stopping, and no foreign long-haul airline intends to fly there before January.

Recent findings suggest that the hotel numbers show the gap. Riyadh’s occupancy fell to 34.3% in October, from 69% a year earlier, according to CoStar’s STR. Dubai’s fell to 68.4%, from 84.9%. Dubai is down but Riyadh has been cut in half.

Cancellations and postponements are rising across the Saudi hotels that Perfetti Hospitality advises, according to its CEO Turab Saleem. Hotels that depend on international corporate and group business are under the worst occupancy pressure, he said.
