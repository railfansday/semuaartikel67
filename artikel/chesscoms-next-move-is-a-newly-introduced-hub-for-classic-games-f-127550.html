Chess.com is ready to go a lot bigger than chess. After launching Gambit, a gambling-free poker site, earlier this year, the company is now adding 10 more games: backgammon, checkers, go, hearts, mahjong, shogi, sudoku, solitaire, xiangqi, and a Scrabble alternative called word. They’re all part of Chess’s latest Classic Games platform, which is launching its public beta on Saturday on the web and through a new app for iOS and Android.

“Our cofounders have had many people come up to them and say, ‘Hey, I run a mahjong night on Wednesdays’ or ‘I go to this local bar in Chicago with like five people, and we love playing go, but we lack a Chess.com,’” chief growth officer Albert Cheng says in an interview with The Verge. “The way that Chess.com has grown this community of almost 300 million members has not really happened for a lot of these other types of classic games.”.

Classic Games is aimed at filling that gap. Some of the new games, like shoji or xiangqi, are strategy games similar to chess while others are further afield, like sudoku, solitaire, and word. The Classic team chose the new games through “a mix of community feedback, market research, and then just our own confidence in our ability to serve that game well,” Cheng notes. It’s focused on classics that “have some sort of lifelong journey of learning and a community.”.

According to recent reports, classic Games will feel familiar if you’ve ever played a match on Chess.com. Each game has lessons, leaderboards, and a rating system. Multiplayer games like checkers or mahjong have options for matches against other players or bots, and they use the same bot characters that Chess.com has. (Martin remains the lowest-rated bot across the board, whether it’s on Chess, Gambit, or Classic.) Not all of the games have the same play modes — word and mahjong have puzzles like chess, while sudoku has a set of daily grids and solitaire has daily deals.

It has been revealed that the expansion hasn’t put Chess.com itself on the back burner, according to Cheng. If anything, it’s never been better. “Chess is more and more so in the public zeitgeist than I’ve ever seen it, frankly, during my time here,” Cheng claims. “Our daily active consumers are higher than they’ve ever been during the manufacturer’s history.” He declined to share exactly how many daily users Chess.com presently has but said the site is “doing amazingly well.”.

According to recent reports, so, while the company is expanding beyond its domain name, chess remains its primary focus. “The amount of folks that are fully dedicated toward building fresh games is less than 1 percent of the company,” according to Cheng.

According to recent reports, AI played a key role in helping Chess.com launch 11 newly introduced games in a matter of months with a team of only a few people. Earlier this year, Cheng told The Verge his team built Gambit with the help of AI, mainly for coding tasks. He states they used “almost entirely the same approach” for Classic, with AI helping to speed up the coding process again.

“Because these games are built on top of the Chess.com code base and design system and bot characters and rating systems, it’s a huge advantage and allows us to move very rapidly alongside AI to bring these types of games to market with a very small team,” Cheng states. “As we create more and more games, the adjacent games that can be created to it just happen much faster because the infrastructure is there, as opposed to standing everything up from scratch.”.

Industry observers note that AI wasn’t used for Classic Games’ art and design, Cheng says. “The look and feel of it is very carefully crafted by humans, and we’re proud of that,” he says. “The truth is, it really is a blend. It’s a small team of really talented folks that have good product and design judgment, and they’re leveraging AI.”.

“We are fortunate in some ways that AI and chess have been intermixed for 30-ish years.”.

Chess.com’s comfort with AI may come from the fact that human chess players have now spent decades playing and learning alongside machines. “We are fortunate in some ways that AI and chess have been intermixed for 30-ish years,” Cheng notes, calling back to Garry Kasparov’s 1996 chess match against IBM’s Deep Blue supercomputer. “Even before the tech giant was founded 20 years ago, the idea that AI was super powerful at the game of chess was embedded in how the cofounders thought about the company.”.

Elsewhere in the games industry, there’s been increasing tension over the use of AI. Cheng states that while Chess.com may be using AI to “accelerate” coding tasks, the creative side of things has become more important. “On the art and the design side, the ratios are shifting a decent amount, where we’re hiring more of those folks,” he says, adding that the company has “shipped a lot more major features in Chess.com than in previous years” using what they’ve learned from Classic Games’ faster development process.

In a recent development, chess.com might not be done growing its library of games, either. Cheng noted it will likely expand to “a lot more” games in the future.
