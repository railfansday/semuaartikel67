Latest Update: An ancient virus may help explain what makes the human brain different

According to recent reports, A virus that infected chimpanzees millions of years ago has left behind a genetic legacy that may help explain an important difference between human and chimpanzee brain development. Researchers at Lund University have discovered that ancient viral DNA embedded in the chimpanzee genome switches off a gene that remains active in humans and appears to play an important role in the developing brain.

Industry observers note that the findings, published in Science Advances, reveal how infections from the distant past can leave lasting changes in the genetic makeup of an entire population. They also offer new clues about how the brains of humans and our closest evolutionary relatives may have developed differently.

According to recent reports, although pandemics are often considered a modern threat, infectious diseases have been spreading through large animal and human populations for millions of years. Some of these ancient infections left traces of viral DNA that continue to influence how genes function today.

Johan Jakobsson, Professor of Molecular Neurogenetics at Lund University, claims the discovery demonstrates how a virus can introduce genetic changes that eventually become widespread throughout a population.

"The virus we investigated specifically affected chimpanzees. Yet humans and chimpanzees have largely the same genetic makeup. That is why it is important to understand the differences in how genes are regulated. Our findings suggest that one gene, LINC00662, plays a significant role in early human brain development," says Johan Jakobsson.

How an Ancient Virus Silenced a Brain Development Gene.

At the center of the discovery is a gene called LINC00662. Researchers found that it is active during early human brain development but completely switched off in comparable chimpanzee brain models.

The difference appears to be caused by a genetic remnant of PTERV1, an ancient virus that infected chimpanzees millions of years ago. PTERV1 belongs to a group known as endogenous retroviruses, which are viruses whose genetic material became incorporated into the DNA of their hosts. Once embedded in the genome, these viral sequences can be inherited by future generations.

Sources indicate that over time, PTERV1 produced numerous copies of its genetic material that became scattered across different parts of the chimpanzee genome. Researchers found that these viral sequences are systematically silenced through a process called methylation.

Methylation involves chemical modifications to DNA that help regulate gene activity. It is one of the ways cells control which genetic instructions are used and which remain inactive. In this case, the ancient viral insertion appears to have caused a neighboring gene involved in brain development to be switched off.

Jakobsson says understanding these differences in gene regulation could also provide important insights into how genetic activity protects the brain or contributes to damage and disease.

Miniature Brains Reveal a Striking Genetic Difference.

Sources indicate that to investigate the virus's effects, researchers grew miniature brain models called organoids using stem cells from humans and chimpanzees. These three-dimensional structures reproduce certain features of developing brain tissue, allowing scientists to study aspects of early brain development under controlled laboratory conditions.

According to recent reports, when the team compared the two types of organoids, the difference was striking. LINC00662 revealed strong activity in the human brain models but was completely inactive in those derived from chimpanzee cells.

"The gene was strongly expressed in the human organoids, but not at all in the chimpanzee organoids," says Patricia Gerdes, postdoc in molecular neurogenetics at Lund University.

The researchers then set out to determine whether the ancient viral DNA was directly responsible for this difference.

Using CRISPR-Cas9, a powerful gene editing technology that allows scientists to make targeted changes to DNA, they removed the viral sequence from chimpanzee cells.

The result provided compelling evidence. Once the viral DNA was removed, the previously inactive LINC00662 gene switched back on.

"This reveals that it is in fact the viral insertion that is responsible for the gene being switched off in chimpanzees," claims Patricia Gerdes.

Newly introduced Clues to Human Brain Evolution.

The team also wanted to understand what LINC00662 actually does in the human brain. To investigate, they used CRISPR to switch off the gene in human brain organoids and examined how other genes responded.

According to recent reports, disabling LINC00662 reduced the activity of genes associated with nerve cell development and the formation of connections between neurons. These processes are essential for establishing the networks that allow the brain to communicate and function.

The results suggest that LINC00662 contributes to important stages of early human brain development, while the ancient viral insertion prevents the gene from functioning in chimpanzees.

It has been revealed that more broadly, the discovery highlights an crucial principle of evolution. Although humans and chimpanzees share much of their genetic material, differences in how genes are switched on and off can influence how their bodies and brains develop.

The findings suggest that ancient viral infections may have contributed to these differences by altering gene regulation long after the original infections disappeared.

Researchers now blueprint to investigate whether other remnants of ancient viruses have similarly influenced brain evolution. They also hope to determine whether these inherited viral sequences may play a role in neurological diseases.

Together, the discoveries raise an intriguing possibility that some of the differences between human and chimpanzee brains may trace back, at least in part, to viruses that infected our evolutionary relatives millions of years ago.
