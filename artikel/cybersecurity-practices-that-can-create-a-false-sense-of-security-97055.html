Cybersecurity Practices That Can Create A False Sense Of Security

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Organizations invest significant time and resources in cybersecurity measures designed to protect their systems, data and operations. However, even practices that seem responsible can create a false sense of security—especially when they’re treated as substitutes for more comprehensive protection and ongoing risk assessment.

Sources indicate that for tech leaders, the challenge is recognizing when established security practices are effectively reducing risk and when they may be simply creating the appearance of preparedness. Here, Forbes Technology Council members highlight common cybersecurity practices and beliefs that can give organizations misplaced confidence and explain what to do instead.

According to recent reports, “We have 24/7 monitoring” is not the same as, “We will catch an matter at 2 a.m.” This is truly a false sense of security unless your teams have fine-tuned alerts very well over time for your situations and context. That means TP/FP are identified, and actual signals are not lost in the high-volume alert noise. To test, inject a true alert at an inconvenient hour and see if ops is detecting it and acting upon it. Monitoring is mostly a comfort unless you make it a security control. - Pradeep Sandhu, Cywarden, Inc.

Industry observers note that the fundamental assumption behind traditional cybersecurity is that all the technology in your security stack will behave exactly as designed, requiring little oversight. This could not be further from the truth. Constant governance and visibility into your security system is no longer an option. It is a necessity that can’t be overlooked or underestimated, and it’s a critical issue that must be addressed, especially as we navigate the uncharted territory of AI. - Kevin Lynch, Optiv.

Forbes Technology Council is an invitation-only community for world-class CIOs, CTOs and technology executives. Do I qualify?

Prioritizing security by a generic risk model describes the average organization, which does not exist. One bank had 362 vulnerabilities under attack in its network that global data never saw. A score trained on everyone is a statement about no one. - Michael Roytman, Empirical Security.

Industry observers note that as a CISO, I’ve sat through HiTrust, SOC 2 and PCI audits, and I value them. But a clean report is a snapshot of scoped controls and evidence chosen for an auditor to see, not proof we’d stop a real attacker. The danger is when “we passed” becomes the finish line and the board sees a green light. I treat audits as the floor, then measure what matters: real prevention, detection and remediation speed. - Lane Sullivan, Concentric AI.

Many organizations mistake end-to-end encryption for security. Encryption answers one question: Can someone read the message in transit? It states nothing about who is on the other end or whether a record survives. Further, the same encryption that keeps attackers out can end up keeping the firm out, too. Security means governing the conversation at the source, with verified identities, managed access and a record the firm owns and can act on. - Dima Gutzeit, LeapXpert.

According to recent reports, treating an approved business application as permanently trusted can create unchecked risk. Vendors now add AI agents and automation to software enterprises already use, giving new capabilities access to existing data, permissions and workflows. Security teams need to reassess what those applications can do as features change, not rely on the risk profile established when they were procured. - Ofer Klein, Reco.

Software from an approved vendor, known repository, signed package or internal developer can still contain malicious or unintended behavior. Source and provenance are crucial, but they do not show what the finished code will actually do. Organizations should verify behavior before execution, not inherit trust from where the code came from. - Ken Ammon, CodeHunter.

Recent findings suggest that early in my career, the problem was, “We don’t know what we have.” Today, most teams have dashboards full of alerts and findings, but visibility doesn’t equal security. If the data from your environment is not contextualized into actionable, cross-domain insights and proactive remediation workflows, you’ve just documented your exposure in more detail rather than successfully mitigating it. - Sivan Tehila, Onyxia Cyber.

It has been revealed that A common misconception is that if an email passes through a security gateway, it must be safe. While traditional gateways catch known threats, they’re less effective against AI-driven attacks that don’t rely on malicious links or attachments. These newer attacks look and sound legitimate, making them harder to spot. That’s why organizations must pay attention to communication patterns. - Eyal Benishti, IRONSCALES.

A green dashboard does not truly mean your business is secure. No alerts may signal gaps in visibility, not an absence of threats. Organizations need security that connects signals across their environment, backed by experts who monitor, investigate and respond 24/7. Regularly test your defenses to confirm teams can detect suspicious activity and act rapidly to contain threats before they do harm. - Neal Bradbury, Barracuda.

One of the biggest misconceptions about open-source security is that finding vulnerabilities means you’re securing your software. Scanners and AI agents can flag thousands of risks, but without a clear remediation path, they create backlogs instead of protection. For end-of-life open-source software, the stakes are even higher. When official patches stop, vulnerabilities don’t. - Aaron Mitchell, HeroDevs.

A dangerous belief is that more security tools automatically mean better security. In reality, too many disconnected tools can create blind spots, duplicate alerts and unclear ownership. A mature security posture is not defined by how much technology has been purchased but by how quickly the organization can detect, understand and contain a real threat. - Dr. Chiranjiv Roy, C5i.ai.

In a recent development, vendor security questionnaires feel thorough, but they capture a one-time self-assessment from direct suppliers and say little about the vendors behind them. Risk changes constantly and often sits several tiers deep, so treat these questionnaires as a starting point, then pair them with continuous monitoring of the extended sub-tier supplier network for proactive supplier risk mitigation. - Yardley Pohl, interos.ai.

It has been revealed that poorly executed phishing tests can do far more to damage employee trust than they will ever benefit an organization’s cybersecurity capability. Activities that penalize individuals, don’t result in meaningful and engaged education, or become so frequent that they annoy and create resentment do little to strengthen your cyber defenses. - Kim Bozzella, Protiviti.

I think relying too heavily on periodic security audits can create a false sense of confidence. They’re valuable, but technology changes promptly, especially as teams introduce latest tools and AI into their workflows. I’ve learned that it’s important to keep reviewing how systems are actually being used, who has access and where new risks may be developing between audits. - Deniz Gezgin, Eightpoint.

Mandatory password rotation is often mistaken for effective cybersecurity. Requiring employees to change passwords every 60 to 90 days can encourage predictable variations and credential reuse, potentially increasing security risks. Organizations should prioritize phishing-resistant multifactor authentication, compromised-credential detection and risk-based access controls over routine password expiration. - Gouri Sankar Dash, Tata Consultancy Services.

Treating successful backups as proof of recovery can create false confidence. A backup job can finish successfully while a full restoration still fails or takes too long. Test recovery of a complete business workflow in an isolated environment, including its dependencies, and measure the time until people can actually use it. - Shahen Minasyan, Twin Peaks Inc. (TPI).

Access and authentication controls are paramount to any internal system, but with AI agents now working alongside humans, impersonation is becoming a growing threat. These tools often rely on being able to access the same files and systems you can access. It may be productive, but left unattended, it’s risky. Building in systemic limitations to what AI agents can do on your behalf is an notable consideration. - Matt Wielbut, Openly.
