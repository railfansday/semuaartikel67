Protecting forest birds may depend on what happens outside park boundaries

Forest birds in small national park sites may depend as much on surrounding forests as on habitat within park boundaries, a new study suggests.

In a recent development, drawing the boundaries of a national park on a map is easy. Trees stay where they grow, but birds don’t follow those boundaries.

Forest birds such as thrushes, warblers, and woodpeckers may build their nests inside national parks, but they often depend on the land outside those parks too.

Researchers examined eight small national park sites in the northeastern United States, comparing forest characteristics inside each park with those in the surrounding county.

Bird presence inside the parks was associated with forest characteristics across the surrounding county at least as strongly as with those across the park itself.

Small parks are therefore unlikely to protect forest birds without help from the land around them.

It has been revealed that the analysis was carried out by Bruna Amaral, who is a doctoral student at Michigan State University. Her collaborator Elise Zipkin is an ecologist who heads the university’s Ecology, Evolution, and Behavior program.

Sources indicate that sixteen species were included in the work. The researchers asked only whether each species was present at a given survey point.

It has been revealed that bird numbers and nesting success were not followed. The results therefore describe a pattern and cannot be used to prove a cause.

Sources indicate that surveys were conducted each May and June between 2006 and 2023. Volunteers with the National Park Service’s Northeast Temperate Network visited fixed points in the forest of every park.

At each point, an observer spent ten minutes recording every bird within 164 feet (50 meters).

According to recent reports, roughly 91% of the records came from birds identified by their sound. Only those sound records were used in the analysis.

Recent findings suggest that all 16 species are classed as forest birds. The wood thrush, the ovenbird, the scarlet tanager, and several warblers are included among them.

According to recent reports, the parks were not created for wildlife. They are national historic sites and historical parks whose main purpose is the preservation of history.

The smallest park covers only 69 acres (28 hectares), which is close to the area of 50 football fields. About 2,860 acres (1,156 hectares) are covered by the largest park.

Acadia National Park was excluded from the study. One reason given was that it is more than ten times larger than the other parks.

Sources indicate that which forest was most closely tied to the presence of each bird? That was the question put to the data.

Three candidates were considered. The first was the forest within a quarter mile (400 meters) of the survey point. The whole park was the second, and the surrounding county was the third.

Five measures were used to describe the forest at each scale. Among them were conifer amount, shrub cover, and the number of large old trees.

Because a bird feeds, nests, and hides in the nearest forest, the team had expected it to matter most.

The researchers identified 40 statistically significant associations between bird presence and forest characteristics, involving 13 of the 16 species.

According to recent reports, county forest accounted for 19 of the 40 links. Another 16 links were to the forest near the survey point. Only five were to the park as a whole.

Recent findings suggest that so the forest averaged across a whole park revealed the fewest links of the three. That is the very area a boundary defines.

It has been revealed that the authors described the county result as surprising. These parks hold older and more varied forest than the land around them.

Three species were linked only to county forest. They were the wood thrush, the scarlet tanager, and the brown creeper.

Bark is where a brown creeper finds its food. The small brown-and-white bird climbs tree trunks in a spiral and pulls insects from the cracks.

Creepers were more likely to be present in a park when its county’s forests had more or bigger trees.

Recent findings suggest that fewer shrubs in those county forests were also tied to their presence. Inside the parks, no forest measure showed a reliable link to the bird.

“Species rarely respect human-made boundaries,” the authors wrote.

Sources indicate that the forest close to each survey point was linked to ten species. Different species, however, were linked to different kinds of forest.

In a recent development, the black-throated blue warbler nests in shrubs. It was more likely to be present where shrub cover was thick and large old trees were fewer.

Downy woodpeckers dig their nest holes in decayed wood. These birds were more likely to be present where large old trees were plentiful and conifers were scarce.

Downy woodpeckers occurred in every park where black-throated blue warblers were recorded, suggesting those parks contain forest conditions suitable for both species.

Recent findings suggest that only weak, mixed links were found between park size and the birds. Five species were more likely to be present in bigger parks, and two were less likely. No pattern at all was seen for most species.

The team didn’t test why county forest would be tied to the birds inside a park.

Sources indicate that one possible reason lies in the weeks after young birds leave the nest, Amaral said. Survival in that period depends partly on the land a young bird moves through before migrating south.

In a recent development, earlier research on wood thrushes found that young birds do move this way. Mature forest is left behind for younger woods with dense undergrowth.

There the young thrushes eat insects and fruit and hide from predators.

Recent findings suggest that edges are another possibility. More forest edge is found in a county with broken-up forest, and nest predators are more active at edges.

Birds returning from migration may also pick a region first. A nesting territory would then be chosen second.

In a recent development, the land around the eight parks varies widely. Minute Man National Historical Park in Massachusetts lies in an open, broken-up landscape.

Marsh-Billings-Rockefeller National Historical Park in Vermont, by contrast, lies in a broadly forested county.

“We need to start to think beyond park boundaries,” Amaral remarked.

Benefits from bird-friendly backyards and farms can reach the parks next to them, Zipkin remarked.

It has been revealed that conservation easements were also suggested by the team. These are legal agreements that limit development on private land. They may be easier to arrange than enlarging a park.

Industry observers note that the counts show whether a species was present. Once birds arrive, their nesting success may still be shaped by the park’s own forest, the authors wrote.

In a recent development, only one park-wide value and one county value per forest measure came from each park. Estimates at those two scales are therefore less precise than the estimates for nearby forest.

Sources indicate that what the measures capture is the kind of forest found in a county. How much forest a county holds was not part of them.

Every one of the eight parks is small, and about 60% of the surrounding region is still forest. No data on bird population trends in the surrounding counties were available to the team.

Trends inside and outside the parks could therefore not be compared.

Sources indicate that the authors identified a next step: testing whether a park’s surroundings influence how birds respond to forest conditions inside it. This could help managers apply the findings.

The full study was published in the journal Landscape Ecology.
