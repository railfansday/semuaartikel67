According to recent reports, marriage usually helps your money through rights a live-in partner doesn’t get: a joint return that cuts taxes when one of you earns much more, spousal and survivor Social Security benefits, and inheritance without a will. Before you decide, figure your tax as a couple and as two singles, since two high earners can pay more tax once married.

Industry observers note that splitting rent saves money whether or not you marry. What marriage adds is legal rights, and for most couples they make it the better financial deal:.

Taxes cut both ways. Take a couple with no children who claim the standard deduction and earn $90,000 and $30,000 in wages. Under the IRS’s 2027 brackets, a joint return would lower their federal income tax by about $2,140 compared with two single returns, since more of the bigger salary is taxed at 12% instead of 22%.

In a recent development, one exception is two high earners. At $200,000 in wages each, they’d owe none of the 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax filing single, but married they’d owe it on combined wages above $250,000, or $1,350 a year. They also share one cap on state and local tax deductions, and they reach the top 37% bracket at less than twice a single filer’s income.

In a recent development, staying single keeps every money decision yours, and no partner’s tax errors can cost you, since each spouse on a joint return owes the whole bill. Filing separately avoids that but generally costs more tax. A single parent filing as head of household can also owe more after marrying a partner who earns. You also skip a wedding, which averaged $34,200 for couples married in 2025, The Knot’s Real Weddings Study found. For most couples, these advantages are worth less than what marriage adds.

Before the wedding, total your tax on two single returns and on one joint return, since your marital status on December 31 decides how you file for the whole year.
