More Subscribers Won’t Save Your Business: The Trap Of Vanity Growth

Vasudeva Akula, VOZIQ AI cofounder and head of data science. Helps recurring revenue businesses improve customer retention using ML.

For years, customer acquisition has been treated as a straightforward equation: Generate demand, identify prospects most likely to convert, optimize sales and measure success through conversion rates, customer acquisition cost and new customers added. These metrics are still important, but especially for subscription businesses, they leave leaders in the dark about whether the business wins today are contributing to tomorrow’s growth.

According to recent reports, compare these two leads: One is highly likely to convert but switches to a competitor before the acquisition cost is recovered. Another has a lower conversion propensity but stays loyal for years. Businesses that optimize acquisition only for conversion can become more efficient at winning customers without becoming better at building a valuable customer base. This gap between acquisition and long-term value becomes more consequential as subscription businesses try to improve their growth trajectory against headwinds.

In this article, I’ll discuss this disconnect and provide three actionable steps that are proven to bridge the gap between acquisition and long-term value.

It has been revealed that A lead-scoring model can become increasingly accurate at predicting purchases without identifying which customers will generate lasting value. When success is measured at the point of sale, a customer who stays for years and one who leaves within months can both count as successful acquisitions. Without customer outcomes informing acquisition decisions, businesses can improve conversion performance without improving the long-term economics of their customer base.​.

It has been revealed that retention and service teams see which customers leave early, which remain for years and which generate sustained value. Acquisition teams, meanwhile, work with prospect characteristics and conversion data.

When these datasets are disconnected, customer outcomes don’t consistently influence future targeting. The same weaknesses can persist across successive acquisition cohorts, even when historical evidence could help identify prospects more likely to become valuable customers.

Recent findings suggest that closing the feedback loop means using retention outcomes to identify the characteristics associated with valuable, long-lasting customer relationships, then applying those insights to future prospect evaluation and lead scoring. As new cohorts mature, their outcomes provide further evidence to refine future acquisition decisions.

According to recent reports, increasing acquisition alone may therefore fail to deliver sustainable growth. Reducing avoidable departures and expanding existing relationships can also contribute to growth. The outcome depends on how effectively the business acquires customers, sustains relationships and increases their value together.​.

Conversion rates and customer acquisition cost remain important, but leaders should evaluate them alongside outcomes. How many acquired customers remain after 90 days or one year? How much value do they generate relative to acquisition and service costs? Which acquisition sources produce stronger long-term outcomes?

These measures help distinguish acquisition volume from acquisition quality and reveal whether better conversion translates into stronger customer economics.

Customer outcomes should inform future targeting and lead scoring. If particular customer characteristics or acquisition sources are associated with stronger long-term outcomes, that evidence should influence where the business invests its acquisition resources.

Predictive customer lifetime value (PCLV) can help estimate the economic value a customer is expected to generate over time. Used alongside conversion propensity, acquisition costs and observed outcomes, it helps businesses evaluate both the likelihood of winning a customer and the potential value of that relationship. The goal isn’t to replace conversion metrics with a single prediction but to make acquisition decisions using a more complete view of customer economics and refine them as actual outcomes become available.

It has been revealed that each of the “get,” “keep” and “grow” life cycle stages require different strategies, but their effectiveness depends on how they inform one another. “Keep” helps identify which customers stay and generate lasting value. “Get” uses those insights to find prospects with similar characteristics, while “grow” focuses on expanding the value of established relationships.

When these levers share a common view of customer value, acquisition is informed by actual customer outcomes, and growth decisions become more closely connected to long-term economics.​.

Acquiring more customers alone won’t improve the quality of growth. Long-term growth and a competitive edge will come from learning from each customer relationship and using that knowledge to acquire the right customers. And sustainable growth depends on whether those customers create lasting value and whether the business uses what it learns to make better decisions.
