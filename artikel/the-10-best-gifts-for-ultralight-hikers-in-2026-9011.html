According to recent reports, hoping to buy a gift for an ultralight hiker? Here’s the hard truth: Whatever you purchase will be weighed, then quietly stuffed in a closet. I know, because after a decade of thru-hiking, I’ve almost completely wallpapered my small room with all the copies of Wild I’ve been given. Thank you, but please stop.

Recent findings suggest that apart from heavy gifts, the last thing an ultralight hiker needs is someone else choosing their significant three. The little things, though? That’s where a good gift can surprise them. This year’s picks tackle ten small annoyances of life on trail, from a rigid bottle that actually works with a filter to a sun glove that doesn’t plaster to your hands.

According to recent reports, single-use bottles crease, crinkle, and need replacing, but my original ThruBottle survived 1,800 miles this summer with plenty of life left. My only gripe was that it was too stiff to squeeze water through a filter. I’ve upgraded to the new ThruBottle 1000, which has a softer body and sheds nearly an ounce, weighing 2.2 ounces for a full liter.

The zip-lock bag you’d rather no one ever rummaged through has a 0.14-ounce alternative: this cap turns a compatible 28 mm bottle into a portable bidet. I find that it works best with a squeezable bottle. Unlike TP, it never runs out halfway through a section.

It has been revealed that I sleep cold, and when the temperature drops on the trail, my ears and neck are often the first places I want extra warmth. The Shag Hood, made from Polartec Alpha Direct, covers them for 1.16 ounces, so I can skip carrying another full layer. Bonus: It’s gorpcore enough to wear off trail too.

My old half-finger sun gloves always came off inside out, and getting them back on over sweaty hands was a nightmare. The éclipse gloves have a thumb hole and mitten-style opening, so they slide on and off. They weigh 0.35 ounces and give me UPF 50+ sun protection on the backs of my hands, where multiple rounds of sunscreen have never been enough.

A camera buried in your pack takes no photos. The three-ounce Capture Clip attaches to a shoulder strap or belt, so I can grab my camera when the light is just right, instead of getting stuck crouching over my pack, still fumbling for it when the light’s gone.

The long handle keeps your knuckles out of dinner at the bottom of a freeze-dried meal bag. The 0.8-ounce titanium spork would outlast my hiking career if I could stop losing it. I found four on my last thru-hike, so I’m clearly not the only one.

A half-empty fuel canister is too precious to toss but annoying to carry. I’ve envied hikers using heavier transfer valves, but couldn’t justify the weight until the Alpenflow dropped. It weighs 0.09 ounces and transfers leftover fuel between two standard EN417 canisters, including those from MSR, Jetboil, and Snow Peak.

In a recent development, leukotape is my holy grail bandage. It stays on through sweat, mud, and sketchy river crossings, and I’ve accidentally left a strip on for weeks. It’s gotten harder for me to find it in stores lately, so a spare roll is a keeper. I wrap a generous amount around my trekking poles instead of carrying the full 3.9-ounce roll.

It has been revealed that for 10,000 thru-hiking miles, my pillow was the head of my dirty air mattress propped up on a water bottle, which rolled out from under me all night. Don’t make my mistake. For 1.9 ounces, the regular size is the real deal, and it packs down to the size of a pool ball. Pricey for a pillow, but I sleep better.

Recent findings suggest that this 0.11-ounce Dyneema pouch stops loose tent stakes from rattling around in your pack. You don’t think you need one until a rogue stake starts working its way through your bug netting. Not that I, a seasoned professional and expert patcher, know anything about that.
