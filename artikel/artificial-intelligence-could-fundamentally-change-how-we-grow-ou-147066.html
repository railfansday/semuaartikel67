Artificial intelligence could fundamentally change how we grow our food

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A latest review explores how generative AI could transform crop breeding and help address future agricultural challenges.

In a recent development, modern plant breeding runs on genome data and decades of catalogued crop genes. For all that, it still takes 10 to 15 years to turn a promising cross into a variety a farmer can plant.

Conventional crossbreeding largely relies on existing genetic variation.

A new review states the next round of breeding won’t start with a plant at all. It will start with a model that writes DNA no plant has ever carried, and a lab that builds the sequence to see whether it works.

Feng Xu, Jun Yan, Xiangfeng Wang and their colleagues at China Agricultural University went through 67 generative AI tools built for biological design.

They sorted the tools by the part of breeding each one could serve, and hardly any were made with crops in mind.

Most were trained on microbes or mammalian cells, and the authors wrote that carrying them over to a maize or wheat genome is unfinished work.

The appeal lies in what the older tools couldn’t do.

Yield that holds up in a dry year is controlled by many genes at once, and a model that only ranks the variants nature supplied has a short reach.

Plant breeders mark their own history in stages, and the review counts four before this one. Choosing by eye and crossing came first.

Molecular markers came next, then genomic selection, then the omics work of the past decade, which filled catalogs with crop genes and the traits attached to them.

Every stage improved the odds of picking well. None of them changed the basic move, which was to choose among variation that already existed in a species.

Breeders have worked those catalogs for particular traits, from tomato flavor to seed size to crops that fertilize themselves.

According to recent reports, breeding 5.0, the authors’ name for what comes next, is meant to change the move itself. Design the genetic part first, then build it.

Artificial intelligence in plant science has mostly sorted things. It scored variants, predicted traits and ranked candidates a breeder could already have grown.

Industry observers note that generative models work the other way around. Trained on enough DNA and protein structures, they produce new examples that fit the patterns they learned.

That buys two things the authors care about: sequences beyond anything in nature, and screening that happens before a seed goes in the ground.

In a recent development, four kinds of model do most of this work. One compresses sequences into a map a researcher can move through in small steps, which suits fine-tuning how strongly a gene switches on.

According to recent reports, one trains two networks against each other. The first writes sequences, and the second tries to spot the fakes.

A third starts from noise and strips it away a step at a time, which gives tight control over three-dimensional shapes.

The fourth treats DNA as a language, with bases as letters and genes as sentences.

Those language models are the ones that can take an instruction in plain words.

In a recent development, the clearest results involve the short stretches of DNA that decide when a gene switches on and how strongly. Biologists call them promoters.

In cyanobacteria, 95% of the promoters one model designed turned out to be active. In E. coli, a second model wrote promoters that worked 70.8% of the time and looked little like anything in nature.

The authors call that design from scratch. Designs can be tuned as well as built. A third model raised promoter activity by as much as 33.43-fold, and in yeast a fourth moved expression across a thousandfold range.

Of that fourth model’s designs, 57% beat the strongest natural promoters they were measured against. Not one of those organisms is a crop.

According to recent reports, that pattern runs through the review. The proof exists, and it exists in microbes. Plant-specific models are appearing, and most of them still score sequences instead of writing them.

Genome editing lets breeders change a gene directly, and Cas9, the enzyme behind most of that work, came out of bacteria.

Profluent Bio fine-tuned a protein language model until it produced OpenCRISPR-1, the first openly available Cas9 designed this way.

It has been revealed that it cut DNA about as well as the standard enzyme, with better specificity, and it still read the short tag that marks where to cut. A separate model produced another Cas9 variant that worked in mammalian cells.

Protein design has gone further outside plants. One model produced 310 proteins that folded as predicted once made, and tools like AlphaFold made that check routine.

In plants, almost all the published work is redesign of existing proteins, including a refined enzyme in Arabidopsis and an improved potato protein.

The authors remarked design from scratch has rarely been validated in crops.

The authors also raised safety and regulatory questions about AI-designed proteins in crops, including their potential unintended effects, how long they persist under field conditions, and whether they are safe to consume.

So the authors suggested starting small, with designed proteins used as laboratory probes rather than put straight into a crop.

The authors’ own contribution is a framework they call DESIGN, named for six capabilities: discover, engineer, simulate, integrate, generate and navigate.

In plainer words, find the patterns in existing data and pull together information of different kinds.

Then design molecules that fit a set of constraints, make parts nature does not, test candidates in simulation, and weigh competing goals.

The authors set out two worked cases. One designs a maize promoter that stays quiet in a watered field and drives a drought-response gene hard when water runs short.

The other designs a protein that traps a molecule the stripe rust fungus uses to switch off the defenses of wheat.

Neither case is a finished experiment. Both are intends, detailed enough to name the tool at each step and the filter a candidate must clear.

The six capabilities don’t run in a line, the authors wrote. They run as a loop: learn, design, build, test, then feed the results back into the model.

Recent findings suggest that three problems sit between the framework and a field. Plant datasets are scattered across species and growing conditions, and well-annotated ones are thin for most crops.

Sources indicate that many models don’t transfer between domains or explain their output. Testing in plants is slow, so designs pile up faster than anyone can check them.

Plant genomes make the data problem worse, being larger and more repetitive than the genomes most of these models learned from.

Sources indicate that the same gap shows up in single-cell work. One plant model, built for Arabidopsis, labeled cell types it had never been shown with 0.91 accuracy.

The goal is to simulate how a root cell handles salty soil before planting.

The authors emphasized that AI is not meant to replace plant breeders. Breeders will continue to set agricultural goals and evaluate real-world results, while AI helps explore new possibilities.

What hasn’t happened yet, by the authors’ own accounting, is the validation of even one designed protein or regulatory element in a greenhouse or a field.

In a recent development, they named the benchmarks and shared datasets that would get the field there. That greenhouse result is the thing to watch for.

The full study was published in the journal Science China Life Sciences.
