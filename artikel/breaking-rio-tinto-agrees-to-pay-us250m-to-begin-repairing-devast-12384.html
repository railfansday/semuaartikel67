Breaking: Rio Tinto agrees to pay US$250m to begin repairing ‘devastation’ left by Panguna mine

Anglo-Australian miner Rio Tinto has agreed to pay a quarter-of-a-billion dollars to begin the clean-up of the toxic Panguna mine site it abandoned more than three decades ago.

Panguna, once one of the world’s most profitable gold and copper mines, was shuttered in 1989 after violent protests over environmental damage and the inequitable division of the mine’s profits sparked a decade-long civil war on the Papua Latest Guinean island of Bougainville.

But the sprawling mine pit, once a mountain, remains a scar in the middle of the island, with vast pillars of rubble at risk of collapse, and polluted rivers running bright blue with toxins.

Rio Tinto was forced from the island and has never returned, but in recent years has been part of a detailed consultation process with landowners and government. It has now agreed to voluntarily contribute US$250m (A$370m) towards the remediation of the mine site.

Theonila Roka Matbob, a former cabinet minister in Bougainville’s autonomous government and Panguna community leader, remarked the fund was a vital contribution towards repairing the “devastation left by the Panguna mine”.

“We have a lot of work still ahead of us, but the establishment of this fund gives us the chance to find solutions to the huge problems we have been living with since the days of the mine’s operation.

“No amount of money will restore the damage caused to the environment in Panguna, but this sets a foundation for us to invest in addressing the environmental challenges and invest in our children’s futures as well.”.

Roka Matbob stated the most fundamental remediation was to improve “accessibility” for the people living around Panguna – “accessibility to clean drinking water that is safe and secure … and infrastructure, in bridges that makes people’s access to trade, to school, to health[care], much easier. This is one critical area that immediately needs attention.”.

The president of Bougainville, Ishmael Toroama, seeking to become the world’s newest head of state at the forefront of a long-running independence movement on the island, noted the money would make a “meaningful difference” to villages and communities that had suffered from the impacts of the mine’s pollution.

It has been revealed that “This work will deliver benefits not only for people today, but for future generations. Continued engagement with our communities will be essential to ensuring the work responds to local priorities and delivers lasting outcomes.”.

The Rio commitment has emerged from the Panguna Mine Legacy Impact Assessment consultation process, which was initiated in response to a human rights complaint brought by the Australian-based Human Rights Law Centre, with Roka Matbob as the lead complainant.

Keren Adams, the HRLC’s legal director, said establishing the fund had taken years of work.

“It is a testament to the courage and persistence of community leaders, and the willingness of all parties to engage in difficult but constructive dialogue,” she noted.

“The significant funding contributed by Rio Tinto creates a real opportunity to now address an environmental disaster that has remained unresolved for decades.”.

Mark Davies, Rio Tinto’s chief safety and technical officer, said the establishment of the fund was an “notable milestone … intended to support the transition from the assessment to practical remedy implementation”.

“Community engagement is an crucial next step to identify priorities and we remain committed to working with communities to shape the activities supported by the fund.”.

The quarter-of-a-billion dollars will not be sufficient to entirely remediate the mine site, but it is being seen as a major step towards rehabilitation.

The funding commitment for Panguna comes at an acutely sensitive time for the restive Bougainville island.

The mine has become inextricably linked with the Bougainville’s political future: restarting operations at Panguna – believed to still hold at least $60bn in unrealised reserves – is seen by many, including the president, as the fundamental financial basis for the island’s independent future. Indian miner Lloyds has been granted a 25-year large-scale mining lease, but any future mining is years from starting.

Bougainville, a group of islands off Papua Upcoming Guinea to the north of Australia, has been variously controlled by Britain, Germany, Japan, Australia and PNG.

It declared independence in 1975 as the Republic of North Solomons, but was absorbed into the newly independent PNG weeks later.

Its close ethnic ties and proximity to the Solomons archipelago means it has always sat uneasily with Port Moresby’s rule.

That discontent came to a violent head in 1988 after a long-running dispute over the lucrative Panguna copper and gold mine.

The mine, carved from a mountain in central Bougainville, was critically important to PNG, at one point generating more than 45% of the country's national export revenue.

Recent findings suggest that multinational Rio Tinto and other international investors earned nearly US$2bn from the mine over 17 years of operation. But less than 1% of the mine’s profits went to Bougainvilleans, whose homes and lands had been destroyed by it.

Under the leadership of a former mine worker, Francis Ona, disaffected Bougainvilleans ran a sabotage campaign which forced the mine’s closure in 1989.

Sources indicate that the PNG Government responded by sending in police, then soldiers, to restore law and order. Bougainville was blockaded and the island descended into a brutal civil war that ran for a decade, and saw as many as 20,000 people die.

PNG later hired international mercenaries to end the conflict (the notorious Sandline affair), before a peace agreement was signed in 2001.

In a 2019 referendum, 97.7% of Bougainvilleans voted in favour of independence. Many argue that reopening Panguna mine will be key to the island’s economic future as an independent nation.

According to recent reports, A recent environmental impact assessment found the abandoned mine remains an acute threat to people’s lives, including from landslides, levee collapses and floods. Untouched, the river delta will stay polluted for a century.

The future of Bougainville’s independence campaign is now before the PNG parliament, which will decide whether or not to recognise a UN-backed independence referendum held on the island in 2019, in which 97.7% of voters were in favour of Bougainville seceding and becoming an independent country.

The PNG parliament, which heard lengthy and impassioned debate over Bougainville last month, appears almost certain to vote against recognising the referendum result.

But Toroama, who has staked his presidency on achieving political independence, has noted, regardless of the parliament’s decision, Bougainville will enter a period of “self-government” from September next year, before seceding, breaking away from PNG as a fully fledged independent nation, by 2030.
