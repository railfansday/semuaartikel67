Latest Update: I Tried The Great British Bake Off’s Most Controversial Recipe—Here’s How It Went

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The “technical challenge,” a blind-baking test where contestants receive the same recipe and ingredients, often with minimal or deliberately sparse instructions, is a staple of The Great British Bake Off. And contestants have seriously struggled with it over the years. But according to Paul Hollywood, this season’s challenge featuring blue cheese and roasted grape flatbreads was the worst yet. After 60 minutes of kneading, grape-roasting, and undercooking, the bakers produced trays of thick, raw flatbreads that Paul and Nigella could barely eat.

After bakers argued that an hour wasn’t enough, Paul noted, “There’s plenty of time.” But fans, critics, and former bakers alike have been plenty vocal about their feelings. The popular opinion? The bakers were set up to fail—and also that the flavors seemed a little weird.

Industry observers note that with all the controversy, I was curious to see if the challenge was even possible—so I tried it myself. While I purposefully did NOT watch the episode before my attempt, I did print the full recipe from the GBBO website. This recipe was definitely WAY more helpful than the one the bakers were given, and I followed it to the letter.

Here’s how it went: the good, the bad, and the very ugly.

Sources indicate that the beginning? Very smooth. I put the grapes in the oven (the online recipe told me to roast them as a whole bunch rather than cutting them up) and made the bread dough, kneading it for the five minutes specified before letting it rest.

Sources indicate that as soon as I set the dough aside to rest, I discovered how absolutely torturous technical downtime is. I was at an absolute loss. I had nothing to do…except read the recipe over and over again and make sure I knew what 8 cm and 23 cm looked like.

Interestingly enough, the bakers on the show didn’t have this downtime; they were busy cutting and roasting their grapes by then. That said, I don’t think that order was necessarily better; with the time spent slicing the grapes, they wouldn’t have had much time at ALL to roast—and not nearly enough to cool.

The grapes came out of the oven. They weren’t very roasted—as one of the bakers mentioned, they just looked like “raw grapes”—but the recipe said 20 minutes and gave no indicators for how roasted they should be (and also I knew they needed to cool before stuffing), so I just went with it.

Industry observers note that I dumped out the dough (a rude awakening after its 15-minute rest) and chopped it into eight pieces. Immediately, I realized I should have weighed the larger ball and divided it by eight so I could make sure all the pieces were equal, but I didn’t have much time to fuss, so I tried to make them roughly the same weight and moved on.

The recipe noted to roll out each portion of dough into an 8 cm circle, fill it with five roasted grapes and one-eighth of the blue cheese, lift the edges of the circle up to pinch them together, then turn the little ball seam side down and roll the whole thing out into a 23 cm circle.

I ran into problems immediately.

To begin, I cut my dough into wedges from the larger ball, and it was hard to roll them out into a circle to fill. But that was nothing compared to the problems I had rolling out the filled dough. First, the dough wasn’t rested enough, so it kept springing back at me as I tried to roll it out. But the bigger glitch was the grapes. Since they were still pretty juicy, they started bursting as I rolled out the flatbread, flying to the edges and tearing holes in the dough as they oozed out.

After over 15 minutes of stuffing and rolling, my workstation was littered with burst grape guts. None of my flatbreads were 23 cm wide (I think the widest they got was 18?) and I still had three to roll out, but I also knew I needed to start cooking or I was going to have eight totally raw flatbreads at the end of time.

In a recent development, cooking the flatbreads wasn’t a total disaster—I guess—but grape juice oozed out of the holes where they burst and left black patches of burnt sugar on the pan, which I had absolutely no time to clean out since I was still racing the clock. Fortunately, the flatbreads didn’t seem to need the full 90 seconds per side. In fact, they seemed to need constant attention to prevent them from burning, which was bad news since I was still trying to roll out the remaining three flatbreads on the side.

Sources indicate that time was up, and I had five cooked (burst) stuffed flatbreads on my sheet tray, three totally raw ones on the countertop, and a smoking pan. I now know how the bakers feel at the end of such challenges: slightly insane and utterly defeated.

Looking back on the challenge, I can honestly say that it was difficult and somewhat impossible. Ultimately, I couldn’t get all the flatbreads cooked in time, even though I was rolling them out much thinner than the bakers were. But while timing was definitely a problem, the bigger issue was the impossible stupidity of stuffing juicy grapes inside a flatbread and then trying to roll it out without the grapes bursting out and ruining the dough.

It has been revealed that since I managed to cook five flatbreads, I could (unlike Paul and Nigella) taste the finished result—but honestly, I wasn’t a fan. The blue cheese was WAY too strong, and the grapes felt like random pockets of juice in an otherwise savory flatbread. Overall, I wouldn’t recommend trying this challenge yourself—unless you want to be scrubbing the burnt bits of grape sugar off your pan for the next week.
