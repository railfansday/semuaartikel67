It has been revealed that CNBC reported on November 24, 2026 that U.S. Magistrate Judge Alex Tse had approved former OpenAI engineer Abdulrahman Alfozan’s release on a $1 million bond while he awaits trial. Alfozan, 35, faces a charge involving a recording device concealed in an executive conference room.

In a recent development, the case concerns the alleged interception of oral communications at an OpenAI office in San Francisco. Defense attorney David Rizk said he expected Alfozan to be released on November 25, 2026, and fitted with an ankle monitor. Tse’s decision granted a renewed release motion and followed an earlier order detaining Alfozan before trial.

It has been revealed that after the hearing, Rizk said in a text message: “We are simply grateful that the Court made the right decision to release Mr. Alfozan and now we look forward to defending the case.”.

The renewed motion said prosecutors had found no new evidence to support theories that Alfozan was stealing trade secrets or acting as an unregistered foreign agent for Saudi Arabia. The defense presented that absence as a reason to revisit his detention.

According to recent reports, in a response filed on November 23, 2026, prosecutors stated the investigation was ongoing and that what Alfozan did or intended to do with the audio files remained under investigation. They argued that his considerable resources, ties to Saudi Arabia and few remaining ties to the United States still made him a flight risk. The filing said his wife, infant son, parents and siblings resided in Saudi Arabia, and that his OpenAI employment had been terminated.

According to recent reports, prosecutors asked Tse to deny the release motion and keep Alfozan in custody. If the judge chose release, they requested a significant secured bond, travel restrictions, location monitoring, and a prohibition on contacting OpenAI personnel or accessing or transmitting OpenAI materials. They mentioned the proposed restrictions reflected concern that Alfozan might still be able to reach confidential OpenAI information through cloud-storage platforms under his control.

The prosecution filing described hundreds of audio files recovered from one of Alfozan’s personal computers, including dozens of recordings of OpenAI meetings or conversations during September 2026. It noted the material included meetings in the executive conference room on 12 dates between September 9, 2026 and September 28, 2026, plus at least one meeting between OpenAI and representatives of a foreign government.

Sources indicate that many recordings contained discussions of what prosecutors described as possible trade-secret material, while OpenAI identified others as protected by attorney-client privilege, according to the filing. The recordings and their contents remain government allegations rather than findings by a judge or jury.

The Justice Department revealed the charge on October 13, 2026, following Alfozan’s arrest on October 9, 2026. It remarked the Saudi national had worked as an OpenAI engineer since July 2025. The alleged offense involves using or endeavoring to use a device to intercept oral communications.

According to the complaint summarized in that announcement, OpenAI personnel discovered a recording device on September 30, 2026, concealed in toilet paper and inserted into a television’s USB port. OpenAI determined that it had been installed around September 28, 2026, and that its contents were encrypted. Security personnel found a second, similar device in Alfozan’s bag on October 5, 2026.

According to recent reports, the charge carries a maximum sentence of five years in prison and a $250,000 fine upon conviction, followed by three years of supervised release. Jury selection in the single-count case is scheduled for January 20, 2027. The prosecution filing said Alfozan intended to abandon his speedy-trial demand if released.

The bond decision determines where Alfozan will await trial, while leaving the central security questions to the proceedings ahead: what was recorded, how the files were handled, and whether the government can prove the interception charge.
