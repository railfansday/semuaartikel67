Nospeaker ─ Make your MacBook's trackpad talk using its haptic motor

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Mute your Mac. Your trackpad will still talk.

Sources indicate that there's no speaker in a MacBook trackpad, just a small haptic motor that taps the glass so you feel a click. NO SPEAKER drives that motor up to 4,000 times a second, fast enough to make sound you can hear, and turns it into a voice. It talks, sings, raps, and becomes an instrument under your finger.

According to recent reports, one command. About a minute and a half. No speakers used.

Watch the demo on X, with the sound on.

Industry observers note that space skips a part. q or Ctrl-C goes to the end.

Recent findings suggest that downloads the latest release to ~/.local/bin/nospeaker and starts the show. a90.com/nospeaker points to play.sh in this repo, so you can read what it runs first.

According to recent reports, grab nospeaker.zip from the latest release, unzip it and run ./nospeaker in Terminal. Signed with a Developer ID and notarized by Apple.

According to recent reports, needs Xcode or the Command Line Tools (xcode-select --install). Builds one program for Apple Silicon and Intel Macs. Built with only the Command Line Tools, the Intel part needs macOS 13 or later.

It has been revealed that A Force Touch trackpad (built into MacBooks from 2015 on, or a Magic Trackpad 2 or newer), and macOS 12 or later. Without a Force Touch trackpad, it indicates so and exits.

Not tested yet: a Magic Trackpad, and versions of macOS older than 15. MultitouchSupport is private, so older systems may lack the functions it uses, and loudness differs between trackpads. If you try it on another Mac, open an concern with your model, your macOS version and how it sounded.

Why you can hear it. The trackpad has no speaker, but the motor moves the glass. Driven back and forth hundreds or thousands of times a second, the glass vibrates fast enough to make sound you can hear. It's quiet because the motor is built for short taps, not for sound.

The motor. MultitouchSupport is the private framework macOS uses for the trackpad. Its function MTActuationCreateFromDictionary takes a description of a vibration, a list of sine tones with a frequency, a strength and a length each, and MTActuationActuate plays it on the motor. Apple's public haptics API only offers three fixed taps.

The range. Measured with the MacBook's own microphone, every semitone from 98 Hz to 3,951 Hz: how loud it is over a quiet room, and how much of the sound is the note itself.

Sources indicate that it's loud from about 100 Hz to 4 kHz, with a few dead spots where the glass resonates. Its loudest, cleanest notes are around 1 kHz, which covers the most important part of a voice.

According to recent reports, the voice. macOS's own voice speaks the line into memory. Every 10 ms, linear prediction finds the voice's two strongest resonances (formants), its loudness and its pitch. The motor plays those two resonances as tones, restarted on every beat of the pitch, so they buzz like a voice instead of whistling. Hiss sounds (s, f, t) become quick bursts of high tones. Two resonances beat three: in a blind test they were clearer, and louder, because the motor's strength is split fewer ways.

It sounds robotic, and it's much easier to follow when you can read along, which is why the show types every line as it speaks.

Sources indicate that the code. One Swift file, nospeaker.swift, with no dependencies.

Sources indicate that the MultitouchSupport function names were learned from the open-source Tactile project. Everything else was measured on a MacBook.
