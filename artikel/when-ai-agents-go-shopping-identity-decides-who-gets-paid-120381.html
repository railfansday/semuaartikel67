​When AI Agents Go Shopping, Identity Decides Who Gets Paid

🌐 Source: Forbes • 📅 25 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 5 min read (1011 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Rodger Desai is the Founder and CEO of Prove.

Industry observers note that every boardroom conversation about AI shopping agents starts the same way: Can we trust it? Is it really acting for a real person? Is it authorized to spend this money? Fair questions. Banks are already answering “yes”: Citi and U.S. Bank were the first to let cardholders’ AI agents pay with their cards on Mastercard Agent Pay. Merchants are still deciding right now, and that decision will define who wins the next decade of commerce.

I’ve watched this movie before, and the ending surprised people. A decade ago, digital wallets promised to remake how consumers pay. Outside of Apple Pay, most failed because consumers never saw enough reason to change their behavior.

AI shopping agents are not following that script. Adoption is happening now, driven by consumers, ahead of merchant readiness. Visa’s research with Morning Consult found that “nearly 40% of Americans have made a purchase they normally would not have considered as a result of using an AI agent or tool.” That turns the 2027 planning question from “if this matters” into “how exposed are we already.”.

Recent findings suggest that the scale on offer is enormous. McKinsey estimates agentic commerce could orchestrate up to $1 trillion in U.S. retail revenue by 2030, and $3 trillion to $5 trillion worldwide. That’s not a niche use case to monitor. It’s a restructuring of retail large enough that whoever sits inside the transaction, and whoever gets routed around it, will largely be decided before 2030.

Industry observers note that what’s lost in the security debate: Identity doesn’t just keep bad actors out. It’s what makes good experiences possible. The same proof that confirms an agent is legitimately acting for a real, consenting person is what lets a merchant skip the account sign-up, skip the guest checkout and recognize that person, and their agent, instantly. Detecting intent, good or bad, and building a frictionless, financeable experience are not two separate problems. They’re the same problem, solved once.

Much of the industry still treats Know Your Agent (KYA) the way it has long treated Know Your Customer and Know Your Business: as an vital compliance function, but one often built separately from the customer experience rather than inside it. KYA doesn’t have to follow that same path. Built in from the start, it can double as the infrastructure for the single highest-margin moment in the transaction: the firm offer of credit at checkout, rather than living only as a cost line.

Three things, all rooted in identity:.

There’s no account to create, no card and address to retype, no form to abandon. A cryptographic token confirms it’s really you, and that you’ve asked this agent to shop on your behalf.

Recent findings suggest that that consent step matters more than it might seem. The same Visa research found 60% of Americans won’t let an AI agent spend any amount without their prior approval, and just 38% are comfortable letting an agent complete a purchase at all. The agent carries your consent and operates inside the merchant’s own rules, not around them. That’s the difference between an agent a merchant can trust at the door and one it has to interrogate and add friction to.

A verified phone can connect, in real time, to a firm offer of credit: a prescreened financing offer the shopper can decline, one that an agent-mediated checkout would otherwise skip past entirely. Adobe found buy now, pay later accounted for $20 billion of U.S. online holiday spending in 2025, up 9.8% from a year earlier. An agent that completes a purchase without ever surfacing that option isn’t saving the consumer time; it’s costing the merchant money.

According to recent reports, merchants should be thinking about co-branding the agent experience itself. A shopper buys from a retailer’s site and checks out with that retailer’s own branded card, whether a store card that works only there or a co-branded credit card that works anywhere.

When the agent checks out on the shopper’s account with that card on file, the merchant knows who is buying and which card is paying, and the sale counts toward that customer’s history and rewards instead of arriving as a stranger’s order. None of that works through a generic guest checkout. All of it works through a logged-in one. Put the pieces together and they form one loop: The agent arrives verified, checkout indicates a firm offer of credit for the retailer’s card, the shopper accepts and that card becomes the card on file the agent uses next time.

Loyalty must also be engineered, not assumed. An agent that optimizes every purchase on price alone will happily trade a decade of brand loyalty for a $10 discount unless the merchant builds consumer preference directly into the transaction. A customer who always flies one airline should be able to tell their agent to stay loyal unless the gap is real. Get that right, and you keep the relationship you built. Get it wrong, and an algorithm commoditizes your brand in a single checkout.

Sources indicate that for leadership teams finalizing 2027 plans, this is the dividing line. Treat agentic commerce purely as a security and compliance problem, and you’ll spend the next year building defenses while your competitors build revenue, financing offers, co-branded cards and loyalty, in front of every agent-driven purchase instead of behind it.

Three decisions belong in those aims: Recognize a verified agent at login instead of blocking it, surface a firm offer of credit for your card when that agent reaches checkout, and build the shopper’s loyalty preference into the transaction so their agent keeps choosing you.

Recent findings suggest that the banks already made their decision. Merchants are still making theirs. The question isn’t whether AI agents will shop on your customers’ behalf. They already do. It’s whether, when they do, your business is still the one that gets paid.​.
