Industry observers note that A share of NuScale Power (SMR -11.81%) cost $53.43 at the close on Oct. 15, 2025, the stock's highest finish ever. On Thursday, Nov. 19, the shares ended the day at $3.81 -- a fall of about 93% for the small modular reactor developer, and the stock's lowest close since early March 2024.

NuScale ended September sitting on about $1.86 billion in cash and investments, with no debt. At Thursday's close, the market priced the whole company at around $1.6 billion (including both its share classes), or over $200 million less than that cash. And because NuScale loses money every quarter, there's no price-to-earnings ratio to weigh against it.

For a stock that's lost almost all its value, that might look like a floor. Unfortunately, I don't think it's one.

The fact that would make NuScale worth owning hasn't changed throughout the slide: the firm still doesn't have a firm order for its power modules.

Industry observers note that nuScale has stayed busy. In October, it signed a contract with a manufacturer in South Korea to make a key piece of its first power module, the upper reactor coolant system.

But it's building that module without a customer.

In a recent development, over 14 months ago, the Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA) and ENTRA1 Energy, NuScale's commercialization partner, signed a non-binding agreement for up to 6 gigawatts of new nuclear power. There's still no binding deal. NuScale's third-quarter earnings presentation says ENTRA1 is still in talks with TVA toward a definitive power purchase agreement.

Sources indicate that even a signed TVA deal wouldn't require anybody to buy NuScale's modules. NuScale's 2025 agreement with ENTRA1 names it a key supplier for ENTRA1's projects, but ENTRA1 has full discretion over whether to buy from NuScale at all.

Industry observers note that "Once we have an order, we work on an OEM agreement, we'll have further clarity on margins," CFO Ramsey Hamady noted on NuScale's third-quarter earnings call on Nov. 5.

Recent findings suggest that showing how little NuScale is selling in the meantime, its third-quarter revenue was $281,000, down from $8.2 million a year ago. The first two quarters of 2026 brought in just around $640,000 in total.

Revenue has sat near zero all year, after the company's engineering work for a planned plant in Romania finished in late 2025. And NuScale doesn't expect the Romanian project's owner to sign its next contract before it lines up financing.

Sources indicate that not for me. A stock selling under its cash can look like a textbook value stock. But NuScale's cash might shrink a lot before an order ever comes along.

Setting aside a one-time payment of around $260 million to ENTRA1, NuScale's operations have burned about $47 million of cash a quarter so far this year. And spending is climbing. Third-quarter research and development expenses grew 63% year over year to $18.0 million as NuScale worked on its module components, and its operating loss of $64.8 million was up from $57.5 million in the first quarter. Granted, management expects to recover those component costs through payments under a future equipment agreement. That agreement doesn't exist yet, either.

In a recent development, even more cash is set to leave under the ENTRA1 agreement. A binding power deal for ENTRA1 triggers a second payment from NuScale to ENTRA1, equal to 35% of the total. The first payment, which came due when ENTRA1 signed the non-binding TVA deal, was 15%.

Put simply, the next step forward would cost NuScale cash before it takes any in.

New share sales are the other way cash per share can shrink. NuScale raised around $1 billion from selling new stock earlier this year, boosting its Class A share count 29% from the end of 2025. And with a $750 million stock-sale program in place since August, it can keep adding shares over time.

Current cash per share, in short, likely isn't the cash per share shareholders wind up with.

NYSE · As of 4:00 PM ET 11/20/26.

According to recent reports, is NuScale stock a buy after losing 93% of its value? I don't think so.

Recent findings suggest that yes, a binding TVA deal could push the growth stock sharply higher for a while. But it would also trigger that second payment to ENTRA1. And until NuScale signs an equipment contract, investors can't gauge what it might make per module.

In a recent development, what would change my mind is a signed order for NuScale's modules, with enough disclosure of pricing and costs to demonstrate the margins Hamady described. I'd be willing to pay a higher valuation for the stock when that contract exists. Until then, I'm not buying shares, even at a price below NuScale's cash.
