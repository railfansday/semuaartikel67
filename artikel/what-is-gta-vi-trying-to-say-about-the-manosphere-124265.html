What’s a man allowed to like these days? Not the Super Bowl, or Christopher Nolan movies, it seems, each of them guilty of sins against masculinity. Now there is a new offender: Grand Theft Auto VI. Over one brief weekend, Rockstar’s long-in-the-works, exorbitantly expensive opus has given us a rare monocultural moment and elicited a wounded reaction from a slice of gamers steeped in the streams and podcasts of right-wing figures like Steven Crowder, Zack “Asmongold” Hoyt, and Braden Eric “Clavicular” Peters.

We asked a physical therapist, developers, and other gaming obsessives for the best tips to help you highjack garbage trucks in Leonida for as long as humanly possible.

Grand Theft Auto VI, these men assert, is emasculating because it asks players to role-play a man that respects his partner and do mundane tasks she would appreciate, like repaint their bedroom or get her a nice necklace. For others, it is less that the game is offensively woke and more that its wokeness is getting in the way of their fun. “You can’t kiss anyone other than your strong female Latina partner,” complains Crowder, his cohosts all lamenting that video games ought to be a vehicle for their every base fantasy. These men object to what they see as politics encroaching on what they think ought to be a politics-free space, “shut up and dribble” but for violent video games. But there’s a delicious irony to this because the game genuinely has a bone to pick with these unfortunate figureheads of the manosphere.

In a recent development, the manosphere, it should be noted, is a (shitty) state of mind. Less a formal designation than a collection of people who promote similar sexist, racist, and/or fascist ideals, a loose web of podcasters, streamers, and influencers who all preach a gospel of grievance in which men are victims of women withholding sex, liberals withholding power, and other men for not performing masculinity correctly. They can run the gamut from disgraced influencer Andrew Tate (right now in jail awaiting trial for a litany of alleged crimes) to the comedians and podcasters orbiting Joe Rogan’s massive audience. But they all have one thing in common: These men and the people who pay rapt attention to them are the targets of Grand Theft Auto VI’s satire.

Recent findings suggest that some background here: While yes, Grand Theft Auto VI casts players as modern Bonnie and Clyde Jason Duval and Lucia Caminos, their story is only one reason to play the game. Another is the incredibly dense setting of Leonida, a feverish and astonishingly lifelike take on Florida, chock-full of weirdos and wildlife to take in. Layered on top of that is a whole media ecosystem: a fake Instagram to scroll named Snapmatic, a news feed app, a suite of radio stations with robust programming including two talk radio stations, three TV networks with entire shows, and magazines and books you can read in excerpts.

It’s in all this background material that Grand Theft Auto VI really tears into the modern grievance grift of the manosphere. The game’s decade-plus gestation means it’s had a lot of time to amass a shooting gallery of noxious conservative male influencers that the algorithm has elevated and buried, ranging from flat-earth conspiracy theorists to MAHA-adjacent cronies. On the Weazel TV network, you can find these views as the punchline of entire Adult Swim–esque cartoons like Right Wing, about a patriotic superhero horrified to learn that a straight pride parade has been transformed by a gay superhero called the Flamer. “All these years suppressing who I was, but now I’m free!” shouts Right Wing’s colleague Great Uncle Sam after the Flamer has liberated him. “Isn’t that what America is all about?” “No,” Right Wing retorts. “America is about guns, and lots of them!”.

Scroll through Snapmatic, and you can find a clear analogue of Liver King—the content creator who promoted an “ancestral lifestyle” that involved consuming raw animal organs—posting under @paleoprimal. In one of his faux influencer videos, he advises his followers to “start beating your meat,” before dropkicking a hunk of raw flesh. “I don’t eat anything unless I’ve conquered it first.” Ashes of Glory, a sendup of historical war fiction that sits by Jason’s bed, is drowning in overly reverent prose that also happens to note that its war heroes are psychopaths. One passage: “'You’ve been charged with murdering civilians, violating every law known to God and man, calling your enemies rude names, and uncontrollable teabagging,’ mentioned the General. 'Which is exactly why we need your help.’”.

But turn the in-game radio dial to WKTT, Grand Theft Auto VI’s conservative talk radio station, and the targets sharpen. Hosts Richard Bastion (Jason Sudeikis) and Dominic “The Brick” Brizzolato (Stavros Halkias) do pastiches of Alex Jones and Nick Fuentes, spouting racist and sexist takes. Ponytails are “the hairstyle of someone who’s about to suck your dick,” per Brizzolato, who also maintains local football team the Manatees have problems to do with “culture” and “not race.” Every so often they are undercut by callers who ask how performance-enhancing drugs and hair implants don’t count as gender-affirming care. Of the many items they hawk—Flaming O’s snacks, Sprunk soda, and Patriot beer—the most absurd and on-the-nose is a special playing card set with “no queens.”.

Recent findings suggest that in its blunt re-creation of the conservative male media ecosystem, Grand Theft Auto VI plainly states what Rockstar thinks of these guys: They’re really fucking stupid. They are stupid in ways that are true to life and moronic in ways that Rockstar exaggerates, but only just so. They drive home this point by underlining the pathetic loneliness subtextual in all these real-life broadcasts, with Richard Bastion asserting that he doesn’t have friends but is building “a fraternity with a mission to save America” and that friends “let you down” and “tell the papers that your wife sleeps in a separate bed and sometimes house.”.

In a recent development, the message hits cleaner than past iterations of the game not because Grand Theft Auto VI is any less lowbrow than before—it is still awash in dick jokes, memes about shitting your pants, and South Park–esque lambasting of White America—but because this more richly detailed, lifelike version of Grand Theft Auto means the cartoonish aspects stand out all the more. What’s a joke and what is not is clearer than it has ever been.

Despite the protests from fans who say the latest iteration of Grand Theft Auto betrays the spirit of the franchise, there was always evidence that the game hated these hypermasculinity-obsessed tryhards, even before they had a manosphere to inhabit. While early games in the series did not have much in the way of plot or characters, they were tongue-in-cheek caricatures of gangster epics and the ambitions of men within them, a touch of arch Scottish humor (Rockstar’s lead Grand Theft Auto team is based in Edinburgh) keeping them from being sincere homages.

According to recent reports, nearly 20 years ago in Grand Theft Auto IV—a series turning point, where the games started to have stories with intentional thematic interests—Rockstar Games incessantly lambasted the pursuit of the hypermasculine. Its protagonist Niko Bellic served as a stoic counterpoint to many of the jacked-up weirdos of the faux New York dubbed Liberty City, arriving in the United States during the game’s opening credits with nothing but the clothes on his back and his cousin Roman’s lies about his own success in his ear. Through Niko’s journey, Grand Theft Auto IV posed the arguments that this country’s obsession with the unfettered pursuit of power and wealth makes people unwell and that culture afflicts men in ways that render them uniquely delusional. It gives his cousin Roman a fantasy of a success that will never come, causing him to submit to the intimidations of two-bit enforcers. It makes men like Brucie Kibbutz, a supplement-obsessed fitness freak, deny his own obvious interest in men in favor of a nonstop stream of homophobic jokes and crude sexual banter. It makes Niko, a soldier who thought he was escaping his criminal past, find himself immediately becoming a different kind of criminal at the behest of men who believe the world is theirs to take. And that’s just in the first few hours.

It has been revealed that grand Theft Auto IV’s skewering came at the expense of others—namely the women and queer people who are the butt of nearly every joke in the game. Seven years later, Grand Theft Auto V would course-correct by being hateful to everyone in its fictional version of Los Angeles: the acai-and-granola liberals and the asshole conservatives alike. It mires itself in the desperation of male entitlement, as three characters—the middle-aged Michael De Santa, the young striver Franklin Clinton, and Trevor Philips, the lewd loose cannon—adjust poorly to a world they cannot dominate. De Santa, arguably the protagonist of the three characters the player can inhabit, sees the world as out to emasculate him, either through his wife’s affair, his deadbeat son, or his daughter, who he must rescue from TV producers that he assumes are predatory. Michael’s world is much simpler when he’s holding a gun, and Grand Theft Auto V is eager to get one in his hands. In this, it feels like the game wants you to agree: Isn’t this more fun than dealing with all that? And it is, because Rockstar made it that way.

Yes, there are missions. But have you seen the kicks?

Industry observers note that there has always been a have-it-both-ways nature to Grand Theft Auto’s satire. Rockstar is happy to facilitate the same American male id it’s lampooning, and the staggering number of copies sold suggests that even conservative commentators out there appealing to incel logic are logging right onto their PlayStations the moment they stop posting. But despite what critics may claim to their audiences, Grand Theft Auto VI’s manosphere parodies are not a huge departure for the series, because the problems with toxic masculinity remain woefully consistent, as do the grifters who seek to profit from it. Insecurity is both a significant business and a renewable energy source.

In a recent development, what’s left out of all these complaints about Grand Theft Auto VI being a game where you embody a simp man is that you do not have to do any of the loving behavior these clowns rail against in the video game. You can ignore Lucia when playing as Jason, let your relationship grow distant and decay. You can leave the walls unpainted, never pick up flowers or groceries, not press the button to hold Lucia’s hand, keep your distance on the dance floor. The game just makes clear what the real world does not: That’s a good way to end up alone. And that’s the one thing the parodied blowhards of GTA VI’s in-game manosphere broadcast loud and clear: that they’re alone.
