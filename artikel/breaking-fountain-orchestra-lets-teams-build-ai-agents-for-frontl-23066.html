Breaking: Fountain Orchestra Lets Teams Build AI Agents for Frontline Work

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Fountain released Orchestra on November 17, 2026, giving employers a way to build AI agents for hiring and scheduling by describing the work in plain language. Drawing on data within Fountain’s software, the agents can call workers and applicants and report whether each assigned task was completed.

Orchestra is designed around recurring frontline work, with agents shared across a team rather than confined to one user. Initial uses include covering open shifts, re-engaging past applicants before an employer pays for job advertisements, preparing newly introduced hires for their first day and following up with applicants. Fountain reports that its hiring and workforce software is used by more than 500 frontline employers across logistics, retail, restaurants, healthcare and staffing.

An operator can describe a job to hand off or specify a goal, such as reducing time to hire, and ask Orchestra to propose an agent. Orchestra then presents the specifications and requires the user to validate the design before building it. Once approved, an agent can run on demand, on a schedule or in response to an event such as a dropped shift or an opened position.

According to recent reports, for each run, the agent sets a goal and a plan. A separate supervisory check compares both the result and the agent’s actual actions against that goal before the task can be marked complete. Work that falls short is flagged with reasons for human review, giving the team a record of what was finished and what still needs a decision.

That mechanism separates activity from completion: an agent must be checked against its assignment, not simply report that it ran. A shift dropped at 2 a.m. or an applicant who goes quiet over the weekend can be addressed when it happens, with unresolved items left for a person. Salim Jernite, Fountain’s chief product and technology officer, described the stakes this way: “An agent you can’t check is a risk. An agent that tells you when it’s wrong is one you can put to work.”.

According to recent reports, orchestra-built agents operate only within the permissions of the person running them, and every action is recorded in plain language. Fountain indicates agents that call workers or applicants check consent, calling hours and daily contact limits on every call. They also disclose that the caller is an AI and honor opt-outs.

Sources indicate that for hiring, those contact rules sit alongside limits on decision-making. According to Fountain, agents contacting people use objective criteria established in advance, refuse discriminatory requests and leave hiring decisions to humans. The design therefore separates automated outreach and task execution from the employment decisions that remain with managers and hiring teams.

Fountain is also using the approach internally. The tech giant reports running more than 180 agents across engineering, customer success, sales and marketing, and finance, with employees reviewing the agents’ work and making decisions. That internal deployment provides the operational background for Orchestra’s emphasis on supervisory checks and reviewable records.

It has been revealed that orchestra extends Cue, which Fountain introduced in April 2026 to execute sourcing, screening, candidate scheduling and shift-gap work. Fountain’s platform documentation describes Cue as the interface coordinating automated workflows across hiring, onboarding and shift scheduling, with built-in agents handling tasks such as applicant screening and onboarding. Fountain reports that customers have asked Cue more than 80,000 questions since launch.

Fountain’s materials describe different levels of control: Cue coordinates the platform’s established workflows, while Orchestra lets a team define an assignment or goal, validate the proposed agent and share it with colleagues. This moves the newly introduced product beyond issuing requests to an existing assistant and into configuring agents around a team’s own recurring responsibilities.

According to recent reports, orchestra is available at no charge to select customers within existing Fountain accounts. Fountain is targeting general availability for early 2027, when it intends to charge for the work each agent performs and let customers control how much work runs.

Under that plan, employers would pay for agent work while people remain responsible for hiring decisions and for reviewing tasks that do not meet their goals. The wider rollout therefore turns on whether Orchestra’s checks can demonstrate enough completed, useful work to support usage-based pricing across larger frontline teams.
