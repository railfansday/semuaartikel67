With sausage fennel stuffing, crispy green beans, and some very fall-appropriate salads, these are our essential additions to the Thanksgiving table.

The sides are objectively the best part of Thanksgiving, and it’s not even an argument. Mashed potatoes and stuffing are the reason we’re all convening at the table, right? Aaaaand the whole “gathering with loved ones” thing too, of course.

While we’ve all got our staples, the sides are also the opportunity to really have fun with the meal and showcase whatever upcoming ideas have been simmering since last year’s big meal. They’re our chance to create a menu with balance, adding a bright, crunchy salad that perfectly offsets the richness of all those buttery potatoes or offering an essential hit of cozy baked pasta with melty cheese.

These are the Thanksgiving sides recipes that Eater editors have vetted as being worth the space on our holiday tables.

Sources indicate that every year, my sister and I have a stuffing-off. Not that we’re all that competitive (we are), but she insists on having her signature mushroom and sage stuffing, and I insist on making the best stuffing. My recipe is just an old Gourmet magazine version of sausage cornbread stuffing with a bunch of fennel, but what makes it exceptional is my own riff on what is so often overlooked as filler when it comes to stuffing (or, depending on where you’re from, dressing): the bread cubes. I take the full loaves of Whole Foods’ signature cornbread, slice them into cubes, stick them in a low oven (around 200 degrees) for a few hours, and eureka! It’s a stuffing that delivers again and again.

Why are the store-bought loaves the key? I’m not quite sure. My hunch is that they’re just dense enough, un-fresh enough, and mass-produced enough to dry out easily, and the store’s recipe strikes some unicorn balance of flavor that compliments the fennel without making the dish overwhelmingly sweet. — Lesley Suter, brand director.

Its varying textures, ample acidity, and subtle sweetness are a welcome addition to any spread of holiday sides. Industry observers note that nobody says they crave a salad at Thanksgiving, but I promise you they do, especially once the heaviness of everything else starts to set in. At the very least, I crave a salad at Thanksgiving, and the kind of salad I desire in an instance like this is something crunchy and bright and which tastes nothing like anything else on the table. Thus, some combination of citrus and fennel is generally my go-to. The citrus lends a brightness that pairs well with the cranberry sauce that’s inevitably on my plate, and I like the licorice sweetness of fennel amid the savoriness that dominates the spread; plus, I think it goes well with the celery that’s in other dishes.

While more complex takes on this kind of salad exist and sound delicious, I find the most pared-down versions easier to slot into my day of cooking. This salad is usually my very last task before we sit down to eat. Sometimes, I don’t even announce that it’s happening, so it seems like the “just threw this together,” afterthought touch that surprises you with how essential it ends up being. — Bettina Makalintal, senior reporter.

Tauba Auerbach, Salad for President by Julia Sherman.

Tauba Auerbach’s Brussels sprouts salad recipe in Julia Sherman’s book Salad for President is a pleasing salad with lots of crunchy textures and punchy flavors that’s become a go-to of mine for the holidays. Thinly shaved Brussels sprouts serve as the base of the salad, while lemon-marinated, toasted almonds add bite to the equation. The dressing adds tartness with sherry and cider vinegars, which is a nice juxtaposition against the season-sweet apple pieces. Don’t skip the pickled mustard seeds if you have the time, as they add another dimension of flavor. — Dianne de Guzman, regional editor, Northern California/Pacific Northwest.

This is less of an elaborate recipe, and more of a plea: Please don’t bring steamed green beans to Thanksgiving dinner (or any holiday, for that matter). They’re hardly offensive, but I’ve always found them to taste a bit ghostly and one-note. Instead, I like this simple but impactful recipe of garlicky green beans. High-heat oil, garlic, and ice cubes at-the-ready will give you crispy green beans with a welcome, garlicky char to liven up the usual parade of sweet potatoes and all the beige classics. — Francky Knapp, staff writer.

I have become the Sweet Potato Bringer at our family’s large, outdoor potluck Thanksgiving, mostly because I am the only one who must have them (or it’s not Thanksgiving, obviously). Every year, I bring a sweet potato casserole with essentially the same elements: mashed sweet potatoes — slow-roasting them in the oven lends a deeper flavor — brightened with things like orange juice or cinnamon applesauce; some sort of pecan-y crumbly brown sugar topping; and blistered marshmallows. It’s all very satisfying but can become somewhat formulaic, which is why, a couple years ago, I kicked it up a notch with Epicurious’s s’moresy take on the classic: In this recipe, the sweet potato mixture gets added earthiness from maple syrup and unsweetened cocoa powder, while the pecan topping’s punch-up is the addition of crushed graham crackers (you can buy them gluten-free to be more inclusive). It’s not a dramatically different flavor from the original, meaning the eternal crowd-pleaser lives on. — Nicole Fellah, dining editorial manager.

Mitchell Davis, Cook and Eat Better.

If I am having a holiday party with a spread, The Cheese Thing will be on the table and will be decimated quickly — my repeat guests have come to expect and crave it. The Cheese Thing defies explanation; what can be mentioned is that it’s very much a personal blog recipe, its name coined by Mitchell Davis and then surfaced more broadly on the internet by Bon Appétit back in 2018. On its surface, it just sounds like a sort of wonky baked ziti. It is the ultimate comfort recipe; cubes of cheese melt into the crevices of penne pasta, melding with a super-simple sweet and buttery tomato sauce. I absolutely shower it in salt, cracked pepper, and fluffy Pecorino, and sometimes bake extra cheese onto the top. I’d consider it a side because it lacks main-course energy and is ideal for a potluck or buffet, but there’s no doubt that it could serve as an entree on a chilly night for two, four, or however many people fit on your couch. — Hilary Pollack, deputy editor.
