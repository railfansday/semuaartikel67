Breaking: Why Men Ghost Their Therapists

🌐 Source: Psychologytoday • 📅 17 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 5 min read (1085 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Posted November 17, 2026 | Reviewed by Lybi Ma.

Counseling men is not like counseling women. If you try to counsel a man like a woman, it can rapidly blow up—not with a bang, but a whimper. That male client may likely drop out. This is not to say that all men or all women are alike. It is to say that male gender-role socialization can shape what men need from counseling and how they engage with it.

Recent findings suggest that this mismatch is understandable—women are the majority. More counselors, counseling clients, and counselor educators are women. Women made up 74.5 percent of the Bureau of Labor Statistics category “counselors, all other” in 2025 (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2026). Women made up 80.2 percent of students and 68.6 percent of full-time faculty in Council for Accreditation of Counseling and Related Educational Programs-accredited counseling programs in 2024 (2025). Women were also more likely than men to receive counseling or therapy in 2024—16.9 percent compared with 10.9 percent (Briones and Villarroel, 2026).

Recent findings suggest that counseling is a female-dominated field, which is interesting because it was not always this way. Among faculty members in accredited counseling programs who earned their degrees in the 1960s and 1970s, only 26.7 percent were women. Five decades later, 63 percent were women (Baggerly and colleagues, 2017).

Men desperately need mental health support, but they are less likely than women to access counseling services (Briones and Villarroel, 2026). Yet, men die by suicide at nearly four times the rate of women (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2026). Men also have higher drug-overdose death rates. In a federal analysis of deaths involving fentanyl, methamphetamine, cocaine, heroin, and oxycodone, the death rate was higher for men for every drug examined (Spencer and colleagues, 2023).

Sources indicate that when men do attend counseling, many drop out. In a survey of 1,907 Australian men who had attended therapy, 44.8 percent reported dropping out. Among the men classified as dropouts, 26.6 percent attended only once and did not return. The most common reason was a lack of connection with the therapist (Seidler and colleagues, 2021).

Men need counseling, but they do not stay; a reason may be engagement. Men do not always engage with counseling when they enter services. This may happen because counselors assume male clients think, behave, and have treatment needs like female clients. But they do not always have the same needs.

Counselors get it wrong. They expect men to disclose emotionally vulnerable information at the outset of counseling. But vulnerable emotional disclosure can be difficult for men. Male gender-role socialization may not teach some men how to identify and express emotions, and it may associate vulnerable emotional disclosure with weakness. Specific messages associated with traditional masculinity, such as “no complaining,” “boys don’t cry,” and “pull yourself up by your bootstraps,” send the message to boys, who then become men, that they need to shut down their feelings, not share or open up about their struggles, and avoid seeking help (Levant and Parent, 2019; Seidler and colleagues, 2016).

For some men, the counseling office is unfamiliar and foreign. They feel like a fish out of water. If a counselor expects men to be vulnerable and disclose how they feel, the counselor may believe they are getting to the real matter. Yet the male client may experience it as being pushed into risky waters before rapport and safety have been built.

Counselors need to tread lightly. They should start the relationship in the safe zone, often at a more intellectual or practical level, until the man feels more comfortable and safety has been established. Counselors can ask what is happening, what needs to change, what the man has already tried, and what he wants from counseling.

This does not mean avoiding emotions or allowing the client to remain emotionally distant forever. It is a starting point. The focus is on pacing the work we do with men, where we progressively build up to emotional disclosure. A review of strategies for engaging men found support for collaborative, transparent, action-oriented, and goal-focused approaches. This also accounts for masculine socialization (Seidler and colleagues, 2018). Once the man feels that the counselor understands him and that the relationship is safe, they can move together into deeper, riskier waters.

Men need counseling. But getting men into counseling is only the first step. Counselors can also build strong connections that encourage clients to stay.
