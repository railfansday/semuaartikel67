Latest Update: Hackers swapped a hosted DeepSeek model for 53 hours and stripped away its safety protections

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that prime Intellect disclosed an unauthorized DeepSeek model swap on Nov. 19. The host noted a modified copy of DeepSeek V4.1 Flash served customer requests on one deployment for 53 hours. The substitute matched the original on two capability tests, the host said. On a safety test called HarmBench, refusals fell from 198 of 240 prompts to zero.

Prime Intellect said the affected deployment ran the substitute from 01:24 UTC on Nov. 8 until 06:35 UTC on Nov. 10, a window of 53 hours and 11 minutes. The firm did not say how many requests went to that deployment during the swap.

Sources indicate that the replacement used publicly available weights that Prime Intellect said had been modified to reduce refusals. Weights are the numerical settings that shape what a model generates. The swap took place behind the DeepSeek V4.1 Flash endpoint. Prime Intellect said the deployment kept its chat template and serving settings, along with the tokenizer that breaks text into pieces the model processes.

Industry observers note that prime Intellect reported unchanged results on GPQA Diamond and τ-bench when it tested the modified weights. GPQA Diamond tests advanced science questions, while τ-bench tests whether agents can use tools and follow rules in simulated customer interactions.

The safety tests exposed a difference. HarmBench was developed to evaluate how models respond to attempts to elicit harmful outputs. Prime Intellect also recorded a drop from 98 refusals out of 100 prompts to one on JailbreakBench, and from 304 out of 313 to two on StrongREJECT. The counts are from the manufacturer’s benchmark tests. The advisory gives no refusal count for customer requests.

Prime Intellect compared the original DeepSeek V4.1 Flash with the copy that served requests on one deployment for 53 hours.

A comparison limited to the two capability tests would have missed the fall in refusals.

Bars show reported refusals on a common count scale. Prime Intellect reported baseline totals of 240 HarmBench prompts, 100 JailbreakBench prompts and 313 StrongREJECT prompts. It described both capability results as unchanged.

Prime Intellect stated it contacted all affected customers and advised those who relied on the model to refuse certain requests to review the outputs they received during the affected window. An answer from the substitute could have passed into an application that was counting on a refusal.

Sources indicate that the tech giant mentioned it examined the disks and memory of the machines involved, checked recovered versions of the intruder’s proxy and reviewed records of their activity. It reported finding no evidence that customer prompts or responses were recorded or left the serving cluster. Prime Intellect also said no other models on its platform were modified and no keys or customer credentials were affected. Its investigation remains open.
