In a recent development, recent advancements in AI have cast a cloud over college students studying software engineering. Jobs seem to be disappearing every day, while coursework can be done by chatbots.

Sources indicate that but at CSEED’s Buildspace Demo Day last week, 60 undergraduates in the University of Washington student club demonstrated that AI hasn’t dampened their drive to create. If anything, it’s allowing them to build things they wouldn’t have been able to before.

In a recent development, “We’re seeing projects that normal college students would not be expected to make,” club co-president Leonard Paya remarked. “As AI becomes more advanced, you can see how fast they scale up their knowledge and execute on things that are truly amazing.”.

In a recent development, CSEED, founded a few years ago and already one of UW’s largest clubs, supports aspiring software designers and engineers. It’s funded by the Paul G. Allen School of Computer Science & Engineering, as well as venture capital firms such as AI House, Madrona and Pack Ventures. Its members have gone on to join startup incubators including Y Combinator, Z Fellows and Founders Inc.

Recent findings suggest that twice a year, CSEED accepts applications for its Buildspace cohort, a program for students with hardware, software or design pitches. Over six weeks, the cohort meets up for build sessions, and they also receive mentorship, supplies and AI credits. Demo Day on Nov. 17 at the UW’s Gates Center marked the conclusion of the sixth Buildspace cohort, with 30 inventions presented to 150 audience members.

“We’ve brought together curious and ambitious students from all backgrounds,” vice president Amy Lu stated. “CSEED gives them the community to turn their ideas into reality.”.

One of the most technically advanced ideas presented was DubDash, a fully autonomous robot for delivering food orders from the dining hall to dorms.

“The best part of this is that these people were all freshmen who hadn’t yet taken robotics classes,” Paya said. “Who would have thought that four freshmen could make a fully fledged robot in six weeks?”.

In a recent development, according to junior and Buildspace co-director Talal Kheiry, that’s a testament to AI. Buildspace members receive AI credits, which Kheiry stated have changed everything.

“AI helps guide them through the gritty parts of the project, so they can just focus on the creative parts without worrying about the technical stuff,” Kheiry remarked.

According to recent reports, other inventions offered a glimpse into what’s on students’ minds these days. From notification-silencing focus apps to social connectors and mental health education platforms, nearly half of them had to do with curbing the effects of social media.

According to recent reports, “Social media isn’t social anymore,” Junaid Akhtar told the audience as he and his three team members presented. “It’s just a place to see media. Our goal is to make social media bring people together again.”.

According to recent reports, his team’s presentation was the audience’s favorite: a satirical pitch for a platform they call FeedPicks, which creates live ranked prediction markets of friends’ social media feeds. Their demo won an award for “best energy” at the end of the night.

In a recent development, other inventions sought to get consumers off their screens altogether. Joe Chae, a freshman at the Allen School, presented Pidgin, a Tinder-style app that helps users who want to practice each other’s languages to meet up in real life.

It has been revealed that “I came to UW hoping to meet some Korean international students here and learn to speak Korean with my relatives, but I didn’t meet anyone,” Chae stated. “So I thought, what if I made an app that could help me find someone who speaks Korean natively and wants to learn English?”.

Also focused on the real world was Sasha Bogatirev’s Hadden Cybernetics. Bogatirev built a wearable gaming console that takes in information from a user’s environment and turns it into a game.

“The person has to pay attention to their surroundings,” Bogatirev mentioned. “The idea is to prompt the imagination as much as possible for someone to experience another world.”.

His prototype used the ESP32-S3 microcontroller to render the visuals onto wearable glasses. An nRF chip on the wristband takes in scripted gestures, which he trained to turn a player’s gestures into shields, lightning strikes and fireballs. Next, he plans to add haptics using hardware supplied by CSEED.

Industry observers note that andrew Edwards also built a video game accessory: cStar, which is an open source, low-cost console that can play a game on any TV screen by loading it onto an SD card and popping it into a 3D-printed box. Inside that box is a microcontroller like you’d find inside a stopwatch or a microwave, unlike the highly complicated circuitry used by an Xbox or PlayStation.

It has been revealed that “We were able to squeeze HDMI video out of the microcontroller, which allows us to show 2D and 3D graphics on a home television set,” Edwards stated. “Since the games are based on standard SD cards, a curious programmer can just write software on the computer, stick it onto an SD card, put it in the console and you’re ready to play.”.

According to recent reports, this cohort was their largest and most competitive yet. Next up, under the guidance of club advisor and Allen School professor Kurtis Heimerl, CSEED is looking to expand. They’ve started hosting build sessions every Saturday that are open to non-students.

Industry observers note that to do that, they’ll need to fundraise. According to CSEED co-president Benya Vahdat, they’ve already used up their Allen School allotment for the year, and they’re now in the process of raising money from donors. They recently incorporated as CSEED Foundation and received nonprofit status in Washington state.

Industry observers note that “We want to grow this mentality of building, and maybe expand across campuses not just in Washington but across the country,” Vahdat said. “Maybe even globally.”.
