Latest Update: Which justices favor the Trump administration the most (and least) on the emergency docket?

Industry observers note that when the Supreme Court rejected President Donald Trump’s attempt to remove Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook in June, the justices divided in a manner that would have been difficult to predict from some perceptions of the court. Chief Justice John Roberts and Justice Brett Kavanaugh joined Justices Sonia Sotomayor, Elena Kagan, and Ketanji Brown Jackson to leave Cook in office while the case proceeded. Justices Clarence Thomas, Samuel Alito, Neil Gorsuch, and Amy Coney Barrett dissented. The administration lost, but the court’s disagreement did not reflect the familiar ideological split.

Industry observers note that four months later, the administration lost another emergency application, this time in a dispute over medical care for transgender federal prisoners. In Trump v. Kingdom, Thomas and Alito indicated that they would have granted the requested stay, but no other justice publicly held that position. Unlike Trump v. Cook, which produced a signed opinion after oral argument, the Kingdom order disclosed nothing about the reasoning or votes behind the court’s denial.

The contrast points to a recurring theme of the Supreme Court’s emergency decisions: Although the conservative justices are often lumped together as “pro-Trump,” their rulings can depend on whether the dispute concerns immigration, presidential removal authority, federal spending, or the scope of emergency relief. And whereas some justices have supported federal executive positions at broadly similar rates across presidencies, others appear far more likely to do so depending on whether the president is a Republican or Democrat.

Recent findings suggest that the prior article in this series examined how frequently the Trump and Biden administrations prevailed. In turning to the individual justices, I reviewed federal executive-action decisions from 2017 through November 12, 2026, consolidated into 106 distinct substantive decisions. The three administrations account for 32, 36, and 38 decisions, respectively.

In a recent development, there is a very important qualification throughout: the court often publishes emergency orders without identifying each justice’s vote. The analysis thus assigned a justice to the court’s majority ruling when they were silent, absent an express position or other evidence indicating otherwise.

So what can we say about the different justices’ support for the Trump administration on the emergency docket?

Different presidents, different justices.

In a recent development, the most consistent record belongs to Roberts. He aligned with federal executive positions in 23 of 31 eligible decisions during Trump’s first administration, 26 of 36 under Biden, and 28 of 38 during Trump’s second administration. Those figures amount to 74%, 72%, and 74%. The disputes changed markedly, but Roberts’ aggregate willingness to leave the federal government’s position intact – or grant it relief – barely moved. In other words, Roberts has typically supported executive power on the emergency docket regardless of who is president.

The next most consistent justice, Barrett (though with a much lower sample size), aligned with Biden in 69% of her eligible decisions and Trump II in 78%. Third was Kavanaugh, with rates of 78%, 67%, and 79%. Then came Gorsuch, with rates of 83%, 54%, and 84%.

According to recent reports, thomas and Alito present a very different picture. Thomas supported the executive in 83% of eligible Trump I decisions, 39% under Biden, and 92% under Trump II. Alito’s corresponding figures were 83%, 47%, and 92%.

The liberal justices show the opposite movement, although their individual records differ. Kagan supported executive positions in 43% of eligible Trump I decisions, 94% under Biden, and 24% under Trump II. Sotomayor’s rates were 27%, 94%, and 16%. Jackson, who joined the court during Biden’s presidency, supported its executive position in 21 of 24 eligible decisions (88%), compared with three of 38 (8%) under Trump II.

The figure above makes these distinctions particularly clear. Roberts remains between 72% and 74% across the three periods, while Thomas’ support moves from 83% under Trump I to 39% under Biden and 92% under Trump II. The middle group of Barrett, Kavanaugh, and Gorsuch is more varied, but each supported Biden’s executive position more frequently than a simple Republican-versus-Democratic framing might suggest.

These numbers are telling, but they do not establish the reasons for why the justices decided as they did. For example, a president defending an environmental rule presents a very different legal issue from one seeking permission to remove an independent agency official. To determine how much of the observed variation is associated with presidential identity, the next step is to examine the kinds of cases that produced it.

The concerns that unite and divide the justices.

It has been revealed that immigration provides a useful point of comparison because it generated substantial emergency litigation under all three administrations. Roberts supported the executive in 10 of 11 immigration decisions under Trump I, three of six under Biden, and 11 of 13 under Trump II. Thomas supported the government in 10 of 10 eligible Trump I immigration decisions, two of six under Biden, and 12 of 13 under Trump II. Kagan’s comparable figures were six of eleven, six of six, and five of 13.

It has been revealed that this is revealing. Roberts and Thomas both supported Trump’s immigration positions at high rates but not as much when Biden defended his administration’s policies. Kagan and Sotomayor supported Biden in all six coded immigration decisions, then opposed most of Trump II positions. But there is, of course, a key caveat: This category covers different immigration rules, as opposed to simple support for one president over another.

Public-health and related health litigation under Biden revealed a sharper division within the conservative majority. In 10 eligible health-coded decisions, Roberts and Kavanaugh each supported the federal executive in seven, compared with one each for Thomas and Alito. Barrett supported it in five; Gorsuch in two. Kagan and Sotomayor each supported the government in nine such cases. (Jackson’s two favorable positions came from only three eligible decisions in this category, making her figure much less informative.).

The court’s January 2022 vaccination decisions supply a concrete example. Roberts and Kavanaugh joined the liberals in allowing the administration to enforce a vaccination requirement for workers at healthcare facilities receiving Medicare or Medicaid funding, while Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, and Barrett dissented. On the same day, Roberts and Kavanaugh joined the conservatives in blocking OSHA’s broader vaccination-or-testing standard. To Roberts and Kavanaugh, health litigation was clearly treated concern-by-issue.

Let’s move on to personnel decisions, or the president’s control over the executive branch. In the nine Trump II personnel and removal decisions, Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, and Barrett supported the executive in eight, while Roberts and Kavanaugh did so in seven. In four foreign-affairs and national-security decisions, Thomas, Alito, and Gorsuch aligned with the government in all of these, compared with two for Roberts and Barrett.

Subject matter therefore helps explain how things break down. The same conservative justice may support emergency intervention in a dispute over immigration enforcement while resisting it in a case involving agency regulation. The legal authority being asserted, the posture of the case, and the requested remedy all shape the decision.

The coalitions inside the conservative majority.

So which justices most frequently aligned? At the front of the pack are Thomas and Alito, who agreed in all 38 coded Trump II decisions. Their alignment is particularly visible when the administration lost. In seven events, both of their positions favored more relief for the administration than the majority granted or would otherwise provide.

Other pairings are almost as cohesive. Roberts and Barrett agreed in 35 of 36 Trump II decisions (97%). Roberts and Kavanaugh agreed in 36 of 38 of these (95%), as did Gorsuch and Kavanaugh. The next figure displays those pairings alongside the liberal and cross-ideological relationships. The high agreement rates show how often the conservatives arrive at the same apparent disposition, but the exceptions reveal competing views about the scope of emergency judicial intervention.

The foreign-assistance dispute in Department of State v. AIDS Vaccine Advocacy Coalition, for instance, separated Roberts and Barrett from Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, and Kavanaugh. The four dissenters would have granted the administration relief from an order requiring payment for foreign-aid work already completed. Roberts and Barrett did not join that position. Their disagreement concerned what a district court could require of the government while the underlying funding litigation continued.

It has been revealed that A different alignment emerged in NIH v. American Public Health Association. There, the court granted partial relief in litigation over terminated research grants. Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, and Kavanaugh would have granted the administration’s request in full, while Roberts joined Sotomayor, Kagan, and Jackson in seeking to deny it altogether. Barrett supported an intermediate outcome.

In Cook, the division shifted again. Roberts and Kavanaugh joined the liberals in rejecting the administration’s attempt to immediately remove a Federal Reserve governor, while Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, and Barrett dissented. In Trump v. Illinois, concerning the Trump administration’s deployment of National Guard forces, Thomas, Alito, and Gorsuch disagreed with the court’s denial of relief to Trump, while Kavanaugh concurred in the judgment on narrower grounds.

The evidence points toward distinctive approaches to removal authority, spending, the judicial supervision of agencies, and emergency remedies. Justices who agree in one field can diverge in another, and partial orders sometimes put a justice between two groups seeking opposing forms of relief.

The liberal justices and the unusual alliances across ideological lines.

The three liberal justices also exhibited some meaningful disagreements. Under Trump II, Jackson aligned with the executive in only three of 38 emergency decisions (8%), compared with six for Sotomayor (16%) and nine for Kagan (24%). Kagan and Sotomayor agreed in 35 of 38 such decisions; Kagan and Jackson in 32. The differences are not as dramatic as those dividing the conservatives, but they complicate the assumption that the liberal trio behaves as a single bloc in every emergency dispute.

Their publicly registered dissents differ as well. Jackson expressly opposed a Trump II executive position in 26 decisions, Sotomayor in 23, and Kagan in 20. In FCC v. Brown, for example, Sotomayor and Jackson publicly indicated that they would deny the agency’s stay application, while Kagan registered no public disagreement.

Trump’s second term has produced more than one identifiable cross-ideological alignment. The five-justice majority in Cook included Roberts, Kavanaugh, and the three liberals, while the NIH grants dispute placed Roberts and the liberals together against complete relief. Barrett also joined certain portions of Sotomayor’s dissent in Trump v. J.G.G., a dispute involving the Alien Enemies Act, although her partial join should not necessarily be counted as agreement with the liberals on the case.

These examples again highlight why the distinction between shared results and shared legal reasoning is important. A justice can favor the same outcome as an ideological opponent while arriving there for entirely different reasons (we see this with concurrences in merits cases all the time). Separate opinions can also reveal agreement on a particular procedural or statutory question even when the justices disagree on the ultimate relief. At the same time, the emergency docket makes these relationships especially difficult to measure comprehensively because many orders contain no individual vote information at all.

My analysis of the emergency docket indicates a chief most reluctant to favor one administration over another, with Barrett and Kavanaugh following closely behind; Gorsuch acting more favorably towards the Trump administration, although not overwhelmingly so; Thomas and Alito disproportionately favoring Trump over Biden; and the three liberal justices acting as a near mirror image of Thomas and Alito – although to varied degrees.

We thus see a conservative majority whose members separate over particular exercises of federal power, a liberal bloc with distinguishable voting and dissent records, and occasional alliances that cross ideological lines.
