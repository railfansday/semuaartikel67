I’ve been cooking dinner for myself (and/or my family) nearly every night for the past 27 years. Suffice it to say I've learned some kitchen tips and tricks during those decades—especially about what is and isn't worth taking up valuable counter space.

Some of my favorite kitchen gadgets may not be the most flashy (a measuring cup, a mesh cloth, a cast-iron pan). Instead, they're the kinds of things that really matter when you cook a lot, whether in service of keeping things clean and organized, or sharp, or preserving your hard work by freezing leftovers for a future busy night.

I've also thrown in a couple of fun nice-to-haves, including a kit for making Giada De Laurentiis' viral (and highly recommended!) sheet-pan lasagna and a 500-piece puzzle that may send you on a soup-making side quest.

Sources indicate that cast iron cookware is unparalleled for its heat retention, durability, and nonstick nature, so long as you keep it well seasoned. But it's also heavy and often difficult to maneuver. You could always use lightweight carbon steel, made with an iron-carbon alloy, but it doesn't really act like cast iron in that it's highly variable in terms of heat. Field's heirloom-quality pans are modeled on smooth vintage designs and are just as lightweight as stainless steel, yet retain all the benefits of cast iron. WIRED operations manager Scott Gilberston likes them a lot, and so do I.

It has been revealed that even though I cook every day, I'm terrible at sharpening knives. I never feel like I'm doing it correctly on the manual honing rods or pull-through sharpeners, but with this electric model, I found success. There's still a learning curve, but once you get the hang of it, you'll be able to sharpen a whole block's worth of knives in about half an hour. I was even able to revive a chef's knife that hadn't been sharpened in nearly 20 years, sitting dull and forgotten at the back of a drawer.

After I lived in Mexico for a year, I became unable to eat store-bought tortillas. Sure, they're easy, with no preparation required beyond warming them. But they're also limp, flavorless, sad little excuses for food. Alternatively, homemade tortillas are things of beauty and exemplify what I love about cooking: using simple, high-quality ingredients to produce something far better than you would expect. Fortunately, making your own tortillas is incredibly easy—well, if you have a tortilla press, and the best I've used is this one from Masienda, which nicely offers the ability to press larger tortillas.

If your foodie friend likes Mexican food, this gift will expand their culinary horizons. The combo pack provides everything they need to get started on making their own tortillas: masa and a press. The key to great homemade tortillas is great masa harina, and Masienda's flour is my favorite (well, outside of Mexico anyway). —Scott Gilbertson.

Industry observers note that instant-read thermometers are something every cook should have. It might be cool to saunter over to the grill and gently prod your steak with a finger and pronounce it medium well, but few of us can reliably do that. The Thermapen One will tell you the exact internal temperature in one second. No need for elaborate guesswork.

According to recent reports, cheap instant-read thermometers litter Amazon, but we like the more expensive Thermapen One for its reliability (mine is five years old and still going strong) and speed. Yes, speed really does matter, because the longer the oven is open or the grill lid is off, the more it cools and the more unevenly you end up cooking. Get them the best thermometer, and you'll guarantee they'll temp your hanger steak exactly the way you like it. —Scott Gilbertson.

Many cooks put a lot of time and effort into making not only meals but things that make the meals—stocks, broths, sauces, chopped vegetables, beans. I have been obsessed with Souper Cubes for years, as they allow me to freeze my hard work for later, as well as preserve about-to-go-bad store-bought sauces, salsas, and even tomato paste in specific increments like half-cups, tablespoons, or even one- or two-cup blocks. I then like to vacuum seal them, but you can also just put the cubes in a zip-lock bag. There are cheaper versions of these out there, but Souper Cubes have steel wire inside so they won't crush, sag, or crumple, plus their lids let you stack them in the freezer.
