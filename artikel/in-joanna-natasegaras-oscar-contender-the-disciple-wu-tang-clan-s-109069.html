The hip-hop legends will be in Detroit to headline the halftime show for the NFL game between the host Lions and the Chicago Bears (U2 will do the halftime honors for the Thanksgiving Eve game in LA between the Green Bay Packers and the Los Angeles Rams, airing on Netflix).

Wu-Tang, founded in Staten Island, NY, has long been considered one of the most influential groups in music history, with legions of fans worldwide and even their own acolytes known as the Killa Bees, a collective of rappers, producers, singers and others affiliated with the group. Among the most notable Bees: Cilvaringz, aka Tarik Azzougarh, a Dutch Moroccan Wu-Tang superfan turned artist whose connection to the Clan goes back to the late 1990s.

The remarkable story of how Cilvaringz became associated with Wu-Tang and then played a critical role in producing the astonishing one-copy only album Once Upon a Time in Shaolin is told in the documentary The Disciple: A Wu-Tang Fable, now streaming on Netflix. Watch on Deadline.

The Oscar-contending film is directed by Joanna Natasegara, the Academy Award-winning producer of The White Helmets, The Edge of Democracy, Virunga and other acclaimed documentaries. The Disciple premiered at the Sundance Film Festival in January and screened in September at the Telluride Film Festival before debuting on Netflix.

In a recent development, DEADLINE: Have you always been into Wu-Tang? When did you learn about the story of Tarik Azzougarh?

Joanna Natasegara: I grew up in the ’90s. It’s exactly my era of music… Wu-Tang was just something me and my friends were very much into. We would go to clubs and listen to hip hop music, but it was something I just didn’t really think about in the scope of my work, clearly for decades, actually. And so, the story [of Tarik/Cilvaringz] itself I hadn’t heard of despite it being something that had gone around the world.

I was on a holiday in Morocco with my family and a script writer friend of mine came for lunch and she remarked, “My brother [Tarik] is having some trouble with his life story and his life rights.” And I was like, “Okay.” I knew he was Moroccan, but I thought [his story] would be something that pertained to North Africa, not to Staten Island. And she said, “Do you know the Wu-Tang?” I was like, “Yeah. I don’t really know where this is going.” [She said], “He’s a Killer Bee.” And I was like, “How is that possible?” And so, she told me a little part of the story, but she said, “It’s so convoluted, complicated, twisty, wonderful. Can I bring him to lunch tomorrow?”.

So, he came to lunch and honestly, over a three-hour lunch, he told me his whole life story. And not only did I think it was a great story, it just really resonated. We’re exactly the same age. He was obviously a migrant growing up in a tiny town in Europe, and a lot of that resonated with me and he was very ambitious to make it [in the music business], and I thought that was really inspiring.

In a recent development, DEADLINE: It’s a very complex story to tell as your feature directorial debut. About halfway into The Disciple, you really start to pivot to the sale of the kind of one-off album, Once Upon a Time in Shaolin. But as an audience we really need to understand how it all came together. We need to understand Cilvaringz, Tarik, and that’s complicated. So, this is not an easy piece of storytelling to do.

JN: No, no, no, it wasn’t… I wanted to build the film as a kind of homage to a Wu-Tang album. There’s a lot of Easter eggs in the film — if you’re a Clan fan, you can see them. But I also wanted the film to work for somebody that didn’t know anything about the Wu-Tang. So that’s a tricky balance to be able to tell a story that’s not too complex, but also dense enough for the mega fans, and there are millions of them.

In a recent development, I was well held by two extremely experienced editors, Chloe Lambourne who cut for For Sama and Chris Dickens who cut for Slumdog Millionaire. And that was a lot of fun.

DEADLINE: The film does work for people who are not steeped in either hip hop or Wu-Tang or martial arts movies or “pharma bro” Martin Shkreli [who bought the one-copy only album] or other elements of the story.

According to recent reports, JN: It’s a coming-of-age story and a story about ambition because I think that’s what Tarik took from RZA, what RZA took from Shifu [Shaolin monk Shi Yan Ming] and what audiences are seeming to take from Tarik. So, the album in a way is kind of a McGuffin. Obviously, some people are very attracted to know about the machinations of the album, but I’m really interested in the mentorship aspect of the film.

Recent findings suggest that DEADLINE: There’s the line that RZA says: “Persistence overcomes resistance,” and it’s wonderful. That motto guided Tarik as a protégé of RZA and Wu-Tang, but it could also apply to you as a filmmaker.

According to recent reports, JN: It’s great, isn’t it? We had t-shirts made.

In a recent development, DEADLINE: Towards the end of the film, you ask Tarik specifically whether producing Once Upon a Time in Shaolin was a career highlight or if it destroyed his career. And his answer is quite interesting, that he doesn’t necessarily think of himself as having a career.

JN: I think he’s quite honest and reflective in the piece. I thank him for that trust because that’s notable, isn’t it, in documentary? And I think he is reflective about that process, but he’s also resilient and knew what that meant to do that [album]. I think they both did, him and RZA, but the discussion [it ignited] was the point [about the value attached to a work of music] and that has worked. People are seeing that stunt and the value of music because of it. And I think it’s inspired many other people to do similar things.

According to recent reports, the album was a perfect protest. It was a perfect way to say, why is this piece of music worth less than something that is put in an extraordinary museum? So, I think it was a really clever protest piece, and to that extent it’s also worked. We’re talking about it years later. So, it’s a conversation that’s endured and I think is really relevant not only to music now, but also to film or any piece of creative, especially as we move more and more into the dangers of AI. How much less will everything be valued, in fact?

It has been revealed that note: After Martin Shkreli’s conviction for fraud, the U.S. government seized his assets, including the one copy of Once Upon a Time in Shaolin encased in its custom-made box. Per a legal agreement, the album will not be available for commercial release until the year 2103. In the interim, it can only be played at limited special events.

DEADLINE: Probably the first question you get asked is, have you listened to the album?

In a recent development, JN: We had a beautiful listening session at Sundance, and then we had another one at Telluride. I haven’t heard the whole thing. And it’s probably the most frustrating thing about this film… I can’t show you how good the album is; that’s going to be a question for people, but it really is good… I still think all of us involved in the film or the album go back and debate, do we hope it’s released? Do we not? Is it better than it isn’t released? But I would really like people to be able to hear it because it’s good.
