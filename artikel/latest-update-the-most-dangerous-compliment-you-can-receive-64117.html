According to recent reports, we have probably all met someone along the way who impressed and attracted us with their charm, charisma, and positive attention. Yet at times, they may have made us feel unsettled with their praise. Their flattery may have been enjoyable at the beginning, but over time, some of the compliments may have gone over the top and made us uncomfortable. From our “stunning” appearance to our “brilliant” intellect, the acclaim was unmerited and even arguably untrue. What is their motivation? Research investigates this question.

In the course of my almost 30 years as a prosecutor, I have observed one of the most seductive techniques used by sexual predators and human traffickers to be targeting young women who are not used to receiving positive attention. Having grown up in the shadow of much prettier peers and classmates, they are uniquely vulnerable to the unmerited compliments and advances of handsome (usually much older) men. Yet this dynamic is not limited to criminal perpetrators; it is used in everyday life by people seeking to manipulate and exploit people and resources.

In a recent development, benjamin A. Rogers et al. (2023) discussed the impact of praise in a study entitled, “Too Naïve to Lead: When Leaders Fall for Flattery.”[1] They identify flattery as one of the “oldest and most commonly used” tactics of impression management, one that frequently benefits the flatterer. Yet flattery also impacts the recipient, who may reward the praise, inadvertently compromising their perception. Managers are apparently not immune from this dynamic, as Rogers et al. note that when they reward flatterers, they risk being viewed as naïve. And “falling for flattery” can compromise impressions of both leaders and the organizations they represent.

Yet if you know what to look for, you can detect it.

In a recent development, as a practical matter, false compliments often target idealized traits you would love to have but know you do not. A false flatterer will comment on your gorgeous figure or uncanny knack for solving complex problems—with very little or no evidence to support their compliments. In such instances, motivation is inherently suspect. “What do you want?” you might ask jokingly, when, actually, that is a very good question. Because many compliments are paired with an “ask,” requesting us to do something for the praise-giver, which puts their motivation under greater scrutiny. This is especially suspect when your denial of the request is met with hostility or expressions of disappointment.

Industry observers note that the more challenging example is when you receive compliments in borderline areas where you may indeed possess some degree of the appearance or aptitude alleged. Here, we wonder whether our vocal admirer is merely viewing us with positive bias through rose-colored glasses. Yet if that is the case, they are unlikely to include a request, and certainly not become hostile or rude when we refuse.

Industry observers note that another red flag is mixing praise with criticism. This method of manipulation is designed to make us unsteady and anxious, leading some people to feel the need to behave in a certain way in order to be rewarded with flattery, instead of disheartened by criticism.

So, whether you are the boss or a fresh employee, a friend or potential paramour, keep your emotional antenna up both professionally and personally, and receive praise responsibly.

Sources indicate that [1] Rogers, Benjamin A., Ovul Sezer, and Nadav Klein. “Too Naïve to Lead: When Leaders Fall for Flattery.” Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 125, no. 6 (2023): 1394–1419.
