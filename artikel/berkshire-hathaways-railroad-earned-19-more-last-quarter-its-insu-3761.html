Sources indicate that in the 2026 third-quarter earnings report from Berkshire Hathaway (BRKA -0.09%) (BRKB -0.08%), earnings for its insurance underwriting business fell, while railroad freight earnings climbed. Initially, that may sound like something is wrong with the insurance segment and that maybe Berkshire should invest more in the railroad side of its operations.

Surprisingly, what those percentages actually show us is why Berkshire is and will continue to be such a strong investment.

Industry observers note that after-tax earnings from insurance underwriting fell approximately 43% in Q3 from the prior-year period. In comparison, Berkshire's freight railroad carrier Burlington Northern Santa Fe (or BNSF) reported that after-tax earnings increased 19.3%.

It has been revealed that the takeaway there isn't about percentage gains or losses from any single division. It's about learning how to build a portfolio that doesn't rely on a single outcome. Former CEO Warren Buffett and his late business partner, Charlie Munger, did exactly that, believing so much in Berkshire's strategy that most of their net worth was tied directly to the company.

"Charlie and I feel totally comfortable with this eggs-in-one-basket situation because Berkshire itself owns a wide variety of truly extraordinary businesses. Indeed, we believe that Berkshire is close to being unique in the quality and diversity of the businesses in which it holds either a controlling or a significant minority interest," Buffett shared in a 1999 letter.

Today, that philosophy of owning shares in a wide variety of businesses explains why, even if any segment underperforms in a given quarter, Berkshire still has plenty of other investments that can offset those short-term declines and drive long-term value for its shareholders.

In a recent development, for retail investors, it's a replicable strategy. The key to spreading out investments is understanding different businesses and industries, which can help build a portfolio that includes companies across industries, from consumer goods to nuclear power. For those investing in Berkshire, building a portfolio of largely unrelated opportunities is something newly introduced CEO Greg Abel is continuing to do.

Even though he's been on the job for less than a year, Abel is following a strategy of focusing more on quality businesses with long-term potential than on a single sector of the market.

It has been revealed that in 2026, Berkshire acquired the homebuilder Taylor Morrison, increased its stake in the technology powerhouse Alphabet (GOOG -3.65%) (GOOGL -3.82%), boosted its stake in the satellite radio operator SiriusXM (SIRI +0.20%), and initiated and added on to a position in Bending Spoons (BSP -0.58%), an acquisition conglomerate. All are different businesses, limiting any common risk threads each faces. Owning a piece of each of them also means Berkshire isn't reliant on just a single tech giant to succeed, nor on them to succeed at the same time.

It's a strategy that has allowed Berkshire to deliver a 5.5 million percent return to its shareholders from 1964 through 2024, far outperforming the S&P 500's 39,000% (including dividends) return over that period. It's also a strategy that can continue to reward its long-term shareholders.
