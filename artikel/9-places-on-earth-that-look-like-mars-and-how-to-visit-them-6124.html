These places that look like Mars, from Chile's Atacama Desert to Jordan's Wadi Rum, show how red-planet terrain appears on Earth and what it takes to reach each one.

No person has ever stood on Mars. Ground that resembles it is already studied, filmed and explored here on Earth. Scientists test instruments built for Mars in deserts so dry that the soil holds very little life. Filmmakers send actors into red sandstone valleys to stand in for the Martian plains. Research groups run simulated missions in remote terrain, where crews live and work under mission rules.

According to recent reports, A short set of features makes a landscape read as Martian. Mars gets its red color from iron oxide in its dust and rock. Iron-rich ground on Earth produces a similar palette. Extreme dryness strips away plants and leaves bare rock and loose sediment. Volcanic basalt and cinder echo the planet's ancient lava fields. Impact craters, salt flats and acidic, mineral-heavy water add further points of comparison with red planet terrain.

It has been revealed that researchers call locations used this way Mars analog sites. An analog is not a perfect copy of another planet. It is a place where one or more conditions match closely enough to test equipment, train people or study how life might survive in harsh chemistry. Some of the places that look like Mars on this list serve that scientific purpose. Others share the planet's colors and textures, which is enough for a camera crew or a traveler.

Sources indicate that the nine locations below cover a wide range of Mars-like landscapes. They include a hyperarid desert in Chile, a sandstone valley in Jordan, a salt basin in California and a simulated habitat in the Utah desert. There is also a cratered island in the Canadian Arctic and a sulfur field in Ethiopia. A red river in Spain, a volcanic summit in Hawaii and a caldera in Iceland complete the set. The list is a selection, not a ranking.

In a recent development, the path to each one varies just as widely. Some sit inside parks with visitor centers, paved roads and regular tours. Others require a guide, a four-wheel-drive vehicle or careful planning around altitude, heat or security conditions. The Arctic crater is reached through research logistics rather than independent travel. Each slide explains what the place is, why it resembles Mars and what a realistic visit involves.

Industry observers note that chile's Atacama Desert is a hyperarid region in the north of the country, and researchers have used its pale, rocky ground as a stand-in for Martian terrain. It is often called the driest non-polar desert on Earth.

Industry observers note that dryness, more than color, is what puts the Atacama on this list. One long-running question about Mars is whether life could ever have taken hold in soil that sees so little water. The Atacama poses a version of that question on Earth. Teams have brought life-detection instruments into the desert to check how they perform on ground this barren.

In a recent development, that research role separates the Atacama from places that resemble Mars only in photographs. Large areas are bare rock, gravel and salt-crusted ground with little or no plant cover. A camera sees an alien surface. An engineer sees a place to learn how equipment behaves before it goes somewhere far harder to reach.

Industry observers note that geography helps explain the dryness. The Andes rise along the desert's eastern edge and block much of the moisture that would arrive from that side. To the west, Chile's coastal mountains and the cold Humboldt Current offshore limit how much rain the Pacific delivers.

In a recent development, for travelers, the most accessible Mars-like scenery sits around the small town of San Pedro de Atacama. Two nearby areas draw most of the attention. Valle de la Luna, the Valley of the Moon, is known for wind-eroded ridges and salt formations. It falls within Chile's Los Flamencos National Reserve. Valle de Marte, the Valley of Mars, lies a short distance away and offers a similarly barren landscape of rock ridges and sand.

Most visitors reach the area by flying into Calama, the main air gateway for this part of the desert. From there, a road transfer of a little more than an hour leads to San Pedro. Tours to both valleys can be booked once in town.

Industry observers note that the altitude needs planning of its own. San Pedro and the surrounding desert sit high above sea level. Travelers $TRV arriving from the coast or from lowland cities can feel the change. Headaches and shortness of breath are common early signs. Taking the first day slowly, drinking plenty of water and holding off on strenuous hikes gives the body time to acclimatize. The dry air and strong sun at elevation also make water and sun protection basic gear for any outing into the valleys.

It has been revealed that wadi Rum is a desert valley in southern Jordan. Its red-orange sand and sheer sandstone and granite cliffs have doubled as Mars in several feature films. Paved road access, a visitor center and established local tours also make it one of the easier Mars-like landscapes for travelers to reach.

The resemblance starts with color. Wadi Rum's sand and rock share the orange-red cast of the Martian surface. Broad sandy valley floors run between steep rock walls and isolated outcrops. Plant life is sparse. In wide camera shots, the ground looks bare and open.

Industry observers note that that look has made the valley a regular film location. Ridley Scott used it for scenes in The Martian (2015), in which Matt Damon plays an astronaut stranded on the red planet. Red Planet (2000), starring Val Kilmer, also filmed there. Last Days on Mars (2013) used the desert to represent Martian terrain as well.

It has been revealed that the valley's screen history predates science fiction. Lawrence of Arabia (1962) filmed in the area. The real T.E. Lawrence passed through Wadi Rum during the Arab Revolt of World War I.

Sources indicate that UNESCO inscribed the Wadi Rum Protected Area as a World Heritage site in 2011. The listing recognizes both natural and cultural value. Alongside the desert landforms, the area holds rock carvings and inscriptions. People who lived in and crossed the region left them over thousands of years.

Most visitors reach Wadi Rum through Aqaba, Jordan's port city on the Red Sea. From there, travelers continue by road to Wadi Rum Village, the usual base for trips into the desert. Entry to the protected area runs through its visitor center.

According to recent reports, inside the protected area, Bedouin-run outfits handle most of the logistics. Jeep tours carry visitors across the sand to rock formations and narrow canyons. Many travelers stay overnight in desert camps. A night in camp puts them in the valley after dark and at sunrise. Low morning light deepens the red tones of the rock.

Desert conditions shape the trip. Summer daytime heat is intense. Nights can turn cold, especially in winter. Travelers $TRV should carry water, sun protection and warm layers. They should also confirm arrangements with their camp or tour operator before arriving.

Industry observers note that death Valley National Park in eastern California pairs two Mars-like scenes: the white salt flats of Badwater Basin and the mineral-stained hills of Artist's Palette. Both show the dry, bare, oxide-colored ground associated with red planet terrain, and both can be reached by paved road.

Badwater Basin sits 282 feet (86 meters) below sea level. That makes it the lowest point in North America. Its floor is a broad crust of salt left behind as water evaporated. The crust breaks into rough ridges and polygon-shaped plates that stretch toward the mountains on either side of the valley.

That salt is part of the Mars comparison. NASA's Mars Odyssey orbiter has mapped chloride salt deposits on the Martian surface. Those deposits are read as traces of water that evaporated long ago. Badwater offers a walkable version of the same basic process, with a parking area and a short boardwalk at the edge of the flats.

Artist's Palette supplies the color. Its hillsides are streaked in reds, pinks, yellows, greens and purples. The hues come from metals that oxidized as the area's volcanic deposits weathered. Iron compounds account for the reds, pinks and yellows. Visitors reach the viewpoint along Artists Drive, a paved one-way loop road that winds through the colored hills.

The park also holds eroded badlands. These are bare slopes carved into tight gullies, with almost no plant cover to soften their shape. Zabriskie Point, a roadside overlook near Furnace Creek, looks across one stretch of them.

Furnace Creek is the park's main hub. It has the main visitor center, lodging and fuel. Badwater Basin and Artists Drive both lie south of Furnace Creek, off Badwater Road. The nearest major airport is in Las Vegas, and the park is reached from there by car.

One feature does not match Mars at all. Mars is a cold planet, while Death Valley's summer heat is severe and can be dangerous. The National Park Service runs the park and posts current conditions and closures. Visitors should check those conditions with the park before going. They should also carry plenty of water and plan hikes for the cooler months between late fall and early spring.

The Mars Desert Research Station is a simulated Mars habitat that the Mars Society has operated in the red desert outside Hanksville, Utah, since 2001. Crews live there during each simulated mission and work in surrounding terrain that resembles the Martian surface. It is a working research site, not a tourist stop.

Industry observers note that the Mars Society is a nonprofit group founded in 1998 to promote the human exploration and settlement of Mars. Its founder, aerospace engineer Robert Zubrin, is known for the Mars Direct mission plan. The Utah station puts that advocacy into practice. Crews rehearse the daily routines of life on another planet in a place that looks the part.

Sources indicate that the station's main building, known as the Hab, is a two-story cylinder. Crew members sleep, cook and work inside it. When they go outside for field work, they wear mock spacesuits and treat each trip as an extravehicular activity on Mars. That routine lets teams practice the planning, confinement and isolation a real Mars crew would face.

In a recent development, the landscape explains the choice of site. The ground around the station is layered sedimentary rock in bands of red, purple, gray and tan, with very little vegetation. Eroded hills and dry washes give the area the bare, rust-toned look familiar from images sent back by Mars rovers.

For travelers, Hanksville is the base. The small town in Wayne County sits where Utah State Route 24 meets State Route 95. It is the nearest town to the station for fuel, food and lodging. Public roads around Hanksville cross the same kind of eroded badlands that surround the station.

According to recent reports, two protected areas lie within driving distance. Goblin Valley State Park, north of Hanksville, is known for clusters of eroded sandstone hoodoos, which are nicknamed goblins. Capitol Reef National Park lies to the west along Route 24.

Visitors should not blueprint to tour the station or drop in on a crew. Missions are designed to simulate isolation, and the facility is not set up as a regular attraction. Anyone hoping to see it should check the Mars Society's own information before going.

The region is remote desert with long gaps between services. Summer heat can be severe, and dirt roads can turn impassable after rain. Extra water, a full tank and a check of road conditions before leaving Hanksville are basic preparation for exploring the area.

Devon Island, an uninhabited island in Canada's Nunavut territory, holds Haughton impact crater. Researchers use this cold, rocky basin for Mars-style field research. It is not a tourist destination, and access runs through scientific expeditions rather than travel bookings.

The island sits in the Canadian Arctic Archipelago, north of Baffin Island. It is often described as the largest uninhabited island on Earth. No permanent community lives there. There are no hotels, stores or public services.

Recent findings suggest that planetary scientists value Devon Island for its mix of cold, dryness and bare rock. The island is a polar desert. Precipitation is low, and vegetation is sparse across much of its surface. Broken rock lies exposed over wide areas. Those conditions resemble the frozen, barren ground that orbiters and rovers have photographed on Mars.

Haughton crater adds another layer to the comparison. Craters cover much of the Martian surface. Haughton gives researchers a place on Earth to study how a large impact reshapes rock and terrain. Geologists can walk its floor and rims, sample shattered rock and compare what they find with craters seen in Martian imagery.

The Haughton-Mars Project has used the site for this kind of research over many field seasons. Teams camp near the crater during the short Arctic summer. They test field methods, vehicles, communications and procedures that future crews on Mars might use. The work treats the island as a rehearsal space for exploring a remote, hostile surface with limited outside support.

The Mars Society also built a habitat on the island, the Flashline Mars Arctic Research Station, in 2000. Crews have lived in it during simulated missions. They follow routines designed to mimic daily life on another planet.

In a recent development, reaching Devon Island is a logistical operation, not a road trip. Research teams typically stage through Resolute, a small community on Cornwallis Island, and fly in on chartered aircraft. Arctic weather can hold up flights. Polar bears range across the region. Field teams strategy for wildlife safety as part of every expedition.

Industry observers note that for readers drawn to this landscape, the practical route is through the research itself. Expedition reports, mission photographs and published findings from projects on the island offer the most accessible view of its Mars-like ground. Anyone hoping to set foot there would need to join an organized scientific or institutional expedition. An independent journey is not a realistic option.

Sources indicate that the Danakil Depression in northeastern Ethiopia is a low, hot volcanic basin. Its Dallol hydrothermal field coats the ground in yellow, green and red mineral crusts that look more like another planet than a desert on Earth.

Recent findings suggest that the basin lies in Ethiopia's Afar Region, near the border with Eritrea. It is part of the Afar Triangle, where three tectonic plates are slowly pulling apart. Much of the depression sits below sea level. Thin crust, active faults and young volcanoes shape the whole landscape.

Dallol is the main reason the region draws Mars comparisons. Hot, acidic water rises through thick layers of salt laid down by ancient seawater. As that water cools and evaporates, sulfur and salts crystallize into terraces, small chimneys and crusts. Fresh deposits tend to show bright yellow and green. Older deposits turn rust brown as iron compounds oxidize.

Sources indicate that that mix of heat, salt, acidity and iron-rich minerals matters to planetary scientists. NASA's Opportunity rover found jarosite, a sulfate mineral that forms in acidic water, at Meridiani Planum on Mars. Researchers have sampled Dallol's pools to test how much heat, salt and acid living things can tolerate. Those limits help define where scientists look for signs of past life on Mars.

Industry observers note that the volcano Erta Ale rises to the south of Dallol. Its name means "smoking mountain" in the Afar language. The crater has held a lava lake for long stretches of its recorded history. The lake's level and activity change over time. Dark basalt flows surround the summit.

Recent findings suggest that the depression is also a working landscape. Workers cut blocks of salt from the flats by hand. Camel caravans then carry the salt toward markets in the highlands.

It has been revealed that visits happen on organized tours with guides. Tours typically start from Mekelle, the capital of the Tigray Region. Many itineraries cover both Dallol and Erta Ale. Heat is severe and shade is scarce. Roads into the basin are rough, and guided tours supply the vehicles and logistics the trip requires.

Recent findings suggest that northern Ethiopia has seen armed conflict in recent years, and security conditions change. Travelers $TRV should read current advisories from the U.S. State Department or the U.K. Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office before booking.

Rio Tinto is a river in Huelva province, Andalusia, that runs a deep rust red because its water carries high levels of iron and acid linked to mining. Spain's Centro de Astrobiología has studied it as a Mars analog because of its acidic water and iron minerals.

It has been revealed that the name means "red river" in Spanish. Its banks and riverbed are stained in shades of rust, ochre and orange. In some stretches, the water itself turns a dark wine red.

The color comes from chemistry. Acidic water dissolves iron from the surrounding rock and mine waste. That iron then settles out as iron-rich minerals along the channel. Over time, those deposits coat stones and sediment in layers. The result is a valley with an oxidized, red-planet look.

People have mined this area for thousands of years. Iberian and Roman workers dug here for copper and silver. Industrial extraction expanded in the 19th century under the British-backed Rio Tinto Manufacturer, founded in 1873. The mining group that still carries the river's name grew out of that venture.

In a recent development, the Mars connection rests on mineralogy. In 2004, NASA's Opportunity rover found jarosite at Meridiani Planum on Mars. Jarosite is an iron sulfate mineral that forms in acidic water. The same mineral forms along Rio Tinto. That overlap gives researchers a place on Earth to study how such minerals develop. It also helps them judge what those minerals might reveal about past water on Mars.

The river is not lifeless. Microbes and algae adapted to acidic, metal-rich water live in it. Those organisms give scientists a working model for the kinds of life that could, in principle, survive in similar conditions beyond Earth.

According to recent reports, the town of Minas de Riotinto is the usual base for visitors. Its mining museum covers the district's long mining history, from ancient workings to the industrial era. A tourist train runs on a restored stretch of the old mining railway beside the river. Passengers get a close view of the red water and stained banks along the route.

Huelva, the provincial capital, is the nearest city, and Minas de Riotinto lies inland to its northeast. Train and museum schedules vary by season. Check current times with the museum before planning a trip.

Mauna Kea, a volcano on Hawaii's Big Island, rises to 13,803 feet (4,207 meters), and its upper slopes are covered in reddish-brown volcanic cinder. That loose, rust-colored ground has made the mountain a test site for equipment built for Moon and Mars exploration.

Sources indicate that mauna Kea is a dormant shield volcano. It grew from layer upon layer of fluid lava over hundreds of thousands of years. Near the summit, later eruptions piled up cinder cones made of fragmented, oxidized rock. Those cones and the loose debris around them give the upper mountain its red-brown cast.

Mars has shield volcanoes too, built in the same basic way. The largest, Olympus Mons, is far taller than any volcano on Earth. A mountain built from lava and ash gives engineers ground made of some of the same ingredients. Equipment can be tried out on rough, loose material rather than on a flat test surface.

Sources indicate that the summit is more than a test site. Mauna Kea is sacred in Native Hawaiian tradition, and it hosts a cluster of astronomical observatories. Its height puts the summit above much of the atmosphere's moisture, which makes it a strong location for telescopes. Visitors share the area with working scientists and cultural practitioners.

In a recent development, the Massive Island's main airports are at Hilo and Kona. Most trips up the mountain start at the Maunakea Visitor Information Station, which sits at about 9,200 feet. That is roughly 4,600 feet below the summit. Travelers $TRV arriving from sea level are already at serious altitude at that stop. Pausing there before going higher gives the body time to adjust.

Altitude sickness is a real risk on this mountain. Symptoms can include headache, nausea, dizziness and shortness of breath. Climbing too fast raises the risk. People with heart or lung conditions, and anyone who is pregnant, should get medical advice before going up.

Recent findings suggest that rules govern access to the summit road above the visitor station. Much of that stretch is unpaved, and four-wheel-drive vehicles are recommended. Conditions, permitted vehicles and opening times can change. Check the current rules before driving up, or book a guided tour that handles the transport.

It has been revealed that weather near the top can turn cold even when the coast is warm. Snow falls on the summit in winter. Pack warm layers in any season.

Industry observers note that askja is a volcanic caldera in Iceland's central highlands, surrounded by dark basalt, ash and pumice. Its bare volcanic ground shows the kind of rock that covers much of Mars, even though its astronaut history belongs to the Moon.

Sources indicate that A caldera forms when a volcano's summit collapses after magma drains from the chamber beneath it. Askja sits in the Dyngjufjöll mountains, north of the Vatnajökull ice cap. The plain around it has little soil and almost no plant life. Lava fields, cinder and loose pumice stretch out in every direction.

A large eruption in 1875 reshaped the area. In the years that followed, a smaller section inside the main caldera sank and filled with water. That basin became Öskjuvatn, a deep lake. Next to it sits Víti, a smaller explosion crater that holds warm, milky blue-green water.

Sources indicate that apollo astronauts trained in the Askja area during the 1960s. That training prepared them for geology on the Moon, not on Mars. The fieldwork focused on recognizing volcanic rock types on open ground with no vegetation in the way.

The resemblance to Mars comes from rock type rather than color. Basalt is the dominant volcanic rock on Mars. The planet also has large shield volcanoes with summit calderas, including Olympus Mons. Askja's ground is dark gray and black, not rust red. It highlights what volcanic Martian terrain looks like beneath the iron-oxide dust that gives Mars its color.

Reaching Askja takes planning. The highland roads that lead there are unpaved F-roads. They are rough and usually open only in summer. Some routes cross rivers without bridges. F-roads require a 4x4 vehicle, and many visitors book a guided tour instead of driving themselves. Tours commonly depart from the Lake Mývatn area in north Iceland.

Industry observers note that check road conditions with Icelandic road authorities before setting out. Opening dates shift from year to year with snowmelt and weather. Highland weather can also change within a single day. Warm, waterproof layers are standard even in July and August.
