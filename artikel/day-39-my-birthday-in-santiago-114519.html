Day 39: My Birthday in SANTIAGO

Before I even got out of bed, my Italian brother ran to me in the dark of the bunk room and gave me a birthday kiss on the cheek.

As I journaled in the common room, the pilgrim from Germany I met last night walked in with another candle for my birthday.

My Argentinian brother walked in shortly after and gave me a big birthday hug.

It wasn’t even an hour into my day and I already felt surrounded by love.

One last time, the boys and I set out as a family on the last four kilometers under the stars.

My Argentinian brother pointed out how three stars were perfectly aligned with the moon.

It truly felt like the stars had aligned.

When we arrived at the plaza, the first thing we saw was tents lined up for the protests in Europe. Not allowing that to divert our focus, we turned our attention to what mattered:.

My brain couldn’t process that this was the cathedral and the town I’d been walking toward.

Recent findings suggest that part of the reason for getting to town so early was to attend the one mass that was being held in the cathedral at 7:30. Leaving our backpacks and everything to our names outside, we entered the legendary church.

Industry observers note that within seconds of entering the cathedral, our Australian friend was surprised to find us, as it was her last day in Spain. The four of us walked around this significant place, the finish line we’d been working toward.

The first thing that caught my attention was the fragrance of incense. I’d seen so many videos of the big swing of incense that went through the cathedral. Since it was under restoration, we wouldn’t get that experience. Instead, the mass was held in a small chapel. It didn’t feel as significant as other experiences I’d had, but I’m thankful I went.

Sources indicate that the cathedral, though, was beautiful. The painted pillars adorning the golden altar caught my attention because they differed from the others I’d seen. My Argentinian brother and I walked around in silence after the mass, entering the legendary tomb of Santiago, which was underneath the choir. To be there in real time felt significant, but it didn’t hold the energy I expected.

Immediately after, we went back to the plaza and found more pilgrim friends. Sharing hugs and tears, we all said our congratulations to each other before a giant group picture.

The brothers and I were hungry, though, so we all squeezed onto a couch in a cafe, classical music playing in the background. After we ate, the waitress brought over a giant piece of chocolate cake with a blue candle. Surrounded by my brothers, I made a wish and blew out the flame.

Sources indicate that to make our arrival official, we went to the pilgrims’ office and acquired our Compostela. The Compostela.

Sources indicate that the man at the desk helping me was so excited to hear it was my birthday that he awkwardly reached over the wide counter to give me a hug. It was just as special as the piece of paper I had in my hand.

The brothers and I went to our accommodation for the night, a private bedroom with our own bathroom and three beds. It was simple, but the opportunity to not be in a bunk room and to share that space together was bliss.

In a recent development, it was exactly what we needed to charge up before heading into the city for our hard-earned tinto de verano and pinchos. It was the first alcoholic drink I had in a week and a half. Boy, did it taste good. It was well worth the wait.

Migrating to the plaza again, we saw more pilgrim friends and congratulated them on their journey. One of them told us that before we arrived, he had internally asked for a sign to know whether to go home that day or stay another day in Santiago. When he heard it was my birthday, he knew to stay. So a group of us lay on the cobblestone ground, listening to the Galician bagpipes in the distance and taking in the view of the cathedral.

The brothers and I had been talking about pizza for days, a conversation that started with the quote “the end of the Camino is like the last piece of pizza”. So the three of us sought out a restaurant and shared the last pieces of pizza together as a physical representation of our last full day together.

My Italian brother was on a stricter timeline than the rest of us due to his plane ticket. So when he said he wanted to get his Camino-finish tattoo, I decided to go with him. What he got was extremely symbolic. On the first day of the Camino, before I met him, he sped past me with his trekking poles on his shoulders and a Spider-Man hat clipped to his pack. I thought it was hilarious, so I took a picture. That’s the picture he got tattooed on himself. It not only showcased the achievement of his walk, but the bond between us that blossomed over the course of a month.

We regrouped with a group of pilgrims for a night on the town and my birthday quickly became the theme of the night. One of my friends disappeared from the bar we were in just to return from the cold outside 45 minutes later with the legendary cake of the Camino, a Tarta de Santiago. I pulled out the candle from breakfast, had someone light it, and sat there basking in the love as the group sang happy birthday to me in Spanish, English, Portuguese, and Italian, all at the same time. That moment was a memory that would live with me forever.

The rest of the night was full of celebration.

Dinner at a seafood restaurant.

Darts at the legendary pilgrim bar, Pub Momo.

An underground bar with traditional Galician dancing and a jam session from the locals.

And at 3:45 am, I finally lay down; the brothers and I stopped laughing, closing off the day of a beautiful birthday.
