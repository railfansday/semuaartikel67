Grayscale’s latest report highlights a hidden risk that still catches market-timing investors off guard.

Upcoming research from Grayscale demonstrates Bitcoin’s (BTC) volatility has fallen to roughly 43%—lower than nearly one in five S&P 500 stocks.

The catch? Bitcoin’s calmer day-to-day trading hides a massive trap for anyone trying to time dips: almost all of its multi-year gains still come from just a handful of explosive trading days.

In its report, “Beyond Volatility: Rethinking Cryptocurrency Risks,” US digital asset manager Grayscale notes Bitcoin’s long-term performance is heavily influenced by a handful of trading days. Its returns have “fat tails,” meaning sharp gains and losses occur more frequently than in traditional stocks.

Over the past three years, Bitcoin delivered an annualized return of roughly 60%. Excluding its 10 best days would have cut that figure to 7%, while excluding its 10 worst days would have raised it to 197%.

Industry observers note that the report also notes that Bitcoin has historically rebounded sharply after major declines. Since 2011, it has experienced seven drawdowns exceeding 50%, although Grayscale notes these declines are not unusually severe compared with stocks and bonds when adjusted for volatility.

Grayscale also sees Bitcoin as a potential portfolio diversifier because it is less exposed to traditional market factors.

The tech giant points out that Bitcoin has historically recovered sharply after significant declines. Since 2011, Bitcoin has experienced seven drawdowns of more than 50% from previous highs.

The report cites Bitcoin’s inclusion in J.P. Morgan Asset Management’s 2027 Long-Term Capital Market Assumptions as a milestone.

Grayscale describes JPMorgan as a $5 trillion investment manager that now examines Bitcoin alongside established asset classes.

The banks’ 10-year base case for Bitcoin projects a 10% compound annualized return for Bitcoin over the next decade, the highest among the asset classes covered. The forecast also assumes 55% annualized volatility and a 33% correlation with US large-cap stocks, the number below international equities, corporate bonds and hedge funds.

In Bitcoin’s early years, volatility routinely exceeded 100% and at times approached 200%, with a market cap in the single-digit billions.

Grayscale ties the decline to roughly 43% to larger market value and liquidity, greater institutional participation, Bitcoin exchange-traded products and regulatory clarity.

In historical tests, modest Bitcoin allocations raised annualized return and Sharpe ratio in a representative institutional portfolio.

In a recent development, over three years, Bitcoin explained about half of return variation across FTSE Grayscale Crypto Sectors. Among the 10 largest non-stablecoin assets, six led quarterly performance over the past two years, while Bitcoin led none.

Sources indicate that JPMorgan’s decision to include Bitcoin in its long-term forecasts, along with declining volatility, could shape how institutions approach crypto investments. At the same time, Grayscale’s data reveals how much Bitcoin’s long-term performance depends on a small number of trading days, highlighting the risks of trying to time the market.
