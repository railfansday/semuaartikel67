📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Deutsche Bank is in damage control mode again after an international consortium of more than 200 investigative journalists published internal documents from the global lender on Tuesday under the name "Inside Deutsche Bank.".

The documents, published in Der Spiegel, Financial Times, Le Monde and The New York Times, among others, cover Deutsche Bank's business dealings with convicted sex offender Jeffrey Epstein, now US President Donald Trump, and a host of Russian oligarchs.

Gleaned from nearly 15 million documents leaked to a journalist from India and another from South Africa, the e-mails and meeting minutes trace the bank's aggressive pursuit of business deals in Russia, and with high-profile Russian oligarchs, right up until Moscow introduced its full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 — despite risk warnings from numerous managers.

It has been revealed that deutsche Bank CEO Christian Sewing was among the executives who took part in the bank's Russia strategy working group. The bank also still employs more than 160 people in Russia and generated 2025 pre-tax profits of €61 million ($67 million) there through a local subsidiary.

In all, the documents cover the past 20 years of the German lender's business dealings and raise questions about the efficacy of internal controls by showing that Deutsche Bank had repeatedly put financial interests before international sanctions compliance and anti-money laundering rules.

Other red flags raised in Tuesday's reports suggest that Deutsche Bank sought to set up tax-optimization schemes for wealthy clients, that criminal networks may have been able to move illicit cash through the institution, that it helped facilitate arms deals, that it helped companies in environmentally damaging industries and that it held funds for the far-right populist Alternative for Germany (AfD) party.

Beyond more recent negative headlines involving alleged anti-money laundering and compliance failures, Deutsche Bank has been trying to restore its public image ever since its involvement in the US subprime mortgage rate scandal helped trigger the 2008 global financial crisis — a fiasco that ultimately cost the bank more than €20 billion in penalties and settlements.

When Sewing later took over in 2018, he promised to turn the institution around. Although he has made Deutsche Bank profitable once again, he did so at the cost of massive job cuts, and some compliance failings have continued.

Sewing to the German business daily Handelsblatt that some mistakes had been made under his leadership.

In a recent development, "When you look at the events today, you naturally ask yourself whether you could have done some things differently," he told the paper. "I am not without mistakes," he said, but added that the course he set for the bank "has always been right and remains so.".

The bank's shares fell by up to 6% in Tuesday trading.

Deutsche Bank issued a statement claiming that the reports were old news and that the institution had long ago turned over a newly introduced leaf.

In an e-mailed statement to the press, Deutsche Bank Supervisory Board Chairman Alexander Wynaendts wrote, "During my tenure I haven't seen a single decision from Christian Sewing that I was not comfortable with.".

A Tuesday statement from the bank described the revelations as "legacy" matters and claimed they would have no lasting negative financial impact on the institution.

Nevertheless, German Finance Minister and Vice Chancellor Lars Klingbeil on Tuesday called on Deutsche Bank to "provide full transparency regarding how it has addressed past failures and internal control deficiencies and how it will effectively prevent these in the future.".

Further, Klingbeil noted authorities must investigate and "take necessary action" where appropriate.
