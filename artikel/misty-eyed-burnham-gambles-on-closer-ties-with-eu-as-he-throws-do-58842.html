Misty-eyed Burnham gambles on closer ties with EU as he throws down gauntlet to Tories and Reform

PM challenges rivals to rebuff strategy he says could add 0.5% annual growth to economy and offer young people hope.

In a recent development, several sentences into a slightly convoluted answer about where UK-EU relations might go next, Andy Burnham abruptly changed tack. “The important point is that the Britain of today and the EU of today is not the one of 2016,” he mentioned. “Life has moved on in so many ways.”.

Industry observers note that this is undoubtedly true. But Burnham’s political gamble on closer ties with Brussels hinges on the assumption that most British voters feel this way as well.

Seeking to push his deal on food checks, emissions trading and a latest youth exchange scheme, Burnham was at first in full consumer politics mode, saying it would bring cheaper food – with a namecheck for Tesco, Asda and other supermarket chains – as well as lower electricity costs and a claimed 0.5% boost to overall growth.

Sources indicate that but as the Brussels press conference went on, the prime minister became more misty-eyed, particularly in describing the youth exchange scheme as a gift to a generation stripped of choices by the 2016 referendum and then Covid. “Hope is a word,” he intoned. “And it starts now.”.

For all that Burnham is a skilled political salesman, his oratory is backed up with some helpful political context, matched with cooperative allies.

To begin with, not only politics but polling results are now different. A upcoming constituency-level survey released on Friday revealed that of 394 constituencies estimated to have voted leave in 2016, voters in 274 of them would back rejoining the EU – including Burnham’s Makerfield seat.

Standing alongside the UK prime minister in the venue, Ursula von der Leyen, the European Commission president, and António Costa, the European Council president, at times resembled proud parents, beaming at every positive phrase.

They, of course, began work when the EU reset was the personal project of one Keir Starmer. His name was not mentioned on Friday, and it is undeniable that Brussels finds life easier dealing with a UK leader who is both bold and popular.

Speaking to British reporters later in the day, Burnham openly challenged political opponents to rebuff a blueprint that, he said, could add 0.5% annual growth to the economy in the long term – more, he said, than any other single intervention.

“Why would anybody turn their back on that?” he said, with particular focus on Nigel Farage, who was, soon after Burnham spoke, hosting a retaliatory press conference of his own, 10 minutes’ walk from the EU building.

Even with the tight timing – Farage had to delay his event as the summit timetable slipped – the Reform UK leader drew a decent crowd of reporters. But there will be a sense in No 10 that beyond Farage, most of the formidable Brexit forces of 2016 have melted away.

Recent findings suggest that kemi Badenoch, who has been advised by her team to talk more about the economy and less about Brexit, spent the day on a visit in Essex. The Conservatives did send out a condemnatory press release about the EU deal, but it focused more on Burnham “reopening the divisions of the past” than the specifics.

More trenchant was a statement from Stay Out, the recently formed Brexit purity group, which floated its argument that rejoining the EU would cost the UK £750m a week.

According to recent reports, for all that the dominance of vote leave alumni in Stay Out gives the campaign the slight air of a historic battle re-enactment society, Burnham cannot ignore them. A resonant sidenote of the summit was how he was happy to talk about the specifics of the deal, but not much beyond that.

While it was the PM himself who provoked a debate about a future in which the UK rejoined the EU, the brakes were firmly on in regard to any discussion of such ideas.

Pre-summit talk of possible closer future links with Brussels was replaced by much vaguer language about mutual “brainstorming” on what would come next, with UK officials saying any progress could be months in the making.

Another sign of the caution embedded within Burnham’s ambition was the lineup of UK cabinet ministers who watched as the prime minister, von der Leyen and Costa arrived on Friday morning.

Alongside those you might expect – Ed Miliband, the foreign secretary, John Healey, the chancellor, and Jonathan Reynolds, the business secretary – was Anneliese Midgley, a close ally of Burnham who has the job of chief whip, No 10’s liaison with Labour MPs.

She was there because a bill setting into UK law the fresh EU deal will be presented to parliament next week. Most Labour MPs agree with Burnham that the world has changed since 2016. But, as with the voters, he needs to bring them along with him.
