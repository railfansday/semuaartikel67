Bitcoin Has Become Boring, Finally

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Bitcoin isn't a shiny new technology any more, it is established, and it is here to stay.

According to recent reports, the 2019 Lightning conference in Berlin was lit: five hundred “cypherpunks” getting each other high on visions of a future with peer-to-peer electronic cash. Attending a bitcoin conference back in the day felt like being a teenager at a friend’s party while their parents were out of town. It was exciting and fresh. The feeling was the opposite of FOMO: something big was happening, we were on the inside, and the rest of the world was going to envy us. It was the kind of party that you could only have when the adults weren’t looking. Whenever someone bought a beer (with bitcoin), everyone would hoot and holler and record it on their phones for Instagram/Twitter (it was still called Twitter).

It has been revealed that lightning was the bleeding edge. We called it “the Lightning Network” (capitalized, if you please), and talking about it felt like talking about string theory: something so far out you’re not even sure it’s real. I spent a lot of time invoking the abacus.

It has been revealed that those are the times some of us remember fondly and keep trying to relive at bitcoin conferences today. That’s why we engage in rituals like drone highlights, Satoshi Rockamoto sets, beefsteak dinners, and video game tournaments for sats, but it’s not the same. The ratio of bitcoin OG speakers to suits is shifting. Many event rooms are half empty, and the halls and meeting rooms are full of people negotiating cap tables and profit-sharing splits.

The adults haven’t shut the party down, but they have crashed it, and it kinda feels like their party now. The most exciting thing I noticed at the last bitcoin conference I attended was that nothing exciting happened.

The road to boring is seldom smooth. Technological innovation benefits from passion and bold ideas. People work harder on a project when they feel strongly about it and identify closely with like-minded collaborators. That’s where tech subcultures, their rituals, and their dogmas come from. But such passion and tribalism feed conflict, at least for a while until the tech goes mainstream, and normies, who couldn’t care less about subculture squabbles, take over.

Sources indicate that this progression from an exciting fresh technology that inspires strong emotions to a humdrum utility is not unusual. World-changing innovations often (always?) start with a committed subculture that identifies fiercely with their weird tech before the mainstream adopts it and soon expects it. In fact, this trajectory has become the standard doctrine of tech marketing. For example, in the early days of the internet, early adopters wrote a Declaration of the Independence of Cyberspace, picking a fight with states and corporations despite having nothing to back it up but chatrooms and dreams.

One case that resembles the early days of bitcoin was the war of the currents between high-voltage AC current and low-voltage DC current in the 1880s-1890s. Both featured big personalities stunning audiences with flashy demos at conferences. Edison and Westinghouse wowed crowds with their lightbulbs that made artificial light, just like bitcoiners in 2019 wowed YouTube by buying beer with bitcoin. And both cases featured rabid subcultures that accused each other of putting the future of the technology, if not of humanity, at stake. In the 1880s, the issue was whether AC was more deadly to humans and animals than DC; in the 2010s it was whether limiting the size of bitcoin’s blocks to 1MB would perpetuate monetary servitude forever.

Sources indicate that in both cases, there were devoted subcultures whose disagreements helped the technology to mature before the tech crossed the chasm of exoticism to become the newly introduced, boring normal. Both are now largely managed by experts on pragmatic principles, not passionate conviction. Conferences went from spectacular showcases for technological miracles to sedate affairs where professionals debate and agree on industry standards. Pragmatism and consensus replaced ideology and dissent. In both instances things got boring, and boring was actually better.

Recent findings suggest that bitcoin is getting boring, although what that means and how it’s come to be that way depends on your point of view. As always, where you stand depends on where you sit.

Generally speaking, there are two broad points of view on what bitcoin is and is for. And though they started in different places, they’re both coming to a common conclusion: bitcoin is more boring than ever. So let’s review these two points of view, how they view bitcoin now, and why both would agree that the status quo is now so awfully, wonderfully boring.

The basic existential question that defined bitcoin’s first decade is settled: bitcoin is not a scam, and it’s not going away. That status is stable largely because bitcoin is now too substantial to fail, with a market cap exceeding a trillion dollars.

Recent findings suggest that much of this scale is thanks to the millions of people who want to own bitcoin as part of their savings, but not necessarily to trade and circulate it. In other words, they see bitcoin as a better form of gold, not cash. These savers want to integrate bitcoin into their fiat portfolio, just like any other asset. They aren’t terribly concerned about bitcoin’s status as a medium of exchange so long as it becomes firmly established as a viable, tradable commodity.

According to recent reports, people investing in bitcoin are happy when the number goes up, and it generally does (you might have to zoom out on the price graph a bit, depending on the week). Bitcoin used to be a rollercoaster; now it’s more of a hike. The ups and downs still happen, but they’re gentler and a bit more predictable. How boring.

Industry observers note that bitcoin’s price started to settle down as mainstream finance started growing more comfortable with it as an asset. Whereas conventional finance used to want governments to shut bitcoin down, there is now about $100B worth of bitcoin held in ETFs and traded on ordinary equity markets. Major banks used to shun bitcoin, and now they accept it as collateral. Bitcoin is a regular feature in corporate treasuries, and some companies exist just to hold bitcoin. Strategy is worth about $55B on the open market, and it holds about $63B worth of bitcoin, so the entire tech giant is a discounted bitcoin wallet. Bitcoin is also a regular feature in retirement accounts, pension allocations, and sovereign wealth funds. Thinking of bitcoin as another kind of asset ripe for institutional and retail investors now makes a lot of sense, because that’s what it is.

Why are these canny investors bored? It seems that all the exciting developments leading to bitcoin becoming a valuable asset have already happened. If the goal was integration into the world of conventional finance, it has incontrovertibly been achieved. You can’t YOLO on bitcoin any more than you can YOLO on gold. The milestones are all in the history section of the Wikipedia page, not on the roadmap.

People who wanted bitcoin to become a tradable asset are bored because mainstream finance has effectively tamed bitcoin. It is easily tradable, and its trading volumes are remarkable. It is digital gold, just with a slightly steeper curve and a little more volatility, which is exactly what these folks always wanted.

Recent findings suggest that the main alternative to seeing bitcoin as a mainstream investment asset is to view it as monetary dynamite to ignite a revolution. People subscribing to this point of view wanted bitcoin to blossom into a system of peer-to-peer electronic cash that would first operate in parallel to the mainstream monetary system before supplanting it. These radicals wanted to see the banks collapse, not accept bitcoin as collateral. It was monetary insurrection, not incremental change.

This radical point of view is still around, but the wind has gone out of its combative sails for a few reasons.

First, there has been a generational shift. Many of the people who were pushing the revolutionary vision of bitcoin in their 20s fifteen years ago have moved on. Some got rich and retired or started to see and use bitcoin more as a tradable asset. For others, bitcoin never achieved the success they dreamt of, but it was successful enough that fighting for its future no longer felt necessary.

Second, AI is the new cool kid on the tech block. Instead of building vending machines for beer that accept bitcoin, enthusiasts who want to hack the system are now decompiling games with AI. No VC will waste a second on a pitch deck that doesn’t have AI on at least half of the slides. Miners are turning their server stacks and power contracts into AI data centers. Curious engineers have a new, much bigger toy to play with.

Sources indicate that third, mainstream bitcoin fans on Wall Street simply consume too much bandwidth. Not surprisingly, banks, publicly traded companies, and financial institutions with billions in their pockets are dominating panels at conferences and supplying the talking heads for financial news demonstrates. They want to discuss topics like volatility and discount rates instead of personal sovereignty and individual financial freedom. Fighting against the system is not a narrative they can sell while representing the interests of that very system.

Industry observers note that fourth, bitcoin usage has moved past the radical mantras and progressed in a different direction. The dream of bitcoin becoming the standard payment medium in a circular economy where everyone buys coffee with bitcoin (why is it always coffee?) is starting to feel like an alternative history in a parallel universe.

A circular economy is simply not the role in society bitcoin has grown into. Instead of everyday payments, bitcoin is the medium of global value transfer. It has evolved into a technology to move real money across borders as remittances, into a settlement layer between various crypto assets, and into a store of value that people constantly buy, sell, and trade. It’s become a valuable network of rails that even stablecoins are happy to use.

According to recent reports, fixating on coffee purchases obscures the remarkable scale bitcoin has achieved. Somewhere between $1.6 and $4.1 trillion was settled on-chain between Q1 and Q3 2026 (depending on how you count). PayPal processed $1.79 trillion in all of 2025. The average payment on PayPal is about $70. The average bitcoin transaction (using the strict counting method) is $10,000. With bitcoin moving trillions in value, we can stop worrying about coffee.

Sources: Bitcoin, Mastercard, PayPal.

It has been revealed that bitcoin may not have cornered retail payments, but it’s a mature and highly liquid asset. It’s an industry unto itself. It has found its place in the economy, and it’s thriving. Despite the lack of revolutionary fireworks, bitcoin is changing mainstream finance by opening fresh possibilities and financially enfranchising new user groups. That’s still worth celebrating.

You know what’s really boring? Electricity infrastructure. You know what no one can live without despite how boring it is? Electricity infrastructure.

It has been revealed that when revolutionary technologies fade into the background of everyday life, when people use them constantly without thinking about them, they become boring, and they become essential. Buying a beer with Lightning is no longer exciting because we can now expect that it will just work without fanfare. Being able to say “it will just work” sounds boring, but it’s a clear sign of triumph.

Bitcoiners and builders need to adjust to this beautiful, boring reality. We can stop annoying our neighbors with orange pills. It’s about utility, not evangelism. Utility means building rails that bitcoin and stablecoins can use together seamlessly, adding bitcoin to mainstream fintech apps, and building new financial applications on top of the bitcoin chain. Perhaps none of this will light the fireworks at the radicals’ victory party or replace T-bills as the base layer of global finance, but every user who discovers the unique utility of bitcoin is further evidence of its mainstream adoption.

What we have is a thriving technology that enables us to do miraculous new things, and it’s achieved an undeniable scale, such that it is now too massive to fail. That’s fantastic. It may not be the future we were dreaming about seven years ago in Berlin, but it’s wonderful in its own right, and it’s worth cultivating and celebrating.

Even though nobody sets out to grow up and become boring one day, it’s still a valid kind of success. It means you’ve made it, and you’re doing fine. Boring is what happens when things just work and things have just worked out. So we didn’t break the world with bitcoin. Oh well. We’re making a newly introduced one. A party with adults present is still a party. And maybe we’re now the adults. So dance while you wait for the dollar to collapse 😉.
