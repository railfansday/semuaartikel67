There are rumours that Bradford City could be up for sale, but any prospective newly introduced buyers of the club should be warned that any purchase of the club could come with one significant complication.

For several seasons, it looked rather as though Bradford City were living in some sort of footballing purgatory. Trapped in League Two and watched by huge crowds for that division despite barely challenging for promotion, the club seemed stuck in a loop which was proving extremely difficult to escape from.

According to recent reports, at the end of the 2024-25 season, though, that run came to an end. Bradford were promoted back to League One following an absence of six years, and they marked their first season back by finishing in fourth place and securing a play-off place, where they lost 2-0 over two legs to Bolton Wanderers.

Sources indicate that that form has broadly followed them into League One this season. The departure of manager Graham Alexander to Preston North End at the start of October was a major loss, and their form has dipped since then. Prior to that, though, the Bantams soared to the top of League One, again demonstrating that this is a club with untapped potential, and this in turn has reportedly attracted the interest of investors from abroad.

Recent reports from journalist Alan Nixon have confirmed that there is interest in buying Bradford City from a consortium of Thai investors who are seeking a route into the English game, with Nixon writing that, "Bradford could be for sale at the right price and the Thais, who have an intermediary with inside knowledge, are considering an official offer.".

Recent findings suggest that stefan Rupp has been the public face of the club's ownership since 2016, and Rupp has been clear that he will only sell to the right buyer. Speaking to the Bradford Telegraph & Argus after the end of the 2025-26 season, Rupp said, "I'm the keeper of the keys and I would never hand it over to somebody where I have the impression he can't do any better.".

Industry observers note that on the surface, there are plenty of good reasons to have an interest in buying Bradford City. The days of them being underachievers in League Two already feel like a rapidly fading memory, and even though they've had a dip in form since the departure of Alexander, they remain one of the promotion contenders in League One again this season.

Crowds remain extremely healthy, at just shy of 20,000, and Valley Parade is a stadium which hosted Premier League football for two seasons at the turn of the century, representing a city of half a million people in which they remain the only professional football club.

But when The Athletic's Matt Slater reported on the possibility of the Bantams being sold in March 2026, he mentioned a potential sale price of around £10 million. This seems very low for an upwardly mobile League One club, but there's a very good reason for this modest valuation, and it comes down to what the club doesn't own.

In an era during which a Championship striker is having a £70 million price tag slapped on his head to deter predators, spending £10 million to buy an entire football club sounds like a bargain, but with the sale of any football club it can be beneficial to pay attention to the detail of what's actually being bought or sold.

Sources indicate that and in the case of Bradford City, the club owns neither Valley Parade nor their training ground. Valley Parade belongs to former chairman Gordon Gibb's family pension fund. Bradford reportedly pay more than £500,000 annually to lease the ground, with the existing agreement due for renewal in 2028. Bradford have been giving consideration to possibly leaving the ground over the cost of the rent for at least a decade and a half.

And in the lower reaches of the EFL, the pure real estate value of the ground can be the biggest single cost aspect of any potential takeover. Without that, any newly introduced owner is essentially buying the player and staff contracts along with a license to operate the club in the EFL, and Bradford City, like the overwhelming majority of clubs, are not a profitable enterprise, with the last available set of manufacturer accounts showing a £3 million loss on a revenue of £8.6 million.

Earlier this year, the Bantams Supporters Trust entered into a consultation period with fans over what to do about the stadium, with a valuation of it put at between £8 million and £10 million. It's not known whether there are covenants limiting its use. But regardless of this, the Trust confirmed that they believe that the club's intention is to seek to continue to lease the stadium when that renewal comes up.

In a recent development, it would be advantageous to any new owners to take ownership of the ground itself. To do so would save them more than they're paying their highest-earning player every year, while its value would increase as land prices increase, and it would allow the club to redevelop the site more easily if they were looking to increase their revenues another way round.

But throwing the ground into the sale mix could double that original £10 million valuation, and although that would still represent stunning value for money were the club able to fight their way back to the Premier League, sale prices can vary wildly even within League One.

Recent findings suggest that when Rob Couhig bought Reading from Dai Yongge in May 2025, for example, it was reported that the sale price was £30 million, but this included the Select Car Leasing Stadium and the club's training ground. But when Leicester City were put up for sale in September 2026, it was for a staggering £224 million, though this did include state-of-the-art training facilities, land around the stadium for redevelopment, and Leicester's Belgian sister club, OH Leuven.

Buying a football club at any level of the game can be a gamble, and the recent IFR report into the state of football finances stated that two-thirds of club have liabilities which exceed their assets and rely on owner funding to survive - making them technically insolvent - while only seven of the 116 professional clubs in the top five men’s divisions did not record a loss for the last available financial year.

According to recent reports, all of this means that, while £10 million to buy a football may sound like a bargain, it's almost certain that this price is reflective of the extreme likelihood being that the cost to any new owner wouldn't end at that point. In the case of Bradford City, it wouldn't include the stadium itself, and this would likely double that price, while the new owner would also be on the hook for covering the club's losses, which at the last available count amounted to £3 million for one year.

According to recent reports, even at a sale price of £20 million to £30 million, buying Bradford City could prove to be a bargain. But so lop-sided are the finances of the majority of English football clubs that they may have to get the club all the way to the Premier League to turn a serious profit on their investment.
