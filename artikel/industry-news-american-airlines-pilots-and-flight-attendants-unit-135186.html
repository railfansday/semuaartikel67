Industry News: American Airlines Pilots And Flight Attendants Unite To Demand New Leadership—Say They’re Done Asking Management To Do Better

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that five days after American Airlines declared it was replacing Chief Operating Officer, the flight attendants and pilots unions say that change at the top needs to go further. And they’re making this case in part based on American’s poor recovery from this month’s FAA nationwide ground stop. Captain Nick Silva of the Allied Pilots Association and Julie Hedrick of the Association of Professional Flight Attendants signed a joint letter that both unions sent out. They tell management, “We have spent years asking you to do better. Those days are over.” A few lines later they say what they want. “American Airlines needs a upcoming direction — and leadership capable of delivering it.”.

According to recent reports, captain Nick Silva of the Allied Pilots Association and Julie Hedrick of the Association of Professional Flight Attendants signed a joint letter that both unions sent out. They tell management, “We have spent years asking you to do better. Those days are over.” A few lines later they say what they want.

“American Airlines needs a latest direction — and leadership capable of delivering it.”.

Sources indicate that in February, the flight attendants’ union board voted unanimously that it had no confidence in Robert Isom. Pilots did not go as far.

And today’s note is being misinterpreted by some as a call for Isom to go. Today’s letter leaves his name out of it, never naming him at all. It’s actually a weaker call than they made earlier in the year.

According to recent reports, this gives the unions room to claim a win even if Isom stays CEO. As I wrote earlier today, American may be looking for an outside president who could eventually succeed Isom, and former Wall Street Journal airline reporter Scott McCartney states a hire could come by year-end.

In a recent development, the unions circulated FlightAware data compiled by BofA Global Research. On Saturday, American canceled 42% of its mainline flights, Delta 28% and United 26%. By Sunday, Delta was down to 5%. However, The chart is misleading by picking only two U.S. airlines and mainline flights only. JetBlue cancelled 44% of flights over the 3 days. They left JetBlue off the chart. Including regional flights, American, United and Delta all performed similarly – American cancelled 28.5%, Delta 25.1%, and United 23%. So the union heads are cherry picking by focusing only on mainline. (American even owns three of its regional carriers.) After Winter Storm Fern at the start of the year, I obtained minutes from a flight attendants’ union executive committee meeting. Hedrick said American sent crews to a service center that couldn’t actually book hotel rooms. What struck me at the time was how much sympathy she had for management. On November 15, her union reported that flight attendants waited hours to get their trip assignments fixed, then waited again for rooms. Some never got one. American needed those same people rested to fly passengers the next day. The unions take a swipe in their note at American investing in better seats and lounges. They need those to generate more revenue. Silva himself said in September he wished American had started investing in customers years earlier. What Each Union Gets Out Of Signing Hedrick’s union is helping members claim compensation for hotel delays and get their expenses reimbursed. Having pilots back them publicly helps her bargaining position. She comes out looking tough, even though the note doesn’t go nearly as far as the union went in calling for leadership change earlier in the year. Silva gets a second union making his case that the people who fly the schedule know where it breaks. He opened contract talks a year early and asked for a seat on American’s board. When the pilots asked to make their case to the directors in February, the board sent them back to management. Weak profits are hurting employees. American paid 0.3% of eligible 2025 earnings in profit sharing. On $50,000 that’s $150. At Delta the same earnings would’ve meant $4,450. American already pays competitive pilot wages. I’d expect this negotiation to focus on quality of life issues rather than substantial raises. But the union is positioning itself to argue that management is at fault for poor performance, shouldn’t get to use that as an excuse for being unable to afford contract concessions, and the union is in fact part of the answer. United pilots got board representation in a 1994 employee buyout involving wage and work rule concessions. They kept it through bankruptcy as part of $1.1 billion in concessions. Delta pilots gained a voting seat in the Northwest merger deal. American’s pilots don’t have a transaction like that to bargain over. Meanwhile, the fight inside their independent union over joining the larger Air Line Pilots Association helped cost Silva’s predecessor his job. Silva wants to show effectiveness taking on management to keep his job..
