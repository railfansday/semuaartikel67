The study highlights that temporarily quieting this reward-and-motivation circuit during adolescence leads to lasting social withdrawal in adulthood, shedding light on why psychiatric conditions like depression and schizophrenia often emerge during the teenage years.

Source: The Ohio State University.

Industry observers note that adolescence is widely recognized as a period of intense behavioral and social transformation, marked by heightened peer seeking, shifting relationships, and emotional turbulence. While neuroscientists have mapped early infant brain development in granular detail, the exact neural mechanisms sculpting social behavior during the teenage years remain far less understood.

Now, new findings from The Ohio State University Department of Psychology pinpoint a critical developmental window inside a pathway linking the brain’s executive command center to its reward hub: the prefrontal cortex–ventral striatum (PFC-vSTR) circuit.

Industry observers note that presented at Neuroscience 2026, the annual meeting of the Society for Neuroscience, by lead author and doctoral candidate Katrina Lin alongside senior investigator Dr. Laurence Coutellier, the study demonstrates that disrupting PFC-vSTR maturation during adolescence permanently dampens adult social motivation.

To establish how social drive naturally evolves across the lifespan, Lin and Coutellier first measured spontaneous social investigation in both adolescent and adult mice, whose compressed three-week adolescent window enables researchers to trace developmental trajectories across adulthood.

In a recent development, “I always say it’s like two dogs in a park. They start sniffing each other. They’re careful at first, and then they go into a different type of sniffing behavior,” explained Coutellier, associate professor of behavioral neuroscience at Ohio State. As predicted, adolescent mice exhibited substantially higher motivation to initiate social interaction than adult mice.

“In humans it’s the same,” Coutellier noted. “A teenager loves to hang out with their friends, and they will seek this interaction. As adults, we will be more careful. We don’t approach a stranger just like that, and we’ll be more cautious about the way we interact. There’s still a strong social motivation, which is necessary for us to have social relationships, but there is less of it.”.

The prefrontal cortex, which governs decision-making, impulse control, and social reasoning, and does not finish maturing in humans until roughly age 25, sends direct signals to the ventral striatum, a core hub for reward processing and motivation.

To test whether adolescent maturation of this pathway is essential for adult sociability, the team selectively switched off activity in PFC-vSTR neurons in both male and female mice during either adolescence or adulthood:.

“We found that actually, yes, if we prevent the maturation of the frontal circuit to the striatum, when mice reach adulthood they have impaired social motivation,” Coutellier said.

Understanding how the PFC-vSTR pathway wires itself during adolescence carries significant implications for psychiatry. Conditions such as depression and schizophrenia frequently manifest during the teenage years, often preceded or accompanied by profound social withdrawal.

Next, Coutellier’s laboratory intends to examine how real-world environmental stressors, such as prolonged adolescent social isolation, alter the maturation of this circuit.

Industry observers note that “We can try to study what happens when our mice don’t get to socialize, and we can think about what happened during COVID-19, when teenagers were told to stay home and stop socializing to be safe. How did that impact this developmental trajectory of the social circuits?” Coutellier asked. “We’re trying to understand what genetic and environmental factors can make these symptoms arise at the time of adolescence — not before, and not in adulthood, but during that sensitive period of development.”.

What is the PFC-vSTR brain circuit, and what does it do?

According to recent reports, the prefrontal cortex–ventral striatum (PFC-vSTR) circuit connects the prefrontal cortex (the brain’s decision-making and social control center) to the ventral striatum (a key hub for reward and motivation). During adolescence, this pathway matures to support healthy social motivation and peer interaction.

Why did turning off this pathway in teenage mice affect them as adults, while adult mice bounced back?

Recent findings suggest that researchers found that adolescence is a uniquely sensitive developmental window when this circuit is still actively wiring itself. Disrupting it during adolescence permanently altered how the circuit matured, leading to reduced social motivation in adulthood, whereas adult brains already have established wiring that recovered immediately once the inhibition ended.

How does this research relate to mental health conditions like depression and schizophrenia?

Industry observers note that many psychiatric disorders first appear during the teenage years and involve social withdrawal. Identifying the exact brain circuits that mature during adolescence helps scientists understand how genetic risks or environmental stressors, such as social isolation, can derail healthy social development.
