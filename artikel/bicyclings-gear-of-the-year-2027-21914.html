Bicycling’s Gear of the Year 2027

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, there’s no shortage of fresh cycling gear promising to make your bike lighter, faster, or more comfortable. Over the past year, our editors have put those promises to the test on road and gravel rides, mountain bike trails, commutes, and in the workshop. For Gear of the Year, we’ve chosen the products that stood out most: the ones that solved a problem, made a ride more enjoyable, or delivered tangible performance that impressed us enough to recommend to you.

Sources indicate that our editors bring different perspectives to testing. Matt Phillips has tested bikes and gear for 30 years and has ridden more bikes than any other cycling journalist on the planet. Tara Seplavy has led product development at global bike brands, while Dan Chabanov has won national and elite titles in cyclocross and crit racing. Nearly all of us have worked in or managed bike shops.

Sources indicate that our picks reflect that range of experience and the different ways we ride. This year, we’ve organized them by price to make it easier to find something that fits your budget, whether you’re looking for a small upgrade or considering a bigger purchase. Whatever you choose, if it’s on this list, we think it will improve your ride.

Sources indicate that I get the skepticism about a product that seems to have figured out a problem that’s existed forever, but trust me, the Tyre Glider has made me completely rethink how I install tires. Instead of fighting the final few inches with my thumbs, you simply hook it under the bead and glide it around the rim. Until you use it, it just doesn’t make sense how simple and easy it is.

That mechanical advantage is especially useful with tight road tires and tubeless setups, where a conventional tire lever can feel precarious with carbon rims and spokes. It won’t replace a traditional lever for every job, but for the problem it’s designed to solve, it does it really well.

According to recent reports, last summer ended with me sporting a very prominent helmet vent hole tan line on my forehead. The reason was obvious: I avoided applying sunscreen there because every formula would sweat into my eyes. This summer I set out to find one that worked.

It has been revealed that when I found Pelotan, I was skeptical of something marketed specifically to cyclists, but it works incredibly well. Although expensive, I found the 6-ounce bottle lasted me the entire season. It goes on light, evaporates promptly, smells nice, and most importantly doesn’t sting my eyes so I actually end up wearing it.

Industry observers note that I often ride without gloves, so I’m picky about grips. After testing both the regular and fat versions of Ergon’s GDH Team, they’ve become my favorites for any bike built to get rowdy. The rubber is pleasantly soft and tacky, providing a secure hold with bare or gloved hands. Subtle fins under the fingers help keep my hands in place without forcing me to squeeze the bars hard.

Knipex Pliers Wrench (86 03 180).

Knipex makes some of the most enjoyable to use tools we have in the Bicycling workshop. The Pliers Wrench might look like an oddly shaped adjustable wrench, but its parallel jaws provide superior clamping force. It grips hardware securely without any slipping and marring.

These aren't bicycle-specific tools; they’re exceptionally good tools that happen to be incredibly useful around a bike. It comes in a range of sizes, but the 7 1/4-inch is the sweet spot: compact enough to live in a bike toolbox while still opening wide enough to handle most of the jobs I throw at it.

In a recent development, A front light is an easy addition to your road or gravel bike, but finding one that fits neatly on your cockpit can be tricky. Lezyne’s Road Drive 500 solves that problem with a slim, 75-gram body that tucks discreetly under a computer mount.

Despite its small size, the light offers a 500-lumen daytime flash and a 400-lumen mode for seeing the road or bike path after sunset. The replaceable battery lasts up to 55 hours in daytime flash mode or 4.5 hours in the 150-lumen enduro mode. Some computer mounts don’t let you adjust the beam's direction, but Lezyne includes a GoPro-style mount that lets you aim the light where you need it, at the expense of making the overall form factor a little less stealthy.

Peak Design Mobile Crossbody Multi-Strap.

As a photographer and cyclist, I’m always looking for easier ways to carry cameras while riding. Peak Design’s Mobile Crossbody Multi Strap has become a useful way to keep small cameras and accessories secure and within reach.

Its mini quick-release mechanism lets me attach anchors to multiple devices and swap between them quickly. I’ve used it to carry a small gimbal camera while shooting the Philly Bike Race and as an added layer of security for cameras tucked into my pockets on rides. It’s also handy when traveling, letting me keep my phone or headphones tethered while making my way through an airport.

It has been revealed that spur Cycle’s R-Pro mirror gives road and gravel riders a simple way to see what’s happening behind them. Its alloy body and glass mirror have a high-end feel that looks at home even on a pricey carbon race bike, and it installs in just a few minutes.

I find it especially useful in heavy traffic, when a radar can be constantly pinging with notifications. A quick glance at the mirror helps me judge the distance to the vehicles behind me. It’s also handy on group rides to check that the person following me is still on my wheel. While the mirror doesn’t replace a radar, it never needs charging or pairing to a head unit.

I have found these Campagnolo 03 rotors to be categorically better than both SRAM and Shimano rotors while also being cheaper (a rare feat for a Campagnolo product).

Recent findings suggest that the main glitch I have with both SRAM and Shimano’s high-end road rotors is that after hard braking, they absorb enough heat to deform and briefly make noise. Riding the same roads after switching to Campagnolo rotors? No noise after hard braking. To me, this indicates the Campagnolo rotors manage braking heat better, which gives me more confidence in their performance; plus, silence is golden. At this point, I have replaced both Shimano and SRAM rotors with these Campagnolo ones and can recommend them highly as a better rotor alternative for any road bike groupset.

Back in 2025, I bumped into an old friend at Sea Otter. He was walking around with what might be the most curious saddle prototype I’ve ever seen. It looked like it was made from plastic and Styrofoam. It was also crazy light and, according to him, fully rideable.

Industry observers note that now, I finally have the production version. It’s still crazy light (146g on my scale). And those funky rails? An absolute joy to use. It’s such a simple solution: the saddle slides into the clamp instead of needing to partially take everything apart in some cases. But more importantly, as someone who has always tended to sit more forward, I love the wider nose on this saddle.

So far I’ve enjoyed about 500 miles on this saddle, and have been very, very pleasantly surprised. It has replaced the S-Works Power Evo saddle (which is quite popular and I also found very comfortable) on my road bike, but given how the Sixpack saddle is roughly a 1/3rd of the price, just as comfortable and 25 percent lighter, I feel no need to go back to the more expensive Specialized saddle.

Vittoria Corsa Pro Control Tan, 700x42c.

Introduced in 2025 as OEM equipment on Cervélo’s Áspero-5 gravel racer, Vittoria’s 700x42c version of its cotton-casing, all-season road tire is now available to the masses, and it’s excellent. The wider Corsa Pro Control features the cushion and grip of wider gravel rubber but rolls swiftly, like a high-performance road tire. It’s the perfect upgrade for road riding on your gravel bike or building a go-fast all-road bike (provided you have enough clearance).

Continental Grand Prix 5000 TT TR.

In a recent development, I’ve put roughly 1,000 miles on a pair of GP5000 TT TRs (set up tubeless) without a flat. That’s not something I can say about many high-performance tires, especially ones intended only for race days. I was actually a longtime Continental skeptic, but the GP5000 S TR started to change my mind, and the TT TR convinced me to finally change my tune. It combines competitive weight and low rolling resistance with a surprisingly long lifespan. If you want a fast race tire that can also handle training miles, the TT TR is worth a look.

In a recent development, in nearly 20 years of riding and racing, I avoided the urge to get custom insoles, even though I knew early on that my feet had some weird fit issues. This year, I finally gave in to my intrusive thoughts, and oh my god, I am not going back.

Industry observers note that I truly didn’t think a custom insole would make as much of a difference as it did. But I’ve noticed all kinds of improvements, like my feet feeling much more stable and secure in the shoe without needing to ratchet down the closures. For the sake of experimentation, I even did a short ride with my previous comfortable (non-custom) insole setup, and I was shocked by how much discomfort I had gotten used to over the previous 20 years of riding. At this point, I would happily put custom insoles into a cheaper pair of shoes than ride stock insoles in a top-of-the-line shoe. I genuinely think the insole makes the bigger difference, and more riders should consider a pair for themselves.

Pearl Izumi Pi PRO/BLACK Mesh Jersey.

At $190, Pearl Izumi’s Pi PRO/Black Mesh Jersey isn’t cheap, but it delivers the quality and performance I expect at that price. It’s a high-end racing jersey with understated looks, a combination that's hard to find in premium cycling kit.

In a recent development, the fit is cut for an aggressive position on the bike while maintaining excellent comfort and heat regulation. The paneled collar reduces pressure across the back of the neck, a common glitch I’ve found with other aero-fit race jerseys. If you’re willing to pay a premium for a well-designed jersey but don’t want to look like a billboard, the Pi PRO/Black Mesh is a great option.

I am very stingy with my saddle recommendations because comfort is so uniquely personal to the rider. I know this because I ride a lot of different saddles, and I’ve discovered that only a very small minority are actually comfortable. But every so often I happen across a saddle that makes me say, “Oh, this one is special.” That’s the Ergon’s SRS Pro Carbon.

Industry observers note that it’s plush without being squishy or saggy, offers even pressure distribution without feeling bulky, and is supportive without hot spots or pinch points. It’s also a rather good deal for a light (about 185 grams in an S/M size).

Typically, a European-made, carbon-railed saddle costs $300 or more, and that’s before you get into 3D-printed tops, so $220 seems almost reasonable.

I use a torque wrench almost every time I work on my bikes. Tightening parts to the correct torque helps prevent component failures, especially with carbon components, lightweight parts, or suspension linkages. Of the several torque wrenches in Bicycling’s workshop, I most often reach for Topeak’s Torq Stick Pro Digital.

In a recent development, the wrench adjusts from 1 to 20 Nm in one-tenth Nm increments. It beeps as you approach your selected torque and buzzes when you reach it. While it costs more than an analog wrench, I find it to be more precise and much easier to use, especially on parts that have torque ratings that include decimal points. Topeak’s set also includes common metric hex and Torx bits, an extender, an 8mm crowfoot for brake bleeds, and a small ratchet driver.

Heat training is a tool many of the world’s best bike racers use to prepare for hot summer races. The Core 2 Heat Sensor makes it easier to track and monitor that process, but I’ve found it useful even without fully optimizing my heat training.

According to recent reports, the sensor monitors skin and core temperature and combines that information with heart-rate data to give you a heat-strain index. That makes it easier to gauge how your body is responding to the heat while riding. I use the sensor all summer to watch for spikes in my body temperature and take preventive measures before I overheat. With more summer events likely happening during periods of high heat, monitoring your body temperature more closely can be not just helpful, but tangibly beneficial.

Q36.5 Dottore Pro Long Sleeve Jersey.

Industry observers note that the Q36.5 Dottore Pro jersey works across a wider range of temperatures than any other long-sleeve jersey I’ve tried. It holds enough heat to keep me comfortable on cool rides, but it also pulls moisture away from my body and feels cool enough for warmer days.

According to recent reports, I’ve used it as a layer on a soaking-wet 50-degree ride, worn it on a 75-degree summer day, and ridden in it in plenty of conditions in between. On some of those rides, there were other pieces of clothing I would have changed, but I would have picked the same jersey every time. Its versatility means it can replace multiple layers, making it well worth the asking price.

The Varia RearVue 820 is Garmin’s first full update to its successful radar light in nearly six years. It delivers more information to compatible Garmin Edge head units, offers brighter light, supports USB-C charging, provides longer battery life, and extends detection range. On the road, the RearVue 820 feels like a noticeable step up from other radar units—notably, it consistently detects vehicles approaching from behind earlier than other devices.

Same-speed tracking was another revelation. Not having to constantly check whether the car that dropped off my screen was still following me or had turned into a driveway is something no other radar unit does reliably, and it's a massive reason I would choose the 820 over other units. If you’re using a modern Garmin cycling computer that supports advanced tracking features, the Garmin Varia RearVue 820 is more than just an update to the RTL 515. It’s a big step forward in cycling radar technology.

Sources indicate that but even if you’re not using the latest Edge device, my comparative testing revealed the 820 was always the first radar to detect an approaching car, sometimes by several seconds. Sure, things like better battery life, a more convenient charging standard, and a brighter light are great. But when it comes to cycling radars, better, faster detection is king, and right now the RearVue 820 wears that crown.

Recent findings suggest that the Apex Aeon bibs are stupid expensive, but great bibs are one of the few pieces of cycling equipment worth splurging on. These have become my favorite pair for long, hard, hot rides, maintaining their support and comfort from start to finish.

The straps and lowers are thick and feel almost overbuilt, a welcome change from cycling apparel that can feel wispy and fragile. The chamois is medium-thick and the firmest I can remember using, but it’s fantastically comfortable without feeling like a diaper. Like the rest of the bib, it shines brightest on longer rides. I’ve worn almost nothing else this summer, and whenever I ride in a different pair, I invariably wish I’d grabbed these instead.

Industry observers note that it is no understatement to say SRAM is on a roll. Thanks to a combination of performance, features, and impeccable timing, its drivetrains are the benchmarks for mountain, gravel, and road riding at the moment. On the road, the newest Red (E1) stormed into the market with superbly comfortable hoods, brilliant brakes, and excellent shifting. One would be wise never to count Shimano out, but Red E1 revealed to the world that it had forever altered the drivetrain status quo.

Other Bicycling editors and I have long argued that top-of-the-line drivetrains, especially in the electronic era, are never the smart buy. Give up a bit of weight, and you can get all the performance of a brand’s top-tier group for a lot less money. And that’s the case with SRAM’s Rival E1. It offers all the performance and improvements of Red E1, along with SRAM’s impressive ecosystem interoperability. The bonus buttons on the hoods are the only feature you might miss: Red and Force have them; Rival does not. Aside from those, Rival E1 offers the benchmark performance of Red E1 without the painful hit to the wallet.

In any given year, I ride between 20 and 30 different bikes (usually with a variety of wheels), and I will also likely test anywhere between five and ten standalone wheelsets. That is, I ride a lot of different road wheels every year, and these Rovals earned my Gear of the Year endorsement because I keep coming back to them.

One of the things I like most: I think Specialized has cracked the secret sauce with their carbon spokes. Every other carbon-spoked wheel I have tried has a much harsher ride than a traditional steel-spoke wheel. Both the Rapide Sprint and the Rapide CLX III feel more like traditional wheels, just with a bunch of weight lopped off. I keep coming back to the deeper Sprint version over the more all-around CLX III because I don’t really feel the roughly 90g difference, but I definitely notice the extra speed on flat and rolling terrain. It’s pretty wild that Specialized has managed to make a set of 60-ish mm deep wheels (63 mm and 58 mm deep, to be exact) this light while also making them feel so smooth.
