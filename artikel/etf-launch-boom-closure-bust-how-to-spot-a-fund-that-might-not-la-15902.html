ETF Launch Boom, Closure Bust: How to Spot a Fund That Might Not Last

Industry observers note that nearly $1.8 trillion in net inflows into U.S.-listed exchange-traded funds (ETFs) this year highlights how popular these funds are. What's not to like? ETFs offer diversification, low costs, and easy trading. They're simple to buy and sell like stocks, while providing access to broad markets, sectors, or investment strategies.

The reality is this, though: Despite ETFs' growing popularity, a fresh ticker doesn't guarantee a long life. U.S. funds debuted 1,023 ETFs through August 2026, a record pace and 52% ahead of the same point in 2025. Yet launching a new fund is easier than attracting enough investors to keep it running.

Recent findings suggest that it takes more than a good idea to sustain an ETF. Here's what to look for if you want an ETF with staying power.

A media-savvy CEO, a clever theme, or a memorable ticker, may attract early interest, but without substance, they don't guarantee long-term success. What matters is whether assets and fund flows grow over several months or whether the ETF remains small, even after it's had enough time to attract an audience.

In a recent development, if a fund doesn't seem to grow, ask yourself whether anything sets it apart from larger, more established alternatives. Does it offer anything that those other funds don't offer?

While a small asset base isn't proof a fund will close, persistently low assets can make it harder for the fund to cover operating costs and earn a healthy return.

Industry observers note that according to FactSet, as of March 31, 2026, 38.4% of U.S. ETFs had less than $50 million in assets. Again, that's not necessarily a bad sign, especially for a newly launched fund. However, given the effort it takes to attract investors, a fund that struggles to gain traction may not last long.

Low trading volume and a wide bid-ask spread can make buying or selling more costly. Review an ETF's spread and whether its market price is above or below its net asset value. While these measures can't predict whether a fund will close, they do reveal how costly or difficult it may be to trade.

The now-defunct U.S. Defiance Next Gen H2 ETF (HDRO) illustrates how difficult it can be for small thematic ETFs to attract investors and remain afloat.

When it was debuted on March 9, 2021, HDRO looked good. It was a thematic stock ETF focused on companies involved in the hydrogen economy, including hydrogen production, fuel cells, and related equipment and infrastructure.

In a recent development, however, by April 2025, the fund declared it was liquidating. While Defiance didn't state a specific reason for the fund's closing, its poor performance likely tells the story. By the time the announcement came, HDRO's assets had dropped from about $65.9 million to $13.88 million.

While it's true that HDRO briefly gained traction, that early interest didn't last, and the fund's value and assets shrank.

According to recent reports, the best strategy is to treat a new ETF as a product to evaluate, not as a promise that a fund will be around forever. Look at its assets, age, flows, trading costs, and strategy, and then review them periodically. It's the least you can do for a well-balanced portfolio.
