As parents, what should you and your kids consider before picking up that controller?

With the release of Grand Theft Auto VI, caregivers may soon find themselves negotiating with teens who insist that everyone they know is playing it.

“I know the difference between real life and video games.”.

And, of course: “Everyone else is allowed!”.

Yes, and everyone else may make dubious choices—not exactly a convincing argument. Grand Theft Auto is known for violence, criminal activity, sexual content, and adult themes. Surprised? It’s the sixth iteration, folks. It’s Grand Theft Auto, not Careful Driving Simulator. Yet beyond the controversy surrounding the release, there are psychological questions worth considering:.

Watching Violence Is Not the Same as Participating in It.

Kids aren’t just absorbing whatever’s on the screen. They interpret it and make their own sense of it. Video games add another wrinkle: instead of only watching a character act aggressively, players make choices and see what follows. Albert Bandura’s (2001) social cognitive theory helps us understand this. Children learn through observation, and behaviors can be rewarded or produce desirable outcomes. However, learning about a behavior is not the same as performing it. A teen who successfully steals a virtual car isn’t necessarily planning to steal the neighbor’s Honda.

Recent findings suggest that researchers still argue about how violent games relate to aggression. Some studies have found links with aggressive thoughts or behavior (Anderson and colleagues, 2010); others found no meaningful link between playing violent games and adolescent aggression (Przybylski and Weinstein, 2019). The answer isn’t as simple as violent game in, violent kid out. Your teenager’s brain is not one-size-fits-all. Two teens the same age can react differently to the same game. One might see the violence as exaggerated satire; another might find it upsetting or miss the point. Maturity, past experiences, and individual sensitivities matter, and age alone doesn’t tell us how a child will respond.

In a recent development, age-rating systems are a helpful starting point, but the ratings don’t explain how parents can understand developmental differences. Adults can consider a child’s maturity, awareness of specific content, and ability to distinguish fictional scenarios from real-world behavior. Understanding that something is fictional doesn’t mean a child is emotionally prepared for it.

For teens, video games aren’t necessarily just games. They’re opportunities for social connection, common experiences, and participation in peer culture. Belonging is especially vital during adolescence. Being excluded from something friends are discussing or playing can feel like being excluded from the group itself.

That doesn’t mean parents should say yes to help their child fit in. But a teen’s interest in the new Grand Theft Auto may be less about virtual crime than about joining a conversation their friends are having. Ask what appeals to them: the story, exploring the world, the humor, or that everyone’s talking about it? Hear them out. This may be better than shutting the conversation down with an automatic no. What does the game teach? Rather than focusing on the violence, consider: What aggressive behavior is rewarded, how are consequences portrayed, how is satire used to criticize society, and would younger players take it literally?

Talk it through with your kids. They’re still learning how to make sense of complicated stories, relationships, and questions about right and wrong. And humor adds a further layer. Grand Theft Auto is known for exaggerated social commentary and dark humor. Still, understanding satire requires distinguishing between what is depicted and what is being criticized.

Before spending money on the latest installment of virtual criminal behavior, parents might want to do a little research:.

Conversations about how the game portrays violence, relationships, and consequences can help kids think critically about what they’re seeing.

Parental controls can manage access, purchases, and playtime. Remember, restricting a game at home doesn’t necessarily prevent children from encountering it through friends, YouTube, or social media. We were once kids too. Don’t tell me that while reading this, you didn't remember something you did outside your home that your parents still don’t know about.

Is Grand Theft Auto VI Worth It?

Industry observers note that adults who enjoy open-world narratives may believe it is worth it. When a 12-year-old states everyone already has it, we might have to reconsider. Recognize how gaming matters to your teen without buying the game. Their interest deserves a conversation; your boundaries continue to matter. We don’t need another moral panic about video games. However, we also don’t need to pretend that all media experiences are appropriate for every child. Sometimes the most responsible parenting decision is to say yes. Sometimes it’s to say no. And sometimes it’s to say, “Let’s find out a little more about this game before I spend my hard-earned money on your virtual crime spree.”.

After all, just because a game allows you to steal a car doesn’t mean parents should abandon the driver’s seat.
