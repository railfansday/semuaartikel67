Sources indicate that the S&P 500 (^GSPC -2.80%) fell about 3% in early trading Wednesday, and the Nasdaq Composite (^IXIC -4.24%) dropped more than 4% as chip stocks kept sliding. It's the latest leg of a rough month. Last Friday, the S&P 500 and the Dow Jones Industrial Average both fell into correction territory, according to Charles Schwab (SCHW -1.81%).

It has been revealed that tom Gardner, The Motley Fool's co-founder and CEO, doesn't make market calls. What he gave members on the Fool's September Quarterly Call was closer to arithmetic. Stocks were trading 10% to 30% above their historical valuation ranges, he noted, and the S&P 500 has historically fallen 10% about once a year.

Put those together, and "a 10% to 15% decline in the S&P 500 should not be considered surprising or a remarkable development for any investor." His advice from that call reads even better now that the decline is here.

Industry observers note that tom warned that a drop wouldn't land evenly. When the S&P 500 falls 13%, he mentioned, "a lot of the high-growth, high-beta businesses around which our portfolios have grown actually get set back even more significantly.".

That's what happened last Friday, when the S&P 500 lost 3.7% and the Nasdaq fell 5.3%, and it's happening again today. If your account is down more than the index, that's the price of owning fast growers. Tom just wanted members to see the bill before it arrived.

His first recommendation was to lean toward "high-quality companies and lower expectations." He described investors who keep some cash on the side and use declines of 20% or 40% to buy more of their favorite companies at lower prices. That only works if the cash is there before the drop. Most people love a 30% sale at the mall and treat a 10% sale on stocks like a fire drill, and the difference usually comes down to whether they have money ready to spend.

Sources indicate that he also lowered the bar on returns. Tom noted he'd be happy with low-double-digit annual gains from here, and he wants investors to weigh not losing money as heavily as making it.

In a recent development, A day like today pushes people toward big moves. Selling everything feels like taking control, and so does pouring cash into a stock that just fell 15%. Tom thinks both impulses miss what conviction really looks like. He remarked "the best form of conviction is the step-by-step incremental moves," because buying or selling in stages makes you rethink your reasons each time.

Put another way, Tom says that "emotional reactivity is the enemy of wealth creation." He even expects more staged selling across certain holdings in Motley Fool portfolios to get members out of weak or aggressively valued positions sooner, since every trim forces a fresh look at the evidence.

His most useful idea had little to do with stocks. Tom suggested families figure out "how inexpensively can you live for a single month with a high enough quality of life?" Then use the answer to size an emergency fund of six, nine, or 12 months. Nobody's kids will beg to do it again, but the point, as he put it, is to "simulate a recession before it arrives." Once you know your bare-minimum monthly cost, you know how much cash to hold and how much money can sit through a downturn untouched.

Tom ended with something his father used to say after a long, happy day with the kids: "Tomorrow's another day." Investors who try to cram every gain into this year tend to burn out, and some quit the market for years.

Sources indicate that the market closes Thursday for Thanksgiving and reopens Friday for a short session. Before it does, figure out how many months of expenses your cash would cover. That number will say more about how the next year goes for you than anything the Nasdaq does on Friday.
