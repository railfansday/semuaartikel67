In a year that has seen artificial intelligence reach one spectacular mathematical breakthrough after another, Anthropic has now claimed the top prize of them all: a full proof of the Riemann hypothesis, the statement that many mathematicians consider the most notable open problem in the field. But on the same day as the Anthropic claim, a separate study claiming to have proven the Riemann also emerged — and this one is entirely human written.

The Anthropic announcement came on 20 November from Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at the San Francisco, California, company. “I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis,” Alpöge wrote, referring to Anthropic’s large language model (LLM).

“If this fully checks out, it is clearly a historic milestone in mathematics,” says number theorist James Maynard, a winner of the Fields Medal — mathematics’ most prestigious prize — at the University of Oxford, UK.

Sources indicate that hours later, Ravi Vakil, a mathematician at Stanford University in California, posted a rival preprint to Alpöge’s online, in what Vakil called a “hurried announcement” of a proof that “is all human, with no AI”. The preprint was written by Alessandro Maria Masullo, a mathematician who received his PhD at Stanford in 2020 under the supervision of number theorist Brian Conrad. “The hope was to have Brian work with Alessandro for a year to polish the writing and then announce it after running it by enough people,” Vakil wrote. “Now it seems best to put out the current draft.”.

The confirmation of either or both solutions would be a spectacular bicentenary present for Bernhard Riemann, the German mathematician who was born 200 years ago this September and who formulated the problem, which addresses the distribution of prime numbers, in the mid-1800s.

The Riemann hypothesis is one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems selected by the Clay Mathematics Institute, which has its scientific headquarters in Oxford, UK, at the turn of the twenty-first century. The institute offers a US$1 million prize to anyone who devises a solution to one of the problems, which are considered some of the most challenging in the field. Mathematicians using AI tools announced earlier this year that they had solved another Millennium problem, related to the Navier-Stokes equation.

Sources indicate that the problem has to do with the ‘Riemann ζ function’ (ζ is the Greek letter zeta), which encodes the randomness properties of the prime numbers. Riemann discovered that the distribution of primes is encoded in the ζ function’s ‘zeroes’. These are points, denoted by z, on a plane satisfying the equation ζ(z) = 0.

According to recent reports, riemann conjectured that apart from some obvious but uninteresting cases, all such solutions had to lie on a certain vertical line in the plane. If this were true, he showed, it would imply that prime numbers are scattered among all whole numbers in a way that is essentially as random as it could be.

Recent findings suggest that the Riemann hypothesis has been a lodestar for generations of mathematicians. Credit: James Brittain/Getty.

It has been revealed that alpöge’s four-page document includes a sketch of the proof. But Andrew Granville, a number theorist at the University of Montreal in Canada, claims that Alpöge should have taken the time to prepare a clearer write-up before making the announcement, which came just days after Anthropic’s initial public offering (IPO). The IPO initially valued the firm at US$1.5 trillion, but the company’s valuation dropped on 19 November.

“What he released is garbled,” Granville notes of Alpöge’s document. Granville cites the downturn in the company’s stock value as the reason for the timing of the proof’s release.

“There is an obvious cynical view, to do with the timing, but it is false,” Alpöge wrote in the document.

Anthropic did not respond immediately to Nature’s request for comment.

According to recent reports, tristan Buckmaster, a mathematician at Latest York University who collaborated with Alpöge on a separate project regarding the Navier-Stokes equation, indicates that Alpöge deserves a lot of credit for developing techniques to produce mathematical results using LLMs. “Levent himself built this amazing set-up to do math.” He adds that Alpöge, who is himself a number theorist on leave from Harvard University in Cambridge, Massachusetts, has been working relentlessly for months on this and other problems, hardly ever getting any sleep.
