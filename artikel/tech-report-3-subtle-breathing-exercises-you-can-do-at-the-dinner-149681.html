Recent findings suggest that there are certain occasions that call for covert calm. You know what I mean. Those times when you’re incredibly stressed, uncomfortable, or anxious but can’t really extricate yourself from the situation. Take, for example, the occasion dinner. If the thought of a large gathering and non-stop (as well as unfiltered) conversation fills you with even a slight sense of unease, subtle breathing exercises might just be your latest favorite mindfulness practice.

Industry observers note that your breath is a powerful tool. Practicing breathwork, even the quiet kind, can affect your physiological, mental, and emotional state, activating your parasympathetic nervous system and easing your entire being out of panic mode. Known as pranayama in yoga, breathwork is a broad category, allowing you to find the breathing that resonates with you and return to it whenever signs of stress arise.

It has been revealed that if you’re already dealing with low-level overwhelm, drawing attention to yourself and your nerves is likely the last thing you want to do. So, rather than jumping up from the table for a quick yoga pose or engaging in a liberating round of lion’s breath, subtle breathing exercises invite you to remain in your chair, appearing engaged as you regulate your nervous system.

According to recent reports, start with a count of 3 or 5 rounds of each of these breaths and see how you feel. If the tension is still palpable, take as many as you need to self-soothe. And if you forget any of the below in your situation, simply lengthen your exhalations.

According to recent reports, also known as diaphragmatic breathing, belly breathing invites you to take your deepest breath possible. At it’s core, this breathing exercise is about (surprise!) inhaling into your belly. This is, of course, anatomically incorrect. But think of the larger goal here, which is to direct your inhalations toward your belly without exaggerating the movement in your ribs. The results is the diaphragm lowers and creates space to breathe into your actual lungs.

In a recent development, belly breathing also gets your breath out of your upper chest, which is the sort of shallow, stunted breathing that often accompanies anxiety. Slowing and deepening your breath signals safety, reminding your animal self that this is not a moment for fight, flight, or freeze—even if it feels like one.

Sometimes evening things out is the answer. Box breathing involves a simple pattern of inhaling for a count of four, holding for a count of four, exhaling for a count of four, and holding for a count of four, thus (figuratively) creating a sort of square shape with your breath. If four is too short of a count for your natural breathing, you can change your number to five, six, or beyond, so long as each part of the breath (inhalation, exhalation, and holds) remains even.

Turning even part of your attention away from the group and toward your own breath gives you a break from the noise while demonstrating the power of your own body and mind. Box breathing is often used for focus, so don’t be surprised if you return to the conversation with more interest and alertness.

According to recent reports, the simplest option on this list, equal breathing is all about matching your inhalations to your exhalations. This is a practice of balance, so track the count of a deep inhalation, and if it feels okay in your body, apply it to your exhalation. It’s also the most adaptable, so feel free to vary the count slightly. You don’t want to stress yourself out, after all.

In addition to providing the aforementioned benefits of breathwork, redirecting your focus places your entire being in neutral, enabling you to consider your current circumstance with more clarity and calm.
