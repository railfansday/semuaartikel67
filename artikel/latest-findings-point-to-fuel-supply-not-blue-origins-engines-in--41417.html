Latest findings point to fuel supply, not Blue Origin’s engines, in loss of Amazon Leo satellites

Blue Origin’s engines look like they’re off the hook, at least for now.

Recent findings suggest that the two BE-4 engines Blue Origin makes for United Launch Alliance’s Vulcan rocket weren’t to blame for the low thrust in Sunday’s failed launch, according to preliminary findings released Wednesday. Instead, it appears the engines didn’t get enough fuel from the rocket, ULA noted.

Industry observers note that in its latest upgrade on the investigation, ULA remarked it had completed an initial integrated review of flight data. Preliminary data indicate the first stage’s low thrust came from “inadequate fuel supply from the vehicle to the BE-4 engines, not from the performance of the engines,” ULA said, adding that work remains to determine the cause.

Sources indicate that on Tuesday, ULA remarked early data suggested the first stage underperformed just after liftoff, leaving the rocket too low and too slow to put 40 of Amazon’s Leo internet satellites into orbit. It didn’t say then whether the engines played a role.

The BE-4s are made by Jeff Bezos’ Kent, Wash.-based Blue Origin. Blue Origin CEO Dave Limp said Wednesday on X that he was proud of the BE-4 team for its “rapid response and support of ULA’s investigation” and that the company looks forward to Vulcan’s return to flight.

The finding matters for Blue Origin beyond Vulcan. Its own New Glenn rocket uses seven BE-4s on its first stage, and Amazon has 24 New Glenn introduces reserved for Leo.

In a recent development, ULA repeated that Vulcan’s second stage appears to have started normally and that its RL10 engines operated as planned but couldn’t make up for the earlier loss of performance. The six Northrop Grumman solid rocket boosters, the source of problems on two earlier Vulcan flights, appear to have performed normally, ULA mentioned.

The Federal Aviation Administration is overseeing the ULA-led investigation. Amazon’s next Leo launch is an Ariane 6 mission carrying 36 satellites, targeted for no earlier than Nov. 23 from French Guiana.

ULA stated it will share more as the investigation continues.
