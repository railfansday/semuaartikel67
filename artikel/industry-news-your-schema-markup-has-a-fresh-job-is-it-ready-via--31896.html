In a recent development, the new job I see for schema markup is to help AI understand your business, not just make individual pages eligible for rich results. That means explicitly describing your products, services, people, and locations, showing how they connect, and keeping those facts and relationships accurate as your business changes.

One of the biggest questions I hear from enterprise marketing leaders today is: How do we make sure AI is getting our brand right? It’s a fair question.

According to recent reports, AI is increasingly influencing how customers discover, research, and choose businesses. Unlike traditional search, where customers could visit your website and form their own understanding, AI now brings information together from across the web to create answers on their behalf.

It has been revealed that that information might come from your website, a third-party directory, customer reviews, or an article published years ago. And not all of it is necessarily accurate, complete, or up to date.

According to recent reports, here’s what I think is crucial for marketers to recognize: You can’t control everything AI says about your business. But you can take greater control of the information you give it, and that starts with your website.

Your website is one of the most valuable sources of information you own. It’s where you define who you are, what you offer, the expertise you bring to market, and what you want your business to be known for.

This is why I believe preparing the information you own for AI is one of the most vital investments enterprise marketers can make today.

According to recent reports, and if your organization already has schema markup, you’ve already started building that foundation. The opportunity now is to think bigger about what it can do, and make sure your strategy is built for the job schema markup needs to do today and tomorrow, not just the one it did yesterday.

The good news is that if you’ve already invested in schema markup, you’re not starting from scratch. But not all schema markup is created equal. Before you focus on doing more, ask whether what you already have gives machines a clear, connected understanding of your business.

The opportunity is to assess the foundation you’ve built, identify where it could be stronger, and build from there.

Here are three questions I’d encourage enterprise marketing leaders to ask.

Having schema markup across your website is a great start. But is it capturing the information that actually matters to your business?

Think about your most notable products, services, locations, and areas of expertise. Does your markup describe them accurately and in enough detail? Or is it simply meeting the minimum requirements for a search feature? The goal isn’t just more schema markup; it’s more meaningful markup.

It’s one thing for machines to recognize the individual parts of your business. It’s another for them to understand how everything fits together. Are your people, products, services, and locations consistently identified and connected? Can machines recognize when different pages are talking about the same thing?

Industry observers note that think about a healthcare system. It might have schema markup describing its physicians, hospitals, and medical services. That’s a great start. But does that information clearly communicate which physicians specialize in which treatments? Where they practice? Which services are available at each location? And how all of those things connect back to the healthcare system?

Move beyond describing individual pages to communicating a connected picture of your business.

Recent findings suggest that your business is constantly evolving. New services, new locations, new expertise. And as your website content changes, your schema markup needs to keep up. Because it’s not enough for your website to tell one story to customers while your structured data tells another to machines.

As your website evolves, does your schema markup keep pace, reflecting the same accurate information and maintaining the relationships that connect your business?

Our Wells Fargo case study illustrates why this matters. Wells Fargo encountered an AI Overview that incorrectly identified a branch as permanently closed. By strengthening the structured information on its website, the team helped address that inaccurate answer.

Recent findings suggest that you can’t control every answer AI generates. But you can take greater control of the accuracy and consistency of the information you’re giving it to work with. And that requires governance to be part of your approach from the beginning.

Recent findings suggest that we need to think differently about what we’re asking our schema markup to accomplish. We should question whether we’re giving machines the information and context they need to understand our business as a whole, and recognize our relevance to the topics that matter most.

But here’s the challenge: Across large enterprise websites, that information is often spread across thousands of pages, managed by different teams, and constantly changing. Even when the information is accurate on individual pages, it doesn’t always tell a clear, connected story about the business.

And sometimes, we’re contributing to the confusion ourselves. As Schema App’s CTO Mark van Berkel discusses in Forbes, AI doesn’t always need to hallucinate incorrect information. Sometimes, it works with conflicting information businesses have already published.

Recent findings suggest that if we want AI to understand our business, we need to give it more than individual pieces of information. We need to help it understand how those pieces fit together. And for large enterprises, that’s not a small challenge.

Think about everything your organization publishes across its website. Your products and services, locations, your people and their expertise, the topics you want to be known for. All of that information helps tell the story of your business. But across thousands of pages, different teams, and constantly changing content, those connections aren’t always clear.

What if we started thinking about our website not just as a collection of pages, but as a connected source of truth about our business? That’s the opportunity I see with a Marketing Context Layer.

A Marketing Context Layer is a governed foundation of information about your business that helps machines understand who you are, what you offer, what you’re known for, and how it all connects. It starts with the information you own and publish on your website.

Through advanced schema markup, you can make notable business facts and relationships explicit, connecting them into a Content Knowledge Graph that can be maintained as your business evolves.

It has been revealed that and this isn’t just a marketing conversation. Forrester has been exploring the importance of context layers in enterprise AI, recognizing the role of connected business knowledge in helping AI systems interpret and use information. I believe there’s an notable opportunity to apply that same thinking to marketing. If enterprises are investing in governed business context to power AI internally, shouldn’t we also be thinking about the context we’re publishing for AI to understand us externally?

Sources indicate that this is where schema markup becomes much more than an SEO tactic. It’s the starting point for building a connected, governed foundation of information about your business. One you can keep improving, maintaining, and putting to work as search and AI experiences evolve.

As our CTO, Mark van Berkel, explores in Forbes, the organizations that want to make meaningful use of AI need to invest in the information foundations that support it.

I believe we’re at an exciting turning point for enterprise marketers. We’ve spent years investing in our websites, creating content, and building our presence in search. Now we have an opportunity to make those investments work harder by helping machines understand the business behind the content.

And as AI continues to evolve, from search and answers to emerging agentic experiences, having clear, connected, and reliable business information will only become more crucial.

Recent findings suggest that because the goal isn’t just to be found. It’s to be understood. And that starts with taking ownership of the information we control.
