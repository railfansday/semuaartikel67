In The Last Animal, historian Kyle Harper asks a provocative question: how have humans affected animal biodiversity?

It has been revealed that explorations of species loss driven by humans are nothing fresh. But other books tend to focus either on the present day or on prehistory — the extinction of ice-age animals such as woolly mammoths and dire wolves, for instance. The Last Animal offers a continuous account of biodiversity from prehistoric times to the present day.

Industry observers note that assembling a historical account on this scale is a Herculean task. But, Harper argues, it is only by zooming out that people can appreciate how slow changes in humanity’s relationship with other species have led to the current biodiversity crisis. Humans have been reshaping animal communities for millennia, long before written records of extinction existed. By reflecting on how humanity got here, he contends, we give ourselves the best chance of turning things around.

The book is a triumphant piece of scholarship, both rigorous and engaging. And Harper’s ability to draw on ecology, Earth science and environmental history is impressive. At the same time, the book reveals limitations in previous research approaches. And it highlights how a lack of crosstalk between research fields prevents scholars from seeing the full picture.

It has been revealed that harper begins by discussing the spread of ancient humans across the world, starting with the emergence of Homo erectus nearly two million years ago. Human dispersal coincides with a marked disappearance of large animals from the fossil record. The reasons for this are uncertain. Some scientists point to climate change, but Harper suspects human hunting activity. There is strong evidence that humans were involved in the extinction of many turtle species, for example, which must have been easy and delicious prey for hungry hunters.

Recent findings suggest that humans were shaping animal life long before the dawn of farming, Harper notes. Mutually beneficial relationships with cattle, llamas and dogs, for instance, opened the door to domestication and selective breeding. And in Cyprus, prehistoric settlers intentionally introduced wild boar and fallow deer as game, permanently changing the island’s ecosystem.

Motorized vessels and industrial fishing practices have reshaped marine ecosystems.Credit: Getty.

Sources indicate that later, deforestation and urbanization affected biodiversity. Since medieval times, commercial fishing has disrupted intricate marine food webs. And from the nineteenth century onwards, industrial farming, railways, refrigeration and pharmaceutical drugs have made it possible to raise vast numbers of cattle and battery hens in unnaturally good health, and in locations far from the cities that they eventually feed. These transformations in how humans live have had unintended and often surprising side effects on animal species. In the 1990s in India, for example, farmers gave the painkiller diclofenac to their ageing cattle, not realizing that it would remain in their systems and poison any vultures that fed on their carcasses. The birds experienced a sharp population decline as a result.

According to recent reports, writing about history with such a wide scope requires evidence from many disciplines. Harper’s chapters on prehistory lean heavily on fossils, archaeological finds and ancient DNA. Meanwhile, his historical chapters draw mainly on written records, including colonial accounts of animal extinctions that occurred as a result of European imperial expansion.

In part, this split reflects the fact that there are limited ways to learn about events that pre-date the invention of writing. But, Harper stresses, academic fields have also maintained somewhat arbitrary limitations on the time periods that they cover. For example, during the nineteenth century, when it became apparent that humans had existed for many millennia, historians decided to study only events that occurred after the advent of writing. They ceded “the murky time before written texts” to archaeologists, anthropologists and Earth scientists.

Recent findings suggest that yet, many changes in biodiversity span that boundary. Although the last woolly mammoths (Mammuthus primigenius) died out at the same time as Egyptian pharaohs ruled, their disappearance was part of a process that began during the Ice Age.

Industry observers note that even when extinctions are well documented, their causes and ecological consequences can remain difficult to disentangle. For instance, European arrival in, and later settlement of, the Mascarene Islands in the Indian Ocean during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries led to the loss of more than 50 animal species, including the dodo (Raphus cucullatus). Researchers don’t know to what extent introduced plants, animals and diseases contributed to these extinctions, or how colonial deforestation and hunting might have affected the island’s animals. These extinctions could be studied using a variety of scientific tools, from ecological modelling to ancient-genome analysis — but these opportunities have remained under-explored.
