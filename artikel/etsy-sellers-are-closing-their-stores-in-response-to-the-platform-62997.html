Etsy sellers are closing their stores in response to the platform's fresh AI training policy

Online marketplace Etsy is facing backlash from users over a recent change to its policies that allow it and other companies to use content created by sellers to train AI models. In response, numerous vendors have closed their online shops, and are encouraging others to do the same. Etsy, however, says the latest policy isn't meant to "replace your creativity," but simply to improve storefront operations.

That seems pretty straightforward—anything you put on your Etsy page can be fed into the AI theft machine like Steve Buscemi in a Cohen Brothers film—and as reported by Cartoon Brew, it led to significant pushback from some Etsy people, including numerous store closures.

Industry observers note that you might have heard of Etsy's upcoming terms and artists fleeing the platform. We're out, too.

In the wake of that reaction, Etsy vice president of customer operations James Ossman posted a separate update saying it's not really like that at all, and that "we do not use your content to train AI models designed to copy your original work or replace your creativity.".

"When we describe how we may use your content, we're enabling actions like translating your listing descriptions so buyers around the world can discover your items, or featuring your listing photos in Etsy marketing campaigns to showcase your creative work," Ossman wrote. "We also use your content to develop tools that make running your shop easier, like listing optimization tools or stats summaries that help you better understand your shop's performance. As always, your needs and feedback help guide how we build and improve these tools.".

As for providing third-party access to that content, Ossman mentioned that too is for "services and tools that can help you run your business," and promised that "when we partner with third parties, we do so thoughtfully to help protect your content and data.".

Sources indicate that that hasn't satisfied everyone. Ossman's commitment doesn't exist within the actual terms of service, which as seen above states only that Etsy and partners can "use Your Content to develop, train, test and improve artificial intelligence and machine learning technologies." Ossman also noted in his update that the terms "prohibit scraping Etsy or unauthorized use of content from Etsy to train AI models," but that (as far as I can tell) applies to Etsy users and unauthorized third-parties, not Etsy itself or those it licenses.

That's not to suggest that Etsy is being underhanded, merely that the binding legal language is imprecise. Others also complain that AI training should be opt-in rather than on by default—and worse, there's apparently no opt-out option available at all, so users who are opposed to having their work used for AI training for any reason are out of luck.

Ironically, the blowup over Etsy's embrace of AI training comes just weeks after it earned plaudits for banning AI-generated recipes and patterns, a move Ossman said was necessary to establish "trust" in the platform: "Sellers need clear guidance and confidence that their creativity and craftsmanship will be represented fairly; buyers need to understand what they’re buying and the human involvement behind it.".

According to recent reports, however this ultimately shakes out, the Etsy AI backlash is emblematic of a growing problem on both sides of the buy-sell divide: People want refuge from the creeping tendrils of the AI behemoth, and overtly or insidiously it's becoming increasingly difficult to find one. The more that happens, the more people are likely to react forcefully when platforms like Etsy start edging in the direction, regardless of any promises (or even carved-in-legal-stone commitments) of good intentions.

I've reached out to Etsy to ask if the fresh AI policy might be changed in response to the complaints and closures, and will update if I receive a reply.
