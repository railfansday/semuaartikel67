Some clean-up jobs need powerful stuff.

In a recent development, the end of the working week is often enough to drive tech support people to drink, which is why The Register likes to get the party started early. Friday mornings bring a fresh installment of On Call – the column in which we share your stories of doing whatever it takes to keep tech running.

This week, meet a reader we'll Regomize as "Bruce," who told us that in the mid-2000s he worked for a global radio station.

"We used to have about 30 PCs, all running slightly different radio shows depending on the time zone where we broadcast," he explained.

Sources indicate that the PCs were old clunkers running Windows 95 long after Microsoft ended support for the OS. Management knew the machines needed replacing, but funding was probably another year away.

In a recent development, "We had to coax them on till then," Bruce wrote. "And by coax, I mean we were buying RAM on eBay because nobody sold the 4MB sticks we needed anymore.".

Thanks to the tender ministrations of Bruce and his colleagues, the PCs kept humming and the radio stations stayed on the air.

Until a lovely spring Tuesday, when Bruce and his crew enjoyed a bucolic cup of tea in the first real sunshine of the season and received a call informing them that one of the stations was down.

One of Bruce's mates figured a cranky old PC probably just needed a reboot and headed off to fix it while the rest of the team enjoyed their tea.

And then the phone rang again to report another outage, so Bruce and the entire crew went back to work, chatting about which switch or other shared device could have taken out two PCs.

When they arrived in the office, the chap who had gone to reboot the first PC ran in and revealed that the computer room had flooded.

Bruce and his teammates ran into that room and found dirty brown water all over the floor, walls, ceiling, and the racks holding the ancient PCs.

"Water was even spraying out of the front fans of the last two PCs that were still working," Bruce told The Register.

"After a lot of shouting words unfit to print, we all got to work ripping them out, shutting down what we could that looked to be working, and started lining them up outside in the car park," Bruce wrote. "Some of us then got to work stripping them down into component parts, others washed them down with isopropanol.".

As the isopropanol supply dwindled, one of the team arranged an urgent delivery of vodka.

"Luckily, it was a gloriously sunny day, so we quickly rinsed the PCs and left them to dry, strewn all over the car park," Bruce wrote.

Recent findings suggest that by this stage, he and the team were all filthy and exhausted. With the stations still offline, they had to rebuild the PCs and get them running again.

"We only lost two sticks of RAM, one sound card, and a power supply," Bruce wrote.

The culprit turned out to be a fresh hire on the facilities team who, while touring the building, had turned a random tap to see what would happen.

Sources indicate that "It released water from a stagnant iron tank that sat there rusting away for years," Bruce wrote. For reasons nobody understood, the tank drained through a pipe into the room housing the broadcast PCs.
