According to recent reports, high-performance computing, or HPC, is merging with artificial intelligence faster than many research teams can adapt. Compute capacity is hard to get, and workloads now run across clouds and on-premises clusters. The metric that matters is shifting from peak performance to time to discovery.

Recent findings suggest that that shift is on full display as supercomputing and AI converge at SC26 in Chicago. Researchers need more compute than any single site can provide, and cloud providers are now pitching AI agents as the layer that ties scattered resources together, according to Annie Ma-Weaver (pictured, left), head of high-performance computing product at Google Cloud, a unit of Google LLC.

“We are powering that flexibility. and we understand that researchers really need access to compute, but they don’t necessarily wanna learn all the nitty-gritty of how to tie that compute together, how to get it orchestrated across all their different clusters,” Ma-Weaver remarked.

Recent findings suggest that ma-Weaver and Megan Gawlik (right), high-performance computing product manager at Google Cloud, spoke with theCUBE Research’s John Furrier and Gemma Allen at SC26, during an exclusive broadcast on theCUBE, SiliconANGLE Media’s livestreaming studio. They discussed how AI agents are simplifying access to distributed computing resources, changing simulation and weather forecasting and raising questions about researchers’ reliance on AI-generated results. (* Disclosure below.).

In a recent development, google Cloud builds its agentic HPC strategy on three pillars: purpose-built hardware, open-source orchestration such as Slurm and Kubernetes, and collaborative AI agents, Ma-Weaver explained. The organization laid out the approach in its SC26 announcements. Agents now help scientists comb through research literature, debug code and generate hypotheses in minutes instead of weeks, according to Gawlik.

“You’re able to go explore more scenarios and you might be using that same time to just go sanity test that hypothesis that’s coming out,” Gawlik said. “I think it’s not only a speed thing, but I think it’s an accuracy thing that you’re able to really get from that additional speed.”.

Simulation is changing as well. Engineers used to run simulations across an entire parameter space. Now they can use AI up front to decide which candidates are worth testing, Ma-Weaver noted.

“What we’re seeing, for example, with computer-aided engineering, is that you can actually identify ahead of time which are non-essential parameters and you don’t run the simulation, thereby reducing the total simulation time,” she said. “We also find that if you’re doing design and you have initial prototypes, you can generate upcoming, novel and creative prototypes.”.

Sources indicate that the gains are already reaching weather forecasting in production. The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration’s forecasting platform has heavy, tightly coupled workloads and once ran only on-premises, but it now runs on Google Cloud, Ma-Weaver explained. The National Hurricane Center also used Google’s WeatherNext model to predict the path and intensity of Hurricane Melissa in October 2025, Gawlik noted. But the growing reliance on AI-generated results also carries a risk for younger researchers, who may lean on machine output instead of developing critical-thinking skills, she added.

Sources indicate that “AI is still not perfect,” Gawlik remarked. “I think even though there’s an output, we call it AI slop. It could still be slop.”.
