Advanced Micro Devices (AMD -9.22%) stock would need to rise about 57% just to get back to its record. As of this writing, shares trade near $413, off about 36% from their Oct. 6 closing high of $649.42.

The fall came fast. Shares first closed more than 35% under the record on Nov. 5, just 30 days after the peak. They sank 17% that day alone.

Sources indicate that A bounce to around $585 a week later didn't last. The stock fell again after a short-seller report on Nov. 19, and a broad sell-off in chip stocks on Tuesday pushed it back near its Nov. 5 close.

Recent findings suggest that AMD shareholders have been here before. In daily price data since 1980, the chipmaker's stock has fallen 35% or more from a record six other times, though never this fast.

Industry observers note that it eventually recovered every time. But the waits ran from around a year and a half to almost 20 years, and none of those drops ended at the 35% level.

Measured from each record close to the next one, the last comeback was the fastest. After peaking in March 2024, AMD shares hit a fresh record in October 2025, around 1.6 years later. The drops that started in 1980 and November 2021 both took around 2.1 years to erase, and the one that began in 1997 took about 3 years.

Recent findings suggest that the other two took much longer. After peaking in August 1984, the shares didn't hit a latest record until March 1997 -- a wait of around 12.5 years. And after its June 2000 peak, AMD shares needed over 19 years, finally passing that high in January 2020.

In a recent development, AMD's record here is mixed. Four of the six drops were made up within three years, but two took over a decade.

Sources indicate that buying when AMD shares first fell 35% under a record never meant buying near the bottom. From the day each of the six drops crossed that line, the stock fell another 41% to 95% before it bottomed.

Measured from the highs, the lows ranged from 63% under the record (in April 2025) to almost 97% below (in July 2015). The 1984 drop didn't bottom until 1990, around 91% below its peak.

The first year was usually tough, too. A year after each crossing, AMD stock was down four times out of six, with losses of 27% to 45%.

Sources indicate that and a rise in that first year didn't mean a fast comeback. The stock was up 30% a year after its August 2024 crossing and hit a upcoming record around two months later. But it was also up 16% a year after its 1984 crossing, early in what became the 12.5-year wait.

This time, AMD's first close under the 35% line was at $404.40. Another 41% drop from there would put the stock around $240. That isn't a forecast, but it's what the smallest of AMD's six past drops would look like from here.

The business, meanwhile, is still growing fast. AMD's revenue for the third quarter of 2026 climbed 45% year over year to a record $13.4 billion, up from $11.5 billion in the second quarter. Management expects roughly $17 billion in fourth-quarter revenue, implying growth of about 66% year over year. This forecast also implies sequential growth of about 27%, accelerating from 16% in Q3.

Data center segment sales, which include the company's AI chips and server processors, almost doubled from the year-ago quarter, hitting $8.5 billion.

Recent findings suggest that the Nov. 19 short-seller report was from Hunterbrook, which disclosed a short position in AMD (a bet that the stock drops). It alleged that a blacklisted Chinese server maker kept getting newer AMD firmware, and Hunterbrook also argued that new U.S. export rules might tighten limits on AMD's foreign business. AMD stated the claims misrepresent its business and that it follows U.S. export laws. None of the allegations has been proven.

Recent findings suggest that based on analysts' average estimate for 2027, the stock trades at a forward price-to-earnings ratio of around 23. For a company expecting growth this fast, that looks like a reasonable price to me.

It has been revealed that overall, I think AMD's business gives investors good reason to own this growth stock. But the stock's own past is a good reason to hold less of it than the business alone could justify.

I'd want a stake small enough that a drop to about $240, or a wait much longer than two years, wouldn't push me to sell.
