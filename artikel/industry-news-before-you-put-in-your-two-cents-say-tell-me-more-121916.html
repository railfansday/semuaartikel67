Industry observers note that you finally get an afternoon alone with your sister over the holidays, and she tells you she’s thinking about leaving her job. Before she’s finished, you’re already wondering what she’ll do about health insurance, whether she has anything else lined up, and how she’ll juggle the kids’ schedules if her next job means going into the office. It’s been about 12 seconds, and you’ve only heard one sentence. You’re her major sister, though. You want to spare her some stress if you can. That’s what big sisters do, right?

There’s something about seeing someone you love, especially if it’s only a few times a year, that makes it tempting to squeeze six months’ worth of help into one visit. You want to hear how she’s really doing, but you also want to put in your two cents while you’re both in the same room. You’ve heard one sentence, and you’re already running it through your own filters, timelines, and experiences. What would you do? How soon would you do it? Pretty soon she’s answering your questions about health insurance, and you still don’t know why she brought up work in the first place. The momentum has shifted. You set out to give her space to talk, and you’re already two steps ahead, thinking about how she’d handle leaving. That’s how rapidly serial fixing can kick in.

What would happen if you paused instead and said something like, “Sounds like this has been weighing on you. Tell me more”?

“I’m probably going to stay through spring,” she notes. “I just needed to tell someone how much I dread going in.”.

Now you get to hear what it’s been like for her to stay.

Sources indicate that this is where "Support, Don’t Solve" can help you set a boundary with yourself and stay present in the conversation. You might feel uncomfortable sitting with what your sister just shared, especially if you’ve always thought that being a good massive sister means stepping in when you see trouble coming. Think of "Support, Don’t Solve" as an internal cue card you can pull out when you feel yourself shifting into fixer mode. What’s mine? What’s my role? You can listen and ask what’s been hardest about going into work every day.

There’s nothing passive about this shift. If she asks what you think, you can tell her. If you have a concern you’d like to share, you can say, “I have a thought about the timing. Do you want to hear it?” She might say yes. She might tell you she’s had enough of thinking about work for one afternoon. Start by acknowledging what she’s going through and giving her space to talk. Then take your cues from her. She owns the decision and the timeline, even if you have a very clear idea of what you’d do in her shoes.

The harder rep comes after you say “Tell me more.” Can you let your sister finish, even if she pauses and you know exactly how you could fill the silence? If you’re listening for an opening to say, “Have you even looked at what else is out there?” you’re probably switching to fixer mode. See if you can catch yourself there and come back to what she was saying. You might paraphrase what you heard to see if you got it right.

Industry observers note that being mindful of the energy you bring to the room and the energy you absorb can be hard work. Give yourself some grace as you practice meeting people where they are. Know when to engage, sit comfortably in the space someone is giving you, and share a little more about yourself. Use what you know from past conversations to recognize when it makes sense to keep things light and let a comment pass.

Industry observers note that it’s all about pacing and setting boundaries internally before you practice them externally. Remind yourself that other people have their own timelines, values, and roles. Some people may have a hard time understanding your life choices and the way you move through life. Your choices are yours, and you can give others the same room to make theirs. Stay curious about how they got there, and give yourself a beat when you feel the urge to fix, solve, or offer guidance they haven’t asked for.

During the holidays, you may need to shift into different gears and draw on more patience than you’re used to. Think about when during the visit you can reset and regroup. Give yourself a chance to check in, debrief if you need to, and then get back to visiting.

In a recent development, plan to go for a walk after lunch, even if lunch goes well. Be deliberate with your alone time as you get ready for the day or turn in for the night. If you’ve spent half the day reading the room, smoothing things over, or feeling like you have to be “on,” give yourself a few minutes to let some air out of your internal balloon. That short reset can make it easier to pause and practice "Support, Don’t Solve" later, when your sister is halfway through a sentence, and you can already feel the advice coming.

It has been revealed that some of the best holiday moments happen when you’ve been sitting around long enough that nobody’s checking the time. Someone tells a story you’ve heard a dozen times, and you still laugh. Your sister tells you something that probably wouldn’t come up on a quick phone call because she feels comfortable in the space you are creating for her. If you’re already thinking about what she needs to do before she heads home, you might just miss the afternoon you were looking forward to spending with her.
