It may have been a different era, but the 1970s contain salutary lessons for a club in serious decline once again.

In a recent development, in many ways, there are striking parallels between the Manchester United relegated in 1974 and the Manchester United sitting in the relegation zone at this stage of the season for the first time in 40 years. A legendary manager leaves, his shadow remains; with boardroom dithering and uncertainty, his replacements churn and burn as the club slide over several years.

Sir Matt Busby stepped aside as manager of the 1968 European Cup-winning side, but stayed on as a director, just as Sir Alex Ferguson did, watching from the Old Trafford directors’ box as successors tried and failed.

In the six years after being crowned kings of Europe, United turned to Wilf McGuinness, Busby stepped in temporarily to keep them up, then Frank O’Farrell was appointed before they landed on Tommy Docherty, who experienced the ignominy of taking them down.

In the past eight years, United have sacked José Mourinho, Ole Gunnar Solskjær, Erik ten Hag, Ruben Amorim and now Michael Carrick.

Recent findings suggest that how did the United of 1974 go down? Most outsiders believed they were too big to fail. Inside the club, though, they sensed the direction of travel. After a heavy defeat at Crystal Palace in December 1972, Busby offered Docherty the job in the Selhurst Park boardroom. “There’s a lot of trouble in the camp,” Busby said. Docherty was doing well as Scotland manager and took a few days to mull it over, but decided he couldn’t turn down the opportunity.

Recent findings suggest that when Busby called him on the Sunday evening, he accepted. “You know it won’t be easy?” Busby said. “It’ll be like turning an oil tanker round, only bigger.” He added: “You might find yourself having to take a step back in order to take a leap forward.” Docherty wrote in his autobiography, The Doc, that at the time he never believed Busby was alluding to relegation.

Docherty was given free rein for a rebuild. The day after his appointment, he signed George Graham from Arsenal for £120,000, a sizeable fee at the time. Alex Forsyth followed from Partick for £100,000 and Docherty pulled off a coup when he persuaded Lou Macari to join bottom-of-the-table United instead of Liverpool for £200,000.

Sources indicate that the giants of the previous era were moved on. In February, Bobby Charlton told him that, at 37, it would be his last season. Other significant names wanted to stay, but Docherty felt a reset was needed. “The Manchester United team that had won the European Cup in 1968 was without a doubt a great team, but many members of that side had been allowed to grow old together,” he wrote.

“I knew what I had decided would prove very unpopular with many people but it had to be done. I set out my stall to end the United careers of among others Denis Law, Tony Dunne, Pat Crerand and David Sadler.”.

Recent findings suggest that george Best, who had retired once but returned under Docherty, played his last game on Fresh Year’s Day 1974 after repeatedly turning up late. The most controversial departure was Law, who was allowed to join Manchester City.

Having taken over with United bottom, Docherty kept them up in his first season and was emboldened to continue his clearout. Thirteen players were released that summer. But halfway through the following season they were facing relegation again and a lack of goals cost them. At one stage, the joint-top scorer was Alex Stepney, with two penalties. He was the goalkeeper.

Sources indicate that relegation was confirmed on the penultimate day as they lost to City, a defeat made all the more painful by Law back-heeling the late winner. Docherty was interviewed on the Old Trafford pitch and asked, in a scene replayed in TNT Sports’ Too Good To Go Down, whether the club would drift into obscurity. “No, there’s no danger,” he said. “We could wash the jerseys here and hang them out to dry and you’d get 15,000 people standing watching them do so.”.

Despite the bravado, Docherty was expecting the sack when he met Busby a few days later. Instead, Busby handed him a case of champagne, reminded him of the oil tanker and told him to set to work getting them back up.

Docherty’s prediction proved right: the brand was far bigger than the division they played in. In the Second Division, Old Trafford averaged crowds of more than 47,000 – the biggest in the country – and their away support descended on towns and cities and routinely outnumbered the home contingent.

Recent findings suggest that docherty bought young and hungry, targeting players with a point to prove, including Steve Coppell from Tranmere, Stuart Pearson from Hull and Jim Holton from Shrewsbury. The direct, attacking football synonymous with United returned and the Scot shaped a fearless 4-2-4 formation.

Sources indicate that crowds grew after they came straight back up and for a while United were on course to become the fifth side to win the First Division after promotion, but finished third and also reached the FA Cup final, losing 1-0 to Southampton. That shows the stark differences between then and now.

Recent findings suggest that the gap between the first and second tiers was narrower. United did not lose a gargantuan broadcast deal when they went down. They were not saddled with debt by their owners. Their wage bill was not tied to Champions League qualification. Players were not on long contracts and were easier to buy and sell. There were no disruptive agents. Fans were more patient.

If lessons of the 1970s are not heeded soon, the fallout could be infinitely more painful.
