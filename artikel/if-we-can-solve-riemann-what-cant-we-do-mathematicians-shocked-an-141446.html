Industry observers note that the news came on a Friday — and it was ground-shaking. For months, reports have mounted of artificial-intelligence models toppling some of the toughest problems in mathematics, plunging the field into turmoil. But the latest claim was the most shocking yet: an AI model had helped to solve the field’s most famous open problem, the Riemann hypothesis.

AI is ‘the end of mathematics as we know it’ — where does the field go next?

AI is ‘the end of mathematics as we know it’ — where does the field go next?

Sources indicate that the proof was released on 20 November by mathematician Levent Alpöge at AI giant Anthropic in San Francisco, California, who used an advanced version of the firm’s Claude model. It came in the form of 17 million lines of code in the open-source programming language Lean, which translates mathematical arguments into computer-certifiable code. In a matter of hours, a separate set of mathematicians rushed out a human-written proof of the Riemann hypothesis, which is in the branch of number theory and pertains to the randomness of prime numbers.

In a recent development, alpöge’s proof has been independently verified by the non-profit organization behind Lean and the Mathlib Initiative, which runs the reference library of formalized mathematics results written in Lean. “We have full confidence that Alpöge’s proof of the Riemann Hypothesis is correct, and we congratulate him,” the community mentioned in a statement on 21 November.

The mathematics community has broadly accepted the claim, although responses differ wildly. Some celebrate Alpöge’s achievement, but others see the development as proof that humans are fighting against machines for the future of mathematics.

Nature hears reactions from five mathematicians on what solving the Riemann hypothesis means to them.

Mathematician Tasmin Chu is part of the Association for Human Mathematics, whose members commit to doing AI-free research.Credit: Elizabeth Xiao.

Tasmin Chu, PhD student in mathematics at the California Institute of Technology in Pasadena.

Industry observers note that A technology that can autonomously prove the Riemann hypothesis is no trivial matter. I think that it is imperative our society acts to pause AI development as soon as possible. Over the past year, mathematical institutions have faced the impossible task of adapting to the rapidly changing capabilities of AI in real time.

It’s enormously disruptive for early-career researchers — the people who are the intellectual future of our field. Soon other sectors of society will face the same complications.

My reaction is grave concern. AI companies urgently require democratic oversight. I do not think AI’s encroachment into math is inevitable, nor is the death of human mathematical practice inevitable. It’s a choice to disempower ourselves, just as it’s a choice to continue building these technologies despite grave safety concerns. I continue to practice mathematics without LLMs, because I believe it’s critical that scientific inquiry continues in the hands of human beings. Some of us at the Association for Human Mathematics — whose organizing committee I am on — are collectively making the same decision.

Harini Desiraju, lecturer in mathematics at the University of Warwick, UK.

Industry observers note that given the events of the past few months, this news wasn't entirely a surprise, but we need to wait to understand the Lean proof and let the experts verify it. It is several million lines of code, after all.

I am not a number theorist, so I might not have the same emotional attachment to the Riemann hypothesis. Nevertheless, I have deep appreciation for the ubiquity and the importance of the problem in several areas of maths and physics.

In a recent development, as for the recent events, I want to distinguish between formal proofs versus knowledge. It is an impressive feat to solve such a problem. However, because it is a Lean proof over millions of lines, it is going to take a while before the community gets real understanding, and validity of the proof. With an AI-independent approach available as well, it could present a great opportunity for discovery of new ideas and mathematics. We need to see how it all plays out.

Recent findings suggest that there is another discussion to be had, which involves the role of frontier labs in research. Most of us see the potential benefits of this technology, but it is of utmost importance that we work towards a future where the agency is with the scientific community and not a couple of companies.

Mathematician Marcus Du Sautoy is anxious about what’s next for his field.Credit: David Levenson/Getty.

Marcus Du Sautoy, mathematician and professor for the public understanding of science at the University of Oxford, UK.

It was real mix of emotions. A part of me claims, how exciting to be alive when this great problem was solved! I think even when Fermat’s last theorem was solved, we had the same excitement and the same melancholy: that great motivator is gone.

Recent findings suggest that mixed in with that, is the complete surprise that it ended up being proved this way. If you’d asked me three months ago, I would never have predicted it.

There’s quite a lot of anxiety about what ‘math 2.0’ is going to look like.

Xin Zhang, assistant professor in mathematics at the University of Hong Kong.
