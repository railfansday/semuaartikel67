Sources indicate that installing HVAC equipment and ductwork in unconditioned attics can increase energy costs, reduce comfort, and contribute to air leakage, condensation, mold growth, and indoor air quality problems. Plenum trusses create dedicated spaces above ceilings for ductwork and other mechanical systems within the home’s conditioned envelope, helping maintain consistent ceiling heights while improving energy efficiency and durability. Successful implementation requires early coordination among builders, architects, HVAC designers, and truss manufacturers, along with carefully planned duct layouts and continuous, airtight control layers to prevent moisture problems and ensure long-term performance.

Sources indicate that for decades, houses have been built with forced-air ductwork and heating and cooling equipment in unconditioned attics. The wide-open, easy-to-access attic space makes the practice common, convenient, and familiar for the framing and mechanical trades. But from a building-performance standpoint, it’s a terrible idea. Vented attics are extremely hot in summer and cold in winter, so every foot of duct placed in that location exposes the conditioned air within the ductwork to temperatures way beyond those inside the house.

According to recent reports, my organization, Aarow Building, has been building high-performance homes in Missouri for going on three decades now. We have a hot, humid climate and—like in many places—attic ductwork is common here, so we’ve seen plenty of examples of what can happen when you install HVAC equipment in an unconditioned space.

For starters, the extreme temperature difference between the conditioned air inside the ducts and the temperature outside them results in a house that uses more energy and is more expensive to heat and cool compared to a house with an HVAC system inside the conditioned space. And the additional expense isn’t limited to operational costs. HVAC ducts and equipment in unconditioned spaces must have higher capacities to compensate for the adverse conditions where they’re located, meaning the systems are more expensive.

Sources indicate that higher costs are not the only downsides. Supply and return air leaks are common with HVAC systems in attics and are problematic from a building-durability and performance perspective. Supply leaks waste conditioned air and pressurize the attic, which depressurizes the house, causing additional air leakage in the conditioned space—and in extreme scenarios, backdrafting of combustion appliances.

Recent findings suggest that backdrafting can release carbon monoxide into the home, creating a serious risk of poisoning. There’s also a lot of water vapor in flue gasses, which can create humidity problems and mold growth. Equally common are return leaks that pull dusty, humid, or hot attic air into the heating or cooling system, negatively affecting energy efficiency, occupant comfort, and indoor air quality.

Sources indicate that air-conditioning supply ducts, which are cold, often become condensing surfaces. Condensing water can ruin finishes and lead to mold growth that may or may not be visible. Mold you can’t see can be especially problematic for sensitive individuals who might experience cold-like symptoms, asthma, or other respiratory problems. Similar problems can occur in cold climates when warm, conditioned air contacts cold spaces, creating condensation and potential mold problems. Even the most reliable HVAC system, when located outside the enclosure, can lead to comfort complaints and callbacks that are hard to diagnose and fix.

Sources indicate that you can avoid all these problems by making room for HVAC ducts inside the house’s conditioned space during the design phase, before the house is built. The concept is not new—building scientists started advocating for ductwork within conditioned spaces in the late ’80s and early ’90s, and conscientious builders and designers paid attention. They developed methods for hiding ductwork in dropped hallway ceilings and in soffits that run along the tops of walls (see the drawings).

It has been revealed that these methods work—but from a design perspective, employing them can be tough on some homes. I’ve found that soffits in bedrooms are almost always unwanted by designers and clients. I think the best way to hide ducts, both massive and small, is to place them in ceiling chases using plenum trusses.

Industry observers note that my first exposure to plenum trusses came from architect Steve Baczek. For a period, Steve was working with a production builder in the Southwest United States that offered a comfort guarantee for every home they built. One of the ways they ensured comfort was by keeping the HVAC system inside the building envelope. In some parts of the house, Steve hid ductwork in soffits, but he also used plenum trusses in places where soffits were impractical. The idea of using plenum trusses stuck with me.

We eventually began using plenum trusses in some of our builds for the same reasons as Steve: greater comfort, efficiency, and building durability. In 2017, our company made the decision that ductwork and HVAC equipment would be installed inside the conditioned enclosure whenever possible. In homes with a conditioned attic, the solution is easy. The ducts can simply live in the attic. The same is true when we have a conditioned crawlspace or basement available for distribution.

Recent findings suggest that many of the homes we build, however, are slab-on-grade with a vented attic above them. Those homes require another strategy, and plenum trusses have become one of our preferred solutions. They allow us to maintain consistent ceiling heights throughout the house while still providing a dedicated space for ductwork and other mechanical systems inside the building envelope. The result gives us the architectural appearance we want without compromising the mechanical strategy, occupant comfort, or energy efficiency.

Plenum trusses are engineered with an open space free of chords and braces that becomes a cavity for ducts, pipes, and refrigeration lines running perpendicular to the trusses. This plenum cavity is lined with an air-impermeable material, typically wood sheathing or drywall for airtightness. Ducts can also be run in angled plenums or in an airtight plenum space made between two trusses, as seen in the photos below.

It has been revealed that parallel Plenum: In this space, eight girder trusses have an opening that allows a duct to pass through them and then turn 90° into a plenum space built on-site from taped sheathing between two of the girder trusses. Both site-built and engineered plenum spaces need a continuous air barrier—in this case, taped sheathing—to prevent air movement through the plenum space.

Recent findings suggest that angled Plenum: To get the conditioned air to its intended registers most efficiently, the main plenum on this project is angled and installed within 10 individually engineered trusses. Each truss has the plenum space offset a few inches from the previous truss’s plenum space, creating the angled plenum. It took about an hour with the truss designer to strategy this layout.

Sources indicate that A plenum truss offers a way to design a house’s framing around the mechanical systems, rather than squeezing ductwork into whatever leftover space is available. In simple terms, the bottom chord of a plenum truss is notched with a framed rectangle above, creating a dedicated service cavity, or chase, above the finished ceiling. Importantly, the chase is within the building enclosure, beneath the air, insulation, and vapor control layers—unlike an unconditioned attic, which is essentially outside the building. In addition to ductwork, the plenum space can also house electrical and low-voltage wiring, pipes, and other mechanical systems while keeping them inside the conditioned space of the home.

According to recent reports, the notable point is that the chase is designed, not discovered. You can’t build the chase with field alterations after the trusses arrive. By then, the rooflines, heel heights, load paths, bearing points, ceiling planes, and mechanical layout are already largely locked in. A plenum-truss strategy can only work when the builder, architect, mechanical designer, and truss supplier are involved early on and work together. In my experience, the biggest challenge has been the truss design. Nearly every builder or designer I know who uses plenum trusses has encountered some degree of resistance or hesitation from a truss manufacturer, which I think is understandable. We are asking the truss designer to create something more complicated than a conventional roof truss while accommodating structural loads, duct dimensions, roof geometry, bearing locations, and the other constraints of the building.

The best results we have had come from working directly with the truss designer early in the process. Whenever possible, we sit down with the designer and work through the layout before the trusses are finalized. An extra hour spent with a truss designer is how you end up with a plenum large enough to fit and efficiently route the ducts while framing the complicated rooflines typical of the custom homes we build. Modern truss-design software makes it relatively easy to look at the proposed truss geometry and determine if the plenum will be substantial enough and correctly positioned for efficiently routing ducts that are the right size.

Plenum ceilings use furred-down framing or drywall-suspension systems to lower the entire ceiling. The system requires a second air barrier above the furred-down section(s), adding significant cost and complexity to the whole ceiling assembly and not just the plenum service cavity.

In a recent development, lowered ceilings refer to a section of lowered ceiling (often in hallways), and were pioneered by building scientists as a way to keep ductwork in the conditioned space. The system is less expensive than dropping an entire ceiling, but many clients find the lowered portion of ceiling makes the hallway or other space feel oppressive or confining.

According to recent reports, wall-to-ceiling soffits provide a space between the ceiling and the top of built-in cabinetry. Unfortunately, the design is often limited to concealing small ducts, and clients and designers often object to soffits outside the kitchen, especially when they obstruct a view or look like an afterthought.

Sources indicate that conditioned attics bring the insulation, air-control, and vapor-control layers to the roof sheathing, typically with spray foam on the underside of the roof sheathing. But insulating an entire attic with spray foam is expensive, and conditioned attics add a lot of space that must be temperature-controlled, increasing costs over time.

Once the house design is finalized, the next step is to determine where the ducts and equipment need to go. That means the HVAC design must be figured out before the trusses are ordered. The air-handler location, trunk lines, branch runs, register locations, return ducts, and grilles need to be finalized during the design process. A real duct layout matters. A rectangular box on a initiative marked “mechanical” is not enough, so we engage our mechanical design team early in the process and make sure the duct size and routing is specified in their HVAC design. Leaving the duct sizing and routing to whoever reveals up on installation day is never the right approach.

Once the duct layout is understood, the truss designer can design the truss framing to accommodate it. The chase needs to be deep and wide enough for the ductwork and anything else other trades require in the plenum space. It also needs to provide reasonable access for the people doing the work. If a duct must be bent, crushed, or forced around a framing member, the truss and/or HVAC design has failed. This is where builders need to be assertive in preconstruction. It’s not enough to simply ask whether the ductwork will fit—the HVAC designers should be able to identify on the plans where it will fit. By the end of the design process, the framing plan, mechanical plan, and truss drawings must agree with one another.

According to recent reports, A plenum truss only improves building and HVAC performance if the service cavity is within the home’s conditioned enclosure, without any gaps in the control layers. The air-barrier location has to be clear, which can be verified using what building scientist Joe Lstiburek and architect Steve Baczek describe as the “red pen test.” You should be able to trace the home’s entire air barrier location on the looks without lifting a red pen. If the ceiling drywall is the primary air barrier and vapor-control layer, then the plenum needs to be detailed so the ceiling drywall connects to the drywall or sheathing surrounding the plenum cavity, keeping the air- and vapor-control layers continuous. In most cases, this means the plenum is drywalled inside, ducts are installed and sealed, and then ceiling drywall is installed below the plenum, creating a flat ceiling with hidden ductwork above.

We use Zip System sheathing as our air control layer in the plenum space and on the rest of the building enclosure. Our framing crews install and tape the sheathing in the plenum space as part of their framing contract. The material might cost a little more than a drywall air barrier, but we find that having the framers install the air barrier works better with job sequencing than having the drywallers at the project twice, first to install and tape the plenum drywall and a second time to drywall the rest of the house. Whether you use drywall or taped sheathing, the takeaway is the same: All three sides (sides and cap) of the plenum, and where it meets the ceiling drywall, must be airtight.

Sources indicate that the enclosure must also be airtight for vapor control, as most water that condenses on building surfaces and in cavities leading to mold and damage is the result of water vapor carried by air leaks that condenses on cold surfaces. Condensation can occur if the plenum space is not properly sealed from air movement, so you need a continuous air control layer in the plenum space and throughout the rest of the building for effective air and vapor control.

There’s no question that plenum trusses require more preconstruction planning than installing ducts and air handlers in unvented attics, and they typically add to the cost of a truss package. But the upfront planning and additional costs should be weighed against the cost of comfort complaints, wasted energy dollars, mold risk, and the labor involved with sealing penetrations and insulating ducts and HVAC equipment in unvented attics. Keeping ducts and HVAC equipment out of unconditioned spaces makes longer-lasting, more comfortable houses, which makes the additional effort and expense a smart investment for our clients.
