Movie Theaters Are Back, but the Rest of Hollywood Seems to Be Crumbling

🌐 Source: Indiewire • 📅 17 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 4 min read (766 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways
  • Industry observers note that the pandemic forced many of them to shut their doors for months.
  • Industry observers note that at long last, things are finally changing.
  • Audiences are showing up for superhero movies and three-hour documentaries about a convicted felon.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Movie theater owners have been through hell.

Industry observers note that the pandemic forced many of them to shut their doors for months. When they could finally turn their marquees back on, Omicron and other variants made audiences skittish about returning to cinemas. Then the writers’ and actors’ strikes upended production, leaving them with fewer movies to screen. A promised moviegoing revival kept getting delayed or disrupted, often by circumstances outside of the exhibition industry’s control.

Industry observers note that at long last, things are finally changing. Domestic ticket sales are up 20 percent year over year, and with potential blockbusters like “Dune: Part Three,” “Avengers: Doomsday,” and “The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping” on the horizon, analysts expect revenues to easily eclipse $10 billion for the first time since COVID. Grosses in North America right now sit at $8.76 billion through November 15, fueled by franchise fare such as “Spider-Man: Brand New Day” and “Toy Story 5,” but also by unconventional hits ranging from “Project Hail Mary” and “The Odyssey” to “Obsession” and “You Can See Everything.” Related Stories Desire, Death, and a Breakthrough Year for Queer Cinema ‘The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping’ Review: They Shoot Franchises, Don’t They?

Audiences are showing up for superhero movies and three-hour documentaries about a convicted felon. And media companies, which treated the theatrical business as the sick man of the entertainment industry, are finally seeing the value in cinemas, with the likes of Amazon MGM Studios and Skydance committing to making more movies for the significant screen than most major studios did over the last decade. Better yet, younger viewers are driving the comeback, with studies showing that Gen Z members are the most frequent moviegoers.

It has been revealed that that’s the good news. Elsewhere, Hollywood seems to be crumbling. Netflix, which has done more to reshape the entertainment industry this century than arguably any other company, is teetering, having jettisoned both chief content officer Bela Bajaria and film chief Dan Lin in a brutal purge. Ted Sarandos, who is clearly feeling the heat after the company’s stock has fallen 35.6 percent year-to-date, has brought in Peter Rice, a well-regarded former Fox and Disney exec whose calm demeanor and unthreatening good looks are out of c-suite central casting, to steady the ship.

As Hollywood has stumbled around trying to develop a better business model, the solution that many companies have landed on is to get bigger, fast. Thus, Fox sold to Disney, MGM was auctioned off to Amazon, and David Ellison’s Skydance bought Paramount and then promptly merged it with Warner Bros. Others, such as Comcast, spun off assets to make themselves more digestible. Has it worked? That’s the $80 billion question at Skydance, where executives have pledged to make 30 movies a year and turn a combined Paramount+ and HBOMax into a true Netflix challenger. Yet Ellison must pull this off while paying down a massive amount of debt.

It has been revealed that so far, their early moves haven’t inspired much confidence. Executives like CBS News chief Bari Weiss have embraced the Silicon Valley ethos of “move fast and break things,” but what they’ve built instead, such as a simulacrum of “60 Minutes,” doesn’t seem better than what they’ve replaced. And if, as a recent Variety story about Skydance’s post-merger strategy suggests, part of their cost savings involves making sure executives don’t send out for “sandwiches and green juices” from Erewhon, they’re in for a world of pain. They can have Jimmy John’s cater lunch, and it won’t make a dent in their debt.

Sources indicate that skydance has already laid off most of Warners’ leadership and offered buyouts to the rank-and-file. Another wave of layoffs is expected at the company any day now. And across town, those types of cuts are the default move, with everyone from Disney and NBCUniversal to Amazon and Sony slashing hundreds of jobs. Are some of these layoffs in anticipation of what a leaner, AI-assisted future might bring? Probably. But with many of these companies’ share prices flatlining or falling, they aren’t being enacted out of foresight or a position of strength.

“I can’t remember a time when things have felt so scary,” an entertainment executive confessed to me recently.

Recent findings suggest that and they’re right. It’s always popular to say the movie business is dying or the television industry is collapsing, and those doomsayers have been proven wrong time and time again. People want to be entertained. It’s just that making money entertaining them has never been harder for Hollywood.
