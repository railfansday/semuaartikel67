According to recent reports, 1) You invest $10 in my company, on condition of (2)2) I use that $10 to buy your product. You make $10 of revenue, and $2 of profit on that transaction (let's assume $0 of operating costs, just to keep it simple)3) I still owe you $10, which you put down as an asset4) Your calculated company value goes up. Let's say you have a "value multiplier" of 10. Had you not made the sale, you'd have $10 of assets, $0 of revenue, and $0 of profit. Your company value would be $0 (profit based) or $0 (revenue based), and at best $10 (asset based). However, after the sale, you have a value of $20 (profit based) or $100 (revenue based). The $10 of assets don't really count here as it's already baked into the value (it's complicated, don't worry about it).

In a publicly traded tech giant, that valuation change means your share price will go up. Shareholders therefore make some paper money until they cash out for actual money. Good for the shareholders. However, the company can issue new shares are the higher price to get money for... more circular financing.

There's a multiplying force if my company grows too. It means I no longer owe you $10, instead I owe you $20 - which is still an asset on your books. In the above example, it could mean your multiplier goes from 10 to 11 or something, which again, bumps your share price up (and around we go all over again).
