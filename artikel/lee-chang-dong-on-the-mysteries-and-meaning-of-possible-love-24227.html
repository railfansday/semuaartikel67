According to recent reports, “He really believes this,” Kim adds. “He even told us stories about the handful of times in the past when the movie god appeared on his sets.”.

The most recent visitation came nearly a decade ago, during the tail end of production on Lee’s beguiling 2018 dramatic thriller, Burning.

In the film’s violent, enigmatic climax, the young protagonist, Jong-su (Yoo Ah-in), meets his wealthy rival, Ben (chillingly inhabited by Steven Yeun), on a remote road outside Seoul. Jong-su approaches as if to chat, then plunges a knife into Ben’s stomach. He shoves the body into Ben’s silver Porsche, strips off his bloodied clothes, throws them inside and sets the car on fire. Within the complicated framing of the story, it’s unclear whether this episode actually happened or was imagined.

Sources indicate that “That scene is shot as one long, continuous take during sunset, and they prepared for it very carefully,” Kim claims, recounting Lee’s story. “And if you remember, just as [Jong-su] is walking back to his truck naked, with the burning car behind him, snow starts falling heavily.”.

“This was the movie god, according to director Lee,” Kim adds. “They didn’t plan for it to snow in that moment; they couldn’t have. But it happened, and it’s so cinematic.”.

Within the Korean film industry, it’s no exaggeration to say that Lee himself is regarded as something of a deity, or at least a national treasure.

In a recent development, born in 1954 to an impoverished but educated family in the southern Korean city of Daegu, Lee began his working life as a high school teacher before establishing himself as a fiction writer. He won acclaim for stories delicately attuned to the agonies and ironies of Korea’s tumultuous postwar period, an era of brutal political repression and breakneck economic development. His characters tended to be sympathetic, disadvantaged outsiders — sensitive people placed in impossible predicaments by the wrenching forces of ideology and history.

Recent findings suggest that in the early 1990s, filmmaker friends recruited him to write screenplays, and in 1997 he made his directorial debut with Green Fish, a social critique disguised as a noirish crime movie. The film featured the first performance from Song Kang-ho, who would later become a national treasure in his own right through films like Park Chan-wook’s Joint Security Area and Bong Joon Ho’s Memories of Murder and Parasite.

Earlier this year, Squid Game creator Hwang Dong-hyuk told me that witnessing his favorite novelist — Lee — suddenly become a filmmaker was the moment that made him want to pursue a life in cinema himself.

“I idolized him, and his career pivot kind of gave me courage to go for it,” Hwang stated. “And then when I actually watched [Green Fish], it was stunning — like every film he has done since.”.

According to recent reports, five films followed before Possible Love, each an emotionally searing and intellectually electrifying portrait of people pushed to society’s margins. With Peppermint Candy (1999), Lee issued a devastating state-of-the-nation statement by telling a ruined man’s life in reverse. Moving backward through two decades of political repression, state violence and economic upheaval, the film gradually reveals how an innocent, hopeful young man became both an instrument and a casualty of his country’s corruption. In Oasis (2002), he portrayed an extraordinary romance between a damaged ex-convict and a woman with severe cerebral palsy, reveling in the desires and imaginative freedom that the world around them refuses to acknowledge. The film earned Lee the Venice Film Festival’s best directing prize.

He returned five years later with Secret Sunshine (2007), which follows a bereaved mother through a crisis so harrowing that even the promise of faith and forgiveness becomes a source of existential torment. Secret Sunshine‘s Jeon Do-yeon won the Cannes Film Festival’s best actress award for her powerful performance, a first for a Korean star.

Then came Poetry (2010), which continued Lee’s fusion of generous, humanist drama with challenging philosophical inquiry, following an elderly woman who takes up writing poetry as she confronts the onset of Alzheimer’s and her grandson’s involvement in a horrific crime — her search for beauty becoming inseparable from the obligation to face a truth she would rather not see. Lee won Cannes’ best screenplay prize for the film.

And Burning (2018), finally, transformed a short story by Haruki Murakami into a mesmerizing mystery of class resentment, thwarted desire and the existential angst of Korea’s contemporary youth. It became the first South Korean film shortlisted for the foreign-language Oscar — an embarrassingly late milestone, given how much indelible cinema the country had already produced. But the Academy quickly made up for lost time at the 2020 ceremony, when Parasite, directed by Lee’s friend Bong Joon Ho, won four Oscars, including best picture.

Industry observers note that lee’s legendary status at home also stems from the fact that his contribution to Korean cinema has gone well beyond his own filmmaking. In the 1990s, he became a leading campaigner for enforcement of the country’s screen quota, which required theaters to show domestic movies for a minimum number of days each year. By protecting local filmmakers’ access to audiences from Hollywood dominance, the policy created space for ambitious Korean cinema to flourish throughout the 2000s — a driving force behind the broader Korean cultural wave still unfolding today. From 2003 to 2004, Lee even served as South Korea’s minister of culture and tourism.

Over the summer, I flew to Seoul to meet the mercurial auteur Na Hong-jin at his office on the city’s east side. A favorite of both action fans and art-house aficionados, Na studied under Lee as a graduate student at the prestigious Korea National University of Arts and later became an international cult sensation with a string of singularly twisted genre movies: the serial killer thriller The Chaser (2008), brutal crime flick The Yellow Sea (2010) and the supernatural freakout The Wailing (2016).

Recent findings suggest that the occasion for our chat was the U.S. release of his divisive but wildly entertaining sci-fi action movie Hope. At the time, both his film and Lee’s Possible Love were viewed as leading contenders to represent South Korea in the international Oscar race. (Possible Love was ultimately selected and is now considered a frontrunner in the category.).

Sources indicate that in what I thought would be a light, throwaway question, I asked how it felt to be competing with his former teacher. Na, who has a rather intense, skeptical bearing even when he’s feeling friendly, now grew more serious, and perhaps more skeptical of his interlocutor.

“As a filmmaker, Lee Chang-dong is basically a god to me,” he said, raising his voice a little and staring at me implacably. “I don’t compete with god, do you?”.

Sources indicate that laughing at my startled reaction, he then softened his tone. “For those of us who came of age in the generations after him, Lee Chang-dong represents an older, in some ways better, era. His movies are difficult, but there’s a tolerance and understanding to his work that I find almost healing. I don’t know if this country still produces artists with that kind of depth.”.

Lee’s movie god never appeared on the set of Possible Love — but the production was not without its moments of magic.

The film, which is presently streaming on Netflix globally, reunites Lee with Sul Kyung-gu, the unforgettable male lead of Peppermint Candy and Oasis, and Jeon of Secret Sunshine. Together, thanks in no small part to their performances in Lee’s films, they are now regarded as screen royalty in Korea, having won seven Grand Bell Awards between them, their country’s equivalent to the Oscars.

Sul plays Ho-seok, an autoworker left unemployed and profoundly damaged after a strike at his factory was violently suppressed by the state; Jeon is his loving, resourceful wife, Mi-ok, who works in a mask factory to keep their family afloat.

Recent findings suggest that the drama begins to turn when their lives become unexpectedly entangled with those of an attractive couple from the opposite end of the class divide. Ye-ji (Cho Yeo-jeong, playing another rich wife as she famously did in Parasite) is an aspiring documentary filmmaker working on a project about workers’ rights in Korea, who becomes drawn to Ho-seok’s story. Her husband, Sang-woo (Hope’s Zo In-sung), is a dashing tycoon who seems happy to support her pursuits. The budding friendship among the foursome is warm, awkward and increasingly fraught, with Ye-ji’s attempts at empathy growing difficult to disentangle from her eagerness to make art from the other couple’s struggles.

Recent findings suggest that the film culminates with a group trip to the coast. Hoping to convince Ho-seok to finally sit for a lengthy interview, Ye-ji invites the other couple to her husband’s luxurious seaside family home for a weekend away. (Production designer Jang Min-woo and his team built the house from scratch to Lee’s specifications.) Dinner on the first night becomes a messy drinking session. At dawn, Ho-seok staggers out toward the sea, and Mi-ok, curious about this exit, surreptitiously follows. The camera tracks them into a wind-tossed forest, where Ho-seok’s suppressed rage fully erupts into a PTSD-driven breakdown: Screaming, he thrashes his body against the trees and branches around him, as his wife secretly watches in silence.

As Mi-ok retreats in distress, she encounters Ye-ji in a clearing, attempting to film this painful private moment. The two women stare at each other from opposite edges of the frame as the wind howls and the trees sway behind them. This tableau, which captures so much of the story’s complex meta-fictional pathos, later became one of Possible Love‘s key marketing images.

Originally, in Lee’s script, the sequence was supposed to take place on a calm, clear morning, notes Kim, the cinematographer.

“But on the day of our tech scout to the coast, it was a really windy day, and director Lee and I both thought it looked great,” he recalls.

One of his country’s most in-demand DPs, Kim is perhaps best known internationally for the baroque, highly stylized camerawork he employed on Park Chan-wook’s romantic thriller, Decision to Leave (2022). He also shot the hit second and third seasons of Squid Game. For Possible Love, Kim set aside his impressionistic approach and strove instead for the most naturalistic look he could achieve, shooting nearly the entire film with a handheld camera he carried himself and relying on natural light as much as possible.

“Director Lee agreed it would add a nice layer if we could shoot it in a heavy wind,” Kim recalls. “And since we would be filming at the house for a month, I convinced him we could get all prepped, but then wait to see if we got lucky with a windy day or two.”.

He adds: “And in my head, I thought, ‘Okay, this is where we’ll meet the movie god, right?'”.

According to recent reports, “But then, that whole month: no wind. Not even a strong breeze.”.

Although Lee is famous for the seriousness and solemnity with which he approaches his work, he is also widely admired in the Korean production world for the collaborative atmosphere on his sets.

According to recent reports, many leading auteurs readily discuss matters of craft but resist interpreting their work, preferring to leave its meaning to the audience. Lee believes it’s vital that his collaborators understand the ideas and nuances each scene is intended to convey. His cinema may ask philosophical questions that are ultimately irresolvable, but he is resolutely clear and intentional about how his choices put those questions into play.

Industry observers note that “If someone asks him something about a scene, no matter who it is on the crew — maybe it’s like an assistant set dresser — he will stop and thoroughly explain the scene, his intentions, and how it fits into the whole movie,” Kim indicates. “I’ve worked on a lot of films, but I had never seen a director who is so patient and generous with his thoughts like that.”.

So, when the winds never arrived for Possible Love‘s poignant sunrise moment, Lee’s team resolved to “create a crew god” for him instead.

“More than 100 people — the assistants, some of the cast, and every member of the crew except for three or four of us huddled around the monitor with director Lee — went into the forest and tied ropes to the trees and pulled like crazy so that the whole forest would sway like in a strong wind,” Kim recalls.

He adds: “It wasn’t anyone’s job, but we all did it, because we knew how much that scene meant to him — because he had told every one of us.”.

Possible Love premiered at Venice in September, winning the festival’s Silver Lion Grand Jury Prize. It later screened at the Toronto and New York film festivals, earning rave reviews at every stop. It currently has the highest Rotten Tomatoes score — 97 percent fresh from 112 reviews — of any major global release this year.

Possible Love was fully financed and produced by Netflix, and if the project seems a somewhat out-of-character choice for both Lee and the streamer (as more than a few critics have noted), it is also true that the film is the most critically acclaimed adult drama that the tech giant has ever made — holding higher average review scores than any of the other entries on Netflix’s conspicuously short list of potential enduring classics (The Irishman, Marriage Story, The Power of the Dog, All Quiet on the Western Front and Frankenstein all rank slightly lower, for example, while other lasting favorites, such as Alfonso Cuarón’s Roma, were acquired but not produced by the streamer).

According to recent reports, it is also the best-supported awards contender of Lee’s long but still-too-overlooked career. Netflix is mounting a multi-front Oscar campaign extending beyond the international category to best picture, best director, best original screenplay and the acting categories, with a particularly ambitious push for Jeon.

Netflix had quietly been courting Lee since the manufacturer rolled out operations in South Korea a decade ago.

“One of the top three rumors that’s always floating around the Korean film industry is that Lee Chang-dong has finished a new script,” indicates Minyoung Kim, Netflix’s top content executive in the Asia-Pacific region, who built the streamer’s formidable Korean operations from the ground up.

In recent years, Lee’s output has grown sporadic, with eight-year breaks between each of his last three features.

Industry observers note that “The reason his films take so long is that he deeply, almost painfully, thinks about why a story needs to be told,” Kim explains. “Many directors are happy when they’ve found an entertaining idea that works, or a great piece of IP to adapt; with director Lee, it all starts with an observation about reality, or a question he can’t resolve about life.”.

She says she’s aware of numerous potential projects — all of which sounded, to her creative exec ear, like compelling, ready-to-go premises — that Lee shelved because their themes didn’t feel urgent enough.

“So, every time we heard those rumors about a new script over the years, we checked in — just to be like, ‘We’re here; we’d love to work with you!'”.

“You could even say we were a little clingy,” Kim adds, laughing.

It has been revealed that netflix’s opportunity to partner with Lee came after the auteur struggled to secure the relatively modest budget he was seeking for Possible Love — reportedly around 8 billion won, or roughly $6 million — after the key cast agreed to fee reductions to help him get the project made. South Korea’s prolonged post-pandemic box-office slump has left its major studios increasingly cautious about backing serious dramas, even with filmmakers and casts of this stature. Kim believes Lee would eventually have found a local financier, but the studios’ hesitancy gave Netflix its opening.

Netflix agreed to a larger, undisclosed budget and put its production resources behind the film. The commitment extended to a limited international theatrical release and substantial awards-season support, while leaving all creative decisions — including final cut — in Lee’s hands.

“On a lot of our shows, we try to understand the director’s vision, and become their thought partner. We debate and discuss, and try to make suggestions on what we believe will make the film stronger,” Netflix’s Kim explains. Describing Lee as a maestro, she notes, “he didn’t need our opinions.”.

She adds: “We just tried to understand his vision as fully as we could, so we could support him at every step of the way.”.

The Hollywood Reporter recently spoke with Lee in Seoul about the creation of Possible Love and the ideas behind several pivotal scenes. The conversation includes spoilers.

In a recent development, from what I understand, Possible Love began more than a decade ago as a project about the workers involved in Korea’s real-life SsangYong Motor strike, which resembles the episode depicted in the film. You wrote a script and went into pre-production, then shelved it. What concerned you about that earlier version?

In a recent development, it’s true that the project began for me with the 2009 SsangYong Motor incident, but when I speak about the origins of the story today, I try not to mention the company or the specific year, because I don’t want too much emphasis placed on that particular incident. There have been many similar cases in the Korean labor movement, and I’d like the film to be viewed in that broader context. But yes, about 10 years ago, that case is what got me started. I did a lot of research, wrote a screenplay and went into preproduction. And then I shelved it — for two reasons.

Recent findings suggest that first, I was constantly asking myself: What meaning does it have for me to turn this episode into a dramatic feature? What does it mean to me personally? What will it mean to the audience? And what meaning will it hold as a film in itself? I just couldn’t find answers I had real conviction in. There was already a documentary about the strike and the lives of the workers involved, and I felt it was a very honest and thorough piece of work. [Lee is referring to Seo Seh-chin’s 2009 documentary Before the Full Moon.] Ultimately, I didn’t see how I could add to what the documentary had already achieved simply by turning it into a dramatic feature.

Industry observers note that the second factor was connected to feelings and beliefs I have long held as a creator. I was writing fiction back in the 1970s and 1980s, often about Korean society and the issues within it. Naturally, I wrote about my times, and because of what our country was going through, that meant writing about people who were suffering — people living under our authoritarian military government, factory workers facing hardship, students participating in the democracy movement and those hurting because of our divided nation.

Sources indicate that and I always ended up asking myself, “Am I worthy of telling this story? Does it hold meaning for me to be the one saying this?” I asked these questions because my life and my situation were often very different from those of the people I was portraying. I wasn’t suffering as badly as they were. So what right did I have?

Industry observers note that as I worked on the early versions of Possible Love, all these questions came back to me, and I couldn’t find conviction in any approach. So I shelved it.

Then, as the years passed, the issue of labor itself began to go through big changes. At the time of the strike, the focus was on the direct pain the laid-off workers were suffering as we entered a more global, capital-driven system — the suffering of those betrayed and abandoned. Now, that pain is becoming universal. The issue today is that labor itself is on the verge of disappearing. There is no longer as clear a line between the working class and the nonworking class, because we are beginning to see the disappearance of labor itself, and all the definitions of self that come with it. These shifts got me interested again, but I was still finding it difficult to move forward.

It was only when my co-writer, Oh Jung-mi, suggested placing a documentary filmmaker within the story that I began to see a path with some legitimacy. Now it was also my story, as the creator and teller of other people’s stories. It allowed me to question my own position: What are my attitudes? What’s built into the gaze I direct toward these people? And what should my ethics be?

Industry observers note that that must have been such an exciting discovery — or simply a relief. With Ye-ji as your avatar, you seem able to express your best intentions as a storyteller while voicing your bitterly ironic self-doubts and unresolved questions about such storytelling. As a viewer, it feels like an ongoing, self-aware conversation with you.

You could also say that I gave myself an alibi. But this approach really did free me to tell their story without those earlier questions constantly tugging at my heart. It put me a little more at ease about telling the story of this laid-off worker and his family.

Strictly speaking, is Ye-ji my complete embodiment, my true avatar? Of course not. She and I are very different. But from a larger perspective, you could say that Ye-ji really does embody my best intentions and represents exactly who I am within the structure of this story. I felt this very keenly all over again at the world premiere in Venice. There I was in a very expensive rented tuxedo, walking a red carpet to present the story of a laid-off worker’s pain. I suppose some would say that is simply the nature of things, because filmmaking is married to capital.

Sources indicate that throughout the film, there are moments where characters seem to give direct voice to your self-conscious misgivings, as we’ve been discussing. Other questions are suggested by the plot, but aren’t stated quite so explicitly. One is related to the matter you just mentioned: Does art carry any real power to critique or change the capitalist system, or is the aesthetic itself just another part of the system’s healthy functioning? You certainly seem to be nudging us in the direction of the latter. Ye-ji, the film’s well-intentioned artist, after all, is married to and supported by a capitalist tycoon. The documentary she is making seems entirely genuine — but the fact remains that her real-life husband is executing a private equity deal to sell off the factory at the heart of her story the entire time. By extension, this feels not dissimilar to the fact that increasingly large swaths of the global movie business are owned by some of the very same tech giants and oligarchs that are driving the technological changes that will potentially bring about the elimination of labor you mentioned earlier.

It has been revealed that this question goes to the very essence of the film industry, and art-making in general — and who in this world could give a clear answer to that, right? That said, I have never felt free of this question since the very beginning of my work as a filmmaker. It’s always there.

Sources indicate that one answer, for me at least, is that I believe films must never turn a blind eye to the real world and the people living within it. By “turn a blind eye,” I mean that the lives you portray in your film should never be consumed simply as material or content for making your movie.

Of course, we’re talking about a very fine line. When you are dealing with people in pain — people suffering from the many paradoxes of life — you must ask yourself: Am I only consuming or exploiting their story, or am I really trying to start a conversation with the audience in a meaningful and creative way? That’s a very difficult question to answer with full conviction.

It has been revealed that as a rule of thumb, I believe that the simpler the objective of your story becomes, the further you have probably drifted from reality. I believe that a film exists to ask questions of the audience. If your audience leaves the theater having only experienced catharsis — entertainment or pleasure — then you have probably treated your subject as little more than an exercise in consumption.

Sources indicate that let’s talk about the film’s dual climaxes at the vacation home. How should we interpret Mi-ok’s actions on the beach? I read them as both an attempt to experience the freedom Sang-woo so effortlessly enjoys and a way to reclaim some power over him.

According to recent reports, meanwhile, Ho-seok is finally sitting for Ye-ji’s camera. I felt this terrible apprehension — I didn’t want him to give her this, as though she might artistically prey on him in the way you just described. But later I realized he’s doing this for his own reasons: He needs to open up and begin healing for his wife and son. Later, back home, he can finally use the little marble drinking station he built, and he seems a touch healed. So, both moments ultimately felt heroic to me. Am I on the right track?

You’re right that Mi-ok’s actions on the beach are something she chooses for herself in the hope of reclaiming her freedom. But it would be wrong to say she’s only emulating what Sang-woo stated he felt as a child. Of course, his story gave her a hint, or planted the seed, but what she’s actually trying to do is a little different.

In a recent development, I believe what Mi-ok wanted in that moment was to face Sang-woo with nothing between them, not even the clothes on their bodies — simply one human facing another. She realizes that everything she and Ho-seok have endured — all the setbacks, all the pain, all the difficulties — has had everything to do with the distorting power of capital, and nothing to do with their own efforts and worth. And she realizes that this power is wielded by people like Sang-woo.

In a recent development, so she wanted to face the person at the heart of that force with nothing between them, and these are the actions she is able to improvise in that moment. In a way, she also wanted to test the happiness of this other couple a little — to throw a pebble at the glass castle of these people who enjoy all the benefits of such power. She wants to face him as just another naked human, but she also wants to provoke him a little.

As for Ho-seok’s sequence, he decides to sit for Ye-ji’s camera even though he is fully aware that his emotions are going to be used to fulfill her hidden desires. But he also knows he needs to somehow bring it all out of himself — all his deep-rooted rage and sadness. At this point, he knows it can only heal him a little. I also believe Mi-ok knew this from the beginning, and this is why she agreed to her family being part of the documentary in the first place. She wanted Ho-seok to take part in the interview so that he might be healed in some way.

Then, after all is said and done, when they’re sitting together at that marble platform at the very end, there’s a sense that they’ve learned something through all of this.

Sources indicate that that marble platform had been a symbol of Ho-seok’s despair and failure. It’s a place charged with his very Korean sentiments — his sense of responsibility for his family and for the co-workers he tried to treat as brothers. It’s symbolic of his typical Korean masculinity, all of which has been brutally betrayed and taken away from him.

It has been revealed that but now, at the end, he is able to sit in that place again — next to his wife, who has always been there for him — and they look up at the moon and share a peaceful moment together again. The moon, you know, transcends everything — class, capital, even time. Anybody can look up at the moon.

Sources indicate that that’s so beautiful. What does the title mean to you? We’re familiar with the notion of an impossible love — a great romance thwarted by circumstance. Here, you seem to give us a “possible” great love story while also showing what it really takes — the loyalty, strength, sacrifice, wisdom and generosity. Perhaps the film is also saying that even amid the loss of meaningful work and the identity that comes with it, dignity can be found in the work of loving one another? Or could the title refer to love between the artist and her subjects — like the budding friendship between Ye-ji and Mi-ok, which seems genuine despite their class differences? Or is it even broader: the love a more successful labor movement in today’s world would require?

Most simply, I wanted even the title of the film to be a question, and I thought Possible Love could do that. Most of the great love stories we remember are about whether the characters can overcome everything and finally end up together — whether their love comes to fruition. So Possible Love is a sort of paradox. I wanted the audience to ask all the questions you’re asking: What does a possible love mean, and what kind of love is really possible?

I was told by various people that the title was too difficult or confusing. But I think as you grow more familiar with it, it begins to feel just right.

You can say the film is about labor and class, and also the philosophy and ethics of filmmaking. But what it’s really about is human relationships. We live in a time when those relationships are deteriorating and we’re being separated. The nature of human connection is fundamentally changing. There is exploitation and disconnection, and sometimes we intentionally cut people out of our lives.

But I believe human relationships ultimately boil down to a question of love. The only path to healing those relationships is love. It doesn’t matter what kind of relationship it is — within a couple, between an artist and their subject or with the people you meet in everyday life. What is your fundamental attitude toward them? With the title, I wanted to ask: How do we answer the question of love? And what questions should we be asking ourselves about love?

It has been revealed that the second time I watched the film, I noticed more ways in which your fictional drama borrows the formal elements of a documentary. We get classic talking-head sequences by watching Ye-ji shoot her film, and your flashbacks start to feel like documentary reconstructions. I even began to reconsider the rainbow motif: Mi-ok tells that poetic anecdote about her mother’s angry reaction to her picking up a work hose as a child, and later we see her son playing with a servant, spraying water and making a rainbow. This feels like a connection a documentary director might discover in the editing room and use as a visual metaphor to tie together the film’s themes. Am I projecting, or were you deliberately closing the gap between your work and Ye-ji’s via the film’s formal construction?

Recent findings suggest that no, you’re correct. Near the start of the film, after we introduce Ye-ji as a documentary filmmaker, the cinematographer and I were very careful to make it clear whether you were looking through her camera or ours. But as the film goes on, we gradually blur that distinction. If you watch the film again, you might notice shots where you really can’t tell who is supposed to be behind the camera — me or Ye-ji.

In a recent development, but I didn’t only want to emulate a documentary’s structure. I wanted to be intimately connected with the content, too. Through my dramatic feature, I wanted to communicate the pain and suffering of these laid-off workers, which is exactly what Ye-ji wanted to capture. In that sense, I would say there is no demarcation between my intentions and hers.

The clearest example is the therapy session just before they go to the vacation home. We see interviews with four people who are either laid-off workers or their family members. They give their testimony, and it’s done in a very documentary style.

Now, that’s not something you would usually see in a dramatic feature. From a purely filmmaking perspective, the sequence is too long and feels quite out of place. If I had cut it, not only would the pacing have been better, but the story would have been more emotionally effective, because we could have brought the characters to the vacation home sooner, with more momentum. However, I thought the sequence was essential because, above all else, I needed my film to also be loyal to Ye-ji’s intentions. So I felt the documentary style had to be integrated into my film.

It has been revealed that it’s incredible how artfully you pulled all of that off. Changing gears a bit, Korea’s great auteurs seem to have converged lately on the same essential question: what capitalism is doing to society and individual dignity. In the global film-buff hive mind, you, Park Chan-wook and Bong Joon Ho pretty much represent the trifecta of Korean masters. Parasite, No Other Choice and Possible Love share many of the same themes and significant-picture concerns, expressed in your very different cinematic idioms. What do you make of this convergence? Is it simply a matter of the shared history and political tradition from which you emerged, even though you belong to slightly different generations?

This is a question I got quite often in Venice, usually posed in a way that suggested most filmmakers in other countries had moved past this. Why is Korea still making films about class conflict? I gave a pretty general answer that I think is probably true: Because Korea experienced such extreme economic development in such a short period, it touched every aspect of society, and it’s only natural that we continue to suffer from the aftereffects. And the filmmakers that you mentioned, we all experienced those extreme changes when we were sensitive young men. So, I suspect it’s as simple as that.

But while we deal with the same issues, I think what’s most different is our attitude toward cinema, or how we think of our films as we’re creating them. I’m probably quite different from both of them. I might be kind of an oddball compared to most filmmakers.

Many filmmakers love cinema for its own sake, and you can feel that love of moviemaking in their work — and it can be a wonderful thing. But I can only speak for myself. For me, making films is not the most vital thing. I don’t know if this will come across the way I intend, but the reality and the ideas I’m trying to portray are more important to me than the film itself. I think that attitude is apparent in my films. That is why, compared to other filmmakers, my films are not entertaining. They’re difficult.

It has been revealed that my last question isn’t my own. I recently interviewed Na Hong-jin in Seoul about Hope. He holds you in very high esteem, so when I mentioned I’d be speaking with you, I asked if he had a question for you. This was his question: Outside of your work as a novelist and filmmaker, what kind of human being do you think you are?

It has been revealed that I would say that I am a very timid person, and because of that, I tend to spend too much time thinking about how I should live — and it makes me very, very tired. (Laughs).
