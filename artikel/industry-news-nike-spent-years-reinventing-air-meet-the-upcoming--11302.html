Industry News: Nike spent years reinventing Air. Meet the upcoming Tuned Air (exclusive)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that for half a century, Nike has been synonymous with Air. The cushioning—developed at NASA, then incorporated inside a 1978 running shoe and the Air Max 1—came to represent Nike’s commitment to cutting-edge innovation. Now, with Nike on its back foot after several years of missteps, the tech giant is counting on a new version of Air to help pull it out of its slump. Today, a day before it announces its financial results, Nike is unveiling Tuned Air, a high-tech evolution of Air that has been in the works for years. It’s debuting this technology in a running sneaker that will come out within a year. But Nike sees Tuned Air as a broader platform that can be incorporated into other sports and even its apparel lines. [Video: Nike] It’s one way Nike hopes to restore its credibility as an innovator and win back runners who have drifted to rivals. Given how much ground Nike has lost in recent years, it’s unclear whether this strategy will work. But the company is optimistic. “There’s no better innovation that speaks to our formula than Air,” states Phil McCartney, Nike’s chief innovation, design, and product officer. McCartney has spent three decades at Nike, where he ran the footwear business from 2016 until last year, when CEO Elliott Hill put him in charge of innovation, design and product across Nike, Jordan, and Converse. Speaking to me from the LeBron James Innovation Center at Nike’s campus in Beaverton, Oregon, McCartney explained that his team has a motto borrowed from two Nike legends: “Create epic shit. Make athletes better.” (The first comes from Kobe Bryant, the second from Nike’s cofounder, Bill Bowerman.) McCartney says Nike is translating that slogan into a formula, “athlete, science, solution,” that will guide the company’s approach to innovation. His teams gather insights from athletes, then tests ideas at the Nike Sports Research Lab in Beaverton and through field teams in Eldoret, Kenya; London, and Shanghai. [Photo: Nike] Introducing Nike Tuned Air The original Air technology created tough pockets of nitrogen using two sheets of plastic. In that case, the air was evenly distributed, creating cushioning throughout the pocket. With Tuned Air, Nike adds nuance. Before the plastic sheets are pressed together, Nike adds a chemical that allows it to create a pattern; it can now fine-tune where the gas is located and how much air is in each pocket. In doing so, it can make one part of the cushion soft and springy, and another part firm and stable. “It allows us to completely change the relationship between cushioning and support, and precision-tune the experience,” says McCartney. Nike has given this technology to runners over the last few years. “The feedback that we’ve had from the runners who have been trying this for a couple of years now is it feels like no other cushioning system out there,” he says. “Incredibly well cushioned, but also has stability. That’s the sort of holy grail that we’ve been trying to unlock in Air.” While Nike’s approach to achieving this balance might be distinct, many other running brands are also trying to balance cushioning with structure with their own innovations. Brooks’s DNA Tuned foam uses a dual-cell construction, with large cells in the heel to cushion landings and smaller cells in the front to make the toes more responsive. Hoka’s H-Frame features a firm strip of foam in the midsole, surrounded by softer cushioning. On’s CloudTec technology uses hollow tubes of foam molded to the bottom of the sole to absorb impact, along with a steel plate in the midsole. With this newfound ability to modulate how much air is in each pocket, Nike can now explore “wildly different expressions” of the technology, McCartney says. Besides running, Tuned Air will be incorporated into other sports, like soccer, and into clothing like the Air Milano jacket, creating air pockets that improve temperature regulation. Nike can even integrate Tuned Air into lifestyle shoes, like vintage sneakers, creating more comfort. “How could we make an Air Force 1 using a sock liner that is Tuned Air, so you get comfort and support?” McCartney says. Nike has streamlined the manufacturing of Tuned Air so that it can move 70% faster than it typically does from idea to product. This is crucial to allowing Nike to be nimble as it seeks to compete with the many rivals that are also spinning out innovative new products quickly. [Video: Nike] Nike Needs a Win Nike needs this innovation to resonate with consumers. Last quarter, revenue declined 4% to $11.2 billion, and the company expects full-year sales to fall by a high single digit percentage. Sportswear, which accounts for nearly half the business, fell by double digits. Sales in China dropped 26% and Converse sank 28%. Nike has declared a restructuring strategy that will cut more jobs after several rounds of layoffs in recent years. Nike’s rivals have been closely observing its missteps, and taken advantage of the opportunity to steal market share. In running, a category Nike once dominated, it’s now in third place, trailing Brooks and Hoka. In the lifestyle sphere, Adidas has had hit after hit by bringing back its classic Samba and Gazelle, and New Balance has turned its retro styles like the 550 into bestsellers. Meanwhile, Nike hasn’t had a lifestyle sneaker hit in years. Nike’s running business is starting to recover. It’s grown by double digits for more than a year, helped by the successful Vomero line. “We’re seeing market share gains almost daily right now,” says McCartney. Historically, Nike has built running technology for elite athletes that tries to shave seconds off on their race times. But that technology takes a long time to trickle down into running shoes that the average person can lace up. Nike doesn’t have the luxury of time anymore. That’s why its Tuned Air is designed for the everyday runner. “The entry point will be that everyday run,” McCartney says. “When you’re just trying to log your Tuesday morning run, you want the maximum amount of running with precision-tuned support.” Nike is making a big bet on Tuned Air. But whether enough consumers get excited about the technology—and the forthcoming sneakers—to boost Nike’s fortunes is unclear. Tomorrow, when Nike lays out its long-term financial prepares, we’ll get a better glimpse into how much ground it has yet to make up.
