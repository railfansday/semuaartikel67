Why the Radical Lesbian Filmmaker Doc ‘Barbara Forever’ Deserves Oscar Recognition

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a recent development, finally, a documentary as radical as the lesbian avant-garde feminist filmmaker it’s about. First-time feature filmmaker Brydie O’Connor’s Sundance-winning documentary “Barbara Forever” is a hypnotic archival collage that retraces the life, work, and death of Barbara Hammer.

Recent findings suggest that the documentary, an early fall Strand release produced in partnership with Pamela Koffler and Christine Vachon’s recently Oscar-winning Killer Films, won in Park City for Matt Hixon’s editing. (Kristen Stewart also jumped on board as an executive producer.) It’s not hard to see why. “Barbara Forever” is an archival documentary that unfolds in the shape and style of its subject, an experimental filmmaker who put together some of the first publicly projected lesbian erotica actually made by a lesbian. Voiceover by the filmmaker spans her early career in the Bay Area (and halcyon days as an out lesbian), her 1992 feature debut “Nitrate Kisses,” and the final years of a cancer battle that ended in 2019. According to recent reports, now Latest York-based, O’Connor centered her George Washington University thesis project on Hammer’s work before they’d ever met. “I read about Barbara and studied Barbara before I even saw her films,” O’Connor told IndieWire. She reached out to Hammer to “buy her DVDs because they weren’t showing anywhere in D.C. And she wrote back to me. At first, she was a little bit hard on me. She was like, ‘I see you’re writing from an edu.edu address. I just want to make sure that you’re actually a student because if you’re faculty, it would be much, much more expensive.’ [Laughs] And I stated, ‘I promise, Barbara, I’m a student. I just can’t wait to see your movies.’ And she sent me her DVDs. So yeah, I think that initial impulse to explore her work was really about … ‘Why don’t more people know about Barbara and her work?'”.

Hammer had degrees in psychology and literature before encountering “Meshes of the Afternoon” director Maya Deren’s films (which also influenced David Lynch) at San Francisco State in the 1970s. They lit the fuse for making her own movies. Circa that time, she’d been living in Santa Rosa and married to a man. She came out as a lesbian — busting through that closet, really, with abandon — and took off with her Super 8 camera to start making lesbian films. Growing up in Kansas, O’Connor, in her early 30s, resonated with Hammer’s early biography, and Hammer’s early lust for self-reinvention.

“She was not really tentative about anything. She picked up a camera in her late 20s as she was getting a divorce from a man when she realized that she was a lesbian and she made her film,” O’Connor said. “She learned by doing. And I was so inspired because, at the time, I was studying American studies and history in school. I had no background in the arts really, except for enjoying consuming them. And I really thought I would be a film critic or a writer… [Barbara Hammer] was not afraid to fail creatively. And she tried so many different ideas, of course, throughout her entire career in making over 85 films, but especially in that first decade of her career.”.

According to recent reports, post-college and in Upcoming York, O’Connor, who’s spent “a decade in the Hammer world, and my entire adult consciousness,” would see Hammer at the Metrograph in Lower Manhattan. “I definitely was ‘the girl who wrote her thesis on Barbara,’ probably one of many, and I was day-playing in the film industry,” O’Connor said. “I would occasionally go up to Yale, where her formal archive is held, to continue doing independent research. I was looking at her love letters and her photographs… I wrote to her in January of 2019 when I was about to make my first short film. I said, ‘Hi, Barbara.’ I didn’t know how sick she was at the time. I was like, ‘I’m making my first film. Can I take you to coffee? Do you have any advice for me?’ And she wrote back to me and said, ‘I’m at the very, very end of my life, but best of luck with everything in your career.'”.

Hammer died in March 2019, which is when O’Connor contacted the filmmaker’s partner, the human rights advocate Florrie Burke. “Florrie was the love of her life. They were together for 31 years,” O’Connor said. Their meeting culminated in O’Connor’s 2022 short film “Love, Barbara,” which she expanded into the feature that hit theaters this year. In a 2026 Oscar documentary race heavy on genocide and social complications — or a three-hour A24 movie that’s shaken up the entire form — “Barbara Forever” is life-affirming, lovely, yet still-important counter-programming.

It has been revealed that the organic archival rhythm of “Barbara Forever” eschews talking heads altogether, instead conversing with what Hammer’s work looked and felt like. One of the film’s most charming snippets finds Hammer showing one of her (SFW!) films to a classroom of small schoolchildren. They end up having an unexpected appreciation for the experimental film clip’s non-narrative sensibilities.

Industry observers note that O’Connor collaborated with Burke on sifting through 35 hours of audio recordings to stitch together a narration unfolding entirely in Hammer’s own words. “Florrie was so happy with [the short], but she always reminded me there’s so much more to Barbara than just our relationship,” O’Connor mentioned. “Barbara’s this huge personality. She’s so charismatic and charming and really both thoughtful, but also very savvy. She wanted to be recognized. She wanted to be known for her work and her efforts, and she was uncompromising in what she made and how she shared her work. So it just made sense that she would be the narrator of the film.”.

The Sundance-winning editing, too, proved a challenge, as O’Connor and her filmmaking team pored over more than 400 hours of visual material — from Hammer’s films, to outtakes, B-sides, home videos, and more. “I really wanted to convey with this film: Why did Barbara turn the camera on herself and her life and her lovers and her body as a method of alternative history making? What did she see in the value of that approach and then making experimental cinema out of it and demanding the value of that within culture?”.

That Hammer’s work wasn’t more canonized during her life — an effort O’Connor has made partly her own, especially as a newly introduced digital restoration of “Nitrate Kisses” is making the rounds — isn’t only because of how confrontational she was about gender and her own sexuality. “Nitrate Kisses” is, after all, an experimental movie about lesbian repression dating back to the early 20th century. Is it also that autofiction and individualism, now a dominant mode of contemporary artistic expression, were also marginalized at that point within her particular corner of the art world?

In a recent development, “She was both an experimental filmmaker and avant-garde artist and a lesbian filmmaker. And she says, and this was something she stated all throughout her life and career, that she didn’t fit neatly into either category where she could just build an audience within that space,” O’Connor said. “She really was uncompromising about being both and working at the intersection of both and having both audiences… The experimental film world didn’t necessarily want to see lesbian work, didn’t necessarily want to see the nude woman’s body on screen. And lesbians, there was not so much representation, especially in the ’70s, ’80s, ’90s, increasingly so, but her work was experimental and thought-provoking, and she felt like a lot of her queer audiences wanted more realistic depictions of queerness.”.

O’Connor, who received a 2026 Creative Capital to not only finish her work on “Barbara Forever” but also continue making docs, noted, “The industry is hard, but that certainly won’t stop me from making nonfiction films. I’m just excited to pivot to my next project.”.

“Barbara Forever” is now available to stream on PVOD platforms.
