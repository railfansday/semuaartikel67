Industry observers note that when my brother Arnie was about four years old, he began chatting about an imaginary and invisible companion named Embley, who seemingly travelled with him everywhere. During a 1970s epidemic of whooping cough (linked to an unwarranted vaccine-safety scare), Arnie became sick with this braying cough, which kept him home from school for more than six weeks.

In a recent development, embley was the “same size, age and disposition as me”, my brother recalled later, and he offered companionship and “exploration of potential sources of adventure”. Although he could not remember other aspects of Embley’s personality or appearance, my brother did add one pertinent detail: “He was always available.”.

I thought of Embley while reading The Imagination Scientist, a deeply researched and highly entertaining look at the world of imaginary companions by developmental psychologist Paige Davis.

Recent findings suggest that it seems that Embley exemplified several features of children’s imaginary friends: he was comforting and constant; he played with my brother; and he perhaps enabled him to process fear and cope with the stress of a serious illness. He might also have insulated my brother from loneliness while his older siblings were at school. Most importantly, my brother himself created Embley’s mind.

That last characteristic of an imaginary friend is key, asserts Davis. “My theory is that the most important aspect of the imaginary creature, no matter whether it is seen or unseen, lies in the creation of its mind by the child,” she writes. “To have an invisible friend or personified object considered as an imaginary companion, the child must create, conceptualize, and interact with the separate and distinct mind.”.

According to recent reports, davis opens a wardrobe door, Narnia-style, into a whimsical world inhabited by imaginary companions — some invisible, others personified in a doll or stuffed toy. Up to 65% of children up to the age of seven have had imaginary companions at some stage, according to research by psychologist Marjorie Taylor. Some imaginary friends disappear at the end of childhood, others continue to accompany their creators into adolescence. But not all made-up playmates are friendly: some are a nuisance and might even pick fights with their creators (or vice versa).

Generally, there’s nothing pathological about creating a pretend friend, Davis contends. On the contrary, the presence of such a pal might fortify the child as they deal with transitions or scary situations. Children do understand the distinction between fantasy friends and real ones, and can grasp this difference as young as three years old. “Imaginary friends are a very common, developmentally appropriate phenomena that occurs in multiple cultures around the world,” Davis writes. They “are not only a healthy form of play, but they can be extremely enjoyable for their creator”.

Imaginary friends can offer a child a sense of protection.Credit: Ian Nolan/Getty.

They’re also great fun to read about. I laughed aloud as I learnt about some of the “human, non-human, and superhuman” forms that Davis has encountered while interviewing children for her research. “I’ve had blue blobs, yellow birds, and rainbow hair,” she writes, “a fire-boy who erupts like a volcano when he becomes angry” and “a genderless imaginary companion that snacks on lightbulbs”. One little girl had twin imaginary companions, who themselves had made their own imaginary friend, “which I thought was quite meta”, she writes.

Recent findings suggest that davis first began immersing herself in the world of imaginary companions as a PhD student at the University of Durham, UK. I “thought about how much fun it would be to work with children, play, and imagination for a job”, Davis writes. It’s clear that, a decade and a half later, she still derives a delicious glee from listening to the odd observations of children (including her two sons).

According to recent reports, in one of her early studies, Davis and her colleagues looked at the relationship between imaginary companions and “theory of mind”, an umbrella term that involves understanding that other people have emotions, thoughts and ideas, just as we do. Thus, writes Davis, “children with a theory of mind are able to put themselves in another person’s shoes to infer what they may be thinking or feeling.”.
