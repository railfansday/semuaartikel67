Unlicensed Plumber Accused of Squatting and Subleasing Rooms in Senior Woman’s Home

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A California woman who hired a plumber to make repairs to her home while she was living elsewhere says he took over the property, moved strangers into the house, and locked her out—sparking a two-year nightmare that left her fighting to reclaim her own home.

Jordy Smith, 76, said the man working on the plumbing at her home in San Diego's City Heights neighborhood allegedly transferred the utilities into his own name and began renting out rooms to strangers, pocketing the payments.

Smith and her partner, who owned the home, later discovered the man was not a licensed plumber; but by then, the situation had spiraled out of control. When the couple returned home, they were locked out of their own house and couldn’t get the latest occupants to leave, she claimed.

"He just took over and, like, before we knew it, there was like 15 people," Smith told ABC 10News. "Once that happened, it really made it look like to the homeless people that were coming in, that made it look like it was his house.”.

The couple tried to confront the people living in the home but Smith remarked they had locked the door and wouldn’t talk.

Sources indicate that "I couldn't sleep at night," Smith mentioned. "I mean, it just seemed like we'd never get back home.".

In a recent development, meanwhile, the property had attracted multiple visits by the police. San Diego police were called to the home more than 30 times between Jan. 1, 2024, and Aug. 17, 2026, for reports of suspected drug activity, trespassing, and smoke coming from the property, according to dispatch records.

In an attempt to get the squatters removed from the property, Smith turned to police and the courts for help but swiftly ran into multiple roadblocks.

One of the complications being that Smith’s partner couldn’t find the trust agreement that had been given to him by his mother decades before, which established ownership of the home.

They ultimately listed the home for sale and reached out to the Squatter Squad, a manufacturer that helps homeowners remove unauthorized occupants.

“He knew that he was the one that was the successor trustee, but they couldn't find the trust anywhere,” Kimrey Kotchick, co-founder of the Squatter Squad remarked, referring to Smith’s partner, who owned the property.

He told 10News that squatter squatter disputes are often treated as civil matters, meaning that even when police respond to a home, if someone claims to be a renter, it is often left for the courts to resolve.

Industry observers note that fortunately for Smith and her partner, Kotchick was able to locate the missing ownership documents through the county recorder's office. But it took another five months before the squatters could be legally evicted.

After years of trying to regain access to the house, Smith and her partner were faced with another loss–the home was left in disrepair and many of Smith's cherished antiques were missing.

The couple has since sold the house but Smith notes the emotional toll from the ordeal has taken its toll.

Now, Smith is urging other homeowners to keep their ownership documents readily accessible and think twice before leaving contractors unsupervised in their homes.
