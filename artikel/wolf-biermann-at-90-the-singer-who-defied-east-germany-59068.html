Wolf Biermann at 90: The singer who defied East Germany

He mocks Donald Trump and Vladimir Putin as "political lovers," denounces the "endless fratricidal war" over the Palestinian territories, and repeatedly invokes "Lady Liberty." It is a celebration of the freedom he once so painfully lacked in the former East Germany, and of the freedom people around the world still yearn for.

These themes run throughout Wolf Biermann's new album "Unterm Haar nur Himmel" ("Beneath Hair, Only Sky") released to mark his 90th birthday. The album's title is a characteristically poetic phrase coined by Biermann himself. It evokes the idea that however restrictive the world may become, the human mind and spirit remain free. Beneath the hair lies open sky: a metaphor for inner freedom, independence and imagination.

In a recent development, biermann remains one of Germany's most influential singer-songwriters, and at 90, he highlights little sign of putting down his guitar. Often described as Germany's answer to Bob Dylan, the outspoken troubadour continues to weigh in on the challenges shaping the world today.

In his recent song "Moritat von den Liebespaaren" ("Ballad of the Lovers"), he delivers a simple but urgent message: no single person can save the world, yet everyone can help make it a better place. Above all, the song is a call to take responsibility for one's own life rather than remain a bystander.

This outspokenness was instilled in Biermann early on. When he was born in Hamburg in 1936, Adolf Hitler was already in power. His father was a dockworker, a committed communist, a resistance fighter against the Nazis — and Jewish.

Wolf was just three months old when the Gestapo, Hitler's secret police, arrested his father. He initially served time in prison before he was murdered by the Nazis at Auschwitz in early 1943.

In a recent development, just a few months later, British and American bombers attacked Hamburg, unleashing a devastating firestorm. "Nothing in my life has shaped me more," Biermann has repeatedly said in interviews. When the sirens sounded, his mother took him into an air raid shelter. The house above them was burning.

Yet Biermann has stated his memories of that time are rooted in a profound sense of trust in life. In an interview with the German magazine Chrismon, he recalled: "I pressed my face into my mother's soft coat so I could breathe. A sense of security in the midst of the end of the world. We stumbled out over the neighboring house and into the open. The firestorm lifted a burning roof into the air. Roaring corrugated metal, smoke everywhere. We searched for a way out. [...] Death was everywhere. 40,000 people died. They had no chance. But I did. My strong mama.".

Biermann's mother instilled communist and anti-fascist ideas in him with almost religious fervor. "My mother had only one goal after those dark years," Biermann once recalled. "I was supposed to survive so that I could, as she put it rather grandly, one day take revenge for my father's death. I was supposed to build communism.".

In 1953, while countless people were fleeing East Germany, Biermann moved there of his own free will — after all, communism was the official state ideology. He was just 16 at the time. In East Berlin, he studied political economy, mathematics and philosophy. His role models were writers Bertolt Brecht and Heinrich Heine. He began working as an assistant director at the Berliner Ensemble, and also began writing and performing his political songs.

Industry observers note that biermann soon realized that life in East Germany was far from the communist paradise he had imagined and he wrote about this openly in his songs. "The party bosses were torn," Biermann mentioned. "They were delighted by this young poet who could play the guitar so beautifully and sing such cheeky songs. They thought they just needed to bring me into line. Even today, I'm amazed that I wound up cast in the role of the heroic dragon slayer.".

In 1965, East Germany's ruling Socialist Unity Party, the SED, imposed a blanket ban on Biermann performing or publishing his work. His East Berlin apartment, which was under constant surveillance, soon became a gathering place for dissidents and critical thinkers.

In a recent development, yet, to his great surprise, the East German authorities allowed him to travel to Cologne, West Germany in 1976. There, around 7,000 people flocked to see him. For four and a half hours, he strummed and sang, mocking the East German regime. His songs were so popular that bootleg copies found their way back to East Germany.

Industry observers note that on November 16, while still on tour in West Germany, Biermann heard the news on his car radio that he had been stripped of his right to continue residing in the German Democratic Republic. The regime accused him of betraying and slandering East Germany. ".

Industry observers note that I felt like the ground had opened up beneath me. I was overcome with fear, everything went dark," Biermann wrote in his memoirs.

Recent findings suggest that in a petition, leading East German intellectuals spoke out against the decision. In response, the regime stepped up surveillance of dissidents, imposed professional bans and jailed its critics. Several prominent public figures left the country.

Industry observers note that "The SED leadership expected an angry media response from the West. What they hadn't reckoned with was that, for the very first time, a group of respected East German writers and artists would make their protest public, disguising it as a humble petition," Biermann wrote.

Industry observers note that afterward, Biermann sank into a deep depression. "Not just because I had been torn away from the friends I knew and loved in East Berlin, but also because I had lost my familiar mortal enemies," he noted.

He became involved in the peace and anti-nuclear movements and continued singing about the injustices of the world. "The world's conflicts take different forms in each generation," he said. "But it's always the same struggle for freedom.".

The singer-songwriter aims to spend his 90th birthday at home in Hamburg with his wife and manager, Pamela. For the first time in his life, he said, he's skipping the birthday party, even with his 10 children.

"I'm so old and I can feel my strength fading. And I know that the next day I have a major concert at the Berliner Ensemble," he told the German Press Agency.

And should he ever die, he said, he wants to be buried at Berlin's cemetery where some renowned German writers and intellectuals have been laid to rest, among them Bertolt Brecht, Anna Seghers and Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel. He still has a thing or two to discuss with them.
