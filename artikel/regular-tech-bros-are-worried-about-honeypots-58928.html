Regular Tech Bros Are Worried About Honeypots

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

When the founder of a cybersecurity firm hired Blaine Anderson as a matchmaker, it wasn’t just because the man was lonely. It was also, Anderson states, because he was “super worried about spies.”.

Sources indicate that the cybersecurity founder isn’t the only one. As Jensen Huang’s affair with an alleged asset of China’s Ministry of State Security dominates headlines and AI companies reportedly warn employees against “honeypots,” matchmakers say that more men with pretty normal lives are worried that their dates may have ulterior motives: accessing corporate secrets.

“Even if you're not a CEO or at a big tech giant, these fears have trickled down to just regular professionals,” Simone Grossman, a Brooklyn-based matchmaker, tells WIRED.

One tech worker, who requested anonymity in order to speak candidly, notes he considers himself “highly selective” about the people he interacts with face-to-face.

“Even though I could vet you from looking you up online, I can't help but still be a tad dubious,” he tells WIRED. On a scale from zero to 10, with 10 being the most anxious about espionage, he puts himself at a seven.

Even though the vast majority of tech employees likely won’t be targeted for romantic espionage, matchmakers say more clients have lately expressed concerns about women exploiting them for their corporate secrets. One Palantir employee told Andersen he wouldn’t date anyone who was a Chinese or Russian national. The anxiety has even become a marketing opportunity: One company is pitching its upcoming line of AI executive girlfriends as a “zero espionage” alternative to human dating.

The concern isn’t entirely without basis. Some companies hire consultants to advise executives and lower-level employees with access to sensitive information on security risks, including the possibility of honeypots. Several of these consultants tell WIRED that raising the subject can be awkward.

“This one is always a very difficult message to deliver—to explain to, say, very sensitive engineers that maybe they're getting a lot of email attention because of their job,” claims Isaac Stone Fish, founder and chief executive of the business intelligence firm Strategy Risks, “as opposed to the charm and force of their personality.”.

In a recent development, amy Andersen, founder of Silicon Valley matchmaking company Linx Dating (and no relation to Blaine Anderson), indicates that for clients concerned about their romantic partners’ intentions, she offers background checks, personal vetting, and NDAs. The security features are part of her “bespoke” packages that range anywhere from $75,000 to more than $500,000 for the “Founders Level.”.

Fear of romantic espionage is just the latest manifestation of growing distrust in the dating world. Shannon Lundgren, a matchmaker who has worked in San Francisco for 15 years, says that while she hasn’t necessarily heard more concerns about honeypots, she has noticed heightened skepticism around dating in general, as well as growing concerns about privacy.

The anxiety is particularly acute among AI workers, whose net worths—and therefore their perceived romantic desirability—are on the rise.

“If you're somebody who is a little bit shy or who is maybe a very stereotypical engineer type, to suddenly have your market value in the dating world change so drastically—you're already suspicious,” Grossman indicates.

To be worried about corporate espionage on top of all that, she notes, is “a mind fuck.”.

Honeypots and their state or corporate sponsors often look for “signs of vulnerability, such as an unhappy marriage, or someone seeking “excitement or adventure,” says Ira Winkler, a security consultant and the chief information security officer in residence at identity protection organization Cloaked. Lonely people are opportune targets, regardless of marriage or relationship status.

The advice security experts give is somewhat obvious: If it seems too good to be true, it probably is.

“Really, if a beautiful woman approaches you, you know it's not for your personality,” Winkler tells his clients.

When tech workers do think of honeypots, they may gravitate toward the stereotype of a beautiful stranger who seduces someone at a bar and then extracts organization secrets. “Any time one of us would take a trip to Asia, someone would always make a comment about getting a foreign girlfriend, or future wife, etc.,” one tech worker tells WIRED about his experience at a majority-male workplace. “We'd joke about them meeting a spy, but nobody really considered that seriously. Mostly they were just thinking of sex.”.

Sources indicate that that setup is largely a misconception. In reality, according to Greg Rinckey, founding partner of the legal services and consulting firm Tully Rinckey, most honeypot victims are groomed over time. “We’re talking sometimes months, could be years, where they're fostering that relationship,” he says. Rinckey compares it to scammers trying to keep victims on the phone: The longer someone talks to another person and feels they know them, the more likely they are to trust them with sensitive information.

Catherine Marinis-Yaqub, a principal at the crisis and security consulting group Control Risks, tells WIRED that when people imagine the beautiful stranger at a bar, they may miss more likely scenarios, like an unexpected guest at a business meeting, a savvy networker at an industry conference, or a flirt at someone’s favorite coffee shop. Jensen Huang met Alice Huang, the suspected Chinese asset, for example, through a mutual business associate (who, according to The Wall Street Journal, also happened to be Alice’s husband).

For the man too scared of spies to make contact with a human romantic partner, there’s always artificial intelligence.

According to recent reports, on Tuesday, in the wake of the Huang scandal, AI-relationship platform Joi AI launched a suite of "zero espionage" Executive Girlfriends. Brand director Julie Levin describes Joi AI as a “safe space” for exploration, where owners can exchange NSFW texts, images, and videos with AI companions without judgment or rejection.

It made sense, she notes, to extend that sense of security to honeypot-phobia.

“If they have some business secrets they don't want to share on a date or they feel nervous discussing with real people,” Levin says, “they can discuss them comfortably with their AIs.” (Levin assures WIRED that Joi AI employs several layers of security and that all of the data is anonymized.).

It has been revealed that high-profile executives are among those most at risk of honeypots, states Winkler, the security consultant. They can also be some of the hardest to protect.

“It's really hard to influence somebody who's in control of the organization and just happens to be a sleazebag,” Winkler claims.

Recent findings suggest that it’s particularly difficult, indicates Rinckey, when bosses think they’re the smartest person in the room and consider themselves immune to manipulation. It’s also not easy, he says, to be in the position of challenging an executive.

“Cocky, arrogant, and boisterous” chief executives make for desirable targets because “they love to talk about their business, and they love to talk about themselves,” Rinckey says. In these situations, he says, “you don't even really have to pry them that much.”.
