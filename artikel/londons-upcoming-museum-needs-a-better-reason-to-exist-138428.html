London’s Upcoming Museum Needs a Better Reason to Exist

The newly introduced London Museum has been a humongous undertaking: 10 years in the making, costing £437 million in total against a budget of £337 million, and yet, after all that time and money, it’s hard to know where any of it went.

In a recent development, from 1976 to 2022, the city’s museum, then called the Museum of London, occupied a site on London Wall at next to the Barbican complex. It moved to Farringdon, a grittier neighbor of the City’s financial district, because the old building was hard for visitors to find and too small to display the full collection. The new space is bright and airy, closer to an Erewhon café than a museum lobby. An on-site café, open around the clock from Thursday to Sunday, serves hot chocolate and ginger beer. The furnishings, though, feel random in such a pristine setting: a flock of large plastic sheep meant for sitting on, and shop signs from London fixtures like the Odeon cinema and Morley’s, the fried chicken chain. When was the last time any Londoner saw a sheep in the city? In theory, all of this should appeal to Londoners who have taken up early-morning run clubs, traded pints for matcha lattes and aren’t above a gimmicky bit of decor.

“People are looking for community, and they will have it in the museum,” Sharon Ament, the museum’s director, told ARTnews.

The architects, Asif Khan and Stanton Williams, working with the conservation specialists Julian Harrap Architects, have designed a space that’s inviting.

“It took people until the 21st century to realize that cultural institutions are for everyone, and that’s what we strive to make here,” Khan told ARTnews at the press preview. He wants visitors to dip in and out of the story of London without feeling as if they’re in a museum. “When we look at audiences outside of the regular museum visitors, many find it challenging and don’t feel welcome. This is a welcome for those people,” Khan stated. Entry is free, there are three separate entrances, and built-in sofas with reading lights invite passersby to stop in, though few people pass through Farringdon unless they’re waiting for the nightclub Fabric or the cocktail bar Space Talk to open.

Industry observers note that still, London is a city of 9.1 million people and a global center for art, film, music, finance and fashion. Finding stories that capture its quirks shouldn’t be that hard.

According to recent reports, the museum is divided into three sections. Real Time, at the main entrance, represents London today and includes Banksy’s Piranhas sentry box. (What could be more London than Banksy?) In the Linbury Hall, Our Time attempts to tie the city together through objects like the Lord Mayor’s State Coach from 1757, the Buckingham Palace switchboard and Syd’s Coffee Stall, an East End food cart that operated from 1919 to 2019. Downstairs is Past Time, perhaps the only part of the museum that feels like a museum. It’s the most educational section, with small rooms on the women’s suffrage movement and the Great Fire of London. The rest of the floor is scattered with a piece of a fatberg, Tom Daley’s diving trunks from the London 2012 Olympics, a chair owned by Charles Dickens, a 2,000-year-old Roman wall painting, Anna Pavlova’s tutu for The Dying Swan and more.

The London Museum is a work in progress and until it figures out what sets it apart from the city’s other museums, it will remain a large crèche, where parents can bring their children to roam freely. But there’s still some hope: in 2028, the museum will open two temporary exhibition spaces, perhaps the museum will finally lean into its London-ness and celebrate the cornerstones that makes the city so glamorous and gritty, from the Teddy Boys of 1950s, Mods and Rockers of the 1960s to the Sloane Rangers of the late 1970s and 1980s.

It has been revealed that until the London Museum gets it right, the city has the Bayeux Tapestry, on view at the British Museum through July 11, 2027, with tickets topping out at £33 ($44). At the preview, word went around that one journalist had skipped it in favor of the 950-year-old cloth. In hindsight, I should have done the same, or stayed home to play Grand Theft Auto VI.
