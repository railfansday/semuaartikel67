Your AI Can Stay in the Country and Still Cross the Wrong Border

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that consider a US bank that has approved an AI agent to help staff review customer disputes. The bank has chosen an AI model hosted in a US data center and checked that its deployment meets the processing-location commitments made to customers.

During one review, the agent needs to extract information from an attached document. It calls a third-party tool that sends the document to an overseas service. The AI model stayed in the US, but the customer’s information crossed a border the bank never intended it to, and the agent may have done exactly what the employee asked. The problem lies in how the agent completed the task and whether anyone checked where the information would go.

This is becoming a significant concern as enterprises give AI agents access to business systems. An agent can retrieve records and call external services while completing a task, introducing processing locations that may not have been included in the original AI deployment assessment. NIST’s research on tool use in agent systems highlights how tool permissions and their ability to access external resources affect the risks of using them.

For CIOs, this means ensuring that every service an AI agent uses complies with the business’s data sovereignty requirements, even when an outage forces traffic to be rerouted.

Industry observers note that A bank may have agreed to keep some customers’ information in the US while allowing other records to be processed in approved overseas locations. Its own security policies may impose tighter restrictions. Those commitments must be reflected in the AI systems employees use, so engineers need to know which models and external tools can receive each customer’s information.

It has been revealed that AWS addresses this in its October 2026 Digital Sovereignty Lens announcement. Its guidance examines the infrastructure companies use, the third-party services they depend on, and where they store and process data.

Once the agent retrieves a customer’s records, it may pass a document to another service for processing. That creates a separate decision about who receives the information and where it goes. The employee’s permission to access the account does not necessarily extend to sharing its contents with an external provider.

Recent findings suggest that the OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications identifies tool misuse and identity-related abuse among the risks that organizations need to address when agents take actions on their behalf. These risks require controls where the action occurs. Agents should have defined permissions for the tools they can use, and outgoing requests need appropriate checks before sensitive information is transmitted.

It has been revealed that an AI gateway may govern requests sent to models, while an agent calls other services through separate connections. If those connections aren’t covered by the relevant controls, governing the model’s traffic will leave a gap.

You shouldn’t trust a service simply because it operates on an approved network. NIST’s zero-trust guidance for multi-cloud applications recommends checking the identity of the application or service requesting access and whether that access is permitted.

Sovereign AI adds another limitation to the supply-and-demand problem that AI infrastructure already manages. A organization might have several AI providers to choose from, but the best option for a request depends on what each provider can offer at the time. A cheaper model may be too slow, while another may already be struggling to handle demand.

For the bank, a fast, inexpensive model is no use if it processes customer information in a country the contract doesn’t allow. The bank also needs to know that whichever provider receives the request is authorized to handle those records.

In a recent development, smarter routing applies those restrictions to each request. A destination that falls outside the customer’s approved options is excluded, leaving the system to weigh cost, performance, and available capacity among the eligible providers.

The underlying policies still need to come from the business, and their enforcement must extend to the relevant model and tool traffic. Routing cannot protect a connection it never sees or compensate for permissions that were incorrectly granted.

I expect more enterprises and service providers to build these policy-aware routing capabilities into their AI control planes as AI traffic spreads across more applications and infrastructure.

According to recent reports, the bank’s primary AI service goes down. Another provider in Canada could handle the requests, and under normal circumstances, switching over would make sense. But if the bank has promised that the affected customer information will be processed only in the US, that Canadian service is not an acceptable recovery destination.

Sources indicate that AWS’s Digital Sovereignty Lens documentation recognizes this trade-off. Strict residency requirements can limit recovery options, creating a direct relationship between sovereignty and continuity planning.

Industry observers note that the bank could prepare for this by arranging backup capacity with another approved provider or spreading its workloads across US locations. It could also have a different model ready to handle essential work if its usual service goes down, as long as the applications can use it.

When conditions change, the system can redirect requests to those approved alternatives without compromising the customer’s restrictions.

There will still be situations where no compliant alternative is available. The business then needs an agreed response. Some requests may have to wait, while certain workflows could continue with reduced functionality or an approved manual process. For a critical service that cannot tolerate those interruptions, the business must invest in additional compliant capacity in advance.

Service providers will need to give customers reliable information about where workloads run and what happens when traffic moves between their facilities. Enterprises will need ways to express their own restrictions and apply them across the AI services they use.

Neither can assume that a model’s advertised hosting location tells the complete story. A request’s actual path may involve other services, and compliance records should reflect those interactions.

That requires the AI control plane to coordinate routing with access policies and information about available destinations. Teams should also record decisions so they can understand why a particular service was selected and whether applicable restrictions were met.

Recent findings suggest that I would encourage CIOs to test this using an actual agent workflow. Follow the request through the model and every tool the agent calls, checking which services receive customer information and where they process it. Then see what happens when the primary inference destination becomes unavailable.

Does the system use another approved service, or does it send the request somewhere the customer never authorized? If the team cannot answer that confidently, it needs to revisit the design before an outage puts everyone under pressure to get the service running again.
