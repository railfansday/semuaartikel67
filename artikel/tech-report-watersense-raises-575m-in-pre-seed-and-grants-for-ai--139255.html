📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

WaterSense unveiled on November 26, 2026 that it had raised a combined €5.75 million in pre-seed investment and grants to expand AI-supported water-quality monitoring across Europe, including a strategy to monitor the Oder River continuously from its sources to its mouth.

The pre-seed round was co-led by b2venture and Movens Capital, with participation from vastpoint. The announcement separately identifies €3.5 million from Poland’s National Centre for Research and Development for further sensor development and satellite-data integration. That work is intended to add measurements for ammonium ions, nitrates, orthophosphates, fluorides, lead and E. coli, extending analysis from individual stations to entire water bodies and watercourses.

Recent findings suggest that waterSense is addressing a monitoring gap created by manual sampling: field teams collect water at individual sites and send it to laboratories, producing accurate results that can arrive days later and describe only past conditions. Its model replaces those isolated readings with continuously operating autonomous stations connected to a cloud analytics platform, giving utilities, industrial operators and public authorities a current view of rivers, lakes and industrial water systems.

The hardware uses hundreds of inexpensive miniature printed sensors, automatically replaced inside the station instead of relying on one expensive sensor that must remain stable through long exposure. WaterSense claims this limits the impact of biofouling, the buildup of aquatic organisms on equipment, and reduces servicing needs. The sensor design originated in founder Filip Budny’s doctoral research at Warsaw University of Technology.

Industry observers note that waterSense’s product description lists more than 25 water-quality parameters measured every ten minutes. The stations use hybrid solar and optional hydro power, supporting continuous operation without cables or external power infrastructure.

Sources indicate that field readings create a growing reference database connected to satellite observations and deep-learning models. WaterSense says the models can forecast water-quality changes up to 72 hours ahead and that AI agents turn the combined data into alerts, operational conclusions and decision support. The operational goal is earlier warning for intake protection and better-informed treatment decisions.

It has been revealed that the ASIR research project, which is developing an autonomous Internet of Things system for water-quality monitoring and threat identification, runs from September 1, 2024 through August 30, 2027. Alongside the station, it includes cloud-based anomaly detection and an interactive national river-pollution risk map for planning measurements and station placement. Warsaw University of Technology leads a consortium whose other members are the Institute of Meteorology and Water Management, MAGLY and NEBUCODE.

Industry observers note that waterSense reports monitoring more than 80 points for over 30 customers in four European countries. Its customers include industrial operators, large water utilities, cities and regional authorities. They receive continuous measurements, alerts, analysis and decision-support tools through a Data-as-a-Service subscription, under which WaterSense supplies the monitoring infrastructure and customers avoid building their own field teams and measurement systems.

Deployments include alpine lakes in Austria and urban water systems in large European cities. In Kitzbühel, Austria, the monitored parameters include water temperature, conductivity, pH, dissolved oxygen and phycocyanin, a pigment that serves as a blue-green algae indicator. The continuous view is intended to help local teams track changes between field visits and plan environmental stewardship and tourism activities.

It has been revealed that the upcoming capital is intended to move WaterSense beyond individual installations toward a denser monitoring network across European rivers, lakes and water systems. WaterSense states each deployment adds to a geographically varied reference dataset and strengthens its predictive analytics. The Oder project would extend continuous observation and decision support from selected locations to the full length of the river.

Recent findings suggest that the announcement cites the 2022 ecological crisis along hundreds of kilometres of the Oder, which affected water utilities and river-dependent businesses, as an example of decisions being made with information that arrives too late. The planned river-wide deployment gives the tech giant’s network model a concrete test: consistent field measurements and useful forecasts would need to support decisions across connected stretches of water, where pollution can affect consumers far beyond an individual monitoring point.
