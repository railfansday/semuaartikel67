Why We Learn More From What Goes Right

Conventional wisdom states that we learn more from failure than success, and our culture tends to support this, often romanticizing failure as a noble precursor to accomplishment. Business literature encourages us to “fail forward” or “fail better”—phrases that have become as ubiquitous as they are clichéd.

We know that globally, around 70 to 80 percent of business start-ups fail within 10 years, varying by industry and region. But wherever you go, the chances of failure of a start-up are overwhelmingly greater than the probability of success. Take the example of 80 in 100 new businesses ultimately failing—that means there are 20 recipes for success and 80 for failure. If you succeed, you have discovered one of the 20 routes to success, whereas if you fail, you have experienced one of the 80 routes to failure.

It has been revealed that in other words, in the case of a start-up business, experience of success is four times more valuable than experience of failure. Success will always be more instructive than failure when the probability of success is less than that of failure, which is virtually always the case in endeavors of impact and scale. They are never easy.

We are told that failure breeds resilience. Winston Churchill famously said, “Success is going from failure to failure without losing enthusiasm.” While that made sense in the bleak early days of World War II, when successes on the battlefield were rare, overall failure is overrated as a staging post on the path to success.

To be clear: I am not saying we can’t learn from failure, or that bouncing back from it isn’t hugely commendable and character-building. Importantly, the courage to fail emboldens people to try, and this is the bedrock of entrepreneurship and ultimately economic growth. But at an individual level, success is a better teacher.

At Discovery, we have learned from both success and failure, but our most profound lessons have come from learning what went right.

In 2008, we exited the U.S. health insurance market after eight years of trying to gain traction. Despite our efforts, we couldn’t attract enough members to create the scale to compete with incumbent giants. It was a very painful and instructive failure. And yes, we learned lessons that helped propel us onto the global stage. Today, instead of competing head-on, we partner with global insurers.

Recent findings suggest that but what has been the biggest cause of our progress? Is it learning from our failure in the U.S.? Or is it the insights drawn from our success in incentivizing healthier behavior and turning that into a scalable global model known as Vitality?

In a recent development, both have been important. But in my view, the lessons from success have been more transformative.

According to recent reports, the reasons why we learn more from success than failure lie in our psychology. Emotionally, failure is difficult to absorb. It feels bad. And when something feels bad, we tune out and don’t absorb the lessons as deeply.

Behavioral science reveals that people tend to avoid personal bad news. For example, various studies show that up to 54 percent of individuals who undergo an HIV diagnostic test will not collect their results.

Cognitively, failure yields less useful information than success. Whereas success reveals patterns and principles that can be replicated, failure tends to be less instructional—it tells us what not to do but rarely illuminates what we should do.

Research by American motivational psychologists Lauren Eskreis-Winkler and Ayelet Fishbach, who aim to better understand the nature of success by studying failure, found that failure undermines learning. In their 2019 paper, “Not Learning from Failure—The Greatest Failure of All,” they reported on five studies with over 1,600 participants, who were asked a series of binary choice questions. For example: How much money do U.S. companies lose annually due to poor customer service? They were given only two possible answers to choose from: $60 billion or $90 billion. They were then given the correct answers to all questions and re-tested. Given that there were only two possible answers in each case, participants ought to have been able to remember the right one. But those who got the answer correct the first time did better on re-testing than those who got it wrong the first time. Why?

Industry observers note that “Our society celebrates failure as a teachable moment,” they wrote. “Yet in five studies, failure did the opposite: it undermined learning. Why does failure undermine learning? Failure is ego-threatening, which causes people to tune out.”.

In the field of medicine, a fascinating study from Harvard Business School—“Learning From My Success and From Others’ Failure”—tracked 71 cardiothoracic surgeons over 10 years and 6,500 operations. The research, by KC Diwas and colleagues, found that surgeons learned more from their own successes than from their own failures—and more from others’ mistakes than others’ successes. Why? Because others’ failures don’t trigger the same ego threat, so we tune in more readily.

Ironically, while failure demands resilience, it doesn’t always build it. Success does. It reminds us that winning and progress are possible.

Recent findings suggest that of course, there are times when failure is immensely instructive. Plane crashes happen so rarely that when they do, they present rich opportunities for learning. If you are flying a space shuttle, you are only as strong as your weakest link, and studying these allows you to avoid the same catastrophic outcome twice.

But in complex networked systems, where processes are not linear, and where the chances of failure are greater than the chances of success, it is success that teaches best.
