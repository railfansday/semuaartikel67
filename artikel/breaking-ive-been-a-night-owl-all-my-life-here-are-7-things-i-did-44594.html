Breaking: I’ve Been a Night Owl All My Life. Here Are 7 Things I Did to Become a Morning Exerciser.

  • Waking up in the morning has never been my strong suit.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that I am a consummate night owl. When I was a teenager, my mother used to begin waking me up by knocking on my door, then taking my pillow away, then my bed sheets. When she’d find me still asleep on the bare mattress, she’d push me onto the floor. That’s where my little brother would find me, still asleep, and begin wailing that I needed to drive him to school because he didn’t want to be late (again).

Waking up in the morning has never been my strong suit. But when I graduated from college and no longer had a coach holding me to a daily afternoon cross-country practice, I found that exercise stopped being a priority. By the time I got off work at 5 p.m., I was too tired to go to the gym, or I had dinner aims, or had eaten something weird and decided it was impossible to run.

It has been revealed that for weeks at a time, exercise just wouldn’t happen. Certain that turning myself into a morning exerciser would solve all my problems, I told my mom I was on a mission to become an early bird. She shook her head. “I’ve been trying my whole life,” she noted. “If you figure it out, you let me know.”.

According to recent reports, most folks consider the night-owl versus early-bird categorization a matter of intrinsic wiring—you’re born one or the other, and you cannot change. While there is a strong genetic basis for your natural circadian rhythm, it is possible to switch. In fact, after about a year of working to adjust my sleep cycle, I now wake up at or before 6 a.m. almost every morning (even when I don’t necessarily want to). That means I now get to the gym or trail four or five days a week instead of one or two. Here are all the things I did to help me make the change.

Industry observers note that telling myself that “I will wake up and exercise” was too vague. “I will wake up, drive to the park, walk three miles, and then get a latte at my favorite coffee shop” is much easier to visualize (and get excited for). In the early morning, I only ever plan on doing fun exercise, whether that’s going to the climbing gym, doing pull-ups at the park, or running my favorite loop. If it’s a workout I’m likely to skip at 11 a.m., I definitely will at 6.

Industry observers note that I’m a very guilt-motivated person. If I only have to meet myself at the gym, I’ll stand me up every time. But if there’s another human being waiting, I’ll be there. I am fortunate to know several very motivated and punctual lady friends who also like to lift weights at 7 a.m. Making intends with them two or three times per week keeps me honest.

In a recent development, if I’ve paid for something, I feel a compulsive need to get my money’s worth out of it—even if that means waking up at dawn to do so. One of the best things I did for my sleep schedule was register for a recreational adult climbing team with 6 a.m. practices. I’ve also tried classes like CrossFit or F45, many of which charge $15 to $30 if you no-show.

Caveat: I find the class strategy is only effective if it’s recurring; I’m much more motivated to show up for a group of people I see every day than for a batch of strangers I’ll never see again.

When I’m warm in bed, tired, and dreading exercise, I make the smallest possible promise: I tell myself that I will go to the gym and only do one set of squats. Or only 15 minutes of stretching. Or only a single mile on the treadmill. If I feel good, I’ll do more (and usually, I do). But if only the minimum gets done, that’s still a win.

In a recent development, the less you have to think about it in the morning, the easier it is to roll out of bed. Do every possible chore the night before. Pack your gym bag ahead of time. Grind your coffee, put water in the kettle, and set your car keys by the door.

Industry observers note that this is my winter hack. When it’s cold in the morning, the last thing I want to do is take off my PJs and put on a frigid sports bra. So, I sleep in my running clothes. When my alarm goes off, all I have to do is put on shoes and hit the road.

It can be tempting to hit snooze on the weekends. Resist. If you need to take a short nap here or there to catch up on hours, do it. But sleeping in too long or too often can mess with your body’s natural circadian rhythm—and send you back to square one.
