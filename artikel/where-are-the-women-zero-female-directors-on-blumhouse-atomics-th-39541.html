Where Are the Women? Zero Female Directors on Blumhouse Atomic’s Theatrical Slate for 2027

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that blumhouse Atomic put a number on just about everything at its “Business of Fear” press conference on Wednesday. The merged and renamed horror studio put its share of this year’s worldwide horror box office at more than 50 percent.

Sources indicate that it has 10 movies scheduled to open nationwide in 2027. Six are originals. Four are upcoming franchise entries. Seven come from “emerging voices,” including three first-time feature filmmakers. At the end of the day’s first panel, IndieWire asked Jason Blum about a number that didn’t make the slides: How many of those 10 movies were directed by women? Zero.

“So we are working with a lot of women,” Blum remarked after running through several names. “They didn’t fall in next year’s slate.” The Blumhouse founder and CEO added that it was “very, very notable to make sure that the directors reflect our audience and look like our audience, so we’re always making a big effort to make sure that that happens.” Related Stories ‘Bedford Park’ Trailer: Sundance Winner Stars Moon Choi, Who Falls for the Other Driver in Her Mother’s Car Accident David Ellison on Elon Musk’s Skydance Investment, Trump and ‘Rush Hour 4,’ and Whether He Would’ve Made ‘Digger’.

It was the last question, and neither Atomic Monster founder James Wan nor Blumhouse president Abhijay Prakash expanded on Blum’s answer. Blumhouse Television president Melissa Aouate then took the microphone to moderate the second conversation.

She was the only woman onstage across both discussions, and though Blum had just cited “Scarpetta” showrunner Liz Sarnoff in his answer to IndieWire, Aouate went on to interview four men, including another company president, about their work while no one asked about hers.

In a later statement to IndieWire, Aouate mentioned she welcomed the chance to bring television into a conversation largely about film and wanted to find “more connective tissue between our film and television slates.”.

“By most measures, opportunities for women have contracted in so many areas of the industry, and we take that very seriously,” she said, citing Sarnoff on “Scarpetta,” Cynthia Childs on the “Worst Ever” franchise, and Emma Tammi on “Five Nights at Freddy’s” as examples of major women storytellers working at the company.

Recent findings suggest that “The industry still has a long way to go, and so do we,” she noted. “That’s work we take seriously, and we’re all working to see our slate reflect it.”.

In a recent development, A Blumhouse Atomic spokesperson told IndieWire that women directed 25 percent of the organization’s 2026 film slate. The spokesperson did not specify which titles or release formats counted toward that figure, but both 2026 films Blum cited onstage at least premiered at home.

Recent findings suggest that “We had ‘The Last Kiss’ by a female director,” Blum said of Yoko Okumura, whose film debuted in September, shortly after Kate Dolan’s “SOULM8TE” went straight to digital in August. Blum nodded to that project, too, and he also mentioned Tammi, who got a nationwide theatrical release for “Five Nights at Freddy’s” in 2023 before getting hired back for its 2025 sequel.

By the manufacturer’s own accounting, that second gig made obvious financial sense.

Recent findings suggest that prakash plainly explained on Wednesday that when Blumhouse Atomic calculates a return on investment, it divides worldwide ticket sales by the production budget. The first “Freddy’s” earned nearly $300 million worldwide on a reported $20 million budget, while 2025’s “Five Nights at Freddy’s 2” brought in another $240 million against a $51 million budget. By Prakash’s formula, the two films grossed roughly 7.6 times their combined costs.

IndieWire counted 27 features from Blumhouse and Atomic Monster that played in at least 600 U.S. theaters from 2021 through 2025. Our review counted co-productions once and included the first “Five Nights at Freddy’s,” which debuted simultaneously on Peacock.

Still, women helmed only two, and Tammi directed both. (Asked whether she would return for a third “Freddy’s” film, a Blumhouse Atomic spokesperson told IndieWire, “No updates to share on the next installment of ‘Five Nights at Freddy’s’ at this time.”).

According to recent reports, looking back to streaming, there’s a meaningful difference in opportunity for women. Over the same five years, 22 features from Blumhouse and the former Atomic Monster debuted in the U.S. via streaming, premium TV, or digital on demand without a theatrical run. Women directed or co-directed nine of those projects, accounting for roughly 41 percent of films. That’s compared with 7 percent of the companies’ nationwide theatrical releases.

“Obsession” and “Backrooms,” of course, came up repeatedly. Curry Barker’s indie topped the firm’s horror chart with more than $500 million worldwide. Kane Parsons’ feature adaptation of his YouTube series earned another $400 million. “Indie horror is suddenly performing as well or better than studio horror,” Blum said. A decade ago, he estimated, those same movies “would’ve made $25 million at the box office.”.

He further credited Barker’s years on YouTube with teaching him how to read an audience, noting he’s producing Barker’s second feature, “Anything but Ghosts,” due out next May. Wan also remembered an early Zoom with Parsons, who was young enough that his dad was on the call then.

Recent findings suggest that by the time IndieWire asked where women fit into the 2027 slate, Blum and Wan had thoroughly detailed what they saw in these two young men and how they helped each grow. On the second panel, Aouate asked Atomic Monster president Michael Clear what it takes for a fresh director to get his attention. He described what he tells agents about someone who hasn’t directed a horror scene.

Sources indicate that “It doesn’t mean I might not work with them in the future, but have them go direct a scare in their apartment at two minutes. That’s what we want,” he said. Of Parsons’ “Backrooms” short, he recalled, “It scared me, and that was it.”.

Sources indicate that the organization didn’t say how often shorts directed by women reach decision-makers like Clear. But asked how Blumhouse Atomic seeks out women directors these days, a spokesperson cited “traditional channels and less obvious ones, like our Screamwriting Lab and the horror shorts and online talent our team scouts.”.

Industry observers note that one woman whose work has caught their attention online is Heidi Wong, who built a following of millions telling illustrated horror stories on TikTok. Last month at New York Comic Con, Wong and Blum announced a deal to develop short films together. Her social media success seems to make her exactly the kind of emerging talent Blumhouse Atomic wants to cultivate. Whether that partnership leads to a theatrical feature remains to be seen.

Eight years ago, Blum apologized after telling Polygon that fewer women were inclined to direct horror movies. In response, researcher Stephen Follows examined three decades of feature films released in U.S. theaters, from 1988 through 2017. Women accounted for just 5.9 percent of horror directors in his sample. Importantly, that figure tells us who got a film into theaters. It notes very little about who wanted to make one or how close they came.

Of course, the disparity extends beyond horror. Across all genres last year, women accounted for 13 percent of directors among the 250 highest-grossing films at the domestic box office. That’s down from 16 percent in 2024, per San Diego State University’s Celluloid Ceiling report.

Blumhouse Atomic also has six distribution partners on its 2027 slate. Plus, films that ultimately premiere at home may have been developed with theaters in mind. The same organization spokesperson emphasized to IndieWire that women on the company’s creative team often find and develop major movies, too, running some of the brand’s most beloved franchises.

Industry observers note that over the five years IndieWire examined, though, Tammi was still the only woman to direct a Blumhouse movie that reached horror audiences in theaters nationwide. Yes, those 10 release dates for next year could be an accident of timing, with real gender parity just around the corner. But years of women getting fewer chances to scare audiences on the significant screen suggest something more enduring than a scheduling coincidence. For a business built on knowing what scares people, Hollywood’s horror industry seems remarkably unafraid of shutting women out.
