Private equity–backed companies face a $300 billion debt wall. They’re looking at your retirement account for an exit

🌐 Source: Fortune • 📅 21 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 4 min read (728 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways
  • The private credit and private equity industries currently face two huge problems.
  • Second, private equity–backed companies face a $300 billion wall of debt coming due in 2028 and 2029.
  • Both of these problems together signal desperate times for the private credit and private equity industries.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The private credit and private equity industries currently face two huge problems. First, the private equity industry sits on a nine-year backlog of holdings that it wishes it could IPO or sell to other companies, but because of recent poor performance it cannot seem to offload these positions. PitchBook has reported that while private equity funds look to exit positions in three to five years, 30% of all holdings out there have been sitting on private equity balance sheets for six or more years (a record high).

Second, private equity–backed companies face a $300 billion wall of debt coming due in 2028 and 2029. Normally, this would not be an matter, but almost all of this debt was refinanced in 2020 and 2021, when interest rates were near zero, and now will need to be refinanced at far higher rates, with no clear buyers in sight.

Both of these problems together signal desperate times for the private credit and private equity industries. If traditional channels of financing are not going to work, then they must find another source of non-traditional financing to offload these positions to.

Sources indicate that and, the private asset industry may have found just the unsuspecting party – retail investors. Given the recent changes in the definition of “sophisticated investors” who were the standard investors that were allowed to invest in private equity/credit and rollbacks in enforcement of vehicles that could hold such products, retail investors are now at the front lines to be force-fed these products through clever marketing.

According to recent reports, not only are private credit and private equity holdings showing up in ETFs and mutual funds, they are showing up in new products like perpetuals that are highly en vogue among retail investors. Even more nefarious, private credit is slowly making its way into 401(k) looks at the behest of the White House, which has pitched it to investors as an effort to “Democratize access to private assets.”.

Now, why is this all so bad? If markets are efficient, then retail investors will just be paying a fair price for such assets, correct? Well, with private credit and private equity assets, no one knows the fair price because they are unlisted and non-traded. Traditionally, it takes months of due diligence by experts to come to a correct price for each individual asset when it is sold in an IPO or to another firm. Now, we are to assume retail investors can do this analysis on illiquid assets in hours or minutes to determine if it is right for them to hold – not likely.

Recent findings suggest that aside from the hard-to-value matter, private assets also come with higher fees. The traditional 2 and 20 fee structure (2% of AUM and 20% of profits) will surely be passed on to retail investors in whatever form the private asset takes in their portfolio. These costs far surpass the average ETF expense ratio of 0.2% in the large-cap equity space. To see how expensive it can get, WisdomTree offers a private credit ETF (ticker HYIN) with an annual expense ratio of 4.34%. That means the fund itself needs to return more than 4.34% a year just for an investor to break even. Next, aside from fees, these private assets understate their inherent risk through a process Cliff Asness has dubbed “volatility laundering.” So if they claim the risk of the product is equivalent to a low-risk bond, it may exhibit the risk of a small-cap equity.

Finally, some high-end investors have already begun to see how these investments can turn sour. Over the summer, many high-net-worth investors asked to redeem their money from several BDCs and other private credit funds. These withdrawal requests were denied (above the contractual level permitted) – what investors thought was a safe place to store money in a deposit-like instrument turned out to be far riskier than they thought.

In all, opacity, high cost, terms to redemption and volatility laundering all spell potential doom for retail investors if they buy into these private asset products, and they need to be on high alert when such products are offered to them as long-term, safe investments for their retirement funds.
