Porto Rocha rebuilt the Kunsthalle Basel identity around one stretchable KB monogram, a custom serif, and color that finally reveals up.

According to recent reports, kunsthalle Basel has spent 150 years showing artists before anyone else caught on, from Marcel Duchamp to Anne Imhof, with no permanent collection and a habit of betting on what comes next. Its old identity could not carry that energy. Director Mohamed Almusibli arrived in 2024 wanting the institution to act as a true host, warm and open to anyone curious enough to walk in, and Porto Rocha answered with what the studio calls an institutional brand with an anti-institutional spirit. Everything hangs on one mark. The K and B merge into a single gesture that reads as either letter, a confident stamp when it sits still and a shape that stretches, compresses and rotates when the context asks it to.

Type does the talking once the mark steps back. Porto Rocha worked with Swiss foundry Maxitype on KB Rhymes, a custom headline serif with roots in Times Upcoming Roman, humanist in feel and slightly condensed so it holds its own at large sizes without shouting over the art. Suisse International handles body copy and exhibition text, plain and highly legible. The specimen page sets artist names in KB Rhymes at poster scale, white on black, and the letters carry the same soft serifs and generous curves as the monogram. Behind the mark, a construction grid pins the K inside an oval, with guide lines marking the geometry that keeps the stretched and rotated versions honest.

In a recent development, the system earns its keep on the small things. A stack of cards carries the K at full bleed, black on white. A flat lay of programs, posters, a keyring and a lighter indicates the mark in a dozen colors at once, from a pink oval to a blue Werkstatt Sessions cover. The matchbox is the best of the bunch, a black box with a white K that slides open on purple-tipped matches.

Sources indicate that out on the street, the Opening Night poster frames a blue K oval on the stone facade, and a brushed metal plaque sets the orange mark under silver Kunsthalle Basel lettering. In the galleries, the identity steps aside and lets the work and the neoclassical rooms do the talking, which is the point. Two color modes run through it all, neutral beside artwork and loud in type-led layouts, where the old identity avoided color altogether.
