Six days, six outages: Why reliability isn’t resilience

UPDATED 20:21 EST / NOVEMBER 18 2026.

GUEST COLUMN by Zeus Kerravala.

A rash of major outages over the past week all seem to follow a similar script. Services go dark, Downdetector lights up, the provider posts a string of status updates, a root cause emerges a few days later, and everyone goes back to work, assuming it won’t happen again. This past week, that script was on overdrive.

Recent findings suggest that none of these was a cyberattack, and as far as anyone can tell, none was related. A dormant bug, overloaded legacy routers, a bad internal enhancement and an on-premises hardware glitch are about as different as root causes get. Some of these systems run in the public cloud, some in a tech giant’s own data center, and some on air traffic control gear that’s decades old. That’s precisely the point. The common thread isn’t the technology. It’s that each organization had a critical dependency fail, and the business stopped with it.

It has been revealed that that’s the lesson I want business leaders to take away. Outages aren’t rare events to be explained away after the fact. They’re a certainty to plan for, and the plan must address what happens when an entire service fails, not just a server or a data center.

I wanted to dig into what real resilience looks like, and Zscaler Inc. is an interesting case study for a specific reason. When a company deploys Zscaler, every user, branch and device connects to applications through its Zero Trust Exchange. That places Zscaler squarely in the critical path. If it goes down, users can’t reach Microsoft 365, Salesforce, internal apps or anything else, no matter how healthy those applications are. That all-or-nothing exposure has forced Zscaler to think harder than most about what happens when its service fails.

At the last Zenith Live, Zscaler’s user event, I asked Chief Executive Jay Chaudhry about this, and he explained that because of the critical nature of their offering, it can’t afford any downtime, so the manufacturer built resiliency into its service design. After all these outages, I asked to speak with Chaudhry and Chief Reliability Officer Misha Kuperman to gather lessons learned for the industry.

It has been revealed that to be clear, Zscaler couldn’t have kept a single Microsoft mailbox online or restarted the FAA’s routers, and no one at the organization claimed otherwise. But its approach to continuity for its own platform is a useful model for how any service on a company’s critical path, including software-as-a-service, should be architected. Here are my thoughts on what this means for boards and C-suite leaders:.

Sources indicate that I came from financial services, where the disaster recovery site was non-negotiable and everyone knew which services had to survive a failure. Chaudhry, who spent years at IBM Corp., made the same point.

It has been revealed that “Most of my large customers had a production data center and a DR data center,” he told me. “If the production data center went down, they’d fail over to DR. That was standard practice. When the world moved to the cloud, they forgot about it, thinking disaster recovery was the cloud provider’s responsibility, even though it is actually a shared responsibility.”.

It has been revealed that he’s right, and the record proves it. Availability zones and multiregion designs protect against hardware failures and localized events, but they share software, control planes, configuration pipelines and often DNS. When a bad configuration or a dormant bug propagates, it spreads everywhere. That’s exactly what happened to Microsoft.

Availability architecture and resilience architecture aren’t the same thing. One keeps a service up when a component fails. The other keeps the business running when the entire service fails. Most companies have the first and assume they have the second, but as we have seen recently, that assumption is incorrect.

During the call, Chaudhry used an analogy that everyone can understand. You can own the world’s most reliable car and service it religiously, yet you still buy insurance for other risks not covered by the warranty, such as theft or a tree falling on it. Having insurance and knowing when and how to use it helps you get back on the road quickly. Zscaler has built the cyber equivalent.

Recent findings suggest that in the real world, people understand it. In cyber and digital, we seem to forget and rely on service-level agreements. A 99.999% uptime commitment sounds good until you translate it into business terms. Chaudhry stated some of Zscaler’s largest banking customers told the organization that five nines wasn’t enough, “because we could lose hundreds of millions of dollars if you went down even for five minutes.”.

Going back to my IT days, I’ve never liked SLAs because they are financial instruments, not operational ones. They tell you what credit you’ll receive after the fact, not how you’ll keep processing payments, as Chase customers learned, or how you’ll keep planes in the air, as United learned. Regulators have recognized this. The EU’s Digital Operational Resilience Act and Australia’s CPS 230 both require regulated firms to assess, test, and mitigate the risk posed by their technology providers. “Our cloud provider went down” is no longer an acceptable defense.

IT executives should stop asking, “What’s our provider’s SLA?” and start asking, “What must our business continue to do when a critical dependency fails, and have we proven we can do so?”.

Recent findings suggest that the instinctive response to an outage like Microsoft’s is to buy from a second provider. Chaudhry shared a story about a chief information officer whose CEO had flagged Office 365 as a business risk and told him to find an alternative. The CIO signed up for Google Workspace. When asked whether documents, links and role-based access controls would work after a switch, even in half the cases, he said he didn’t think so.

According to recent reports, that’s the reality. Email alone is hard enough because users lose their history the moment they switch. Modern productivity suites aren’t storage anymore. Every email includes links to shared documents, permissions are tied to identity, and workflows span multiple apps. Kuperman framed the problem in terms of scale: “I can switch between Gmail and Outlook, but can 10,000 people? Will they be as productive?”.

The same problem applies in security and networking, where the stakes are arguably higher. The vendor A/vendor B model requires two policy sets kept in perfect sync, two client software sets and a way to move tens of thousands of customers and devices from one to the other in the middle of a crisis. Kuperman described one customer for whom it “used to take seven days to switch over and 30 days to switch back. You can’t run a business like that.”.

According to recent reports, A backup that’s never tested at full scale isn’t a backup. It’s a hope. And a backup that changes the user experience will fail exactly when it’s needed.

Recent findings suggest that another instinct is to turn off security entirely; however, when a crisis occurs, threat actors will rally and launch campaigns. During the CrowdStrike outage, phishing campaigns were mobilized almost immediately, and the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency had to problem warnings not to take the bait. Threat actors are present both internally and externally, so security must remain on guard during critical failures, especially when they are publicly known.

This brings me to what Zscaler has done with its Business Continuity Cloud and why I think it’s the right template for the industry. Rather than asking customers to bolt on a second vendor, Zscaler built a dedicated, per-customer environment that’s architecturally separate from its primary Zero Trust Exchange.

Sources indicate that the design choices are important to understand. The Business Continuity Cloud runs in third-party data centers, not Zscaler’s own, on separate networks, autonomous system numbers, DNS, and cloud domains. Software and threat-intelligence updates are deliberately staggered so a bad production release can’t land in the backup at the same time. Policy and identity data sync one way into a read-only, last-known-good state. It’s offered across nine regions, enough to keep operations in-country if a nation were cut off.

Sources indicate that “No linkage — no network, no shared routers, no switches, no shared network operations,” Chaudhry noted. That’s the old production/DR discipline, rebuilt for the cloud. After watching overloaded routers ground the nation’s air traffic, “no shared routers” doesn’t sound like overkill.

There were two operational details I felt were worth noting. First, Zscaler encourages customers to continuously route synthetic or trusted-user traffic through the backup environment in peacetime, so the failover path is validated daily rather than in an annual tabletop exercise. Second, the failover is automated to optimize for a low recovery time objective or RTO, or it can be triggered by the customer, ensuring it works even if Zscaler itself is unreachable.

Industry observers note that zscaler says nearly 100 customers now use its service, including a global bank with more than 300,000 people, a global pharmaceutical firm with more than 100,000, and a U.S. telco with more than 75,000. The company charges what Chaudhry called a nominal fee to cover the dedicated infrastructure, network and always-on capacity.

The lesson from the Microsoft outage, in particular, isn’t that customers need a second productivity suite. It’s that the primary provider should offer a fate-separated continuity environment as part of the service. Zscaler has shown it can be done for a global security cloud. I’d like to see every SaaS provider that sits in a tech giant’s critical path, and Microsoft in particular, be pressed by large customers to offer the same.

According to recent reports, the Microsoft outage also exposed a concentration risk that many companies have faced through enterprise licensing agreements. Microsoft noted the incident affected not only Exchange and Teams but also Defender XDR, Defender for Office 365 and Intune. For many organizations, Microsoft is the productivity suite, the identity provider and the security stack. When the shared infrastructure beneath them fails, all three can fail together.

In a recent development, kuperman described the minimum set of capabilities a business needs to keep operating during a disaster: people, identity, network and security, and business-critical applications. Each must work, and any one can fail. “You have to think about where your eggs are and which baskets they’re in,” he said.

In a recent development, I’m not arguing against platforms. But boards should understand where consolidation has created a single point of failure across identity, applications and security, and ensure those layers don’t share the same fate.

This won’t be the last major outage, and the next one may be a cyberattack, a war, or a configuration push rather than a dormant bug. Here’s what I recommend IT and business leaders do now:.

Industry observers note that the cloud delivered on its promise of agility and scale, but in the process, the industry let a hard-won discipline lapse. This past week reminded us that everything fails eventually, whether in the cloud, a data center or an air traffic control tower. When it does, an SLA credit won’t keep the business running. A real continuity blueprint will.
