Astrophysical jets launched from black holes aren't shy. They announce their presence with enormous blasts of energy that extend extreme distances into space. They're the most long-lasting and luminous sources of electromagnetic energy in the Universe.

Recent findings suggest that supermassive black holes (SMBH) emit the largest astrophysical jets, and they come from active galactic nuclei (AGN) which are SMBH that are actively accreting material. As material gathers in an accretion disk around a SMBH, it rotates and heats up. Some of the material is ejected out of the black hole in the powerful jets.

These jets are impossible to ignore. No astrophysicist could feign their interest. Exactly how these powerful expressions of cosmic energy are created isn't clear, though some things are known.

Astrophysicists know that they're powered by a SMBH's rotational energy and their extremely powerful magnetic fields. They know that they move at relativistic speeds. And they know that they can inject gigantic amounts of energy into the intergalactic medium, heating up gas and introducing turbulence, two things that act against star formation.

One thing that puzzles researchers is how the different regions in a black hole jet have different brightnesses. The expectation is that the center of the jets would be brightest, because this is where energy is most concentrated. But when researchers examine the jets coming from the SMBH in Centaurus A, the closest radio galaxy to Earth, that's not what they see.

Since it's so close and so bright, Centaurus A is one of those natural laboratories for extreme physics that makes astrophysicists' eyes light up. They've been observing the jets in Centaurus A in detail for many years. Observations with the Event Horizon Telescope spanning a decade have revealed that its jets are brighter at their edges than in their centers, in a phenomenon called limb brightening. Why is that?

Industry observers note that the researchers performed a magnetohydrodynamic simulation of an accreting supermassive black hole and how it debuted it jets to observe the plasma dynamics involved. Since these jets are relativistic, the simulation included general relativistic effects. They also adjusted their simulation to match the properties of Centaurus A. "In order to match our model to the EHT observations, we adjust several free parameters..." the authors write, including black hole mass and the viewing angle.

The simulation successfully mimicked the recent observations of Centaurus A with the EHT.

"Our results demonstrate that the edge-brightening observed in Cen A can be naturally reproduced by an anisotropic particle distribution," the researchers write.

Sources indicate that that means that the electrons emitted in the jets aren't distributed evenly over pitch angles. Pitch angles are the difference between the magnetic field and each electron's velocity. This is where synchrotron radiation enters the picture. Synchrotron radiation is when particles are forced to follow the curve of a magnetic field.

The emission of synchrotron radiation is strongly dependent on the pitch angle. So as a result, the observed brightness of the radiation changes depending on the viewing angle of the magnetic field the particles are forced to follow.
