On a tight budget, decide what the whole year of gifts can cost before the first invitation arrives. Save toward that total each month, and agree with your family on fewer gifts before anyone shops. Then choose gifts that cost little to give, since people rarely appreciate a pricier gift more than a cheaper one.

It has been revealed that in three studies by researchers at Stanford Graduate School of Business, people giving gifts expected a pricier one to earn more appreciation, but the people receiving them felt no more appreciative when a gift cost more. The thought behind a gift counts for more than its price, which leaves room to spend less on each one.

Recent findings suggest that birthdays, showers, weddings, and holidays arrive all year, and a gift paid for only when its occasion comes up often ends up on a credit card. The moves below cover planning ahead to prevent that, plus presents that cost you little or nothing to give.

Every other move on this list helps you stay under the total this one sets, and saving monthly means a June wedding gift comes out of money already set aside.

It has been revealed that budget for a whole year of gifts at once instead of paying for birthdays, weddings, showers, and the holidays as they come. Total what you spent on gifts last year from your card and bank statements, set a cap for each person and occasion, and save a twelfth of this year’s total every month in a separate account.

It suits a family whose gift list grows every year with in-laws, nieces, and nephews, and once everyone agrees on a latest rule, nobody expects you to explain a homemade gift.

Recent findings suggest that if your family’s gift giving has outgrown your budget, say so plainly, weeks before the first gift is bought. Then propose one new rule, such as gifts for the kids only, a price cap, or a year off from presents. A specific plan is easy to say yes to, and no one gets caught off guard.

A draw keeps the holiday exchange going, so it suits relatives who would miss unwrapping something, and it works as well for a group of friends or an office as for a family.

Sources indicate that in a major family, buying a gift for every adult adds up fast. Propose a name draw before Thanksgiving instead, where each person draws one name and buys that person a gift under a price cap you all agree on. Everyone gives and gets one gift instead of a stack, and that one can be better.

Office showers, retirement parties, and milestone birthdays have many people giving to one person, so the small amount your budget sets for one occasion can buy a share of a present you couldn’t afford alone.

Industry observers note that pool your money with coworkers, friends, or siblings for one group gift instead of each buying a small present. Have one person choose it, set a share that fits the tightest budget in the group, and collect everyone’s money through a payment app before buying. Then give it with one card everyone signs.

If you have time to spare but little cash, homemade gifts cover a long list of neighbors, teachers, and coworkers for less than buying each of them a store-bought present.

It has been revealed that homemade gifts save the most when you make one or two ideas in a batch. Buy the supplies in bulk and make a dozen at once, so each gift costs a few dollars. Pick something people will eat or use up rather than a decoration that ends up in a closet.

Newly introduced parents, a friend who just moved, and a relative who no longer drives often need a hand more than another thing to unwrap, and they’re the people this gift suits best.

It has been revealed that when you can’t afford a present, give your time instead, matched to something the person needs, such as a night of babysitting, a home-cooked dinner, or a chore they keep putting off. Write it on a coupon with exactly what you’ll do and by when, and book the first session as you hand it over.

You choose the amount, so a donation fits whatever is left in your gift budget, even late in the year when most of it is spent.

In a recent development, A donation in someone’s name makes a fitting gift for a person who has everything or cares deeply about a cause, as long as it goes to a charity they already support. Use the charity’s option to give in their honor, add a note in your own words, and keep the receipt, since the tax deduction is yours.

Gifts you already own cost nothing to give, so a shelf of unopened ones can cover a birthday you forgot or a gift for a dinner host.

Regifting is a fine way to save money as long as the gift is newly introduced, unopened, and something you’d have bought the recipient anyway. Keep a list of who gave you what, and check it before you wrap anything, so a regift never reaches the original giver or anyone close to them.
