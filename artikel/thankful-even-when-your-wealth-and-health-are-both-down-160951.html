Thankful Even When Your Wealth And Health Are Both Down

Industry observers note that I don't know what's worse. Feeling incredibly sick or losing a ton of money in a very short time. What say you?

Industry observers note that lucky for me, I'm both sick and my public investments are down ~15% from their peak. In about 45 days, the market wiped out over three years of our living expenses. Getting beat up both ways hurts.

First, there's feeling dumb for not reducing more risk before the correction. I've been doing this for over 30 years. I should know better. Greed is a hell of a drug, especially when the 10-year bond yield is at ~5%. And the pain is probably not over.

It has been revealed that second, there's not being able to function at full strength. Every task takes twice the effort and gets half the result. It's hard for me to breathe and move at the moment. My lungs are bruised from all the dry coughing. Then there's the achy bones.

Sources indicate that if you're thinking about having both spouses retire early, think thrice. When the market tanks and your health falters, having one spouse who still brings in a paycheck and subsidized health insurance is incredibly comforting.

According to recent reports, but I'm still alive and somewhat kicking. So this Thanksgiving, I'm thankful.

Recent findings suggest that A healthy man wants a thousand things. A sick man wants only one: to feel better.

When you're sick long enough, your mind starts wandering to dark places.

Sources indicate that what if I don't get better? What if I only get worse? Thank goodness both my wife and I have matching 20-year term life insurance policies to get our kids through to adulthood.

Then my mind wanders to the market. What if this correction turns into a full-blown bear market as Congress throttles AI growth, which may lead to a recession? Anything is possible, which is why having an appropriate net worth allocation by age is key.

In a recent development, for the longest time, I've kept public equities between 25% and 35% of my net worth. That's the range I'm comfortable with for handling all the funny money volatility.

But I got lazy and let my exposure drift up to about 45%. Then I got hammered.

If you're feeling down and out, it's worth going through a list of things you're thankful for. It will make you feel better. Here are some off the top of my head.

Recent findings suggest that the highlight of the week came on Monday. I felt just strong enough to take the kids and wife to Alcatraz on a gorgeous, sunny San Francisco day, right before the rainstorm rolled in. Tuesday was when my energy began to plummet.

Recent findings suggest that it was their first time visiting the island, and their first time ever on a boat funny enough. Watching them stand at the railing as the city skyline got larger on our return was a lovely moment.

There's something fitting about touring a prison while feeling trapped by your own body and a falling market.

Sources indicate that I spent extra time just sitting in one of the cells, taking in what it would like to be locked up and constantly being told what to do. It brought back old memories when I got in trouble the summer after high school for prank calling. Prison living is the opposite of FIRE living.

Recent findings suggest that the visit also turned into a real-life lesson for the kids about the consequences of bad decisions. Standing in one of the dark solitary confinement cells will hopefully help them appreciate their nice rooms and the importance of being a good person. That noted, my almost 7-year-old admitted she was a little scared by the end.

Monday reminded me that time with the people you love is the best antidote to a falling portfolio and failing health. It's also the one asset that never comes back once it's gone.

If you've lost a lot of money and are dealing with a health glitch, or any other personal setback, here's what's helping me. The number one goal is to hang on until the good times return.

1) Health is the real net worth. No amount of money makes you feel good when you're sick. If you're healthy right now, you're richer than you think, regardless of what your brokerage account claims.

2) Measure your losses in time, not dollars. Losing a significant chunk of your portfolio sounds catastrophic. But ask how far back the losses take you. Often you're only back to where you were a year or two ago during a correction or bear market. However, if you're sick of work, then a more conservative portfolio adjustment should be made.

3) Don't make big decisions while you're sick or distraught. Selling everything at the bottom feels like relief in the moment. It's usually the most expensive relief you'll ever buy. Give yourself a cooling-off period before any major move. Leg out or into positions. No wholesale decisions.

In a recent development, 4) Focus on what you can control. You can't control the Fed, AI spending, a CEO's escapades, or a virus. You can control your spending, your asset allocation, your sleep, and how you treat the people around you. Lean into spending time with people and things that bring you the most joy.

Recent findings suggest that 5) Do one thing that gets you outside your head. For me, it was taking the kids to Alcatraz and painting my downstairs hallway. For you, it might be a walk, a phone call with an old friend, or cooking a meal. Action beats rumination.

6) Write down your gratitude list. When your mind keeps spiraling toward worst-case scenarios, putting the good things in writing is a way to fight back.

Perhaps what I'm most thankful for is the opportunity to try again.

Sources indicate that we've been through many corrections and bear markets before. None of them ever felt good. But over time, the stock market recovered, and we moved on with our lives.

The great thing about being at a low point is that there's likely more upside than downside. Yes, things can always get worse. But unless my illness turns fatal and the stock market falls more than 60%, forcing us to return to work, we'll be OK.

Recent findings suggest that finally, I can't talk well enough to record a podcast right now. But I can still move my fingers and write. So I'm thankful for the ability to keep going, no matter what.

Do you find that losing money makes you more or less grateful for what you have? And what's one thing you proposal to do this holiday season to get out of your own head?
