The performance of computer systems is extremely sensitive to workload mix.

Recent findings suggest that letâs start with a simple simulator, looking at a system with a table of locks. Requests arrive with a key, hold the lock for their key for some period of time, and then free the lock and complete. This isnât an unreasonable mental model of a relational database, where each transaction takes a lock during its execution.

Each request takes one of 16 worker threads, waits in a FIFO queue for the lock on its key, holds the lock for an exponentially distributed time (mean 1 ms), then releases it and frees its thread. Keys are drawn from a Zipf distribution over N keys; a coefficient of 0 is uniform.

It has been revealed that as we may expect, the more skewed the distribution, the less throughput you get out of the system. A small number of keys are highly contended, leading to under utilization of the rest of the resources of the machine, driven by the global concurrency limit.

According to recent reports, each request takes one of the same 16 worker threads and reads its key. A hit is served from an LRU cache; a miss goes to storage (exponentially distributed, mean 1 ms) and the key is added to the cache. Keys are drawn from a Zipf distribution over N keys; a coefficient of 0 is uniform. Note the log scale on the throughput axis.

According to recent reports, this curve goes the other way! The more skewed the distribution, the better the performance, over something of a broad range. This is what makes it so notable that workload mix in load tests and benchmarks matches real production traffic: throughput can vary by multiple orders of magnitude, in both directions, depending on things as subtle as key skew in the benchmark workload. This is especially true in relational database-shaped things, but applies to systems of all kinds.

In a recent development, aside: This is also a reason I like optimistic concurrency control, and architectures with a single cost/latency mode. Aurora DSQL reflects this thinking on how to make things less workload sensitive: all reads go to a single fast storage layer, and nobody is holding resources waiting on locks.
