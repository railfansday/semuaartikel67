June, 1977. Cliff Booth runs the projector at the New Beverly Cinema, tends a couple bars, and drives a tow truck to a demolition derby. These are not his only day jobs, and they’re all at night. In his spare time, the ex-stuntman disappears blackmailers with a payoff or a bullet. Something called Star Wars has taken over theaters, threatening a half-century of blockbuster family fun. Cliff inhabits an older world, gnarly yet noble, gory and shagadelic. He runs with blaxploitation macks, bored Malibu wives, underworld assassins, the snobby former critic of the Superfood Gazette and whoever that body in the trunk used to be. These are Cliff Booth’s people — and Quentin Tarantino’s, too. Once upon a time in the 1990s, Tarantino was the breakout bad boy of independent film, surf riding the crest of a youth tsunami. Now he’s our reigning entertainer-historian, overstuffing hard-R sensations with motion picture lore. He owns the real New Beverly in actual Los Angeles. So when Netflix agreed to make his Once Upon a Time in Hollywood follow-up, they paid him a fortune to advertise his theater. Franchises have been no-brainers since, well, Star Wars. But a sequel on a streamer is something new for Tarantino. And The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth marks the first time in 30 years that someone besides him has directed one of his screenplays. Let’s get rambling to find out where it ranks in his filmography.

According to recent reports, he swears he will make only 10 films. We’re starting at 17. Is he lying, or is this list stupid? Probably both. However, this ranking seeks totality: his screenplays, his TV diversions, the simple fact that Kill Bill counts as two. Let’s count all his official writing and directing credits — plus this middling erotic thriller, which earned him a co-producer credit for doctoring the script. (He later polished Crimson Tide and The Rock.) A beautiful social worker with a bodacious lake house gets knocked up by an ex-con who maybe killed his pregnant wife. You hear trace Tarantino elements emerging in Past Midnight’s dialogue, especially when a cop says the phrase “natural born killer.” And a very minor character, the snarky physician, is named “Dr. Zastoupil.” Quentin Tarantino was Quentin Zastoupil through childhood, keeping his stepfather’s surname even after his mom’s second divorce. A teen dropout with acting ambitions, he snagged “Tarantino” back from his biological father. Not because he loved the guy, matter fact he barely knew the son of a bitch. It just sounded better.

Recent findings suggest that after Pulp Fiction won him his first Oscar, he hosted Saturday Night Live, got his head blown off in his buddy Robert Rodriguez’s Desperado, and played an animistic “god with a small g” named Johnny Destiny in the interminable Vegas comedy Destiny Turns on the Radio. Then came this dire anthology. Miramax must have hoped for generational showcase, with studio mascot Tarantino joining Rodriguez and their Sundance youngfriends Allison Anders and Alexandre Rockwell. Each filmmaker writes and directs their own Latest Year’s Eve farce, set on different floors on a Tinseltown hotel. Tarantino also stars in his entry, playing a fresh-minted shirtbird celebrity who complains if you call his Cristal mere champagne. It’s a wearisome (if admirably self-aware) snapshot of his peak overexposure, and a disastrous victory lap for the renegade age of indie cool.

The rest of Four Rooms’ band of outsiders started directing the regular independent way, with little money and amateur pluck. Anders’ and Rockwell’s first features were black-and-white cheapos about arty goofs, and Rodriguez wrote a book about El Mariachi’s $7,000 budget. Reservoir Dogs looks insanely professional by comparison: soundtrack money, a prosthetic ear, Harvey Keitel. Tarantino’s missing link is this early unfinished comedy he directed about DJ pals on a manic night out. Acolytes peg My Best Friend’s Birthday as a True Romance beta test, though the fuzzy half-hour cut online suggests an alternate timeline where he made loosey autobiographical romcoms instead of meticulous profane epics. Pompadoured with earrings like some rockabilly nerdpunk, his character plainly admits he has a foot fetish.

He owns a couple theaters, co-hosts a movie podcast, and wrote a great book of film criticism. Still, deep down, I wonder if Quentin Tarantino is more of a TV guy. His work chases an old forever feeling of channel-surfing into some random weekly episode about characters walking from place to place, meeting people, and getting into adventures. In the 1996 biography King Pulp, author Paul A. Woods memorably quotes Steve Buscemi: “When I saw Pulp Fiction, the little boy watching this big TV, being alone in the room, the TV being his friend — to me, that’s Quentin.” Does that explain why, fresh off Kill Bill’s impressive exertion, he directed a couple hours of a hit procedural he adored? Wiliam Petersen’s brainiac cop Gil Grissom reveals an abiding fandom for Roy Rogers. Frank Gorshin and Tony Curtis play themselves. George Eads’ niceguy Nick hallucinates his own autopsy while buried alive.

CSI was already half-lame by 2005. This NBC hospital drama was still blazingly cool in its first season. And while Tarantino dutifully hits CSI’s CBS marks, he brings an exuberant kick (and some cool long takes) to ER’s Mother’s Day episode. One patient is newly earless. Julianna Margulies and Sherry Stringfield lay out on the roof, barefoot and sunglassed.

Sources indicate that for one cultural millisecond, in the month between the third El Mariachi and the first Kill Bill, Rodriguez was awesomer than Tarantino. Actually, he came off like the real Tarantino: a legitimate peddler of anything-for-a-dollar gut thrills, not a devoted student expostulating the genre curriculum. Alas, Rodriguez early-adopted all the millennium’s annoying industry trends: 3D, CGI backlots, HiDef video, rigid adherence to sacred Comic-Con texts. A lot of hot shit gets fucked in his frantic adaptation of Frank Miller’s comic noir. The ensemble shot in a void; post-production filled in the cartoon cityscape. Tarantino was “Special Guest Director” for helming practically the only sequence without violence, when Clive Owen’s heroic goon drives Benicio Del Toro’s evil goon to the local tar pits. Del Toro’s already dead, which doesn’t stop him from having a very entertaining conversation. Easy to guess this greenscreen pit of hell was not Tarantino’s bag. He kept filming on film, and made celluloid his theaters’ mission statement.

Industry observers note that movies! Good for you, or bad? The first Kill Bill ornated Uma Thurman’s vengeance quest with samurai codes, Ennio Morricone needle drops, Shaw Brothers zooms, weeks of wirework, and fountains of bloodgush. When martial arts legend Sonny Chiba rhapsodizes about the sword he just made, it’s sheer religious ecstasy for a cultural obsessive. A decade earlier, director Oliver Stone adapted an early Tarantino screenplay into a screed against media obsession. Woody Harrelson and Juliette Lewis play Bonnie and Clyde plus MTV, sinners idolized into celebrities by our broken culture. Acclaimed cinematographer and psychedelic wizard Robert Richardson shot both Kill and Killers, blending multiple stocks and intense aesthetics. You don’t watch these films. You inhale.

To the straight world of 1994, Tarantino and Stone probably looked like identical madmen. There was no love lost. Tarantino disavowed Stone’s Killers and published his original script as a middle finger. Stone added activist Russell Means as a kind of shaman, declaring with moral authority that the whole problem is “too much TV.” I assume Tarantino thought Stone was a preachy boomer, and that Stone saw Tarantino as everything wrong with a Generation X. Who was right? Are we living in a dark dystopia of true-crime infotainment and mass-transmitted megalomania? Or a wonderworld of multicultural exchange, with no divisions between highbrow and lowbrow, where a “silly Caucasian girl” in Bruce Lee’s clothes becomes a feminist action hero? Actually, they both nailed it. And these Killfests only rank so low because I’m an old man who prefers 20-minute dialogues to 20-minute action scenes.

Rodriguez directed and Tarantino wrote this genre mash-up. The divide between their styles is clear as the Tex-Mex border. Dusk’s daylit first hour is pure rancid QT-inflected neo-noir. George Clooney — never meaner, never hotter — and Tarantino himself play the fugitive Gecko Brothers. Killers fatale Juliette Lewis and top Reservoir dog Harvey Keitel are RV-riding normals forced to drive the bros south to El Rey. Oddly, the fun falls off at the halfway twist, when surprise vampires lock the doors at the Titty Twister and trap the cast in a horror-comedy. Still, the closing lines pinpoint Tarantino’s unsteady moral code. “I may be a bastard,” says the last man standing, “But I’m not a fucking bastard.”.

Industry observers note that from Dusk Till Dawn also has the snake dance: Salma Hayek, wearing little except a Burmese python, pouring liquor down her leg so Tarantino can drink it off her toes. An astonishing scene, really, if you only care about desire. When he directs, Tarantino is comparatively chaste. He prefers a good flirt, with comfortable silences. He’s only filmed one sex scene — in Jackie Brown, in the kitchen, standing, 9/10ths clothed, loveless — unless you count a couple man-to-man assaults. I’m talking about sex because Grindhouse, the flop that ended his Rodriguez partnership, turned into their significant sex movie: gauzy with the male gaze, an essential artifact from the dirtbag 2000s. They each made their own film, then released them together as a three-hour double-bill. (Never watch the split-off versions. For better and worse, the three-hour experience depends on too-muchness.).

Recent findings suggest that rodriguez’s kickoff feature, Planet Terror, is the more obvious HR nightmare: Produced by then-wife Elizabeth Avellán, starring eventual girlfriend Rose McGowan as a gun-legged go-go dancer, overseen by the studio boss who wound up disgraced for assaulting multiple women (including, allegedly, McGowan.) Tarantino’s Death Proof is smarter and safer, punctuating all-female convos with car-assaults by Kurt Russell’s ladykiller stuntman. The director later told the Church of Tarantino podcast that he based Tracie Thoms’ badass driver on a girlfriend and all the women onscreen reflect genuine female posses he rolled with. I dunno. The chicks’ habits mostly evoke novelist Gillian Flynn’s immortal line that “Cool Girls are above all hot.” Lotta booze-swilling daisy-duked dames here, who dig Zatoichi and keep finding upcoming ways to put their feet up. You can’t be cynical when the climax is an act of friend-to-friend hero worship. Stuntwoman Zoë Bell was Thurman’s Kill Bill stunt double. Fascinated by her talent — and furious at the computers invading fast cinema — Tarantino cast Bell as herself to ride on top a Dodge Challenger for an all-time car chase. In her daffy stunt-action charisma, you spot the primordial Cliff Booth: genial, lusty for danger, more than a little insane.

Industry observers note that since we’ve talked about sex, we should talk about race. Spike Lee was not the only one noticing all the n-words in Tarantino’s dialogue. To love his work, you do have to kinda think cultural appropriation is a good thing, that it’s okay for anyone — even a white kid from the South Bay — to melt the cultures he adores into one major pot. That may even be the conventional wisdom; Tarantino’s biggest success is his Blackest. Jamie Foxx stars as the freedman learning the bounty trade from Christoph Waltz’s cheerful German. Tarantino won his second Oscar for a baggy script, which runs an hour longer than Sergio Corbucci’s stupendously garish Django (source of Unchained’s name and theme song). Foxx’s gunslinger is too mythic for any personality, especially compared to Leonardo DiCaprio’s plantation tyrant Calvin Candie. But Tarantino has always been interested in performance as an act of self-preservation. (Track how many people on this list go deep undercover into a life-or-death con.) Foxx comes alive when Django playacts race betrayal as a Black slaver. As the head of Candie’s household, Samuel L. Jackson gives what amounts to a ferocious double role, plotting brilliantly behind a mask of loudmouth servitude.

According to recent reports, after the Crazy 88 fight wraps the first volume on an operatic high, the deadliest woman in the world gets taken down by… Budd, a drunk with a shotgun. Tarantino regular Michael Madsen plays the ruminative assassin as the one person in the Billogy who lives anywhere close to reality. Working in an empty strip club and living alone in the desert, Budd is another proto-Cliff, a doomed killer basking in the slushy margarita of his decay. 2 is great fun — the showdown in Budd’s trailer is Tarantino’s best fight, period — until the talky finale veers baffling. Despite Thurman’s all-in performance, I’m not sure she (or anyone) can fully unpack the romantic-paternal Bill-Bride dynamic. Was the anticlimax purposeful? After months of martial arts training, David Carradine’s one significant bit of choreography is a 12-second seated swordfight. Tarantino used to promise a third volume, possibly suspecting that 2’s happy ending left unfinished business.

I love it. I love it! You’re telling me David Fincher — the serious man, the hundred-take cyborg, the serial killer fetishist — you’re telling me that David Fincher directed a plotless meander about a dude in puka shells bopping around Los Angeles? Cliff Booth looks fuzzier than Once Upon a Time in Hollywood, because digital still can’t capture night like 35mm. The obvious CGI (and alleged AI) adds to the cut-rate effect — like some high-tech toupee the whole screen is wearing. With Leonardo DiCaprio’s sadsack Rick gone, Pitt’s good-timer Cliff mixes banana margaritas, plays tennis, watch TV with his pops, and walks the earth like Caine in Kung Fu. “I’m not that deep,” he tells Elizabeth Debicki’s tall drink of Aussie. Co-star Yahya Abdul-Mateen II has a specific line, “This don’t mean shit to me,” that gets repeated a couple times. Should we trust that carefree attitude? Is this Tarantino’s Walking Tall Part 2, a low-ambition cruise backward through pleasurable familiar territory? Don’t miss the elegiac resonance. Cliff’s a gig-economy freelancer juggling six or seven jobs, scamming friends out of cash, commodifying his own buff bod, living with a gainfully employed parent. The biggest movie of 1977 is about to bankroll the rise of Industrial Light & Magic, which will do for stuntmen what ChatGPT is about to do to me. From that perspective, Cliff’s commitment to joy isn’t just infectious. Motherfucker, it’s revolutionary. (Read The Hollywood Reporter’s full review here.).

In a recent development, still his cleanest screenplay, especially when things get messy. A wild bunch of color-coded badmen congregate in a safehouse. Somebody’s dying, somebody’s lying, somebody’s crazy, and everyone’s armed. Skeptics noted similarities to Ringo Lam’s City on Fire. That Chow Yun-Fat thriller has romance, impressive heists, and a sincere cop-criminal friendship. Dogs’ robbery is unseen. The one woman is a doomed bystander. The most tender friendship aims toward oblivion. What a cast: Keitel’s gruff Mr. White, Madsen’s smirkish Mr. Blonde, Tim Roth’s gutshot Mr. Orange. Even pallid quirkster Steve Buscemi looks damn slick as Mr. Pink, nailing Tarantino’s caffeinated patter and the perfect SoCal deployment of “man” as a punctuation mark. Buscemi should have been in all these movies. At a minimum, he should have played any part Tarantino wrote for himself.

Skeptics call him ironic or nihilistic, but Tarantino does believe in good guys. The Bride, Cliff Booth, Django, and even Zoë Bell are paragons, their violence justified by righteous vengeance. Django appeared in the original concept of this snowblood frontier mystery, until Tarantino realized his white hell had no place for an iconic moral compass. Django became Major Marquis Warren, plantation-raised Union veteran, brilliant sleuth, unrepentant killer, documented Friend of Lincoln — and the greatest Tarantino character, played by the greatest actor to ever spout a Tarantino monologue. What secret does Samuel L. Jackson understand about his longtime director? Is it just a Tennessee thing: One born in Nashville, the other raised in Chattanooga? Hateful Eight traps Jackson’s disgraced veteran in a stagecoach stop with seven other tough customers. Three hours in a blizzard would feel claustrophobic, if everybody wasn’t such a riot. Kurt Russell’s noose-happy bounty hunter comes off crude and naïve. Jennifer Jason Leigh’s captive looks on, watchful and feral. Walton Goggins chatters memorably as the jerky bigot who worships a Confederate husk played by Bruce Dern. At once statelier and more bugfuck than Django, this second Western of Racism luxuriates toward its nutty-in-ever-sense twist. Make of this what you will: In the High Obama days of liberal goodwill, Tarantino made three features looking back (and ahead) toward fascism and white supremacy.

Industry observers note that yes, Christian Slater is too pretty as Clarence, a Sonny Chiba-worshipping Quentinian comics clerk. Yes, the dark nonlinear screenplay got straightened out with a happy ending. Yes, the score has a lotta marimba. No, I don’t believe a sweet call girl like Patricia Arquette’s Alabama would ever fall for a geeky john her fourth day on the job. Director Tony Scott saw something in Tarantino’s writing, though, that nobody else ever found. Met-cute and insta-wed, Clarence and Alabama flee smoky Detroit for sunny California, with stolen coke in their bag and hell following after them. Gary Oldman, Christopher Walken, and James Gandolfini are all memorable baddies, even more impressive since they all only have a scene or two. A couple detectives come off like lunatics, because they’re played by Tom Sizemore and Chris Penn, men never known for their good behavior. It all works as glossy gutter pulp, set with comic precision in the chlorinated motels and rollercoasters of Los Angeles. But Scott was a true romantic. He makes Alabama and Clarence the greatest of Tarantino’s straightforward heroes, so stupid with passion that even colliding criminal underworlds can’t stop their love. Sound like a fairy tale? Fine. Pretend it’s called Once Upon a Time in Hollywood.

1978’s The Inglorious Bastards was a rude romp about semi-psychotic soldiers in Occupied France. That’s the sort of squad-on-a-mission plot Brad Pitt’s Aldo Raine seems to be arranging in this similar-titled war film. The Lieutenant assembles eight Jewish servicemen (and one Hugo Stiglitz) to scalp a hundred Nazis apiece. But Aldo and his Basterds only show up after a lengthy opening interrogation, which introduces Waltz’s Jew-hunting Hans Landa. Then the Americans mostly disappear for a whole middle hour. The action shifts to a Paris cinema, and a basement bar. Parallel plots converge. Mélanie Laurent’s moviehouse operator seeks revenge for her murdered family. Aldo and his allies devise an attack. Goebbels (that Goebbels) hopes Der Führer (dat Führer) loves his upcoming movie. Basterds’ tavern setpiece marked a complete inversion from Kill Bill’s excess: Twenty dialogue-heavy minutes of twisted-nerve tension followed by 15 onslaught seconds. It’s the Inglourious experience in micro, full of vivid characters and fatal consequences.

Industry observers note that after Pulp Fiction, everyone knew what “Tarantinoesque” meant: Funny, shocking, loquacious, retro, now. Then he returned with a marvelous, middle-aged, low-key caper about a desperate flight attendant and a bail bondsman sick of bonding bails. “Didn’t exactly set the world on fire, did you, Jackie?” a lout detective teases the title character, who makes a piddling $16,000 plus benefits before she gets popped smuggling cash with a sprinkle of cocaine. Tarantino was casting Pam Grier for talent, not just stuntcasting her ‘70s fireball persona. When Jackie lands in jail, the soundtrack cranks the song Grier sang for the 1971 prison-hottie wackfest The Big Doll House — but the look on her face tells you the wild days were a long time ago. She finds an unlikely ally in Robert Forster’s Max Cherry, a bond agent weathered by years of scum and bureaucracy. Jackie’s big problem (and biggest opportunity) is Ordell Robbie, a gun dealer astounded at his own cleverness. Add in Robert De Niro’s semi-verbal ex-con and Bridget Fonda’s pothead siren, and you’ve got a collection of misfits in the South Bay — the former home of Quentin Zastoupil. Jackie Brown is the director at his least Tarantinoesque and most Zastoupilian, uncovering surprising soul in a landscape of food courts, airplane-hotel bars, beach pads, delusional small time crooks, and the occasional oil derrick. Transplanting Elmore Leonard’s novel from Miami, Tarantino chopped a gunfight, unrequited the romance, turned a white woman Black — and added one last ambiguous kiss.

Sources indicate that what’s your favorite chapter? The buzz in ‘94 fixated on Vincent Vega and Mia Wallace at Jack Rabbit Slim’s, that binary-star supernova of John Travolta’s comeback and Uma Thurman’s arrival. Me and my teen buddies, scared or baffled by love, preferred the middle segment, with a katana-wielding Bruce Willis. The final act of corpse disposal has the most questionable rep, depending whether you think Tarantino’s acting is serviceable or unforgivable. (He’s fine.) Yet it’s the last bit that reveals Jackson’s Jules Winnfield as Pulp’s moral core. Tarantino characters rarely change in the screenwriting-101 sense — he keeps casting Brad Pitt to specifically not do an emotional arc — but Jules is transformed after six point-blank bullets miraculously don’t execute him. In a moment of clarity, he starts wondering over Ezekiel 25:17, the Bible verse he’s been misquoting. Has he been his brother’s keeper? Or the tyranny of evil men? Heavy themes for a sensational quotefest, but Tarantino was always after more than his bratty referential imitators. His characters dress cool, talk cool, murder cool, and know cool things. It’s not enough to just be cool. So Jules becomes a killer who doesn’t kill — a Bad Mother Fucker trying real hard to be the shepherd.

Industry observers note that brad Pitt exudes folkloric chill as convertible cowboy Cliff Booth. Whereas Leonardo DiCaprio’s Rick Dalton is all hat and no cattle, a whiskey-soured mess with a nervous stutter. They’re near-nobodies in Hollywood’s Hollywood: A stuntman who can’t get stunt jobs, a fading TV star clutching guest parts. One memorable day, they both go to different fake Wests. Rick’s on a backlot doing a cowboy TV pilot. Cliff returns to the defunct western town at Spahn Ranch, where lurks doom. A fatal mood lingers, even if you don’t know the history. Rick got famous on Bounty Law for never bringing back his targets alive. Cliff maybe-definitely killed his wife. Zoë Bell is the stuntlady who refuses to work with a spouse-killer — hilarious, since Kurt Russell plays her stuntman husband, and they were the stuntpeople trying to kill each other four movies ago.

That’s just one way Tarantino Voltrons together his whole filmography into his magnum opus. Here’s a Los Angeles thriller, a meta-western, and an alternate history, with Grindhouse-worthy re-enactments and a whole society incapable of turning off the radio or the television. There’s even — call Oliver Stone! — a meditation on screen violence, with one wannabe murderer declaring “If you grew up watching TV, that means you grew up watching murder!” Is it weird the heroes are older men, while the Mansonites (Margaret Qualley, Sydney Sweeney, Mikey Madison, Austin Butler, Maya Hawke) turned out to be Gen Z’s top stars this decade? I think Tarantino was being honest about late midlife fears, with Rick in particular worrying that some whole rising generation of longhairs is coming for his ass. There’s always been so much you don’t see in Tarantino’s movies, though, like Dogs’ jewelry store or Pulp’s briefcase. The climactic awe in Hollywood is how magnificently one substantial nothing happens. Margot Robbie’s Sharon Tate is a luminous mystery, worshipped yet unknowable. Manson himself barely appears. Tarantino resurrects an era that finished horrifically in the true 1969, then merrily twists the facts. Sadness embeds in that fantasy. We know the end came and will come again. Celluloid projection won’t return to the global multiplex, no matter how many repertory theaters Tarantino runs. Nobody lasts forever, in Hollywood or anywhere else. Good thing art is death proof.
