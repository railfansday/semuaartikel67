Meta reconsiders 2030 net-zero goal amid record emissions from AI data centers

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Meta’s executives are rethinking the company’s original goal of achieving net-zero emissions by 2030. That revelation in Meta’s latest sustainability report comes as the company reported a record percentage increase in greenhouse gas emissions that exceeds any other Massive Tech competitor racing to build new AI data centers.

In a recent development, the organization’s 2026 sustainability report claims that “our long-term ambitions haven’t changed, but our operating environment has,” according to an introduction from Rachel Peterson, vice president of data centers at Meta, and Blair Swedeen, global head of net zero and sustainability at Meta. In the November 19, 2026, report, they attributed the changing conditions and the need for a rethink to how “demand for AI infrastructure has grown faster than almost anyone projected.”.

“We are re-evaluating our timelines, sequencing, and interim milestones—including the 2030 timeframe for our net zero emissions goal and our near-term intensity targets—so our approach reflects current operating realities while keeping progress transparent year over year,” Peterson and Swedeen wrote in the report.

Meta’s statement about re-evaluating its 2030 emissions goal is a significant public acknowledgment, given that Amazon, Google, and Microsoft have all stuck by their original sustainability goals so far.

But all the companies have acknowledged challenges in balancing their original sustainability goals against the breakneck buildout of hyperscale data centers. Despite efforts to invest in more renewable energy projects, Meta and its rivals are still often pushing forward on AI data centers that rely on burning natural gas to generate electricity.

Meta’s report described the manufacturer’s total emissions as surging from 15.6 million metric tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent in 2024 to more than 21.7 million metric tonnes in 2025.

It has been revealed that that would make Meta’s annual carbon footprint roughly comparable to total emissions for the state of Idaho, while surpassing the total emissions of states such as Hawaii, Maine, South Dakota, and Latest Hampshire. The firm attributed much of this emissions increase to “capital goods,” like as data center servers.

That also represents a nearly 40 percent increase in Meta’s carbon footprint between 2024 and 2025, which is the highest percentage year-over-year increase for total emissions reported by any of the four major US tech giants.

In a recent development, for comparison, Microsoft reported a 25 percent increase in its overall carbon footprint, Google reported an 18 percent increase, and Amazon’s report described a 16 percent increase between 2024 and 2025. However, Meta still likely has one of the smallest total carbon footprints among the Major Tech companies.

Meta’s approach to achieving net-zero emissions has included developing latest renewable energy projects to match emissions and using carbon removal technology.

But even when factoring in such efforts, Meta still reported net emissions increasing from more than 8.1 million metric tonnes to more than 11.8 million metric tonnes for 2025. That represents a 45 percent increase.

From a water consumption standpoint, Meta reported progress in reducing facility consumption from 825 million gallons in 2024 to 790 million gallons in 2025.

Yet that positive trend is tempered by the fact that Meta also described an increase in water consumption from “areas with water stress,” rising from 198 million gallons in 2024 to 246 million gallons in 2025.

Industry observers note that meta also reported a sharp increase in water consumption related to purchased electricity—the amount of water consumed by related power generation—from 19 billion gallons in 2024 to more than 26 billion gallons in 2025. This once again highlights that, for data centers, the far bigger water usage comes from the power plants that supply electricity to data centers as opposed to data centers directly using water for cooling.

Still, some minor credit is due. The publication Data Center Dynamics pointed out that “Meta is the only hyperscaler to report indirect water use, a growing concern as data centers shift to closed-loop cooling systems that, despite using less water at source, require more electricity to run.”.

It would very much be in the public interest for other Big Tech companies to follow suit on the reporting front—and public pressure continues to build after the AI backlash impact on US midterm election results.
