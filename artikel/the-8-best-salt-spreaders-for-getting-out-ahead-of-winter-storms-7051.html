But putting down a simple layer of snow melt can easily prevent any accidents. It can also reduce the amount of time you have to mess with shovels or snowblowers. But believe it or not, how you put it down matters, and the best salt spreaders are there to guide you.

It has been revealed that snow melt, typically rock salt or calcium chloride, melts existing ice and prevents upcoming ice from forming. Using a salt spreader, which spins and sprays your snow melt all around, helps ensure you get a nice, even coat over any area you prep. Gardeners may recognize the salt spreader as a close cousin of the fertilizer spreader, but with a more robust construction to endure winter weather and the corrosive nature of salt.

Salt spreaders come in a wide array of sizes to suit different jobs, including small handheld spreaders for front stoops and short walkways, walk-behind models for a long driveway, and large tow-behind spreaders for prepping parking lots. As a rule of thumb, you’ll need to spread about 3 pounds of snow melt for every 1,000 square feet of area. Though there is some variation from model to model, picking the right type of spreader is the first and most key step.

Most handheld spreaders only hold enough salt to de-ice a short walk and a front stoop; walk-behind spreaders can hold about 65 to 130 pounds of ice; and towable spreaders often hold up to 185 pounds of salt.

Spread width measures how far your spreader will throw salt as you use it. Most spreaders have a range between 6 feet for walk-behind models and as far as 30 feet for larger towable spreaders.

Sources indicate that while a wide spread can save you time, keep in mind that it may also lead you to spray salt onto your lawn and landscaping. Salt will dry out plants, so you want to avoid spraying green areas as much as possible. If you need to salt sidewalks and driveways that border grass and landscaped areas, look for adjustable spreaders with baffles so you can narrow your spread and avoid overspray.

You have a few options when it comes to ice melt. Calcium chloride is the best option. It melts ice even at extreme sub-zero temperatures, won’t harm your plants, and is less toxic than rock salt, but it’s also pricier.

In a recent development, rock salt is the cheapest, but‌ it’s more toxic, won’t work in extreme cold, and can clog spreaders more easily. Magnesium chloride is the least toxic option and works at temperatures as low as 5 degrees Fahrenheit.

Whichever ice melt you choose, be sure to use it as directed, as all of the above can eat away at wood, steel, and concrete.

Sources indicate that while they operate similarly, we don’t recommend putting rock salt in a fertilizer/seed spreader. Dedicated salt spreaders store corrosive salt and endure icy winter conditions. Using a fertilizer spreader to spread salt once or twice won’t break it, but it won’t work as well and may shorten its lifespan in the long term.

Salt spreaders typically have hoppers made from thick, durable plastic that sit on heavy-gauge steel frames and feature pneumatic rubber wheels with heavy tread that can grip slick paved surfaces. Industry observers note that if you do put fertilizer in your salt spreader (or vice versa), make sure to clean out the salt thoroughly before placing any seed or fertilizer in the hopper. Any lingering salt in the mix will dry out the grass, damaging your lawn.

Bottom line: We would recommend buying two separate spreaders, despite their similarities.
