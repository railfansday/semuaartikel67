Net zero emissions won’t reverse climate change — some nations look to ‘net negative’

Net zero is no longer enough. Among countries at the COP31 climate summit under way in Antalya, Turkey, there’s a growing recognition that meeting climate targets will require the world to go ‘net negative’, which means removal of more carbon dioxide from the air than is added.

According to recent reports, scientists say that climate ‘overshoot’ — exceeding 1.5 °C of warming, a target set in 2015 in the landmark Paris Agreement — is now inevitable in the next few years. Reaching net-zero status would halt the rise in temperatures. But only achieving net-negative status can bring temperatures back below the 1.5 °C threshold this century.

It has been revealed that that reality has made net negative and the enormous amount of CO2 removal that would be needed to achieve it a prominent idea at the summit. A coalition of eight countries called the Group of Negative Emitters (GONE) has been working to convince other nations to adopt net-negative targets and get serious about CO2 removal. The group announced that it will be co-chaired by Denmark and Ethiopia, which is hosting next year’s climate summit and could put net negative at the top of the agenda.

“We are in an era of overshoot, and we have to do a lot in terms of removing carbon dioxide,” Ethiopian ambassador Samuel Addis Alemayehu stated at a GONE event on 18 November, touting the nation’s progress on reforestation.

Some climate campaigners worry that CO2 removal technologies won’t work and that focusing on them could distract countries from cutting emissions. Despite those concerns, the latest draft of the main statement from COP31 recognizes that CO2 removal has a role in addressing the overshoot, and welcomes efforts to “urgently and rapidly” scale up development of removal methods.

According to a United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) report1 released ahead of the meeting, the world can expect about 1.8 °C of warming this century, even in the most optimistic emissions scenario.

Recent findings suggest that every fraction of a degree above 1.5 °C raises the risk of climate disasters such as wildfires and powerful hurricanes. “COP31 will be judged a failure if we don’t address the overshoot,” said Ilana Seid, Palau’s ambassador to the UN and chair of the Association of Small Island States, at a press briefing held during the summit.

Many countries at the meeting have discussed slashing emissions to limit the size and duration of the coming overshoot. Turkey has pushed to implement a target of 35% of global energy demand being met with electricity by 2035, a proposal that has gained support from more than 75 countries at the conference and is also included in the draft statement.

Industry observers note that but the UNEP report estimates that to recover from overshoot, at least 330 billion tonnes of CO2 would have to be pulled out of the atmosphere this century after the world reaches net zero. Removing that much CO2 would require a vast expansion of existing efforts. Today, about two billion tonnes of CO2 per year are removed by nature-based projects such as reforestation, which takes advantage of the carbon absorbed by growing trees. Scaling up carbon removal quickly enough to achieve net negative and storing carbon in a more durable form will require technological CO2 removal solutions as well.

A decade ago, ideas for such technologies seemed “whiteboardy” and theoretical, says Chris Neidl, who was appointed by the COP president to advocate for carbon removal. Now, there is a nascent CO2-removal industry based on a range of technologies, such as systems that use chemicals to pull CO2 from the air. “There is evidence that we can do it,” he says.

So far, carbon-removal technologies have cumulatively taken about 1.7 million tonnes of CO2 from the atmosphere, less than 0.1% of the minimum amount that would need to be removed annually to recover from overshoot. And many people are sceptical that this capacity can be increased. “These technologies simply do not exist at any meaningful scale,” remarked Mary Church, a campaigner who advocates against geoengineering at the Center for International Environmental Law in Edinburgh, UK, during an event at the COP. The draft decision has sparked furore at the COP over its failure to mention the need to transition away from fossil fuels, even as it highlights measures such as CO2 removal.

According to recent reports, even nations that embrace the goal of returning from overshoot don’t recognize that a massive amount of carbon removal would be needed to do so, say observers at the COP. The European Union, for example, has said it will be net-negative by 2050 but it lacks a clear plan. “Everyone is trying to avoid the hard job and the hard questions,” states Sylvain Delerce, the chief scientist at Carbon Gap, a non-profit organization in Brussels that advocates for carbon removal.

It has been revealed that the members of GONE have tried to lead by example. The group was debuted at COP28 in Dubai by Denmark, an oil-and-gas producer that has promised to phase out all fossil fuels by 2050, Finland, which aims to reach net-negative by 2035, and Panama, which already counts itself as a net-negative emitter thanks to its small population and lush forests. The group now includes eight member countries, including Kenya, the Netherlands, Sweden and Suriname, which, like Panama, is already net-negative owing to its natural forests.
