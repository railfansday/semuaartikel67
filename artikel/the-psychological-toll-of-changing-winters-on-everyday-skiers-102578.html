The Psychological Toll of Changing Winters on Everyday Skiers

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that last winter, Taos Ski Valley got just 76 inches of snow. It’s the leanest winter on record for the northern Newly introduced Mexico ski resort, which saw 91 inches the previous season. Both numbers are far from the annual average of about 170 inches. Whole swaths of the mountain (my home hill) remained closed until late in the season, and the resort shut down early when a warm spring made quick work of what thin snowpack remained. For the first time in my life, I rode my mountain bike in February. I admit, I’ve been a little blue.

Skiing has always been an organizing principle in my life. As a kid, my parents dutifully hauled four children and an impossible amount of patience into the Cascades every winter weekend. During stints in the Midwest and on the East Coast, I skied on landfills and ice sheets. I decided I could stay in Newly introduced Mexico forever after one good season at Taos, where winter transforms a series of steep, high-altitude ridges into endlessly compelling skiable terrain.

Skiing is about pleasure, and perhaps about taking a break from bills and taxes, work and chores. Done well, the sport can also invite deeper connection with the natural world and the seasonal cycle. Which means it has the paradoxical effect of being both a source of escapist joy and a front-row seat to a rapidly changing climate. Increasingly, a skier’s pleasure brings them closer to their pain.

As winters grow more unpredictable, individuals and the industry have to grow more adaptable. The wise among us are inviting in a deeper and more complex relationship with winter, one that welcomes fear, loss, and grief—casting the beauty and the joy in even sharper relief.

In a recent development, when I got on the phone with Ewan Mackie, the mountain manager at Treble Cone, the popular Newly introduced Zealand ski area had just shut down for several days after a heavy snowfall followed by rain. He doesn’t hesitate to acknowledge that climate change threatens the industry.

“You’d be bonkers to not state that as a fact, with the evidence that’s being played out,” he says. “But I don’t believe that we are going to shut up shop in ten years. There’s a future. It’s definitely not easy. But you just have to change the way you do things.”.

He points to the increasing adoption of snowmaking technologies that allow resorts to make snow at warmer temperatures. The SnowFactory, from Italian company AlpinTech, can create snow in temperatures up to 86 degrees Fahrenheit, and man-made snow forms a denser, more durable snowpack than natural snowfall. Snowcats equipped with fresh technology allow operators to assess snow depth and repair shallow areas. At Treble Cone, earthworked trails protect the snow from the harshest angles of sunlight.

The bad weather days have offered Janina Kuzma a welcome chance to train and prepare for her final guide exams. At 40, she’s reinvented her career several times, first finding success as a competitive big-mountain athlete, then as a halfpipe skier. She qualified for the Olympics in 2014 and 2018, and when an injury sidelined her before her second Games, she pivoted again, beginning a years-long journey to become a fully certified backcountry ski guide.

Recent findings suggest that kuzma’s versatility and resilience offer a framework through which anyone can shape their relationship to the sport. When the snow is lackluster, she’ll spend the day in the terrain park, on man-made snow and features. It’s better to ski a beautiful groomer than nothing at all.

“You have to make the most of every winter, whatever the conditions,” she says.

Ski guide Christine Feleki, who works for Bella Coola Heli Sports in British Columbia, reframes her clients’ expectations—and her own—to focus on simply having a mountain experience. In the Coast Mountains, climate change is painfully apparent: monster wildfires, glacial retreat, an increasingly difficult-to-manage snowpack. It’s also wildly stunning, and that’s enough for Feleki.

“I can have fun skiing crappy, hard boilerplate in a beautiful place,” she says.

We can vote and protest; we can change our habits; we can educate ourselves and others. But if you feel profoundly overwhelmed or powerless about a changing climate, that makes sense too. This is a global cataclysm. The scale is beyond what a single human mind can really comprehend. This is why bearing witness is imperative: to go and be present with the Earth as she’s changing, to try and understand.

But then, of course, you will probably get a little sad. And what do you do with that?

Recent findings suggest that “Grief is a measure of our love, and there’s a lot that we can learn from it,” says Britt Wray, a Canadian writer and researcher focused on climate change and mental health. “It is an opening for relearning the world and our place within it as we move forward, after having recognized the difficult realities that we’re in.” Anxiety and sadness are valid, reasonable responses to what is being lost.

“Positive psychology that tells us certain feelings are good to have and feel, like hope, optimism, pride, love, and happiness, and that others are bad to feel, like shame, guilt, depression, grief, and anxiety,” she says. “[But] emotions are neutral and they carry information.” She encourages becoming curious about and learning to sit with difficult feelings.

Industry observers note that it’s a tough assignment, sitting with grief on the chairlift. But mountain athletes have never been far from uncertainty and loss. Skiing is inherently ephemeral, played out on a landscape formed by a fickle and ever-changing substance. People die every year in the mountains, and plenty more return home with injuries or psychological trauma. As a community, we’re already well-equipped to bring major-hearted depth to our relationship with the mountains. And as dutiful witnesses to ever-changing winters, we’re already practicing acceptance.

The next step is to practice something much more fun: savoring. “Studies show that taking time to savor experiences that might be going away soon can make us appreciate them more,” states Laurie Santos, PhD, a cognitive scientist and professor of psychology at Yale, who teaches a wildly popular class on happiness. In her course, Santos talks about a process called hedonic adaptation, in which we get used to—and thus stop deriving deep pleasure from—good things. Savoring, which involves slowing down, being in the present moment, and practicing gratitude, is a powerful tool against this.

It has been revealed that “Climate change may be taking some of these activities away, and we won’t be young, fit, and able-bodied enough to enjoy them forever. I bet 90-year-old you would love skiing on powder that wasn’t perfect with the knees you have now,” she states. “Remembering that things are finite can help us appreciate them more.”.

Recent findings suggest that if, on a bluebird, uncrowded powder day, you find yourself thinking This is how it should be, or I deserve this, check yourself. It’s a recipe for feeling like anything but the best is somehow wrong. The key is to reframe not just a perfect day, but the entire pursuit of skiing, as the profound privilege and gift that it is. And if the idea that someday it’s all going to end makes you nervous, just remember—that’s nothing newly introduced. A keen awareness of what we stand to lose makes it even sweeter.
