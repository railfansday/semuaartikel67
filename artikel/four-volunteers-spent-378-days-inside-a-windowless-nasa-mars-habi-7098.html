Four volunteers spent 378 days inside a windowless NASA Mars habitat

Four NASA CHAPEA volunteers left a 3D-printed Mars habitat in Houston on Oct. 31, 2026, after 378 days.

Ross Elder, Ellen Ellis, James Spicer and Matthew Montgomery walked out of NASA’s Mars habitat in Houston on Saturday, Oct. 31, 2026. They had gone in on Oct. 19, 2025, and stayed 378 days with no windows and no live calls home.

In a recent development, ellis, the crew’s medical officer, later told NASA that on most days she did not feel isolated. She mentioned she relied on her three teammates every day to get through the mission.

In a recent development, the year inside was CHAPEA Mission 2, NASA’s second yearlong simulation of life on the surface of Mars. NASA posted the crew’s reflections on Nov. 9. The health data are still to be interpreted in two weeks of post-mission evaluations.

The habitat is called Mars Dune Alpha. It was 3D-printed, covers roughly 1,700 square feet (158 square meters) and sits inside a building at Johnson Space Center.

On entry day in October 2025, the crew rode to the habitat in the Space Exploration Vehicle, a prototype pressurized rover, according to NASA’s Human Research Program.

In a recent development, suzanne Bell, the mission’s co-principal investigator, spoke to them at the door. Two alternate crew members posed with them for photos before the start.

Sources indicate that CHAPEA is short for Crew Health and Performance Exploration Analog. The first mission used the same habitat and ended on July 6, 2024.

Before the second crew went in, the prepares for a second yearlong rehearsal in the printed habitat put crew autonomy at the center.

NASA astronaut Jack Hathaway spoke about the time they were away. While they were inside, he flew a 237-day mission on the International Space Station and came back. The Artemis II crew also traveled around the Moon in that period and set a distance record for humans.

Hathaway said the year required “immense physiological and psychological strength, teamwork, and human grit.”.

Messages between the habitat and mission control took up to 22 minutes each way. The crew spoke with their families only by email, with a Mars-like delay, NASA said in a one-year upgrade on Oct. 19.

In a recent development, on May 7 the crew reached day 200. That week they were in a two-week loss of signal, a planned blackout like the one when Mars passes behind the Sun, according to a NASA update from May. They worked with no contact with mission control and used preplanned procedures.

The crew also had to handle simulated equipment failures and limited supplies. Michael Stenger, chief scientist of the Human Research Program, said the mission was starting to show NASA what year-long missions need, including medical and psychological care across communication delays.

Elder was the commander, Ellis the medical officer, Montgomery the science officer and Spicer the flight engineer. As part of their personal item allowance, the four brought musical instruments into the habitat.

In a recent development, elder is a former U.S. Air Force experimental test pilot. According to NASA, he found the Marswalks and robotic work very similar to working as part of a flight.

For a Marswalk, the crew put on suits, went through an airlock and walked on red sand outside the habitat. That area is still inside the building. From inside, crew members also ran a drone and a rover to survey the ground and find or collect rock samples.

Elder stated that getting suits ready, doing airlock procedures and making long traverses took time and stamina. He said food, exercise and rest were needed to keep up that pace.

Sources indicate that montgomery helped identify the rocks brought back from the walks. One NASA photo highlights Ellis and Elder doing a maintenance task outside during a simulated walk. Another shows photos the crew placed around a boot print in the sand.

Spicer worked in satellite communications and spacecraft development before the mission. At the exit, he was thanked for keeping the habitat’s systems running and was called the crew’s resident data scientist.

Recent findings suggest that he compared the year to living on a submarine or a boat. The crew counted supplies from dish soap to respirator filters, and they rationed food and water.

Recent findings suggest that spicer said he was surprised by how little he missed the news and social media, even though the crew could get news. He told his crewmates they made the year “not a chore, but a joy.”.

According to recent reports, montgomery had worked in hardware engineering, robotics, tech startups and independent films. He set up the habitat’s 3D printing capabilities and ran a seed germination test with simulated Martian soil and a control.

He also grew vegetables in a hydroponic system. NASA has run other work on growing lettuce, peppers and tomatoes in microgravity, because packaged food can lose flavor and vitamins.

Sources indicate that ellis is a U.S. Space Force officer and has worked at the National Reconnaissance Office. Inside the habitat she helped organize biological sample collections and prepare supplies for the health studies.

It has been revealed that she said it was hard to explain to other people what life inside was like. “It would be difficult to convince someone who has never lived in isolation that most days, I don’t feel isolated,” she said.

Industry observers note that ellis thanked the exercise, behavioral health, nutrition and medical specialists on the ground. She mentioned the main lesson for her was that a Mars crew will need a closely integrated ground team.

Isolation has also been studied at Concordia Station in Antarctica, where researchers linked living in close quarters to more conflict and mistrust.

Sources indicate that the crew started two weeks of tests and debriefs with NASA scientists after the exit. Scientists will compare the data with what the crew reported. Mission 2’s results are still to come, and each crew has only four people.

In a recent development, grace Douglas, the principal investigator, noted the results will help NASA weigh health risks against crew time, communication capacity and food and water systems. She also said many of the resource problems in space are like problems on Earth.

Sara Whiting, the project scientist, noted the data will feed into intends for NASA’s Moon Base near the lunar South Pole. “This is really the now moment for this kind of data,” she said.
