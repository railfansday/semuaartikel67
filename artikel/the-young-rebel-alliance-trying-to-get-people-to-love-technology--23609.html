In a spare and cavernous former eyewear emporium near the luxury stores of San Francisco’s Union Square, a motley group of tech devices is spread out across display counters and pedestals. One is a desktop computer shaped like a cartoon frog. Another is an e-ink laptop controlled by buttons made of felt. Nearby, there’s an AI assistant hidden inside a fuzzy parrot meant to perch on a user’s shoulder. The space is like some bizarro Apple Store, but instead of offering futuristic devices you didn’t know you needed, it’s a showcase of devices you didn’t know could even exist. The devices are part of TIAT’s latest group exhibition, Every Thing Computer, which explores “playful computing” and experimental interfaces. Some are clearly one-off art projects (see the punchable oversize keyboard escape button and the computer mouse shaped like female genitalia), but others are more earnest and commercial attempts to reimagine what we want our devices to be. Industry observers note that later, after an internship at Apple, she got her own job in Big Tech working for Humane, the company cofounded by former Apple executive Imran Chaudhri that raised more than $230 million to create a post-iPhone screenless AI device. That device, the AI Pin, flopped, and Dorman was not surprised. The fact that its form factor so closely resembled a police officer’s body-worn camera was one major problem, but so were more aesthetic concerns. Dorman, for one, thought there should be more variety than the single black-and-white color palette, and more of an embrace of the flexibility AI offers.

“I hated the voice and personality we chose for the device. It was the equivalent of giving everyone an iPhone and being like, you can’t change the wallpaper. And I was like, ‘Well, I want to change it,’” she says. And she did, hacking her AI Pin to use the voice of the blogger-narrator from the television teen drama Gossip Girl. Dorman cofounded Era in 2025, teaming up with two other young AI-leaning technologists who were similarly concerned about the direction Substantial Tech and AI companies were heading.

Recent findings suggest that “I think something that’s really true across the consumer landscape is everybody feels left behind by AI,” Era cofounder Megan Gole says. Seeing how the tech establishment envisioned AI’s rollout into mainstream culture struck her as too limited.

“Our options right now are join or die. Keep up with the AI bus or be a Luddite and get off your phone,” Gole says. Era offered a third way, built more from the ground up. “We want people’s feedback, and we want to create this upcoming world that you guys are the center of,” she says.

Era’s intent is to provide the building blocks that make it easier for people to harness AI in their own specific and bespoke ways, breaking free from reliance on a phone to mediate its usage. The tech giant’s puck device is envisioned as the computer inside whatever device a user wants to make, with the ability to access more than 15 different AI models and an interface that can adapt it to whatever task a user might imagine.

“Your tools can feel more retro in the sense that you know what each one is for, you use it, each interaction is meant to end, and you put it away,” Gole notes.

In the AI-enabled future Era’s cofounders envision, people get to determine what, precisely, their devices are supposed to do, whether it’s a fridge magnet that delivers French cooking lessons or an e-ink display that reacts to the double-tap of a button to display the current time in Canberra, Australia.

“It enables people’s individuality to exist within an AI world that makes a very smooth median of everyone,” Gole adds.

Mina Fahmi also sees potential in niche tech devices. His startup, Sandbar, is building a wearable AI ring for note-taking, which sounds not all that different from the AI pins, pendants, and charms coming out from companies like Meta, Plaud, and Friend. But Sandbar’s ring, called Stream, isn’t trying to record everything. Instead, Stream is intended to be used to record specific thoughts from its wearer, who pushes a small integrated button and then whispers those thoughts directly into the ring.

“A lot of people want to remember things without pulling out their phone,” Fahmi states.

On a recent video call, he brings his Stream ring up to his mouth and begins whispering. It’s so quiet I can’t hear him through his computer’s microphone, yet the ring picks up every word he notes. It’s a way of reducing what Fahmi calls the friction of using voice to interact with technology, especially for personal thoughts or sensitive subjects. Stream, with its highly attuned microphone, is meant specifically for people to promptly record what they have in mind as easily as possible.

According to recent reports, it’s a use case that Fahmi admits is on the fringe. There are plenty of other AI tools for capturing and summarizing conversations and work meetings, he concedes, but says not enough of them are built to help people record their own thoughts, ideas, and reflections. “We started Sandbar because we believed we would need newly introduced interfaces to fully realize the potential of AI for expanding human expression,” he says.

“We’re not out here trying to sell a million units in year one,” Fahmi adds. “What’s been clear to us is that it’s more important to create a thing that people really get value out of than to litter the earth with electronics.”.
