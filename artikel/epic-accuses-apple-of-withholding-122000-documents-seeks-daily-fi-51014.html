Epic accuses Apple of withholding 122,000 documents, seeks daily fines

Recent findings suggest that in a newly introduced filing, Epic Games accuses Apple of withholding more than 122,000 documents related to its proposed App Store commissions and asks the court to impose daily fines until the firm complies. Here are the details.

As a result of the lawsuit Epic Games brought against Apple in 2020, Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers ruled in 2021 that Apple would have to, among other things, allow developers to direct owners to alternative payment methods outside the App Store.

According to recent reports, apple responded by allowing external payment links, but imposed a commission of up to 27% on purchases made through them. This, in turn, led the court to find Apple in civil contempt for violating its injunction. Since then, Apple has been trying to overturn that ruling, a dispute that has now reached the Supreme Court.

According to recent reports, at the same time, district court proceedings will determine what, if any, commission Apple may charge on purchases made through external payment links. Apple submitted its proposal on August 13, calling for commissions ranging from 5% to 15%, and Epic is expected to respond after Apple completes its document production.

In a fresh filing, however, Epic is accusing Apple of withholding more than 122,000 documents related to how it arrived at its proposed commission rates.

One of the many aspects of the ever-sprawling battle between Epic Games and Apple centers on document production.

In its new filing, Epic indicates Apple has repeatedly failed to produce documents detailing how it arrived at its proposed commission rates, accusing the firm of withholding materials it deems irrelevant to the proceedings.

Epic argues that Apple previously used questionable claims of attorney-client privilege to withhold documents during the contempt proceedings, but has now changed tactics.

In a recent development, apple does introduce one tactical change from its prior campaign of delay. Whereas during the contempt proceeding Apple used bogus claims of privilege to evade its discovery obligations, this time Apple withholds thousands of documents on bogus claims of irrelevance. For example, Apple is withholding well over 99% of the documents returned by running the agreed-to search terms on the files of the four custodians Apple claims decided on the proposed fee (Tim Cook, Eddy Cue, Carson Oliver, and Kevan Parekh (Apple’s new CFO)) and three other key individuals (Nate Barton, Alex Roman, and Phil Schiller). Apple claims these withheld documents are not relevant to this proceeding-even though they hit on case-specific terms such as “proffer,” “contempt,” “injunction,” and “link-out.” To put this into context, while Apple produced 632 custodial documents returned by these search terms (including families), Apple is withholding over 122,000 based on a claim of non-responsiveness. Moreover, Apple claims that it completed its production from the files of these custodians, yet its production does not include a single substantive email discussing the rates Apple ultimately proposed; any notes or communications reflecting what occurred at any meeting where these rates were discussed or decided; any internal analysis of the financial or business impact of the proposed commissions; any App Store profit and loss statements; or any support for Apple’s assertion that its incremental link-out costs are “essentially zero.”.

Epic goes on to state that “Apple cannot be trusted to fix this problem on its own,” proposing instead that the court order Apple to produce all non-privileged documents matching the search terms agreed upon by both parties, regardless of whether Apple considers them relevant.

Industry observers note that under this proposal, Apple would still be allowed to review documents for attorney-client privilege, but it would be up to Epic to determine which of the remaining documents are relevant to the case. Epic argues that existing confidentiality protections would prevent it from using any of the information for purposes unrelated to the litigation.

Given Apple’s position, Epic respectfully submits that the Court should order Apple to produce to Epic all non-privileged documents that hit on the Parties’ agreed-upon search terms, so that Epic itself can sift the wheat from the chaff. Doing so will avoid further delays and place no burden on Apple. Moreover, doing so would not raise any of the concerns that required Special Master involvement in the prior proceeding, namely concerns around the production of privileged Apple information to Epic. Under Epic’s proposed remedy, Apple would be allowed to continue its review for privilege. For these reasons, Epic asks this Court to order Apple to produce all non-privileged documents that hit on any of the Parties’ agreed-upon search terms by December 2, 2026 (one week after Apple’s deadline to substantially complete production), irrespective of any relevance or responsiveness assessment.

Finally, though Epic does not propose a specific dollar amount, it “asks that the Court impose on Apple a daily monetary sanction for each day Apple fails to comply after December 2, 2026.”.

Sources indicate that according to the court docket, a hearing on Epic’s motion was initially scheduled for November 24 before Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers. However, a new order filed today, which has yet to be made public, refers the discovery dispute to a magistrate judge and includes a notice terminating deadlines and hearings.
