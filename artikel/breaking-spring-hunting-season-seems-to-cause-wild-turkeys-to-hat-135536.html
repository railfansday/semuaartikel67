Breaking: Spring hunting season seems to cause wild turkeys to hatch mostly female chicks, putting them at risk of extinction

According to recent reports, A turkey is the centerpiece of the Thanksgiving table, but for a wild male turkey, the riskiest time of year is spring. That’s when the birds breed, and it’s when hunters go after the males.

Recent findings suggest that in parts of Georgia and Louisiana where the males were hunted, wild turkey hens hatched about two daughters for every son. At a South Carolina site where the birds weren’t hunted, hens hatched sons and daughters in nearly equal numbers.

Recent findings suggest that the finding, from a study of turkey nests, suggests that spring hunting may do more than kill adult males. Hunting may also affect how many sons and daughters hatch.

In a recent development, erin Ulrey of the University of Georgia’s Warnell School of Forestry and Natural Resources was the study’s lead author. Ulrey’s team had predicted the opposite: more sons than daughters.

Recent findings suggest that the team compared three sites with turkey hunting to just one site without it. That comparison can show a pattern. It can’t prove hunting is the cause.

In an earlier study, researchers from the same school tracked 109 male turkeys during the hunting season to see which ones were killed by hunters or predators.

Ulrey’s team started by catching wild turkey hens in all three states between 2020 and 2023, using nets baited with cracked corn. They fitted each hen with a GPS tracker and let her go.

When a tracker showed a hen staying close to one spot for several days, the team knew she was nesting.

In a recent development, the researchers kept their distance. They checked on each nesting hen daily from more than 65 feet (20 meters) away, using the radio signal from her tracker.

After the eggs hatched, the researchers collected the empty shells. The thin lining inside an eggshell contains the chick’s DNA. A lab tested each lining for a version of a gene that only female birds have.

According to recent reports, the lab couldn’t read the DNA from every shell. In the end, the team knew the sex of 724 chicks from 83 nests. Of those chicks, 446 were daughters and 278 were sons.

Sources indicate that three of the four study sites were open to spring turkey hunting, one in Georgia and two in Louisiana. On those three sites, 285 of the 422 chicks were female, about two-thirds.

According to recent reports, the fourth site was the Savannah River Site, a U.S. Department of Energy property in South Carolina. The birds the team caught there weren’t hunted.

In a recent development, the only turkey hunts on the property during the study were one-day events for 25 hunters with limited mobility, in 2022 and 2023. Those hunts took place away from where the team caught birds.

Hens on that site hatched 161 daughters and 141 sons. A gap that small could be due to chance.

Sources indicate that the researchers also checked other things that might matter, including a hen’s age, the date she started laying, and the number of eggs she laid. None of them was linked to the sex of her chicks.

With those things taken into account, the researchers estimated the chance that any one chick would be male. Their estimate was 26% on the sites with hunting and 49% on the site without it.

By that estimate, a son was about half as likely where males were hunted.

In birds, the chromosome that determines a chick’s sex comes from the mother. So a hen may be able to control the sex of her chicks, the authors wrote. Scientists don’t fully understand how.

Researchers have reported uneven numbers of sons and daughters in several other bird species. For the turkeys, Ulrey’s team offered two explanations, and both could be true.

The first explanation is about which sex is more likely to breed. A daughter can breed in her first year. A son generally doesn’t start breeding until about age two.

Industry observers note that on the sites with hunting, hunters killed 29% of the adult males on average, and they usually killed the most birds in the first few weeks of the season. Some of those males likely died before they could mate.

By that reasoning, a hen living where males are hunted may leave more descendants by hatching daughters.

“If a female is visiting a male over several days and he disappears, she has to move on and try to find another male. If the females are experiencing this over time, they might be aware male mortality is higher,” Ulrey remarked.

Sources indicate that the second explanation is stress. Hunting began just as hens started laying eggs. The hunting may have stressed the hens, and stressed hens may have higher levels of stress hormones.

In earlier studies of other birds, mothers with higher levels of those hormones tended to have more female chicks.

For the earlier study, Nickolas Gulotta of the Warnell School and three colleagues looked at which males died.

The team analyzed GPS records from 109 adult males that wore backpack-style trackers between 2014 and 2023. Of those birds, 59 lived in Georgia and 50 in South Carolina, all on land open to hunting.

From those records, the team worked out each bird’s habits. Those included how far he stayed from forest edges and open ground, and how fast he traveled. A forest edge is the strip where trees meet open land.

Each male tended to stick to his own habits from day to day.

During the year each bird was tracked, hunters killed 27 of the 109 males, and predators killed 17.

In both states, males that stayed closer to forest edges were more likely to be shot. Predators were more likely to kill them too.

Earlier studies that tracked turkey hunters by GPS help explain why. Hunters mostly use logging roads and gravel roads to find turkeys, and those roads are bordered by forest edges.

Bobcats and coyotes, two of the turkeys’ predators, travel and hunt along the same edges.

In a recent development, forest edges aside, hunters in the two states didn’t shoot the same kinds of birds. In Georgia, hunters were more likely to shoot the males that traveled faster.

In South Carolina, it was the reverse. Hunters there were more likely to shoot the males that traveled more slowly.

Predators in both states were more likely to kill the slower males.

So in Georgia, hunters were less likely to shoot a slower male, and predators were more likely to kill him.

“It’s kind of a catch-22 where if they can survive that hunting season, they’re most likely going to be fine,” Gulotta said.

“But at the same time, too, if they don’t travel that fast and are predictable, then they have the potential to be killed by a predator.”.

If hunters kill the birds that take more risks, he noted, the turkeys that are left could be harder to find and harder to hunt.

Industry observers note that turkeys would only become harder to hunt that way if the habits are inherited, meaning parents pass them to their chicks through genes. The study didn’t test that, and Gulotta’s team called for future studies to do it.

The tracked males also didn’t avoid open ground once hunting began. In both states, they stayed closer to open ground during the hunting season than before it. That’s likely because hens use open ground throughout the breeding season.

Ulrey’s study had just one site without hunting. Only one earlier study had looked at the sex of wild turkey chicks. It was done in a part of Texas with very little hunting, and it found slightly more sons than daughters.

Ulrey’s team didn’t report measuring stress hormones in the hens. So they couldn’t test the stress explanation.

In about half of the 83 nests, the lab couldn’t tell the sex of one chick. Even if all those chicks were male, daughters would still outnumber sons, the team calculated.

Recent findings suggest that over the past two decades, wildlife managers have seen wild turkey numbers drop across large areas where the birds live. If hens in hunted populations keep hatching mostly daughters, the authors wrote, that could eventually affect how those populations grow.

Knowing what part hunting plays in turkey breeding can help people manage the birds “in a sustainable way so that hunting can continue for years and years,” Ulrey noted.

The authors recommended that managers keep checking the sex of latest chicks. They also encouraged similar studies elsewhere, especially in places with different spring hunting rules.

The full study was published in the journal Journal of Avian Biology. The earlier study of male turkeys was published in the journal Royal Society Open Science.
