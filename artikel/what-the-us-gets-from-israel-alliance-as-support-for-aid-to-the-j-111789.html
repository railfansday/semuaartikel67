The U.S.-Israel alliance was tested when Washington and Jerusalem found common cause against Iran — and when defending Israel drew heavily on American weapons stocks. Meanwhile, that ongoing conflict has now sharpened a question for Washington: what does the alliance deliver, and what does it demand in return?

A new report from Advancing American Freedom (AAF) and the Zionist Organization of America (ZOA) argues that Israel’s military reach and regional role make the alliance a strategic asset for Washington.

Sources indicate that its authors call Israel "the largest U.S. aircraft carrier" in the Middle East. But this is in direct opposition of critics who say that the alliance costs the U.S. a lot and delivers very little in return.

According to recent reports, the report, dated Oct. 26, 2026, arrives as public support for Israel continues to fall across the political spectrum — even among Republicans and MAGA supporters.

FROM INTELLIGENCE SHARING TO MISSILE DEFENSE, HOW ISRAEL'S PARTNERSHIP PROTECTS AMERICAN SECURITY.

In a recent development, president Donald Trump and Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu unveiled joint military action targeting Iran. (REUTERS/Kevin Mohatt).

Recent findings suggest that president Donald Trump and Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu have discussed ending direct U.S. military aid, but the U.S. leader has also called Israel "a great ally, much more reliable than most" and said Washington would help if Israel needed it.

The report specifically points to intelligence sharing, joint weapons development and military cooperation as returns on what the U.S. gets for providing support to Israel.

One of the clearest examples is missile defense. The U.S. and Israel develop systems including David’s Sling and Arrow, while American companies manufacture components for several programs. More than 100 companies across 34 states participate in missile-defense co-production, according to the Congressional Research Service. In August, a Raytheon-Rafael facility in Arkansas delivered the first Iron Dome Tamir interceptor built entirely in the U.S.

Those programs support U.S. manufacturers and give the Pentagon a role in developing missile-defense systems. During the Iran conflict, however, U.S. forces also expended large numbers of interceptors — costly weapons that can take years to replace.

US REBUILDING WEAPONS STOCKPILE COULD DELAY ORDERS FROM ALLIES.

In September, the Congressional Budget Office (CBO) estimated that the U.S. had probably used between half and two-thirds of its combined inventory of Patriot, THAAD, SM-3 and SM-6 interceptors since June 2025.

The estimate includes weapons used during the earlier 12-day war with Iran and is based on publicly reported expenditures compared with the number of those interceptors the War Department has purchased. CBO mentioned rebuilding stocks could take at least five years, even if production increased.

Intelligence sharing is another return cited by supporters of the alliance. Former CIA Director John Brennan said Israeli intelligence was among the "thousands of bits" considered during the years-long effort to find Usama bin Laden. He did not specify what Israel provided or how much it contributed to the operation.

A more recent exchange reveals how Israeli reporting can prompt U.S. action even when its accuracy is uncertain. Israeli officials warned the U.S. of a possible Iranian plot to kill Trump during a trip to Turkey. The Secret Service took precautions, including moving him to another aircraft. U.S. intelligence agencies could not independently verify the threat, however, and the CIA assessed some of the Israeli reporting with "low confidence," according to Reuters and The Washington Post.

NETANYAHU REVEALS HE WANTS AMERICAN FINANCIAL SUPPORT FOR ISRAEL TO 'DRAW DOWN TO ZERO'.

Sources indicate that the financial relationship has also become part of the debate. Under the current agreement, the U.S. provides Israel with $3.3 billion annually in Foreign Military Financing and $500 million for joint missile-defense programs. Most of the military aid pays for weapons and services from U.S. contractors, according to CRS. Israel’s ability to spend a portion of the funding on domestic purchases is scheduled to phase out by 2028.

The report's authors call the $3.8 billion in annual U.S. military aid "one of the highest returns on investment of any U.S. foreign assistance.".

Trump has said he wants to end annual aid, while Netanyahu has also publicly called for phasing it out over time. U.S. and Israeli officials have been negotiating a fresh agreement that could wind down direct assistance while expanding joint defense projects and weapons production. The current agreement runs through 2028, Congress controls appropriations, and no successor deal has been reached.

The debate is also becoming more politically consequential as Americans question the relationship’s terms.

A 2026 Chicago Council survey found that 56% favored reducing or ending U.S. military aid to Israel. Republicans remain more supportive than Democrats and independents, but the right is divided: Joe Kent blamed Israeli pressure for the Iran war, Rep. Thomas Massie has pushed to cut aid, and Vice President JD Vance has called for changing the relationship while continuing to describe Israel as a strategic ally.

The two countries have cooperated against Iran and developed defenses against missile attacks, but their goals have not always matched during the war. A Stimson Center analysis claims Israel sought to broaden the conflict beyond what Washington preferred. That does not establish that Israel drew the United States into a war it opposed. It does show how cooperation can leave Washington managing military costs even when its preferred limits differ from Israel’s.
