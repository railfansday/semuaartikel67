Posted November 27, 2026 | Reviewed by Lybi Ma.

In Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim’s documentary You Can See Everything, the camera cuts to a tight close-up of the upper half of Elizabeth Holmes’s face. Her large blue eyes hold Fielder’s gaze for an extraordinarily long time without blinking. It was quite distracting. I found myself counting the seconds between blinks and trying to match them. I couldn’t keep up.

According to recent reports, holmes’s partner, Billy Evans, has the same wide blue eyes and unblinking gaze. It was unnerving to watch them even in the safety of my own living room.

Blinking is a coordinated sensorimotor action that protects the eyes while reflecting shifts in attention and cognitive load. Adults typically blink about 14 to 17 times per minute, with each blink lasting roughly 100 to 300 milliseconds.

People naturally blink less when they’re working hard to focus. Studies in cognitive and auditory neuroscience show that people systematically blink less during moments of high cognitive demand. For example, people blink less in noisy environments so they don’t miss visual cues like lip movement.

Holmes and Evans don’t just blink less; their wide-eyed look demonstrates white around the iris. This effect comes from how far the eyelids open, not how often they close. Put the two together—wide-open lids and a low blink rate—and you get a jarring laser-focused stare. The iris, an orb floating in a white sea of sclera.

In a recent development, research by Tamami Nakano found that people watching someone speak tend to sync their blinks with the speaker's. When I tried to match Holmes's blinks and failed, I was consciously doing what viewers do without noticing.

Psychologists Sharon Leal and Aldert Vrij found that people blinked less when they lied and blinked more than usual right afterward. Their 2008 research concluded that lying is cognitively demanding. Blinking slows while the lie is being told, then rebounds once it's over.

Recent findings suggest that I’m not suggesting Holmes is a liar. Someone who blinks less might be lying, but they may also be concentrating, self-conscious in front of a camera, on medication, or simply a person who blinks less than average. A low blink rate, in and of itself, suggests someone may be working hard. It doesn’t tell us at what.

Holmes’s stare also reminded me of Anthony Hopkins’s Oscar-winning performance as Hannibal Lecter in The Silence of the Lambs. Hopkins said in an interview that he deliberately minimized blinking while playing the cannibalistic serial killer. The result was an unflinching performance that made Lecter feel oddly calm, predatory, and menacing.

Sources indicate that while popular culture associates unblinking stares with sociopathy, the research doesn’t support it. In fact, some studies suggest that people high in psychopathic traits pay less attention to other people’s eyes, not more. An unblinking stare is not a reliable indicator of a personality disorder. It can be an effective tool for an actor or for a young founder delivering a TEDMED talk, as Holmes did in 2014. But it’s not diagnostic.

It has been revealed that in the 2005 bestseller Blink: The Power of Thinking Without Thinking, author Malcolm Gladwell argued that snap judgments and first impressions formed within the first two seconds can be just as accurate as slow, deliberate analysis. “There can be as much value in the blink of an eye as in months of rational analysis,” he wrote.

Gladwell wasn’t writing about literal blinking, but he attached a condition that matters here: fast judgments are trustworthy only when they’re grounded in real experience.

Sources indicate that my first reaction to Holmes and Evans was a snap judgment. I don’t have any real expertise in reading blink rates. What I have is a cultural script (Hannibal Lecter, the wide-eyed villain) and an expectation about conversational rhythm, which includes blinking and looking away occasionally.

That doesn’t make my unease any less valid. It was real information about me. It told me that Holmes’s stare had broken a social rhythm, and that I was primed to read that break as menace.

The documentary title promises we can see everything. The close-up on Holmes’s eyes reminds us we can’t. All we can see is that she isn’t blinking. We can’t see why.
