NTT and ispace have signed a collaboration agreement to study optical communications between Earth and the Moon and laser-based power delivery on the lunar surface, pairing telecommunications research with the practical demands of lunar missions.

The companies will jointly examine use cases, system architectures, technical requirements, and demonstration methods before considering phased tests on Earth and in space. The agreement starts a research and feasibility effort toward future infrastructure services, rather than launching an operational lunar network.

Its two priorities address basic constraints on a larger lunar economy: how machines exchange the data they collect, and how mobile equipment receives the energy needed to keep working.

Sources indicate that cislunar space is the region around Earth extending to the Moon and its vicinity. As activity expands there, communications requirements extend beyond sending instructions to an individual lander.

Scientific instruments, resource surveys, rovers, and construction equipment can produce imagery, sensor streams, and operational records that need to move between surface sites, orbiting spacecraft, and Earth. Communications also support space situational awareness: understanding where spacecraft are and how they are moving.

Industry observers note that NTT and ispace say their work will consider infrastructure supporting Japanese public- and private-sector lunar activities, including the ability to operate communications capabilities independently. That gives the agreement both a commercial purpose and a role in Japan’s broader space infrastructure ambitions.

Recent findings suggest that optical communications encode information onto light, using laser transmitters and receivers to connect terminals. A narrow beam can carry large amounts of data, but the terminals have to acquire the link and keep it accurately aligned as spacecraft move.

It has been revealed that NTT’s explanation of its space networking research describes adapting techniques from terrestrial fiber networks to optical links in space. One approach, digital coherent transmission, uses properties of the light wave to carry information, then reconstructs the signal through digital processing at the receiver.

Movement adds another complication: relative velocity changes the received light’s frequency through the Doppler effect. The terminal must account for that shift while meeting tight spacecraft limits on size, electrical power, and weight.

These are engineering capabilities NTT is developing under its Innovative Optical and Wireless Network, or IOWN, initiative and Space Integrated Computing Network concept. Extending them to the Moon requires evaluating the actual mission geometry and environment, rather than assuming a terrestrial design transfers unchanged.

Sources indicate that higher communication capacity could help mission teams retrieve more data and coordinate more equipment. It cannot remove the propagation delay imposed by the distance between Earth and the Moon. Autonomous machines will still need appropriate local control for tasks that cannot wait for instructions from Earth.

The second part of the agreement concerns delivering usable energy with a laser. A transmitter directs optical power toward a receiving device, which converts the light into electricity. This is different from a communications link carrying information, even though both rely on optical technology.

The attraction on the Moon is flexibility. There is no established electricity distribution grid, and connecting every moving rover or construction machine with a cable would constrain where it could operate. A suitable line-of-sight power link could provide another way to supply equipment away from a fixed power source.

It has been revealed that NTT has already tested elements of this technology on Earth. In a 2025 experiment with Mitsubishi Heavy Industries, the companies transmitted approximately one kilowatt of laser optical power over one kilometer and extracted 152 watts of electrical power at the receiver. They reported 15% efficiency measured as received electrical power divided by transmitted optical power, with continuous delivery maintained for 30 minutes.

That result establishes a terrestrial research baseline, not a demonstrated lunar power service. Receiver conversion, beam control, terrain, thermal conditions, and the equipment’s energy budget all shape whether a proposed lunar system would be useful. The new collaboration will explore those application requirements and possible demonstrations.

According to recent reports, ispace brings experience developing and operating landers and rovers, along with plans to expand from transportation and surface data services into communications and positioning infrastructure. Its Mission 3 is targeting a lunar landing in 2028, according to the announcement.

The firm’s existing Lunar Connect Service plans provide important context. In September, ispace-U.S. finalized a satellite delivery agreement with Argo Space for Mission 2.5, which could launch as early as 2027.

According to recent reports, under that blueprint, Argo’s Argonaut vehicle would deliver the Alpine relay satellite to lunar orbit. ispace describes Alpine as the first asset in a constellation intended to provide communications, positioning, observation, and space situational awareness services, with at least five satellites envisaged and the remaining deployments expected by 2030.

Those intends explain why an optical technology partnership could be relevant to ispace’s business beyond landers. They do not establish that the newly studied NTT systems have already been selected for Alpine or assigned to a particular flight.

Recent findings suggest that NTT places the collaboration within NTT C89, its umbrella brand for space-related business initiatives. ispace contributes the mission planning, transportation, and operational perspective needed to turn a promising communications or energy technology into something a lunar customer could use.

The immediate work is to define what those customers would need and how the systems could be tested. The companies have not announced a service start date, a commercial price, or a performance specification for the joint lunar infrastructure.

Industry observers note that for future robotic exploration and construction, the significance lies in the supporting systems. Better autonomy can help a machine decide what to do, but sustained operations also require dependable communication and power. NTT and ispace are beginning with those foundations, studying whether optical technologies can help connect and supply the next generation of lunar activity.
