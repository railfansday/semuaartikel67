Thai Luxury Leads Minor’s Quarter Despite Tourist Slump

Minor is earning more in a country drawing fewer tourists because its luxury guests are paying up and because it owns most of its hotels. That means rate gains land directly in its results. The same ownership set-up cuts the other way in the Middle East, where demand has turned and four in 10 of its hotels are its own.

Thailand’s international visitor numbers are slipping, but Minor Hotels reports that its luxury guests in the country  are each paying more on average.

Industry observers note that minor’s Anantara-branded high-end hotels in Thailand reported a 19% like-for-like increase in RevPAR in the third quarter, on a 13% rise in average daily rate, Minor said in its quarterly results statement. Across all of Minor’s Thai hotels, RevPAR rose 13%, the strongest growth of any major market in the group, on a 9% rise in ADR and a two-point gain in occupancy to 64%.

In a recent development, the strong numbers landed despite Thailand cutting its 2026 international arrivals forecast to 33 million from 36.7 million, with arrivals through mid-October down 4.1%. Cushman & Wakefield told Skift luxury was holding up better than the rest of the market because guests in that segment are less price sensitive.

Industry observers note that thailand led the way for a quarter in which Minor’s core profit rose 13% year over year to THB 2.1 billion (about $62.9 million), with the Maldives and Europe also contributing growth. Core revenue rose 6% to THB 35.4 billion (about $1.06 billion), and EBITDA rose 4% to THB 7 billion (about $210 million). Company-wide RevPAR rose 5%, mostly on rate.

Part of the Thai performance story is the fruit of Minor’s recent investments. It finished a $50 million renovation of Anantara Siam Bangkok in September, refurbishing all 335 rooms along with the hotel’s restaurants and event spaces. The Maldives, where Minor operates nine resorts, also improved: RevPAR rose 10% as occupancy climbed four points to 46%.

RevPAR across Minor’s Middle East & Africa hotels fell 11% in the third quarter, with occupancy down about three points and rates down roughly 5%. Minor cited the regional conflict and associated weaker travel demand. Over nine months, the region’s RevPAR is down 15%.

The softening performance puts Minor in line with its larger peers. Skift reported last week that Marriott’s Middle East RevPAR fell 12% in the third quarter, while Accor’s fell 3% with a roughly 12% decline assumed for the fourth quarter, and Hilton mentioned it expected a low double-digit full-year decline in the Middle East and Africa. In Oman, Minor executives told Skift they were seeing shorter stays, later bookings and fourth-quarter demand down.

Minor remarked rates in the region held broadly steady over nine months, while occupancy fell around nine points. Its Gulf strategy is to protect price rather than chase volume. The third quarter suggests that is getting harder to hold: rates fell for the first time this year.

Sources indicate that where the losses land matters more for Minor than for many of its rivals. Marriott mostly earns fees in the Middle East, and the region accounts for only about 3% of its global fees. Hilton has mentioned the region’s decline will cost it more than $20 million in earnings this year, mostly in lost management fees. Minor, for its part, owns about 40% of its Middle East & Africa hotels, the tech giant told Skift, with the other 60% managed or franchised. That’s lighter than Minor’s portfolio as a whole, where almost 70% is owned or leased. But it still means the regional slump hits Minor’s own earnings directly at about four in 10 of its hotels there, which is a heavier exposure than a fee-based chain carries.

Europe & Americas, home to NH Hotels in Minor’s portfolio, grew RevPAR 7% in its seasonally strongest quarter, led by Madrid (up 11%) and Barcelona (up 8%). East Africa also grew, with Kenya and Tanzania up 5%, while Australia & New Zealand both held at 80% occupancy.

Over nine months, Minor’s core profit rose 3% to THB 4.2 billion (about $126 million). The third quarter accounted for half of it, after a first-quarter core loss.

According to recent reports, minor expects mixed trading in the fourth quarter. Forward bookings for Thailand and the Maldives are ahead of last year for both the fourth quarter and early 2027. The company gave no specific outlook for the Middle East.

“Our third-quarter performance demonstrates the value of having a geographically diversified portfolio, particularly when trading conditions vary so widely between markets,” stated Dillip Rajakarier, group CEO of parent Minor International, in a statement. He said the company would focus on protecting margins and pricing discipline while accelerating the shift to fee income.

Recent findings suggest that that shift is visible in Minor’s pipeline. Asset-light arrangements make up more than 90% of Minor’s active development pipeline, and it signed 38 hotels in the first nine months, putting it on track to beat its 2025 record of 40 signings. Until those hotels open, Minor’s results will keep moving with the markets where it owns the buildings.
