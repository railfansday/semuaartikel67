Latest Update: The Brain Creates Body Ownership Before Touch Occurs

Industry observers note that researchers have mapped the exact millisecond-by-millisecond sequence of brain activity that creates the feeling that a hand belongs to our own body. The magnetoencephalography (MEG) study reveals three distinct neural phases, beginning even before physical touch occurs, and explains why right-hemisphere strokes frequently cause patients to disown their left arm.

It has been revealed that we effortlessly take for granted that our hands, arms, and limbs belong to us. Yet this seamless sense of body ownership is an active perceptual construction, one that requires the brain to continuously merge visual, tactile, and spatial signals in real time, and one that can shatter dramatically following neurological injury.

While prior neuroimaging studies identified the general brain regions involved in self-perception, the exact temporal choreography, when each region activates and how signals cascade across the cortex, has remained elusive.

Now, in a study published in Nature Communications, neuroscientists at Karolinska Institutet led by Professor Henrik Ehrsson (Department of Neuroscience) and co-author Professor Daniel Lundqvist (Department of Clinical Neuroscience) have charted the birth of body ownership millisecond by millisecond.

To capture how the brain constructs the feeling that a limb is its own, the team recruited 45 healthy volunteers for a precision robotic version of the classic rubber hand illusion.

Participants watched a lifelike artificial hand resting in front of them while their real left hand remained hidden behind a screen. Two robotic arms delivered brief, synchronized touches to both the visible rubber hand and the concealed real hand, while high-speed magnetoencephalography (MEG) recorded whole-brain magnetic fields. By comparing synchronized touches (which trigger the illusion that the rubber hand is part of one’s body) against asynchronous touches and real-hand control conditions, the researchers isolated the neural signature of body ownership across the 28 participants who vividly experienced the illusion.

Reconstructing brain activity every four milliseconds produced a real-time movie of self-perception unfolding across three distinct phases:.

According to recent reports, “We know quite a lot about which areas of the brain are involved in the sense that the body is our own, but considerably less about when different areas become active and how they interact. We have now been able to track how activity patterns in the brain develop over time,” explained Ehrsson.

“The fact that the activity began even before the hand was touched highlights that the brain’s expectations of what we are going to feel are closely linked to the conscious experience of the body as our own,” added Lundqvist. “This study shows when, in what order, and how different brain regions help combine information from different senses into a unified experience of our own body.”.

Recent findings suggest that notably, during the first two phases of the sequence, neural activity was confined almost entirely to the right hemisphere. This lateralized timing aligns strikingly with clinical neurology: following a stroke, patients who experience somatoparaphrenia, the disturbing condition in which a limb no longer feels like part of one’s own body, almost always disown their left arm after damage to the right hemisphere.

According to recent reports, “When stroke patients lose the sense that an arm belongs to them, it is usually the left arm that is affected, following damage to the right hemisphere of the brain,” Ehrsson noted. “In future studies, we want to compare patterns of brain activity associated with the right and left hands to better understand the different roles of the brain’s hemispheres. We also want to collaborate with stroke researchers to investigate this phenomenon in patients.”.

How does the brain create the feeling that your hand belongs to your body?

Industry observers note that karolinska Institutet researchers found that body ownership arises through a precise three-stage sequence of brain activity. It starts with predictive expectation before a hand is even touched, sweeps rapidly across the right frontal, parietal, and cerebellar regions, and finally engages the left hemisphere to unify sensory information.

Why did brain activity start before the hand was physically touched?

According to recent reports, seeing an object approach the hand triggers predictive brain activity based on what we expect to feel. This pre-touch expectation primes the brain to merge sight and touch into the conscious experience that the limb is our own.

How does this study help explain disturbed body perception after a stroke?

Because the first two critical stages of body ownership occur almost exclusively in the brain’s right hemisphere, damage to that side from a stroke disrupts the early multisensory integration needed to recognize the left arm, explaining why stroke patients frequently lose the sense that their left arm belongs to them.

Spatiotemporal brain dynamics of limb ownership perception.

We experience our limbs as our own, but how does the brain generate this fundamental sense of body ownership?

Previous studies have relied mainly on fMRI or lesion mapping, providing a static view of the neural mechanisms underlying body ownership.

Using magnetoencephalography, robotically controlled visuotactile stimulation, and multivariate analysis across the rubber hand illusion, a real hand condition, and multiple control conditions, we identified source vector dissimilarity patterns reflecting ownership of the left arm.

Three phases of ownership-specific activity patterns emerged: (i) anticipatory patterns in medial frontal cortex, right posterior parietal cortex, and right posterior cerebellum before tactile stimulation; (ii) multisensory integration patterns in right dorsal and ventral premotor cortex, right posterior parietal cortex, and anterior cerebellum at 116–156 ms poststimulus; and (iii) later bilateral insular and left hemisphere patterns at 184–232 ms.

These findings provide a whole-brain, time-resolved map of activity patterns underlying body ownership, revealing its dynamic neural construction.
