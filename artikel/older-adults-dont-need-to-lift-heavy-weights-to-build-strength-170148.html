Older adults don’t need to lift heavy weights to build strength

Strength training with lighter weights may help older adults build strength and maintain muscle mass without heavy lifting.

Strength training is recommended for older adults to help maintain muscle, balance and independence. Heavy loads are commonly used in these programs.

It has been revealed that earlier research has reported that pain and high physical demands can lead older adults to drop out. The authors note that those who drop out often have traits associated with frailty.

A pilot trial in Perth, Western Australia, compared heavy training with lighter training in 40 adults over 55. Strength increased in both groups over 12 weeks.

Neither group revealed a measurable change in muscle mass.

According to recent reports, the study was led by Brett Buist of the School of Human Sciences at the University of Western Australia. Training intensity was chosen by the participants rather than assigned at random.

“Both high- and lower-intensity resistance exercise appears equally effective at improving muscle strength while maintaining muscle mass and quality of life in older adults,” Buist said.

Recent findings suggest that frailty has been linked to falls, hospital admissions, disability and death. Low physical activity and the loss of muscle mass and strength are listed among its common contributors.

Industry observers note that the condition is not always permanent. Ohio State University’s guide to frailty states that it can often be slowed or improved with support.

Industry observers note that low strength is reflected in poorer walking and balance. These changes are known to raise the risk of a fall in older adults.

In a recent development, world Health Organization figures cited in the paper put the share of people over 60 at 12% in 2015. That share is projected to reach 22% by 2050.

In a recent development, lifting weights appears on the National Institute on Aging’s list of strength exercises. The Perth study examined how heavy those weights need to be.

Of the 40 recruits, 33 were women. All participants were living independently in the community.

Sources indicate that sessions were held twice a week at the university under the supervision of exercise physiologists or physiotherapists. Every session included a leg press, a deadlift and a bench press.

Drop jumps from a low box were also performed in each session. The box was about 4 inches (10 centimeters) high, and each landing delivered a firm impact through both feet.

A four-week introductory phase at low-to-moderate intensity was completed by all participants. From week five each person could either move to the heavy program or continue at the lighter level.

In both groups the sets were ended while two to five more repetitions could still have been completed.

Industry observers note that the number of sets multiplied by repetitions was matched closely between the two programs. In this way the load on the bar remained the main difference.

According to recent reports, to be classified as a heavy lifter, a participant had to leg press more than 80% of their estimated maximum. That level had to be reached in at least three out of every four sessions.

Twelve participants met this standard and 14 trained at the lighter level.

By the end of the trial both groups were able to press more weight with their chests and legs. Strength was assessed using the one-repetition maximum, which is the heaviest weight a person can lift once.

Increases in chest press, leg press and leg press relative to body weight were found in each group. Substantial loads were still handled by the lighter group.

Leg press loads in that group averaged about 71% of maximum. Deadlift effort was rated at a median of 5 on a 10-point scale.

Earlier reviews of research in older adults have generally favored heavy lifting for building strength. In some of the studies behind them, heavy training was compared with loads as low as 20% of maximum.

Sources indicate that according to Buist’s team, loads that low were probably insufficient to increase strength. Unequal amounts of total work have also complicated earlier comparisons.

Leg press strength relative to body weight was the only outcome that differed significantly between the groups. However, the authors advised caution when interpreting this result.

“Clinicians can target factors that contribute to frailty using resistance exercise with intensity tailored to individual preferences without compromising outcomes,” said Buist.

Recent findings suggest that neither group indicated a measurable gain in muscle. Lean mass measured by whole-body X-ray scans stayed close to baseline in both programs.

This stability was viewed by the researchers as a useful result because lean mass usually declines with age.

According to recent reports, lean tissue already accounted for about 64% of the volunteers’ body weight at the start. This proportion was higher than in a comparison study cited by the authors.

It has been revealed that no protein supplements were provided during the trial. The training volume was modest as well, with 12 working sets in each session. Some studies that did produce muscle growth used as many as 39 sets.

A small reduction in body fat as a share of total weight was observed in the lighter group. Higher general health scores on the SF-36 survey were reported by the heavy group.

According to recent reports, other scores on the questionnaire changed very little in either group. Most volunteers began between the 50th and 75th percentiles for Australians of their age and sex.

Postural sway was measured on a force plate during standing trials of 30 seconds each. After training the recorded sway path was longer under some of the test conditions.

Recent findings suggest that A longer sway path is normally taken as a sign of poorer balance. However, scores on the 14-item Berg Balance Scale did not decline and balance confidence was maintained.

The authors suggest that participants may have been exploring their limits of stability. Resistance training is known to extend those limits.

The participants who completed the trial were not the group of greatest concern. Of the 39 people tested at baseline, 12 withdrew for reasons including joint pain and holidays.

An average baseline weight of about 162 pounds (73.7 kilograms) was recorded for those who withdrew. Participants who stayed averaged 133 pounds (60.4 kilograms) and had greater leg strength relative to body weight.

Heavier and weaker adults are of particular concern in frailty research, and they were overrepresented among the dropouts.

The final analysis included only 26 people, so small differences between the groups may have been missed. Physical activity outside the supervised sessions was not recorded.

Bonnie Furzer, a co-author from the University of Western Australia, presented the findings as guidance for those who prescribe exercise.

“For older adults who are not tolerating high-intensity resistance and impact training, lowering exercise intensity should be considered to assist adherence whilst maintaining benefits,” stated Furzer.

Longer trials and studies in frail adults have been recommended by the authors.

Until that evidence is available, a weight that people continue to lift may matter more than the heaviest one possible.

The full study was published in the journal Translational Exercise Biomedicine.
