Another chastening week in the unhinged world of Chelsea has shown Xabi Alonso that the thing he most craves is what his team lack more than anything.

Managers are neurotic creatures and Alonso’s great obsession is “control”, which you’d have to say is rather concerning from what we have seen of his tenure so far.

Recent findings suggest that ask the Spaniard to name his favourite performance from Chelsea’s opening 11 games of the Premier League season and he will cite last month’s 2-0 away win over Everton. A rare victory up north delivered a first top-flight clean sheet since January and, more importantly, was professional and diligent.

There is a time and a place for football as entertainment, but Alonso is in a results-based business and knows, from his unbeaten campaign leading Bayer Leverkusen to Bundesliga glory, that the less memorable wins are key to building a team in control of its destiny, delivering consistent results.

Chelsea are many things; they are not that.

The club were understandably excited in the summer when they secured their first-choice candidate, and appointing Alonso as manager, not head coach, has given the impression that he has more control in the overall running of first-team operations than his recent predecessors.

That is not translating to the pitch, though. Far from it. In every area of the field, control is absent without leave.

Four months in, Xabi Alonso is yet to imprint his identity on Chelsea.

Joao Pedro’s thigh injury has left Chelsea hamstrung in attack. Cole Palmer looks lost and ineffectual; Morgan Rogers is left carrying an excessive burden.

It has been revealed that the performances with and without another player, Moises Caicedo, are just as strikingly worlds apart from each other. An overreliance on the Ecuadorian has set alarm bells ringing for months.

The constant need for one midfielder to fill in alongside the centre-backs leaves Chelsea brutally outnumbered in the middle of the park, just as in the defeats by Brentford and Tottenham.

Both before and since Maxence Lacroix’s untimely injury, Alonso has been so unable to trust Levi Colwill and Wesley Fofana that 20-year-old Josh Acheampong has proven the only broadly solid defender.

Chopping and changing between a back three and back four is merely a product of Alonso’s desperation to find a defensive unit that works, yet it has been an enemy to that search for control.

Chelsea have seen little of the ball in some games, but the early wins over Fulham and Brighton came through slick play in transition, and Alonso believes control is not just about slow and steady possession.

“We have spells where I would like us to have better control, and I’m not meaning just control with the ball,” he explained.

According to recent reports, “You can have control without the ball when you feel comfortable [because] you know the opponent has the ball where you want them to have it. Then you feel in control. That is the way I like to play.”.

Yet there remains a huge disparity between Alonso’s vision of control without the ball and Chelsea’s actual reality.

In a recent development, teams are consistently occupying the ball in very dangerous areas and creating the better chances. Leeds, for example, could have scored far more than four on Saturday.

Scoring 20 goals and conceding the same number doesn’t spell control, and it is the first time in a decade that Chelsea have lost as many as five of their opening 11 league fixtures.

Friday night against Nottingham Forest must be more like that mature win at Everton with less of the chaos seen from Chelsea since.

Alonso was considered the brightest new tactical mind in management less than two years ago, but is yet to imprint his identity on Chelsea four months in. He is not under immediate pressure of the sack, but grace periods don’t last forever.
