After dodging death, battery startup Our Next Energy is now going public

🌐 Source: TechCrunch • 📅 16 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 7 min read (1452 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways
  • It’s never been easy to run a battery startup in the U.S.
  • It has been revealed that not all of them die, though.
  • Sources indicate that ONE says it is valued at $600 million pre-money, and it expects the merger with Wen Acquisition Corp.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It’s never been easy to run a battery startup in the U.S. There have been more failures than successes over the last 20 years, and whipsawing federal policy has caused more than a few battery companies to shut shop.

It has been revealed that not all of them die, though. Our Next Energy (ONE) had some close calls, but soon it will go public via SPAC, the company exclusively told TechCrunch.

Sources indicate that ONE says it is valued at $600 million pre-money, and it expects the merger with Wen Acquisition Corp. will bring $400 million in gross proceeds assuming no redemptions. The combined manufacturer will trade on the Nasdaq under the ticker “ONEE,” and the pro forma implied equity value is $1 billion.

Over the years, ONE has raised $483 million, according to PitchBook, and at one point was valued at $1.3 billion. It has also received tens of millions of dollars in incentives tied to investments and job creation in Michigan.

In a recent development, ONE is the brainchild of former Ford and Apple engineer Mujeeb Ijaz, who designed a way for electric vehicles to draw power from two batteries of different chemistries. In ONE’s first setup, it paired lithium-iron-phosphate (LFP) and with a manganese-rich chemistry, which would let each chemistry play to its strengths: LFP could be used day in, day out, while the more fragile but energy-dense, manganese-rich one would work for long, infrequent road trips. An initial prototype traveled over 600 miles on a single charge.

According to recent reports, but the EV market didn’t take off as quickly as anticipated, and automakers wanted to buy at lower prices (usually from China). Both of those reasons helped sink ONE’s first attempt at a Series C in late 2023. The startup had to lay off a chunk of its staff, and eventually vacated its lease for a planned $1.6 billion factory in Michigan.

The rough patch led to Ijaz’s ouster as CEO in December 2023, only to have him return to the position in March 2025 after a newly introduced round of funding closed. (His brother’s investment firm co-led the round.).

Part of the critique aimed at ONE during Ijaz’s first tenure as CEO was that the company spent too much time developing fresh products instead of focusing on one or two, as most startups tend to do. When Ijaz came back to the helm last year, he said that ONE would leave the automotive market and focus on grid-scale batteries.

Sources indicate that ONE isn’t alone in pivoting away from vehicle batteries. American automakers, which had similarly rosy views of EV adoption, also bet massive on battery factories and supply chains. When sales didn’t pop in the U.S. like they have in China or Europe, they had to find an outlet for that capacity, and the grid started looking like the obvious target once it started groaning under the weight of AI data centers. Both Ford and GM have declared their own energy storage products.

Many new grid-scale batteries use LFP, which is safer and cheaper than those used in most EVs in the U.S., and Ijaz saw an opportunity for ONE there as well. “We go back to the origins of LFP in this country and even to some of the factories that are in China,” he said. Indeed, just over a decade ago Ijaz led the automotive division at A123 Systems, the firm that first commercialized the chemistry.

Soon, Ijaz saw other opportunities for ONE’s batteries, including defense and locomotives.

The defense play isn’t surprising. Nearly every battery firm I talk with these days mentions defense, in part because it’s a market that Chinese companies can’t play in. Building an American supply chain means that a company can sell batteries at a premium, shielded as they are from Chinese competition. It’s a higher-margin market, though much smaller than EVs.

Industry observers note that my recent conversation with Ijaz last week was the first time I’d heard a tech giant pitch rail companies as potential customers, though. Ijaz said ONE is “finding a lot of traction in” that market. The company has built a hybrid locomotive that uses a 2.8 megawatt-hour battery, and another he calls a micro-hybrid, which uses a smaller battery to start the diesel engine faster, similar to a stop-start system on a fossil fuel car or truck.

“Our customers are seeing a 50% improvement in diesel fuel consumption, which is a massive deal,” Ijaz noted.

It has been revealed that currently, ONE has three business lines, which could be a sign of its old culture creeping back into the picture. But Ijaz mentioned the addition of defense and rail were driven by the bottom line: Grid-scale battery customers are also “very much like automotive customers, where they were willing to buy from China as long as the price was low enough,” he said.

“Believe me, I would have loved to have made that grid business into the single business of the company. But the customers would look and then be taken away to a Chinese option because of the pricing, and the tariffs also leading them to that conclusion,” Ijaz said. “And so we decided that we needed a more durable market, and we looked for who in the customer base valued an American battery company.”.

Today, ONE operates a pack assembly plant in Novi, Michigan, which can put together packs from cells at a rate of 3 gigawatt-hours per year. The plant uses cells from China-based and non-China-based suppliers, as well as ONE’s own cells from a smaller factory nearby, which is currently being upgraded to make 50 megawatt-hours of batteries per year.

Recent findings suggest that in its investor pitch, which TechCrunch has reviewed, ONE distills its business lines into defense and rail. Drones and submarines make up the majority of what ONE sees as a $16.2 billion market. Defense-focused data centers get lumped in there, too, contributing $4.1 billion to the defense business. (That appears to be the main outlet for grid-scale batteries.) The other line, which serves the rail industry, is expected to be worth $28.2 billion.

It has been revealed that both markets are sizable, but they pale in comparison to the EV sector, which is worth about $73 billion today, and could grow to $200 billion by the end of the decade, according to Grand View Research. This year, ONE expects to bring in $30 million in revenue, and hopes to make double that next year. The company said it has $200 million in binding, multi-year contracts with railroads and defense contractors.

ONE hasn’t closed the door on the automotive industry, Ijaz noted. “Automotive still is a race to the bottom on margins,” he said. “As time goes on, if we win an automotive contract for any purpose, we’ll take a look at it, but not at any cost.”.

In a recent development, ONE’s pitch centers on the so-called foreign entity of concern (FEOC) restrictions: The U.S. government can’t buy batteries made in China or those made using minerals from China-controlled supply chains, so ONE will target them first.

But it’s a pitch that many battery companies are making today. Whether investors buy into ONE’s vision will depend on if they think the tech giant has parted with its tumultuous past and found a focus. It will need both to succeed in the increasingly cutthroat battery industry.
