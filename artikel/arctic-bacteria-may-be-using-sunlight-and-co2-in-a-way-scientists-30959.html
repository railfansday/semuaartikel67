Arctic Bacteria May Be Using Sunlight and CO2 in a Way Scientists Never Expected

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

An Arctic bacterium’s genes hint at an unexpected way of using carbon dioxide, raising the possibility that similar survival strategies exist in oceans far beyond the polar regions.

According to recent reports, in the Arctic Ocean, where nutrients and usable organic matter can be scarce, a tiny bacterium may have found a remarkable way to survive. Think of it as having a built-in solar charger and an unusual food supply. By capturing energy from sunlight and using a chemical called acetone, it may be able to turn carbon dioxide into some of the building blocks it needs to grow.

The bacterium, known as Porticoccus arcticus, lives in the frigid waters of the Beaufort Sea, north of the Yukon in the Arctic Ocean’s Canada Basin. At some locations, it accounted for up to 9 percent of detected bacteria, suggesting it is an important member of the region’s microbial community. In a study published in Nature Communications, researchers at Concordia University uncovered genetic evidence of an unusual metabolism that could help explain how these organisms survive in such a challenging environment.

Sources indicate that one of the keys appears to be acetone, a chemical produced naturally by marine organisms, including microscopic algae, and through reactions between sunlight and dissolved organic matter. Scientists already knew that some marine bacteria could consume acetone, but P. arcticus appears to have a different way of putting it to use.

Its genes point to a process called acetone carboxylation, which combines acetone with carbon dioxide to produce compounds that can enter the cell’s central metabolic pathways. These chemical pathways allow cells to process carbon and make the materials they need to function and grow.

Recent findings suggest that the researchers found the complete set of genes required for acetone carboxylation within the bacterium’s relatively small genome. They also identified multiple genes for proteorhodopsins, light-sensitive proteins that help cells capture energy from sunlight. Genes involved in both acetone processing and light capture were among the organism’s most active, suggesting that it may be able to use sunlight for energy while supplementing its carbon supply through acetone metabolism and carbon dioxide assimilation.

What makes this combination especially intriguing is that the researchers had not set out to look for bacteria with these abilities.

In a recent development, “The genomic composition of these organisms led us to reconstruct the different types of energy and carbon metabolisms that exist,” noted David Walsh, a professor in Concordia’s Department of Biology and the study’s corresponding author. “That is how we found this very specific set of genes associated with the use of acetone, which was unexpected. We discovered that the organism’s genes were telling us about something we did not suspect was happening in an ecosystem. This was a surprise, as we were not actively looking for acetone-degrading bacteria that can use light.”.

Walsh explained that the findings point to two distinct metabolic abilities, including a way of obtaining energy that researchers call photoheterotrophy.

Recent findings suggest that “This study introduces two fresh discoveries,” he stated. “The first is a group of organisms, P. arcticus, that has a unique metabolism that uses organic carbon while at the same time has the capacity to use light as an energy source. We call this process photoheterotrophy.

Recent findings suggest that “The second discovery is the way the organism consumes acetone to also consume carbon dioxide out of the atmosphere. This acetone-mediated inorganic carbon assimilation combines what animals do — eating food, in this case, acetone — with what plants do: fixing CO2.”.

Recent findings suggest that the genetic evidence suggests that P. arcticus has developed a way to take advantage of several resources available near the ocean surface. However, the findings describe its potential metabolic capabilities rather than directly measuring how much carbon dioxide the bacteria take up.

Whether these abilities will help the bacterium cope with a warming Arctic Ocean remains uncertain. Walsh does not yet know how changing conditions will affect P. arcticus, although shifts in sea ice could alter the availability of sunlight and acetone, potentially changing where the organism can thrive.

Sources indicate that the researchers also plan to investigate whether bacteria in other oceans and freshwater environments possess similar capabilities. Such investigations could help determine whether this unusual combination of sunlight capture and carbon assimilation is a specialized Arctic adaptation or a more widespread microbial survival strategy.

Reference: “Evidence of an acetone carboxylation pathway in photoheterotrophic bacteria from the Arctic Ocean” by Susan McLatchie, Sara Palestini, Asher Woodhead, Tony Gutierrez and David A. Walsh, 26 September 2026, Nature Communications. DOI: 10.1038/s41467-026-78082-y.
