Two-thirds of the world’s population will be living in cities by 2050, according to the latest estimates by the United Nations. Coinciding with the mass movement of people into urban areas is the rapid integration of technology into many of those spaces. From automated subterranean waste-collection systems to driverless vehicle zones, these ‘smart-city’ solutions are changing the way that people live, work and move through their environments.

Some cities are embracing these technological advances at a much faster pace than others. Places such as Beijing, Helsinki, London and Singapore stand out for the way they are integrating smart-city technology into existing infrastructure and services.

Other cities are emerging with these technologies integral to their operations from their inception. But as the examples of Songdo in South Korea and Hengqin in China illustrate, building smart infrastructure from scratch is only half the challenge: these places must also attract and retain the research and innovation talent needed to develop, operate and improve it. It’s a ‘chicken-and-egg’ problem, says Songdo-based urban-planning researcher, Dakota McCarty: talent follows world-class universities and thriving economic zones, which need a critical mass of expertise and investment to be attractive.
