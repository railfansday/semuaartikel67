How to tell a cyberattack from a plain old outage

Over the past week, anyone could be forgiven for wondering whether a Diehard IV–style cyberwar had finally arrived in the US.

Industry observers note that across just four consecutive business days, a mass Microsoft exchange outage broke IT services for thousands of companies, a Federal Aviation Administration tech screw-up grounded airplanes across the country, a software problem affecting JPMorgan Chase caused another round of disruption, a United Airlines system glitch delayed flights yet again, and an NSA classified network’s downtime left the agency’s staff sitting at home waiting to return to their office at Fort Meade. In the midst of a US war with Iran and rising fears of AI-enabled hacking, this still-unfolding comedy of IT errors colliding across American infrastructure felt to plenty of observers like more than a coincidence.

In a recent development, yet there’s still no evidence that any one of these clustered technological failures were related, much less caused by hackers. Their improbable, sequential timing left Americans anxiously doomscrolling for signs of foul play. But in cases like these, it’s the job of cybersecurity analysts (and reporters at WIRED) to determine—vibes aside—whether the latest tech outage was actually an accident or the result of a cyberattack.

The baseline for that fact-finding, as anyone tasked with investigating these incidents will tell you, is that the vast majority of these digital breakdowns are not the result of malicious intent but rather casualties of IT admins’ constant war to keep computer systems from collapsing under the weight of their own complexity.

“I’ve just seen enough times where we thought maybe it’s a cyberattack and it turns out it’s not. Sometimes it will be, but typically not,” claims Matthew Prince, CEO of the Internet infrastructure tech giant Cloudflare. “Right now, most of what we’re seeing is the standard background noise. Stuff breaks, and that’s just the nature of complex systems.”.

According to recent reports, so in the interest of soothing the country’s rattled nerves and helping us all find ground truth amid seemingly endless tech catastrophes, we at WIRED present our general guide to determining whether a tech outage is the result of a cyberattack. So take a breath—maybe open the bunker door to let in some fresh air—and read on.

The process of determining the cause of an IT service outage should begin with the assumption that it was an accident, states cybersecurity researcher Doug Madory.

As the head of Internet analysis at the network security firm Infoblox, Madory states he monitors as many as a dozen network outages a day. Only around “one in a thousand,” he says, turns out to have been caused by hackers. “One’s default position should be that it isn’t a cyberattack,” he says, “unless there is some concrete evidence to the contrary.”.

Given the rarity of cyberattacks compared to outages as a whole, it’s wise to look at other potential causes first. “Most big outages that look like attacks are self-inflicted,” states João Tomé, the Internet research editor at network data company IPinfo. (In response to WIRED’s questions, Tomé also wrote his own guide for distinguishing outages from cyberattacks.) The usual suspects, he says, include “a bad revision, a configuration change, a cable cut, or a power failure.”.

In the scramble to understand what’s really happening with a given outage, it can sometimes be difficult to tell which sites and services are actually down. And there can be a few reasons for this muddled picture. Sometimes, an outage occurs within a foundational service like a big cloud provider. Major downtime within Amazon Web Services, for example, can cause a cascade of other failures among the company’s customers, who are all dependent on that infrastructure.

Recent findings suggest that in other cases, multiple services can seem to be having outages and producing errors but are functioning normally and only having problems when they interact with an entity that’s down. This appears to have been the case in this week’s bank outage: Multiple banks appeared to be having complications, but they all seem to have been linked to certain inter-institutional transactions that involved JPMorgan Chase. Another good example of this scenario is situations where it may appear that multiple wireless carriers are struggling, but all of the failed calls actually involve one network that’s having an outage.

On top of these, there are also scenarios where one or more large services are down, and false (typically accidental or anecdotal) reports of additional outages create further confusion and opacity.

It can all feel overwhelming, even impossible, to attempt to parse these situations yourself—but there is a concrete place to start. If you think a website or service from a major tech company may be down, look for acknowledgment of the situation on their verified social media accounts or in the “newsroom” press release section of their websites. And keep in mind that you can find these pages and social media accounts through search engines, so you definitely don’t need to be familiar with every company’s communications strategy. Use this same approach for government agencies and other institutions.

Another concrete and vital source of information that you can find through search engines is what’s often known as “status dashboard” pages. Large digital platforms specifically offer these informational pages to provide transparency into whether different components of their service are functioning normally or dealing with issues. In a major incident, companies often thread incremental updates about system restoration on the status dashboard. AWS, Google Cloud Platform, and Microsoft Azure all offer these landing pages, and there are similar ones for other services like Meta business products, Google Workspace, telecom companies, AI companies, and so on.

In a recent development, when a cascading tech failure causes outages across many services at once, and the point of failure isn’t clear, sources of network data that monitor the Internet at a broad scale can also help determine the underlying matter. Amanda Meng, a research scientist at Georgia Tech who helps run its Internet Outage Detection and Analysis service, or IODA, points to not only IODA’s own data—and its social media accounts on BlueSky and Mastodon—but also tools like Cloudflare Radar and Watch the Net. Infoblox’s Madory also posts his own findings about network outages on his social media accounts.

When IODA sees evidence of a network disruption, “we’ll look at the timing of it, the start, the end, the network affected, anything we can find out about it, and then try and connect it with what we see from reporting,” Meng states. That scope, location, and timing of an outage can help determine a suspected cause, whether it’s a single network that might be the victim of a buggy upgrade, a region cut off by damage to a fiber optic cable or, in rarer instances, a cyberattack or even a government purposefully cutting off Internet access to its citizens to stifle protests.

Even with those network clues, however, Meng advises returning to step two: Once an affected service is identified, its actual owners are often the best source to monitor for updates. “Usually the operators have the most information about what’s actually going on,” she says.

Industry observers note that most often, mea culpa updates from companies or government agencies confirm an outage is caused by a tech mishap. In the rarer cases when that downtime results from an actual cyberattack or hacker breach, however, the hackers responsible often claim responsibility in some form.

According to recent reports, ransomware gangs, perhaps the most common source of serious malicious Internet disruption, publicize their breaches on dark web pages that list their targets for the purposes of extortion, sometimes with a countdown timer designed to pressure victims to pay before they leak stolen information. In other cases, like the NotPetya or WannaCry worms—two of the worst cyberattacks in history—the hackers’ self-perpetuating code left behind ransom messages on machines that swiftly spread on social media as the attacks unfolded. Ideological “hacktivist” groups—or state-sponsored hackers working for military or intelligence agencies but posing as hacktivists—also often take credit for their cyberattacks on platforms like X, Telegram, or their own websites. These messages then quickly spread to social media and through news reports.

Even these statements, however, should be taken as just another significant data point: Hacker groups exaggerate, misdirect, or lie when it serves their purposes. And some significant mass hacking incidents—such as the Mirai attack on the Internet infrastructure company Dyn in 2016 that took down thousands of websites—have been accompanied by no public message at all from the hackers responsible.

Industry observers note that in cases where neither the perpetrator of a hacking incident nor its target has confirmed the attack, confirmation can sometimes come first from a government agency—such as the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency—or even from threat intelligence researchers at private cybersecurity firms. Government and industry investigators sometimes post their findings to their websites or share them with media, but it often takes days, weeks, or even months to come to confirmed conclusions. Hence, step five.

Sources indicate that if all these other measures of determining the cause of an outage don’t offer a clear understanding of the situation, the best approach may be patience. “It’s going to get a little conclusive at some point,” Madory claims. “Sometimes you just have to just sit and wait.”.

Aside from the time it takes for companies to admit to their screwups—or hackers to claim a win—the length of an outage can also sometimes offer a clue to its cause. A faulty update, power outage, or cascading bug can often be resolved promptly. Determined hackers who have maximized their damage, who shift tactics to outsmart defenders, or who may still be in a network they’ve breached can cause far longer and more severe outages. “A rollback takes minutes or hours,” Tomé states, referring to reverting to older code as a fix for a faulty update that introduces a bug. “Ransomware takes days or sometimes even weeks.”.

In other words, as with all breaking news, when facts are in short supply, it’s better to maintain skepticism about early rumors or conspiracy theories and wait for more concrete evidence.

In the information vacuum that precedes that evidence, it can be easy to see connections between events that may just be coincidences. What are the odds, you may ask, that five major IT outages occurred on a sequential Thursday, Friday, Monday, and Tuesday?

Recent findings suggest that for Doug Madory, who tracks those network outages every day of the week, that string of failures may just be a sign of the increasing complexity of technology—and how fundamentally our civilization has come to depend on it. “For better or worse, modern society is completely mediated through Internet-based services. They have outages from time to time. And that’s a reality I don’t think we’ll ever completely eliminate,” Madory says.

“If you’re immersed in that world of looking at outages for years on end, then they don’t really surprise you,” he adds. “You just try to understand what’s different about each one. And maybe what we can learn from it.”.
