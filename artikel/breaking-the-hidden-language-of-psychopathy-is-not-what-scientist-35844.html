Breaking: The hidden language of psychopathy is not what scientists expected

It has been revealed that the way people describe their lives may offer surprising clues about their personalities, including traits associated with psychopathy. A new study from the University of Mississippi challenges the familiar image of psychopaths as cold and emotionless, revealing that their patterns of language can be far more complex than expected.

In a recent development, researchers examined hundreds of personal life stories and discovered that three major traits associated with psychopathy, boldness, meanness, and disinhibition, were connected to distinctly different ways of using words. Some of the findings were particularly unexpected. For example, people who scored higher in meanness tended to use more positive language, while those with greater boldness used fewer words associated with anxiety.

The findings, published in the journal Personality and Individual Differences, suggest that psychopathic traits can influence everyday communication in subtle ways that conventional stereotypes often overlook.

Sources indicate that "The goal here is not to diagnose psychopathy based on language," said Ky Bray, an Ole Miss doctoral candidate in psychology. "It's to point to the fact that psychopathy reveals up in everyday behaviors, even subtle behaviors like language.

"If we want to understand psychopathy and the different traits that comprise it, we need to think creatively about how these traits shape social interaction.".

What 450 Life Stories Revealed About Psychopathy.

The researchers used a psychological framework known as the Triarchic Model of Psychopathy, which examines three distinct personality dimensions. Boldness generally involves confidence and fearlessness, meanness reflects tendencies toward callousness and reduced empathy, and disinhibition involves difficulties with impulse control. According to Laura Drislane, an assistant professor of psychology at the University of Mississippi, these characteristics can affect ordinary behaviors, including how people describe their own experiences.

According to recent reports, to investigate these connections, Drislane and Bray recruited 450 participants, including college students and individuals who reported having been incarcerated. Each person responded to open questions about important moments in their life, including their greatest highs, lowest points, and major turning points.

After writing their personal accounts, participants completed a questionnaire designed to measure their levels of psychopathic traits. The researchers then analyzed the written responses using specialized software that identifies patterns in word choice.

The analysis focused on how frequently participants used language associated with different psychological categories, including positive and negative emotions, achievement, social influence or clout, and risk. By comparing these language patterns with the personality scores, the team identified several intriguing relationships.

Surprising Differences in Positive and Negative Language.

Participants with higher disinhibition scores generally used more negative words in their personal stories. They were also less likely to mention friends or use language connected to positive emotions and personal achievement.

Meanness, however, showed a very different pattern. People scoring higher in this trait tended to use more positive language, along with more words related to achievement and rewards. They also used fewer expressions associated with negative emotions.

Sources indicate that boldness was associated with another distinctive pattern. Participants with higher scores used fewer anxiety-related words, including terms such as fear, afraid, and worry. Their personal accounts also contained fewer references to mental illness.

These differences suggest that psychopathic traits do not produce a single recognizable style of communication. Instead, different characteristics are associated with different ways of describing emotions, goals, relationships, and personal experiences.

Recent findings suggest that "What this tells us is the story we tell about our life has meaning," Drislane said. "It helps people make sense of their experiences, develop a worldview and a coherent sense of who they are.

"What we're starting to see is that in psychopathy, there are traits that relate to differences in meaning-making and the ways people think about their life.".

Why Psychopathy Is More Complex Than the Stereotypes.

The researchers also discovered that the relationship between personality traits and language changed depending on which question participants were answering. Someone describing a difficult experience, for example, might use language differently than when recalling a personal success.

This variation is important because it challenges the assumption that psychopathy is characterized by consistently flat or emotionally empty communication. Instead, the findings indicate that language patterns depend on both the particular psychopathic trait and the context in which someone is telling their story.

Sources indicate that "People tend to think of psychopathy as one thing, but it's actually multidimensional," Bray noted. "What we mean when we say that everyone exists on this spectrum. Everyone has some degree of meanness, boldness, and disinhibition.

It has been revealed that "Viewing it from a multidimensional perspective allows us to observe how those traits might manifest in nonforensic populations, in people who have not committed a crime. That allows us to see how these traits affect college students, business professionals, or interpersonal relationships.".

Industry observers note that like many personality conditions, psychopathy is better understood as a spectrum rather than a simple distinction between people who have it and those who do not. At one extreme is the familiar cultural image of a "psychopath," while people at the opposite end score low in all three dimensions.

In a recent development, this approach allows researchers to examine psychopathic traits throughout the general population, including among people who have never committed a crime. It also helps explain why the same broad label can encompass individuals with very different personalities and behaviors.

How Understanding Psychopathic Traits Could Improve Treatment.

Sources indicate that recognizing these differences has implications beyond understanding everyday speech. Researchers have linked dimensions of psychopathy to serious outcomes, including criminal behavior, repeat offending, and intimate partner violence. Examining each trait separately may provide a clearer picture of how these risks develop.

It has been revealed that "That's in part why there's a movement away from calling people 'psychopaths,' because we don't view psychopathy as a category," Drislane said. "But psychopathic dimensions can predict vital outcomes, things like crime, recidivism, or intimate partner violence.

"What our research, including this paper, suggests is that it's crucial to look beyond that label and into the different components or dimensions of psychopathy, because each of those dimensions actually has a unique set of outcomes or things that it can predict.".

Researchers also emphasize that psychopathy can be treated, with interventions aimed at reducing problematic symptoms and behaviors. Understanding the psychological processes behind boldness, meanness, and disinhibition could help scientists develop more targeted approaches and potentially identify ways to prevent severe problems from emerging.

In a recent development, "If we want to be able to treat or ideally even prevent psychopathy from developing, we need to understand the mechanisms at play," she noted. "That comes down to understanding the unique contributors for all three of these dimensions.".
