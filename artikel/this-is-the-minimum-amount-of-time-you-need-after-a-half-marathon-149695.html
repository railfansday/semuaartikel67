This Is the Minimum Amount of Time You Need After a Half Marathon to Run Your Next Race

It has been revealed that generally speaking, the shorter your race distance, the more frequently you can race. You might only get a few chances per year to run a marathon, but you can stack up multiple half marathons, 5Ks, and 10Ks per season, as long as you balance those harder efforts with sufficient recovery time.

But jumping right into another race without a solid chunk of rest time means you risk injury, burnout, and potential disappointment in your next race results. Even if you feel ready to race again soon after a half marathon, specifically, you might need more time than you think. So what’s the ideal reset time after 13.1 and when should you be able to line up again?

Below, run coaches lay out guidelines for when to race again, depending on how you tackled that initial 13.1 miles and your goals for the next one.

In general, it’s a good idea to take two to four weeks off from high-intensity work after a half marathon before racing again, says Laura Norris, a Seattle-based certified personal trainer and run coach with a master’s in applied exercise science.

You should first take three to five days postrace off completely to clear up any muscle damage and let your body fully recover, then stick exclusively to easy running for the rest of that two- to four-week period.

“There’s new research that highlights the half marathon stresses the heart pretty decently for two weeks postrace,” Norris says. While it’s not the same level of stress as a full marathon, it’s still vital to take sufficient time to recover before tackling another high-intensity effort.

With that in mind, you can make adjustments to your timeline:.

The harder you push, the longer it takes to recover, so Norris suggests waiting those three to four weeks before racing again if you just ran your fastest half marathon, even if you feel good.

However, you can consider your upcoming race distance and goal: If you’re jumping into a Turkey Trot 5K for fun, for example, you can probably get away with just two weeks of recovery, but wait at least the entire four weeks before gearing up for a full marathon, Norris suggests.

After a race where you feel really beat up, you’re extra sore, mentally drained, or fatigued, it’s a good idea to take those first three to five days off followed by two to four weeks of really easy running, Norris claims. But that doesn’t mean you jump right into another race.

In this case, “it might be worth spacing your races at least six to eight weeks apart, maybe even a full training season apart,” she notes. Otherwise, you could enter into a vicious cycle of overtraining, not recovering fully, and entering another race underprepared and beating yourself up again, Norris explains.

It can feel really disappointing to train hard for months for your race and not have results to reflect that work, but it’s crucial to honor your recovery and health more than performance.

The amount of time you give yourself to recover from a bad race depends on the reason the race went poorly, Norris says. If you just had a bad day, dealt with a difficult course, or struggled with fueling, you might stick to three to four weeks before another race.

But if you were dealing with an illness after your event or you undertrained leading into it, then in addition to taking days completely off and a few weeks of easy running, you probably want another few weeks of adding in speed before jumping into another race, Norris suggests. This would give you an opportunity to prepare more for your next race, while allowing your body to heal.

Industry observers note that wait a minimum of six weeks before racing again. “That’s at least two weeks to recover, four weeks to navigate training again, and also a few weeks to make sure that you aren’t impulsively making a decision based on high postrace emotions,” Norris says.

It has been revealed that norris notes that you have to evaluate how poorly you feel and listen to your gut, too. It’s always a good idea to get pain or potential injury addressed by a physical therapist and navigate your timeline from there.

In a lot of cases, you can use post-half marathon fitness to your advantage to tackle another race and see how much better you can do. “If you want to race in a couple weeks once your legs feel better and you’re not sore, it might not be a bad time to do a 5K,” claims Christine Rockey, an exercise physiologist, RRCA-certified run coach, and principal lecturer at Coastal Carolina University.

In a recent development, but there’s a difference between setting your sights on another PR and jumping into a fun family 5K. Here’s how you should prepare in training for your next race, depending on your goal.

Norris suggests slowly ramping back up with about a week of 30-minute easy runs after the three to five days of rest. “They won’t feel great at first, but we just want to get the legs moving now that most of the muscle damage has started to repair,” she indicates.

Industry observers note that then, continue with easy running during the following week, but try to aim for about 75 percent of your peak volume during half marathon training. For example, if your peak long run was 14 miles, you might aim to cover 10.

If you have two more weeks, you can add in some race pace work to sharpen your speed during the first of those two weeks, then give yourself a week to taper. It’s always easier to maintain fitness than it is to build, and you want to avoid being overtrained before your next race, Norris says.

As you lace up for your next race, keep a realistic goal in mind. It’s always good to be an ambitious runner and gain more race experience, but remember to keep your expectations in check, Rockey indicates. “I think if you go in thinking the next five races you do are all going to be PRs, I think you’re going to end up disappointed and you might even get so frustrated that you stop running,” she says.

You have a bit more leeway to do lower volume and run a few short easy runs every other day, for example. Norris says you can coast on 50 percent of your peak half marathon volume and know that you’ll still be fit enough to jump into a last-minute 5K.

Sources indicate that it’s important not to go back to a completely sedentary state if you initiative on racing again. “If you were to sit on the sofa [instead of easy running] and do absolutely nothing for three weeks, you would begin to detrain,” Norris adds. “It might not be that serious, but it would probably make the race not feel as good.”.

Plus, going from the couch to another half marathon can lead to aches and pains, Norris adds. “If you go from doing nothing for three weeks to doing 13 miles, that’s a huge jump in single-session load,” she states, pointing to a study published in 2025 that shows jumping up your single-run volume by more than 10 percent in a 30-day span can increase injury risk.

Keeping up with a couple easy runs in between efforts will help you race your best—even when your best means having the most fun.
