This post is Part 2 of a series.

Brief self-affirmation activities have been reliably shown to help people change and grow by reducing a perceived threat. But what kind of threat do they reduce? And how does self-affirmation reduce it?

The vulnerability to stereotype threat we discussed in Part 1 of this series is grounded in a basic human need to see ourselves as “adaptively and morally adequate”: good, capable, reasonable and sensible people who are able to achieve the outcomes that matter to us.1 Named the self-integrity motive by Claude Steele, the psychologist who identified it,2 it differs from the better-known construct of self-esteem in that it is our motivation to view ourselves as “good enough,” rather than to maximize our positive thoughts and feelings about ourselves.

Our sense of self-integrity can be based on any valued qualities we believe we possess.3,4,5 Unfortunately, our self-integrity can also be threatened by anything we see as reflecting negatively on who we are: failure, bad things that happen to us, criticism or rejection, information that challenges what we believe, recognition that we’ve acted in ways that conflict with our values—any experience we take as evidence that we are bad, wrong, incompetent, irrational, foolish, or unable to deal effectively with life.

We feel bad about ourselves (ashamed, humiliated, unworthy) when our self-integrity is threatened, which triggers defensive efforts to protect or restore it: We may dismiss or minimize the threat; attack its source; marshal evidence against it; rationalize or justify our behavior, or deny being responsible for it; try to puff ourselves up through self-aggrandizing statements or comparing ourselves with people who we believe are worse.6,7,8 Any of these strategies may reestablish our self-integrity, but only temporarily—and they leave us vulnerable to more of the same kinds of threats.

This is where self-affirmation can change the story. Focusing on important or valued aspects of the self that are not affected by the self-integrity threat bolsters our global sense of being “good enough”— protectively, if we self-affirm before we feel threatened, or restoratively, if we self-affirm afterward. It also makes all our defensive responses unnecessary, and in doing so promotes honest self-awareness and constructive responses to the challenges we face. Put simply, self-affirmation creates a protective buffer by putting the threat in perspective and helping us take a broader view of ourselves: Yes, I may have been wrong, made a bad decision, acted in a way I regret—but I’m a good and capable person, and my flaws and mistakes don’t define me.2,4,9.

The research on self-affirmation shows how it benefits us in concrete ways. Self-affirmation increases our acceptance of information about risks to our health that would otherwise feel threatening.10,11,12 It increases our openness to evidence that challenges dearly held beliefs, and decreases our tendency to evaluate arguments for and against those beliefs in favor of what we already believe.13,14 It reduces the mental energy spent ruminating on failures and flaws and frees our mental resources for more productive activities, improving performance under stress on tasks that require focused attention, problem-solving, creativity, and insight.9,15,16,17 It increases our self-compassion and fosters helping behavior toward others.18.

It has been revealed that most broadly, self-affirmation activities have consistently been shown to enhance the well-being of those who engage in them:9 It improves the general sense of feeling good, of having community and belonging; it offers relief from symptoms like depression and anxiety that create barriers to well-being; and it improves one's sense that they are a person of competence and value. And just as in the case of the studies of women dissatisfied with their weight that we began with in Part 1, these effects on well-being are long-lasting and get larger with the passage of time.

According to recent reports, can self-affirmation make a difference for you? Here’s an activity that will allow you to experience it for yourself. (For the most meaningful experience, please have paper and pen ready. And please do each of these steps in turn, waiting until you’ve finished the one you’re doing to go on to the next, rather than reading ahead to the end.).

It has been revealed that 1. Think of an area of your life that you have been struggling with. It could be a behavior you’ve been trying to change, but have not changed; something you’ve been wanting to do, but have not done; a decision you’ve been trying to make, but have not made. Once you identify it, please spend the next minute just thinking about the struggle and what it has been like for you.

2. From the following list, please choose one value that a) is very important to you, and has been important in your life, b) is not related to the area of your life you have been struggling with.

It has been revealed that 3. Think of a time when the value you chose was key to you. For the next few minutes, please write about that time and why that value was important to you.

Self-affirmation is most powerful when we’re facing a challenge of high personal importance, which is when our self-integrity is most likely to be threatened.19 Because self-affirmation works by focusing us on an aspect of ourselves that we feel good about and that does not feel threatened, it’s crucial to choose a value that’s unrelated to the challenge at hand; trying to affirm a threatened aspect of the self often makes us more defensive rather than less.19 And self-integrity is strengthened the most when we affirm “self-transcendent” values (values that express concern for others), rather than values that primarily benefit ourselves.4.

The positive effects of self-affirmation on how we feel and how we act reinforce a key tenet of motivational interviewing: we’re most likely to change and grow not when we feel sufficiently bad about ourselves, but when we feel accepted and valued.20 When you embark on a path toward change, don’t neglect to focus on what makes you worthy of value and respect just as you are.
