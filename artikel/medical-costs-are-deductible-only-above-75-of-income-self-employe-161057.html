Medical Costs Are Deductible Only Above 7.5% of Income – Self-Employed Premiums Skip That Rule

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

You can deduct only the medical and dental costs above 7.50% of your adjusted gross income, your income minus a few adjustments, and only if you itemize. Count the health premiums you pay yourself, Medicare’s included. If you’re self-employed and can’t join an employer’s proposal, deduct your premiums separately, with no threshold or itemizing, up to your business’s profit.

Industry observers note that for a retiree with $60,000 of adjusted gross income, which is her income after a few adjustments, the 7.50% threshold is $4,500. With $9,000 of medical costs, Medicare premiums included, she can deduct the $4,500 above it if itemizing beats her standard deduction, which is bigger for anyone 65 or older.

Count what you paid out of pocket for doctors, hospitals, dentists, prescriptions, glasses, and hearing aids, plus the miles you drive to appointments at the IRS’s medical mileage rate. Premiums count too, including Medicare Part B and Part D, coverage you buy yourself, and qualified long-term care insurance up to an IRS limit set by age.

It has been revealed that nonprescription medicine other than insulin, most cosmetic surgery, and gym dues for general fitness don’t count. Neither do premiums your employer takes out of your pay before tax, or bills you pay from a health savings account, since that money was never taxed. IRS Publication 502 lists the rest.

In a recent development, if you’re self-employed, premiums for health coverage you buy on your own go on Schedule 1 instead, where they lower your adjusted gross income without a threshold or itemizing. Medicare premiums count there too, but the deduction can’t exceed your business’s profit. Any month you could join a subsidized plan through your job or your spouse’s doesn’t qualify, and premiums the deduction doesn’t cover can count with your other medical costs.

Costs count in the year you pay them, and a credit card charge counts in the year you make it, even if you pay off the card later. Say the retiree’s income stays at $60,000 and her costs run $6,000 a year. That clears her $4,500 threshold by $1,500 each year, or $3,000 over two years. The same $12,000 paid in one year clears it by $7,500, and that larger total is likelier to beat her standard deduction.

Once this year’s costs get close to your threshold, have the dental work, hearing aids, or eye surgery you’ve been putting off done before December 31, and pay the bill by then.
