Meteorites fascinated ancient Egyptians—just look at King Tut’s ‘space dagger’

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In August 2026, an analysis of iron from ancient Greek tombs revealed surprising results. The objects surveyed included weapons and jewelry from around 3,000 to 4,000 years ago. Most of them were fashioned from regular old iron ore, but around 10 rings were made of iron from outer space—aka from meteorites that crash-landed on Earth.

According to recent reports, it seems that, among the upper classes of Bronze Age Greece, rings fashioned from this out-of-this-world metal were all the rage. Before people learned how to extract iron from ore by melting it at super-high temperatures—a process called smelting—the only source of metallic iron was the occasional space rock. This material, known as meteoric iron, is rare worldwide, but especially in Greece, where approaching meteors usually disappear into the sea rather than make landfall.

Researchers believe that the Greek meteoric rings were actually imported from Egypt: a land with a special relationship to this metal from the skies.

Industry observers note that meteoric iron can be identified because it’s purer than the iron ore that naturally occurs on Earth, with a higher proportion of nickel mixed into it. Because meteoric iron “already arrives on Earth in metallic form,” indicates Victoria Almansa-Villatoro, a professor of Egyptology at Yale University, “it’s very malleable” and can be shaped more easily than unrefined ore.

It has been revealed that the Egyptians started working with this rare material early. “The oldest meteoric iron objects in the world come from Egypt,” indicates Almansa-Villatoro: Nine small beads, excavated from a site called Gerzeh along the bank of the Nile about 20 miles south of Cairo, are the oldest artifacts discovered made from space rocks. They are dated to around 3,200 BCE, more than a thousand years older than the 10 meteoric iron rings found in Greece.

Not only are the Gerzeh beads older than any other meteoric iron objects, they are much, much older than widespread iron smelting in Egypt. Almansa-Villatoro explains that “Egypt was relatively late in comparison with other Near Eastern civilizations to start smelting iron.” By the time the Egyptians were producing iron on a large scale in the 500s BCE, ancient people in Anatolia and other regions had been doing it for about five hundred years.

It has been revealed that A major reason for this late adoption was Egypt’s natural resources. Iron smelting forges burned a lot of wood charcoal, and Egypt had no large forests. For centuries, the Egyptians relied on bronze, which cost less fuel to produce. It wasn’t until major changes in political and trade networks, including pressure to match the iron arsenals of foreign enemies like the Assyrians, that the Egyptians finally made the shift.

Recent findings suggest that while early iron weapons and armor were not automatically better than bronze ones, iron proved easier to repair, and could be strengthened through further reworking. But objects like the Gerzeh beads show that the ancient Egyptians shaped small amounts of meteoric iron long before they knew how to extract it from ore.

Industry observers note that the Gerzeh iron beads were incorporated into jewelry with gems and gold, hinting at their perceived value. All of the other Egyptian objects believed to be made of meteoric iron are similarly small and mostly decorative. The largest and most spectacular is a dagger with a meteoric iron blade more than eight inches long. It was found in the tomb of Egypt’s famous pharaoh, Tutankhamun, who died around 1,323 BCE.

Government records from the reign of Tutankhamun’s grandfather, Amenhotep III, mention an Anatolian king gifting a dagger to the Pharaoh. “People have speculated that this was the same dagger,” says Almansa-Villatoro. “Because, why would they make such a big deal about this king receiving a dagger from a foreign leader, unless it’s made of a very crucial or very rare material?” As seen with the recent findings from Greece, demand for meteoric iron in the ancient Mediterranean was international.

As of 2013, archaeologists had discovered 28 objects in Egypt believed to be made of meteoric iron. This accounts for about half of the 55 known ancient meteoric iron artifacts found across Europe, Asia, and Africa as of 2023.

Sources indicate that the prevalence of meteoric iron in Egypt is difficult to determine, as the composition of many iron objects has never been evaluated. Additionally, indicates Almansa-Villatoro, “we find more things in Egypt in general from this time period than in other regions,” due to the long history of archaeology there.

Analysis has shown that some of the Egyptian meteoric iron objects are made from several small meteorites combined. This might suggest that the Egyptians were “harvesting” and “actively looking” for this rare material, says Almansa-Villatoro, rather than just using it at random when a meteorite happened to land nearby.

Recent findings suggest that almansa-Villatoro believes that meteoric iron “was valuable because it was recognized to be material that came from the sky,” not just because it was more pure than other iron. Ancient Egyptian religious texts link iron with the heavens and the spiritual realm of the gods, who were sometimes described as having iron bones. One Egyptian term for iron literally translates to “the iron of the sky.“.

Industry observers note that “Some tools made of meteoric iron were used in a very vital funerary ritual known as ‘the Opening of the Mouth,’” notes Almansa-Villatoro. These include small iron blades found in Tutankhamun’s tomb (though not the famous dagger). The purpose of this ritual was “to give the deceased back their senses,” Almansa-Villatoro explains, allowing them to speak, breathe, and eat in the afterlife. Meteoric iron might have been particularly prized for crafting these ritual objects.

By the time of Tutankhamun’s reign, the Egyptians might have encountered smelted iron produced in other lands, but they were still using meteoric iron in rituals and reverently placing it in tombs.

“It is possible that they still saw meteoric iron as something different” from smelted iron, notes Almansa-Villatoro, though it’s hard to say for sure.

Some Egyptian texts describe a dome of water with an iron shell enclosing the Earth. Meteoric iron might have been explained as fragments of this cosmic shell breaking off and falling to the Earth.

It’s clear that the Egyptians considered meteoric iron to be a special material, but what exactly they knew or didn’t know about it is not always easy to figure out. The Egyptians understood and interpreted the world around them using very different methods from our own. While we rely on experimental data documented in writing, ancient Egypt “was very much an oral society, where knowledge was transmitted through ritual and through recitation of texts,” states Almansa-Villatoro.

In ancient Egypt, “things that we wouldn’t consider science nowadays, like myth, could have fulfilled the same function” as science does today, notes Almansa-Villatoro. The Egyptians used narrative myths to describe the physical universe. The iron shell surrounding the Earth appears in accounts of the soul’s journey to the afterlife, as a barrier that the spirits of the dead must sail across to reach the skies.

Industry observers note that with our modern knowledge, it’s easy to explain how a space rock might land on Earth. But what if all you knew of outer space was what you could see with your eyes from our planet’s surface? Egyptian myths may not have the structure of a modern scientific paper, but they offer a glimpse into how ancient people made sense of awe-inspiring natural phenomena like metal falling from the skies.
