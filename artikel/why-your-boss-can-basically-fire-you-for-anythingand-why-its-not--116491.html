Why your boss can basically fire you for anything—and why it’s not like that outside the U.S.

We’ve all seen the scene play out in media: A character stands up at work, dramatically declares they quit, and walks out of the office for the last time. While many people will surely empathize with that desire, the reason why a sudden resignation is possible in the U.S. isn’t as great as such a display makes it out to be. Mostly because it goes both ways: an employer can axe you at the drop of a hat, in theory—even without a warning, PIP, or other chance to improve. The U.S. has something called at-will employment, which means that you can leave—or lose—your job without giving any notice. A job that’s at-will “can end at any moment, for good reason, bad reason, or no reason at all, and with no procedural mandates,” says Gali Racabi, an assistant professor at Cornell’s School of Industrial and Labor Relations and an associated faculty member of Cornell Law School. Right now, at-will employment is the law of the land in Washington, D.C., and every U.S. state except Montana. “This makes work in the US really easy to sever,” Racabi says. He adds: “The at-will rule is a travesty, and a mark of moral flaw in our economic order. We need to do away with it ASAP.”  Critiques of at-will employment Despite being the default in America—a country whose culture is associated with entrepreneurialism, independence and one that favors employers without much safety-net protection for workers—at-will employment causes a number of concerns. Most prominent, perhaps, is a person suddenly losing their income and the many repercussions that can come with that. The practice is also especially risky in the U.S., where individuals often rely on their job for necessary benefits like healthcare—something many other nations provide outside of employment, Racabi says. Say, for instance, a person has an operation scheduled for next week and gets laid off today. Suddenly, they don’t have coverage for a necessary procedure. That’s a more extreme example. But even losing health insurance without a major medical issue coming up, and no time to find an alternative, can have devastating repercussions around the cost of medicine, seeing a doctor, or having security in an emergency. At-will employment can also complicate the process of proving retaliation or discrimination as the reason for a layoff. “Having the at-will rule also makes it really difficult to enforce other work-related laws, like anti-discrimination law,” says Racabi. “If an employer can simply terminate someone without notice, reason, or hearing, it makes proving discriminatory intent extremely hard.” The employer doesn’t have to prove any other justification for the firing. In a 2022 survey from the National Employment Law Project, 69% of U.S. workers who had experienced a layoff or firing reported that they were either given no or an unfair reason. On top of that, 72% of these participants reported being terminated without a chance to improve or any warning. Overall, 66% of workers surveyed remarked they support the enactment of just-cause policies. Of course, not everyone thinks at-will employment should be thrown out. Carson Young, an associate professor at West Virginia University’s John Chambers College of Business and Economics, argues that at-will employment is “complicated.” It can allow workers to earn higher salaries and have more flexibility than with a just cause system, since the setup potentially lowers employer costs around hiring extra people, with less incentive to keep anyone onboard. Young also believes that at-will employment grants workers more freedom to leave a job as they please. He also argues that it could also make employers more likely to hire new employees, as they don’t have to make as big of a commitment to the employee if it ends up not being a good fit, a project ends, or the thing they were hired for doesn’t work out. “Employers in [countries] with more job protections are thus more reluctant to hire, because they face greater costs if the hire doesn’t work out,” Young says. How the U.S. compares to other countries The U.S. is fairly unique in having at-will employment, with many other developed countries requiring just cause and notice to terminate an employee. In turn, the individual is often also required to provide notice when leaving a position. “U.S. courts tend to favor freedom of contract and employer-employee flexibility over job security,” says Young. But “employment law in most other countries places a greater emphasis on job security over freedom of contract and flexibility.” In the U.K., arguably the country most directly compared to the U.S., an employer can only let you go for “a valid reason that they can justify” and if they’ve “acted reasonably in the circumstance.” An employer will also typically have to give notice of a layoff between one to 12 weeks prior to termination, depending on the length of employment. An employee must give at least a week’s notice, but it can be longer depending on their contract. Yet, the U.K. also has zero-hour contracts—which mean an employer doesn’t have to guarantee any work and the individual, in turn, doesn’t have to accept any. These casual contracts are common in positions such as retail or hospitality. Critics of the U.K.’s zero-hour contracts point to challenges similar to that of at-will employment: Workers on zero-hour contracts “will find it difficult to initiative their ordinary lives and pay their bills because they are guaranteed no shifts and will find they have no regular or fixed income stream,” says David Cabrelli, a professor of labor law at the University of Edinburgh’s Law School. “Seen from the perspective of job and earnings security, both employment-at-will and zero-hours contracts put workers in a very vulnerable and precarious position.” Could the U.S. get rid of at-will employment? The U.K.’s Employment Rights Act 2025 has set new regulations for zero-hour contracts, including a right to guaranteed hours and pay for last-minute shift cancellations. The changes have not yet rolled out, with some employers and unions criticizing the new measures, the Guardian reports. Generally, the U.S. is not typically seen as a country that strongly values workers’ rights, instead valuing the good of the employer or business. But could the U.S. also pursue changes? Some caveats already exist in circumstances such as whistle-blowing or alleging sexual harassment. “However, these exceptions are issue-specific and the legal position could be improved for workers by the introduction of a just cause dismissal regime,” says Cabrelli. So, then, could just cause ever become the law of the land in the U.S.—a place where workers’ rights are rarely prioritized? “At-will is law, and we can change laws,” says Racabi. “We can enact statutory just-cause or procedural mandate laws at the federal, state, and local levels. We can do those without significant legal pushback. Racabi continues: “We can also support unionization; that one of its byproducts is increased workers’ protection via collective bargaining agreements.”  At-will employment might be the norm in the U.S., but that doesn’t mean it’s the best option. Or that it has to be around forever.
