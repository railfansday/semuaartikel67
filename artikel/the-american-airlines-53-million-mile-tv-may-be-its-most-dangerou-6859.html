The American Airlines 5.3 Million Mile TV May Be Its Most Dangerous Award—It Makes Quitting The Program Too Easy

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that american Airlines will let you spend 5,275,000 AAdvantage miles on a 98 inch Samsung television. It’s a poor return for your miles, but I’d be more worried if I were running the AAdvantage program that a person might actually take them up on it – because it seems likely to be one of the program’s most valuable members, and that member’s last transaction with the program. A reader flagged the offer, joking that they could “watch the flight delay updates in glorious 8K during the next ground stop.” I think there’s a more fundamental problem here than another overpriced merchandise award. American has made it easy for someone with an enormous balance to cash out without ever flying them again. A $35,000 Television For More Than 5 Million Miles The Samsung 98 inch QN990F Neo QLED 8K TV is listed through AAdvantage Exchange, the merchandise marketplace operated by Vonality. The reader’s screenshot demonstrates a price of 5,275,000 miles, matching the public listing. Prices can vary by member and elite status. This really is an expensive television. Best Buy lists the model at $34,999.99. Against that price, you’re getting about 0.66 cents per mile. The mileage price includes sales taxes and shipping. At 8.25% sales tax on the Best Buy purchase brings the comparison to about 0.72 cents per mile. The award includes delivery into your room of choice and removal of the packaging, but not installation or setup. You can also do better on the cash price. Provantage lists the exact model factory new for $27,527.33 on special order. Against that price, the return falls to about 0.52 cents per mile before tax. You don’t even have to move to a refurbished television to make the redemption look worse. Still, roughly seven tenths of a cent against a major retailer’s price is about what I’d expect from this sort of merchandise redemption. It’s the old miles-for-a-toaster problem, on steroids. A Bad Deal For You Doesn’t Automatically Mean A Cheap Award For American I’ve explained before why merchandise usually delivers poor value. Someone has to buy the television. The program or its fulfillment partner may get a discount, but a wholesale discount on electronics doesn’t produce the same opportunity as an airline seat that would otherwise go unsold. Whatever savings they’re getting aren’t turning this into an attractive deal for the member. Offering merchandise gives a program another way to burn miles at a low, predictable return and can help bring down its average redemption cost. But it doesn’t offer the value they can deliver on an award seat. American doesn’t spend the retail price of a business class ticket every time you redeem for one. An otherwise empty premium seat can deliver thousands of dollars in value while costing the airline relatively little more to provide. The Member Buying This TV May Be Leaving The Program The customer who can redeem for this television has accumulated more than 5 million miles in one account. They’ve flown, spent on credit cards, used partners, and leaned into AAdvantage. A great award is supposed to make that member want to earn again. They’ve taken their family somewhere they couldn’t otherwise afford, or flown in a cabin they wouldn’t pay cash for, and feel like they won. It validates all the decisions they made to accumulate those miles. I’ve written about why emptying an account after a successful redemption can actually increase future earning. The zero balance itself isn’t the problem. It’s whether the member comes away wanting to do it again. When I covered the launch of AAdvantage Exchange, I recalled sitting with a then-president of AAdvantage during a session on points conversions and merchandise. She leaned over and told me, “these low value redemptions don’t drive accrual behavior.” The member who knows they’re getting a bad deal and takes it anyway is likely doing it because they’re done with American. They no longer care about squeezing the most from the miles. They want the balance gone. They’ve stopped believing it’s worth the trouble to find good awards, or they’ve decided their future flying and credit card spending belong somewhere else. They’re willing to light their points on fire to finish the relationship. American has given that person a clean way to leave. They don’t have to find award space, coordinate dates for a family, or spread the redemptions over several years. They don’t even have to take one last American Airlines flight. They can turn millions of miles into a physical object that represents the end of their relationship with AAdvantage. American’s dynamically priced premium cabin awards can swallow enormous balances too, but at least a redemption on American puts the customer back on the airline. And that brings them back to the airline for another experience and perhaps reminder them why they wanted the miles in the first place. The television gives that opportunity to Samsung. If I were running AAdvantage, I’d want personal outreach to every member redeeming for this TV. Before it ships, I’d want someone on the phone finding out whether they’re celebrating their loyalty or ending it, and what it would take to keep their business.
