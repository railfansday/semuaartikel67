What genetic innovations enabled the human cerebral cortex, the wrinkled outer sheet of neural tissue responsible for language, abstract reasoning, perception, and memory, to grow vastly larger and more intricate than the brains of most other mammals?

For decades, evolutionary neurobiologists have hunted for uniquely human genes that either expand the starting pool of neural progenitor cells or prolong the developmental window during which those cells divide.

Now, a collaborative team of researchers in Taiwan and Japan has uncovered a third evolutionary mechanism: rather than inventing upcoming genes from scratch, evolution rewrote the regulatory instructions controlling an ancient, shared gene to alter which types of brain-building cells are manufactured.

Led by investigators at National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University (NYCU) and Academia Sinica in Taiwan, alongside collaborators at Kyoto University in Japan, the study pinpoints a small noncoding DNA element that drives the production of basal radial glia (bRG), a specialized class of neural stem cells essential for cortical expansion.

To conceptualize how embryonic brain development evolved, the researchers compare the developing cortex to a biological factory staffed by neural stem cells. Evolution can scale up output by enlarging the workforce or extending factory operating hours, or it can modify the factory’s operating manual to produce a specialized mix of workers.

According to recent reports, in developing primate brains, basal radial glia (bRGs) serve as high-capacity cortical builders. Abundant in humans and non-human primates yet remarkably scarce in rodents, bRGs undergo repeated rounds of self-renewing division within the outer subventricular zone, dramatically amplifying the neuronal output needed to fold and expand the cerebral cortex. They also generate upper-layer projection neurons that form long-range connections bridging distant cortical regions and hemispheres.

“What interested us was that the gene already exists in both humans and mice. The notable difference may not be the gene itself, but the instructions controlling how it is used during brain development. That gives us another way to understand how evolution could produce different kinds of brains,” explained Professor Pei-Shan Hou at the Institute of Anatomy and Cell Biology, NYCU, Taiwan.

The research team focused on DACH1 (Dachshund Family Transcription Factor 1), a developmental gene conserved across mice, monkeys, and humans. Comparative genomic analysis revealed a compact regulatory DNA sequence acting as an enhancer switch for DACH1; while conserved across primates, this regulatory motif differs in non-primate vertebrates.

To determine how this evolutionary edit shapes cortical assembly, the investigators combined cross-species stem cell engineering with live-tissue neuroimaging:.

Further molecular dissection highlighted that DACH1 drives this cell-fate shift by suppressing an opposing signaling pathway that otherwise restricts neural stem cell diversification, ultimately shifting the balance of newborn neurons toward subtypes that wire the brain’s two hemispheres together.

While the authors emphasize that brain evolution and human intelligence emerge from complex, multi-gene networks rather than a single regulatory switch, the findings demonstrate how subtle tweaks in noncoding DNA can profoundly reshape cellular architecture.

By illuminating how regulatory DNA coordinates progenitor identity and developmental timing, the work provides both a clearer framework for understanding primate brain evolution and improved benchmarks for engineering high-fidelity human cortical organoids to model neurodevelopmental disorders.

How did evolution help make the human brain larger without creating latest genes?

In a recent development, researchers discovered that evolution modified a small regulatory stretch of noncoding DNA, acting like a genetic switch, that controls an existing gene called DACH1. By altering when and how strongly DACH1 is used during development, primates produce far more cortex-building stem cells than mice, even though both species share the DACH1 gene.

What are basal radial glia (bRG), and why are they vital?

Recent findings suggest that basal radial glia are specialized neural stem cells that are abundant in developing primate brains but rare in mice. They divide repeatedly to generate huge numbers of neurons for the cerebral cortex—the brain’s outer layer responsible for memory, language, and complex thought—as well as nerve cells that connect the brain’s two hemispheres.

How did scientists prove this DNA switch controls brain cell production?

Industry observers note that when researchers deleted the primate regulatory DNA region from lab-grown human and rhesus monkey brain organoids, the organoids produced significantly fewer basal radial glia cells. Conversely, inserting the human DNA switch into developing mouse brains increased the production of bRG-like cells.

An LHX2-DACH1 regulatory innovation drives basal progenitor expansion in the primate neocortex.

A defining feature of the primate neocortex is an expanded cortical progenitor pool, especially the basal radial glia (bRG), contributing to complex intracortical circuitry underlying higher cognition.

Here, a comparative transcriptomic analysis of developing human and mouse neocortical cells identified DACH1 as a transcription factor enriched in human neurogenic RGs.

In a recent development, using both primate ESCs and mouse models, we found an LHX2-DACH1-miR-215-5p program regulating bRG production. DACH1 loss in human cortical progenitors impaired bRG production and shifted neuronal output toward corticospinal neurons, whereas Dach1 depletion in mice had no effect.

It has been revealed that conversely, ectopic DACH1 expression in the rodent neocortex induced bRG formation and promoted callosal neuron production. Further, we found that DACH1 is regulated by LHX2 via a primate-evolved enhancer. Introducing this enhancer into mice promoted bRG generation, while its removal in primates impaired bRG production.

It has been revealed that mechanistically, DACH1 promotes bRG generation by suppressing miR-215-5p–mediated down-regulation of centromere- and centrosome-related genes. These findings reveal a mechanism by which cortical progenitor expansion shapes the primate cortical connectome for cognitive evolution.
