The BBC is under investigation over allegations of “a hostile work environment” for Jewish employees.

Lord John Mann, the government’s antisemitism adviser, told the broadcaster’s Today program on Thursday that he’s opening an investigation “because there seems to be a growing feeling of discomfort among Jewish staff,” adding: “Some of the things that I’m being told have been quite disturbing sometimes, how little the senior managers of the BBC seem to get it, and so I’m opening this up.”.

Mann encouraged any Jewish people currently working or who used to work for the corporation to come forward and talk to him. In a statement, a BBC representative said the public broadcaster takes “any and all allegations of discrimination extremely seriously — there is no place for it at the BBC. We have introduced compulsory training on antisemitism for all our staff, and we actively encourage colleagues to report any incidences or concerns they have.”.

Concluded the statement: “We are determined to ensure the BBC is a place where everyone can be themselves and feel accepted.”.

Mann won’t be probing any department or program in particular, but stated an “underlying atmosphere” hostile to Jewish members of staff. “The personal validations that I hear from former and current BBC Jewish employees and contractors is that, fundamentally, there is an institutional culture that demands that Jewish people must be expected to justify their existence, their identity, how they define their Jewishness,” he said.

The adviser maintained he has “no agenda against the BBC” and remarked to Today: “Look, there is a problem. The question is — is the problem just a few people? Is it a problem that’s easily solved? But if it’s an institutional problem, that’s much deeper and more difficult to solve. It may be. I’ll report the facts.”.

The news of the investigation comes just a few weeks after an Oscar-winning producer, Lucie Kon, said she received a £400,000 ($525,000) payout from the BBC after alleging antisemitic remarks from her colleagues while she worked there.

Kon, a commissioning editor on several documentaries at the broadcaster, including the Academy Award-winning Mr Nobody Against Putin, mentioned at the time: “I really did feel like I had Jew stamped on my forehead. I became a problem.” The BBC did not uphold the claims of antisemitism, but accepted there were “shortcomings” in how Kon was treated, and didn’t go into further detail.

Deputy director general Rhodri Talfan Davies said there are “very, very robust processes” that are reviewed regularly at a Broadcasting Press Guild lunch earlier this month. “Of course, in any large organisation there can be instances of discrimination, which you deal with when they arise, but there is no evidence of any systemic issue within the BBC that would merit anything like that,” said Davies. “I don’t believe there is institutional discrimination of any sort in the BBC, but I think as an organisation you always have to look harder and review how you go about identifying issues, and that work is never done.”.
