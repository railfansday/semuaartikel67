What’s in My Pack…

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that well, let’s see. There’s raingear, gloves, hat, a blanket, sweatshirt, a calculus book, some water….

Hold up. What was that? A calculus book?

In a recent development, yes. I also have my astronomy and chemistry books in there. Back when I was in college – before the internet but after color TV was apparently invented – our college professors assured us we would all be using calculus in our engineering careers. I’ve wondered for decades when I would ever use this… material (putting it politely), other than to teach it to my son or other engineering students who struggled with higher-level math. So, after thirty-five years, I’ve finally found a use for it.

I don’t expect to be calculating any derivatives out on the trail, though I suppose there could be some deep analysis on rates of change over time, especially involving hiking distances per day.

For astronomy, I love looking at the stars. Sure, it’s engineering astronomy with lots of galactic calculations for weights, trajectories and distances, but it has a few very pretty star charts that would look great at night. Not so much at 10 a.m. on a random Saturday, but considering Michigan, the probability of unusual weather events is never zero.

What about chemistry? Its really more of a canary in a coal mine for me… or more accurately, a canary in my backpack. The only chemical reaction I’m investigating is whether the chemistry book turns out wet and its pages become more pulp-like, in which case I’ve learned something.

My pack liner is no longer waterproof.

I’d take my statistics book, which is a heavier, but I actually like that one. Yes, I know. I have a bit of a twisted mind to enjoy statistics. The other choice was my Engineering Project Management book. While hiking the AT is, in itself, an entire project management adventure, and I suspect Change Control may be a solid fireside topic while hiking, I still need that book for work-related activities.

A better question may be WHY do I have these three books in my pack?

It has been revealed that weight. They help my pack get up to 30 pounds, which I hope is my peak hiking weight when I’m on the trail next March. The calculus book is a whomping 5 pound and a touch over 15 ounces. Lots of “good times” in that book. Chemistry is another five pounds and almost 2 ounces while astronomy is in between with 5 pounds and 5 ounces.

Perhaps a better title for this post would be “How Am I Preparing for My AT Hike?” In which case, those books are involved, but no, they aren’t going on the trail with me in four months.

For preparing my body for the trail, there are three strategies I’m using:.

Recent findings suggest that southeastern Michigan doesn’t have mountains, and the hills are more bump-like, but they are there. Tossing a 30-pound pack on my back and hiking 5–6 miles is a decent warm-up. I’m trying to do that every week.

Right now, I just finished a five-day continuous stretch of doing this every morning, as I had some vacation time still to burn from work. I’m assessing how my body handles it, what that strange feeling is in my legs, why my ankles are sore, and trying to force myself to get up, out of a comfortable bed, and hike in sub-30-degree weather. It’s been challenging but so far, OK. I am glad, though, to take a break over the next few days and instead keep working on the other two strategies.

Besides just working on my body, I’m also testing how various software works while on day hikes – my Garmin In-Reach Mini has passed my previous checks and now has its subscription suspended for another few months until I really need it, but testing Day One (a journaling app), Grammarly (to well… help with my grammar) and hopefully, WordPress to see how a post or two work here when I’m coming off a hike when I tired, hungry and just want to start my drive home with the heat on full blast.

It has been revealed that what would be considered the easiest is actually one of the harder ones for me. A good workout at the gym is two hours. That’s enough time to get at least three miles on the treadmill (at 3.4 mph with a 6–7° uphill grade), core work, strength training, stretching and maybe finishing by laying on a hydro lounger at the end.

Sadly, my job takes up longer hours than I want to admit at times, but at least I enjoy doing what I am doing at the office as well as the people I work with. The problem comes at the end of the day. If I don’t leave early enough, I don’t get to the gym until after 5 p.m., in which case it’s impossible to get a decent work out and get home in time to cook dinner for my wife. I’m left wondering where the day went.

In a recent development, yes, it’s true I could get up earlier in the morning, but… that isn’t happening. Probably won’t happen on the trail either, to be fair. Getting up before the sun rises is something I consider a necessary evil to continue getting a paycheck. Getting up even earlier to willingly work out is far worse and would fall into the category of a malicious act of cruelty to my body.

Industry observers note that this is strangely enough, the easiest and most satisfying. I’m doing a routine of about thirty minutes with ankle strengthening, core work and stretching. It’s something casual to do while watching TV and the time just passes. It’s not the most useful, but hopefully it will help prevent something like an ankle sprain.

In a recent development, as for my actual gear list. Yes, I’ll post it. It will probably be right after the Christmas holidays, as I have some upcoming gear that absolutely needs to be weighed, manhandled and experimented with. My hope is less than 20 pounds base weight for starting in March, with a fully loaded pack of under 30 pounds. Whether I hit that target or not, is in jeopardy with the requirement of a bear can, but that’s another discussion….

According to recent reports, about me? Sure. I’m a native Detroiter and have lived my whole life in Michigan where I live with my wife – more than 25 years – and a couple of college-aged children. Well, technically the kids live off at college but they do come home to make the house look a natural disaster struck while we weren’t looking. I’m an engineer, working for one of the Big Three automakers and have well over 30 years of experience doing everything from breaking transmissions to quality improvements and even designing cooling systems for both trucks and cars. Outside of work, I love exploring and spending time outdoors. I’ve learned a lot about camping over the last few decades, including relearning wonderful lessons like not leaving an unlocked cooler in camp and proper flow directions on my water filter. Somewhere in there, I fell in love with backpacking and found a whole new world of mistakes I can learn from. Eventually, I discovered long-distance thru-hiking and convinced both my job and my family that taking a vacation from me for six months would be beneficial to them (suckers!). Catch me on a good day, and I’d be happy to talk about camping, backpacking, video games, books, music or just about anything else fun. Just don’t ask me about spreadsheets, checklists, FMEAs or change control meetings.
