Latest Update: The Terminus of the CDT is Now Behind a Border Wall. Here’s What Hikers Can Expect

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that the southern terminus of the Continental Divide Trail has changed a lot in the last year. The granite obelisk once stood in the open Chihuahuan Desert, in a remote area visited by few except hikers either starting or ending their journeys. The dirt road approaching it, Commodore Road, was unmaintained and required high-clearance vehicles, limiting access and traffic.

Now, though, the monument stands between two imposing 30-foot border walls currently under construction, in a National Defense Area (NDA). Government contractors graded and widened the road from 8 feet to 24 feet to accommodate construction vehicles. For now, hikers can still enter the NDA with permission and touch the monument—whether they’re starting northbound or ending their CDT hike. However, security guards will eventually usher them away, out of the construction zone.

In November, Teresa Martinez, the executive director of the CDT Coalition (CDTC), and Audra Labert, the CDTC director of communications, traveled to the southern terminus. Neither had been there since construction began in early 2026.

Industry observers note that “We wanted to see on the ground what was actually happening and what people were experiencing,” Martinez told Backpacker. She described the scene as “surreal.”.

Arriving at the terminus monument, Martinez and Labert watched as two hikers approached in the distance. The two women, trail names Geisha Girl and Fearless, marched toward them, border walls standing on both sides. In an emotional moment, the teary-eyed hikers walked right up to the monument.

Recent findings suggest that “I think if they hadn’t been able to put their hands on the physical monument, even though it was between the two barriers, it would have been devastating,” Martinez said. “The importance of having access to that now while it’s under construction is so fundamental.”.

According to recent reports, while these changes are striking to someone like Martinez, who has visited this terminus hundreds of times during her work with the non-profit trail organization, she says she sees a silver lining. The construction traffic is annoying, loud, disruptive, and changes the experience of finishing or starting your CDT hike in a quiet corner of the desert. But the improvements to Commodore Road greatly increase access to the trail’s southern terminus.

It has been revealed that “Building a road invites more people, which means more people can get there, which is a beautiful thing. They can drive their loved ones to the trail and pick them up from the trail much easier than it ever has been,” she said. But at the same time, the improvements make it feel less remote, and could make it significantly busier. “So it’s bittersweet,” she said.

Sometime “in the near future,” Martinez mentioned the terminus monument will be moved out of the NDA. Then, the CDTC will create a new one with accurate longitude and latitude markers. She sees that as an opportunity to rethink how the monument will be dedicated and to include indigenous voices that weren’t involved when the original monument was dedicated in 1974.

Asked what hikers should expect when approaching the southern terminus of the CDT, Martinez paused thoughtfully.

Sources indicate that “I think they should be prepared for an amazing experience in one of the most beautiful places on the planet,” she said. “But you’re going to see this infrastructure now that didn’t exist before. And I would ask people to lean into the good stuff and then also use that moment as an opportunity to reflect on how the experience is making them feel.”.
