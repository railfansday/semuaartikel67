Rocks once buried in mountain belts suggest Earth's ranges grew taller in two steps, each near a rise in oxygen and in complex life.

Picture the young Earth and you probably see mountains in your mind. For the planet’s first billion years or more, it may not have had any that would resemble the majestic mountain ranges our planet has today.

It has been revealed that rocks that were once buried deep inside mountain ranges suggest the ranges grew taller in two steps, a newly introduced study finds. The first step ended about 2.2 billion years ago and the second about 700 million years ago.

Each step came around the time Earth’s air gained oxygen and life grew more complex.

Matthijs Smit, an associate professor of earth sciences at the University of British Columbia, led the work.

Recent findings suggest that the link to life is a match in timing, not a demonstrated cause. The authors call it a hypothesis that still has to be tested.

Recent findings suggest that when two plates collide, rock gets shoved downward as well as up. Minerals that form under that squeeze keep a record of the pressure they felt, and pressure translates into depth.

According to recent reports, the deepest-buried rocks that later return to the surface mark roughly how thick a mountain belt’s crust had grown. Smit’s team gathered pressure and age estimates for such rocks from around the world.

In a recent development, the team relied on garnet, a mineral that forms during the burial itself and can be dated precisely. They also added new measurements from ancient rocks in Cameroon and the Democratic Republic of the Congo.

In an interview with Earth.com, Smit said the debate over the early Earth has run between two pictures.

“Some would argue that it was an almost alien world, with no emerged lands, whereas others would argue that it operated very much in the same way as the modern Earth, with large mountain chains, active tectonics and widespread emerged lands,” he said.

Before about 2.7 billion years ago, the rocks record burial no deeper than roughly 19 to 22 miles (30 to 35 kilometers). In the long middle era that followed, mountain belts buried rock to 34 to 37 miles (55 to 60 kilometers).

The middle-era depth is roughly double the earlier one. In the current era, rocks have come back from about 56 miles (90 kilometers) down, about half again as deep.

The modern figure matches the crust under the Himalaya and Pamir today.

What surprised Smit was how steady the maximum depth stayed within each era, and how consistently it changed from one era to the next.

“There does seem to be a specific tectonic style for each episode in the tectonic evolution of the lithosphere, which dictates in a broad sense how thick (and tall) mountains could become,” he noted.

According to recent reports, thicker crust generally stands higher above sea level. Using the relationship between thickness, density, and elevation seen today, the team converted the depths into rough heights.

Before 3 billion years ago, the highest terrain comes out at sea level, within the error bars. Through the middle era, the highest peaks could have stood about 3.1 miles (5 kilometers) tall, roughly half the height of Everest.

Mountains of that era were real, then, but they were not the giants of today.

For the modern era, the method gives about 5.5 miles (8.8 kilometers). That is the height of Everest, K2, and Kangchenjunga, to within the uncertainty.

The authors note the sea-level comparison holds for recent geological time and probably for the middle era too. In the earliest era, the oceans were larger and the crust denser, so those early heights are rougher guesses.

Most of the sediment that rivers carry to the sea comes from mountains, and the amount depends on erosion above about 1.9 miles (3 kilometers) of altitude.

Taller mountains mean more rock washed into the oceans, and with it phosphorus, which limits how much marine life can grow.

According to recent reports, both growth periods line up, broadly, with the two great rises in atmospheric oxygen. Each oxygen rise was followed by a Snowball Earth ice age and then by a jump in the amount and variety of life.

According to recent reports, strontium in ancient seawater, which tracks how much material was coming off the continents, also rose during both periods. Phosphorus would have behaved the same way, the authors argue, because it is held in similar minerals.

“It’s likely not a coincidence that life on Earth developed and diversified approximately during the same period that the two growth spurts occurred,” mentioned co-author Ellen Kooijman of the Swedish Museum of Natural History.

The match isn’t perfect, and the authors say so. Some records of oxygen and living matter don’t move in step with the mountains. That could mean the hypothesis is wrong, or that the planet responded to extra nutrients in thresholds and delays.

Sources indicate that why would mountains grow taller only at certain times? “We suspect that these increases are ultimately linked to the cooling of our planet,” Smit remarked. “As a result, the process that makes mountains changed, allowing mountains to grow taller.”.

In the authors’ reading, the first change was the arrival of plate tectonics as we know it, with one plate sliding beneath another and crust piling up.

It has been revealed that the second came as the cooling mantle let continental margins be dragged deeper before snapping free. That is how the thickest mountain belts are built today.

Old, deeply buried rocks rarely survive. Such rocks are the most likely to be altered by later events or lost back into the mantle, so the record is thinnest exactly where the first step happened.

Smit told Earth.com that the first change is pinned down only to a window of several hundred million years, ending around 2 billion years ago.

“We don’t know precisely how this change occurred in time and whether it was even one continuous change or whether there were short bursts by which mountains were able to get higher and higher,” he said.

Until that window narrows, the timing can’t be checked closely against the changes in the oceans and atmosphere, Smit noted.

In a recent development, that work starts with more field research in ancient terrains, sampling rocks and measuring their pressures and ages. He also suggested Earth system models that include step-like, tectonically driven increases in phosphorus.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances. Co-authors are at Utrecht University, the Swedish Museum of Natural History, and the Geological Survey of Canada, among others.
