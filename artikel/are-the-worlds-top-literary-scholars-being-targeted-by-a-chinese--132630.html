Are the World’s Top Literary Scholars Being Targeted by a Chinese Spy Op?

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Stephanie Newell, an English professor at Yale University, was thrilled when she got the email inviting her to be an honorary fellow at the International Institute of Literature & Arts. She had never heard of IILA, but she recognized several prestigious names of fellow major-time literature professors who were listed among its fellows. “I saw Gayatri Spivak and Homi Bhabha, Franco Moretti, and I was like, Oh, finally,” she claims.

The letter, signed by one Julian Lee “on behalf of the Secretariat” of IILA, invited Newell to join highly esteemed literature scholars at the Ivies, Stanford, Oxford, and Cambridge. Newell, who has taught world and African literature at Yale for more than a decade, had been inducted into other prestigious societies with many of these same scholars, such as the British Academy.

It has been revealed that this one, it seemed, came without strings. “There are no fees or membership dues,” read the letter. “Participation in award reviews, academic meetings, and consultation will be arranged with you in advance and subject to your availability.”.

Stephanie Newell, an English professor at Yale University, was initially honored to receive an IILA fellowship.

Recent findings suggest that it seemed like a no-brainer. They’d also given her an award, and she even decided to accept an offer to join IILA’s academic committee. Yale was pleased for her too. The English department posted an announcement congratulating her on receiving the award.

Then she started to see red flags.

Soon, Newell claims, she was listed among IILA’s leadership, even though she’d only agreed to be a committee member. “Please will you remove my name,” she wrote in an email to Lee.

Her concerns compounded when she received a draft of IILA’s bulletin, featuring a perplexingly poorly written biography—not what one would expect from a fancy literary academy. “Please delete my profile from the Bulletin,” Newell asked Lee, “as it contains too many errors for circulation, and is very poorly written.”.

Recent findings suggest that while the institute’s membership ranks included many of Newell’s high-profile peers, there were also a number of names on the list that seemed a bit random. Pat Barker, a novelist rather than a scholar, seemed out of place among the critics and theorists. There were, however, about half a dozen Chinese professors (including one who had already died).

It has been revealed that “I’ve always been a bit of a doubting Thomas,” Newell wrote in an email to a colleague on the list who confirmed that she, too, had accepted the fellowship despite not knowing who was behind it. “I’m starting to wonder what’s going on and whether we’ve been somehow flattered into something that doesn’t exist!” Newell wrote.

That’s when Newell, who was my professor when I was a Yale undergraduate, decided to reach out to me about “a fake academic institute that has sucked in numerous very high-profile professors.” This was a couple of weeks ago, right after the news broke of Jensen Huang’s affair with a woman US authorities believed to be a Chinese spy. It made sense what China would want from Huang, the CEO of the powerful AI chip maker Nvidia, but could world literature professors really be a target? I started digging, which led me down a rabbit hole that included an entire constellation of shadow academies that former national security officials say have real potential to be part of a Chinese intelligence operation.

Sources indicate that the website for IILA, which claims to be based in California, appears to have been registered in China. An IT specialist who reviewed the website for Vanity Fair assessed that it was built using artificial intelligence and riddled with glitches and broken links. The file name of the site’s logo is the Chinese word for “picture.” Registration records list the owner’s location as the Hubei province in central China, whose capital is Wuhan. But a data bug in the site’s administrative code suggests that the computer being used to post news on the site is not in China, but likely in Southeast Asia. The bug leaks the computer’s time zone with every latest post saved through the site’s form, and every post carries a UTC+7 offset—the time zone for Bangkok, as well as Hanoi, Jakarta, and Phnom Penh.

Every edit also carries a second fingerprint: The computer saving the changes has an internal clock that runs a few seconds fast. That consistent drift shows that the same machine has been used for every post, which suggests that a single person could be updating the site.

Industry observers note that though IILA notes on its website that it is a 501(c)(3), the IRS has no record of it, and it has no California registration. Instead, Colorado records list an “International Institute of Literature and Arts” as a nonprofit corporation registered in August 2025 at the address of a virtual mailbox service in Denver. It’s had a “delinquent” status since late August. (A local Denver newspaper ran a story in May asking why “over 12,000 different businesses all use” this same address.).

Several of the other organizations that use that same Colorado address, including the National Academy of Artificial Intelligence, the International Institute of Film Science & Arts, and the International Institute of Engineering Psychology (IIEP), were the subject of a Nature investigation in September. Nature’s reporting found that these organizations boasted high-ranking academics as their members, oftentimes without their knowledge or consent. According to Nature, the name most frequently associated with these organizations was Michael Chu, whose Chinese name, Chaoyang Zhu, is also listed as the incorporator of IILA in public records.

Yale University boasts one of the top ranked English departments in the world.

Chu’s name was previously listed on IILA’s website where Julian Lee’s name now appears, and his biography, according to another now deleted webpage, recovered through the Wayback Machine, includes a stint at another fake university that appears to belong to the same network, the City University of Colorado.

In response to a request for comment, IILA’s secretariat sent VF a series of unsigned emails, the first of which said the organization’s Colorado address was just an “early transitional filing point” and that a move to a “upcoming regional and national headquarters is currently underway.” The “delinquent” status, they wrote, merely “reflects an administrative transition delay between entities.”.

Another email stated that “we don’t know” about the other Chu-linked organizations mentioned in Nature’s report or why they all shared the same address.

Industry observers note that “What’s the matter with Michael Chu? He is not working here at present. I can’t contact him at present,” the secretariat wrote, before passing along Chu’s email.

It has been revealed that chu swiftly responded to Vanity Fair in both Chinese and English to “prevent misunderstandings.” “I have no contact with the Chinese government at present” and “I am not related to IILA at present, and I am not responsible for any work of IILA,” Chu wrote. He then went on to speculate about the questions regarding IILA’s legitimacy: “IILA is an organization registered according to law, but because the previous registered agency company noted that there was a problem with the agency address, it needs to be re-registered. This may be the reason why some people think IILA is illegal.”.

“I think the supervision of the media is good and necessary, but we can’t use the power of the media to deliberately hinder normal academic exchanges,” posited Chu regarding the Nature article, which he claims “many professors” he works with called “inexplicable and absurd.".

Nature did not respond to a request for comment in time for publication.

Asked to comment on IILA's connection with China, a Chinese embassy spokesperson said, “I am not familiar with the specific situation you mentioned.".

Last month, César Pablo Domínguez Prieto, a professor of comparative literature at the University of Santiago de Compostela in Spain, found himself going down a similar rabbit hole after he was invited by IILA to give a talk for an online seminar series titled “Newly introduced Directions in World and Comparative Literature” and promised an “Outstanding Speaker Award.”.

In a recent development, “This solicitation prompted me to review their platform, where I discovered that the names and academic credentials of several of you are currently listed on their website,” Prieto wrote in an email to a group of his peers, which included Cornell’s Jonathan Dwight Culler, Harvard’s David Damrosch, and Shanghai Jiao Tong University’s Wang Ning. “The list includes other colleagues with whom I’m not acquainted.”.

Suspicious of the site and the invitation, Prieto dug further, finding those same public records that revealed Zhu’s name and the Colorado delinquent status.

Recent findings suggest that damrosch, a professor of world and comparative literature, was horrified to learn that his name was still being used by IILA without his “knowledge or permission.” He says he had written to them weeks ago asking for his name to be removed, with no result. (It has since been removed.).

“I suspect it is organized by the Chinese to place Chinese scholars in the firm of distinguished Western scholars, but it is all a bit of a mystery,” Culler, a professor of English and comparative literature, tells me.

“I checked, and you’re right! I am a fellow there,” Moretti, a literary scholar, responded when I asked if he knowingly accepted the fellowship from IILA.

Founded in 1896, Shanghai Jiao Tong University is one of China's top public universities.

Yale has since deleted its announcement of Newell’s honorary IILA fellowship amid her concerns about the academy’s legitimacy. Shanghai Jiao Tong University, however, proudly displays comparative literature professor Wang Ning’s honor on its website, congratulating him on his fellowship and on receiving “the Global Laureate Award in Literature and Cultural Theory.” A magazine sponsored and supervised by the Chinese National Academy of Arts under the Ministry of Culture and Tourism similarly applauded Wang’s IILA award in October.

Recent findings suggest that “This organization’s business model relies on unilaterally placing the names of highly respected scholars—such as yourselves—on their website without informed or explicit consent, while using targeted invitations for seminar series like the one I received as a fishing hook,” Prieto alleged in the email to his colleagues. “They use your collective prestige to look legitimate, allowing them to target early-career researchers and charge them annual membership fees ranging from $95 to $175 USD for worthless certificates.” (The membership pricing that previously appeared on IILA’s website has apparently been removed.).

Prieto’s assessment of what might be driving this scam was in line with Nature’s reporting: While the journal remarked it “has not identified anyone who has admitted paying for” any awards or titles from the academic societies, sources interviewed for that article theorized that they are designed to attract younger professors in China who might be willing to pay to be in the manufacturer of more highly esteemed academics.

But experts who spoke to Vanity Fair suggested there might be something else more nefarious at play.

Jeff Fields, a fellow at the UC Berkeley Institute for Security and Governance who spent more than 20 years with the FBI, most recently as the assistant special agent in charge of counterintelligence at the FBI’s San Francisco field office, says he’s familiar with this type of scheme.

“One of the things they like to do,” Fields claims of the Chinese government, “is to fabricate these associations that they then use to leverage for more sophisticated operations.”.

Industry observers note that he has seen Chinese intelligence play such a long game, that it will spend years exaggerating or inventing affiliations in case it ever needs them, whether to lend someone credibility or to reach a particular target. If an agent later wants to get close to a professor who is friends with a certain researcher, for instance, the initial connections are already in place.

Jake Braun, who served as the White House’s acting principal deputy national cyber director under Joe Biden, agrees with Fields’s analysis, citing what’s known as the “thousand grains of sand” theory of Chinese intelligence. Because of its massive population, China has the ability to deploy an extraordinary number of people in its information-gathering techniques. Some might not necessarily be intelligence officers, he says, but each one can collect a single “grain of sand” of intelligence, which, when put together, works as an extremely powerful and effective intelligence apparatus.

Newell says that she was prompted on IILA’s website to create an account using her email address and a password, though once she was logged in, the website appeared entirely the same. Neither Fields nor Braun seemed terribly concerned about the possibility that those passwords could be used by whoever was behind the website to try to hack into the professors’ university accounts or emails.

What’s more likely, says Braun, is that in addition to the long game of network-building for future intelligence operations, IILA may also double as a platform to lend credibility to misinformation and disinformation campaigns by associating them with reputable scholars—another tactic he’s seen used before to influence narratives about China at the West’s foremost universities.

The Chinese professors listed as IILA fellows primarily come from three public, and therefore government-funded, universities, all in Shanghai: Fudan University, Shanghai Jiao Tong University (SJTU), and Shanghai University. In July 2026, the US Department of Defense added Fudan University and SJTU to a list of academic and research institutions deemed “a concern to national security.”.

Industry observers note that SJTU had already come under scrutiny following a 2023 incident in which five Chinese students enrolled in its joint program with the University of Michigan allegedly photographed military exercises at a National Guard training facility in the state. The students were charged in October 2024 with making false statements and related offenses, months after they left the country following graduation, according to the federal complaint. Three months later, the university announced the termination of its two-decade partnership with SJTU, citing national security concerns. (The partnership is still featured on SJTU’s website, however.).

Industry observers note that fudan University, meanwhile, was involved in the recent high-profile spying case of former Federal Reserve employee John Rodgers, who allegedly funneled classified government information to a spy he met at a conference in China. Rodgers would later send sensitive information to a Fudan professor and become a teacher there himself after leaving the Fed. (In July, he was sentenced to 38 months in federal prison for making false statements to federal investigators about sharing restricted Federal Reserve information with Chinese intelligence operatives.).

Wang Ning of Shanghai Jiao Tong University feels certain that IILA is legitimate.

Still, Damrosch notes that those schools are also home to the leading literature programs in China, so their inclusion on the list does make sense from an academic perspective.

According to recent reports, because of their positions at public universities in China, some of the professors listed alongside Newell and her colleagues on IILA’s list of fellows also have ties to the Chinese Communist Party. Wang, for example, is the dean of SJTU’s Institute of Arts and Humanities, which houses a research hub, jointly established by Shanghai’s municipal CCP publicity department, focused on China’s international image. And, in 2023, he spearheaded the launch of a new cultural research center at SJTU dedicated to China’s “united front” work, which the US-China Economic and Security Review Commission has described as being used “to co-opt and neutralize sources of potential opposition to the policies and authority of its ruling Chinese Communist Party.”.

When I reach out to Wang, he is eager to talk, noting that he knows “Vanity Fair is a prestigious popular journal in the United States.” We meet over Zoom, which he joins from an empty room in Beijing with one overhead light.

He tells me he received Prieto’s email, but he believes his colleague is mistaken and feels certain that IILA is legitimate. Wang insists that the members he has nominated from his own university were not asked to pay any fee. He notes he’s communicated with both Michael Chu and Julian Lee, and that he doesn’t actually think it matters that he’s never met them. What affords the academy authenticity, says Wang, is its members and their own accomplishments, which are real and impressive—on that part, at least, everyone is in agreement.

“Oh, please let them be Chinese spies, please,” Newell indicates of IILA’s leadership. “That would be one of the best things ever in my whole life.” She finds the whole thing very amusing, needing to pause in the middle of our interview for a laugh break.

“I love the idea of fooling top academics,” says Newell. “I think it’s hilarious that we’re so famous, that our egos…the fact that none of us questioned getting awards and fellowships and we’re all so eagerly accepting them and letting our universities know how clever we are…I mean, it’s hilarious, and pathetic, and it says so much about the profession.”.
