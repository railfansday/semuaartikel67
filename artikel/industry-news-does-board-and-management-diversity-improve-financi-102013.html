In 1954, the economist Darrell Huff published How to Lie with Statistics, a slim volume that became, improbably, one of the best-selling statistics books in American publishing history. Huff’s central insight—that correlation dressed in the vestments of causation can seduce even careful readers—has aged better than most mid-century paperbacks. It’s due for a revival.

Especially in the wake of a upcoming report, Capturing the Diversity Benefit, which claims that companies with more racially diverse management teams perform better financially.

The report is not without ambition. It draws on federal EEO-1 workforce filings matched to financial data for 1,482 publicly traded companies between 2016 and 2024, and it contains some genuine quantitative analysis.

Industry observers note that but its empirical research design is deeply flawed, and in ways Huff would have recognized instantly. The analysis may document correlations inside a selected dataset, but it does not establish that management diversity improves corporate performance, that DEI programs create shareholder value, or that companies incur financial risk by scaling those programs back.

Recent findings suggest that consider, first, the sample, always the place a skeptical reader should look, and always the place such reports hope one will not. The number of observed companies changes substantially by year, reaching 1,059 workforce observations in 2020 and falling to 523 in 2024. Earlier data combine voluntary disclosures with records obtained through a Department of Labor Freedom of Information Act release. Later data rely heavily on voluntary disclosure alone, a shift roughly analogous to switching, mid-poll, from a mandatory census to a call for volunteers.

Put simply, DEI and ESG scores vary so markedly across providers that they have largely lost their meaning as fixed facts about a organization.
