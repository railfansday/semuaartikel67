Arrival of payments in contentious legal saga that began in 2020 is ‘a weight off my shoulders’, one survivor said.

Hundreds of clergy sexual abuse survivors have received more than half of the $305m bankruptcy settlement that the archdiocese of Upcoming Orleans reached with them last year, a milestone that marks the beginning of the end of a years-long legal saga.

It has been revealed that the trustee overseeing the settlement fund, Don Massey, confirmed on Friday that $180m had been distributed to 450 of about 740 survivors, either directly or through their lawyers if they retained legal representation. Massey remarked he and his staff are working hard to get payments in the rest of the survivors’ hands by the end of the year but are still waiting on hundreds of distribution forms from survivors and their lawyers.

Some who had received their payments by Friday – more than six years after the archdiocese filed for federal bankruptcy protection, in May 2020 – expressed a mix of relief, appreciation and sadness.

According to recent reports, “It’s a weight off my shoulders because it’s been such a long time,” said Theo Jackson, who was abused as an 11-year-old in the late 1980s at the Hope Haven Catholic orphanage in suburban Upcoming Orleans. “I don’t have to worry about any more filings and litigation, and I’m just glad all of that is over.”.

Jackson was echoed by Aaron Hebert, who was molested in his childhood in the late 1960s by Lawrence Hecker, a priest who would go on to admit to the Guardian and its Upcoming Orleans reporting partner WWL Louisiana on camera that he had sexually abused several other boys – before being convicted of rape and dying in prison in 2024.

“In a way, it’s a relief because I could say it’s finally over,” Hebert said.

It has been revealed that but Hebert mentioned he also felt that he and his fellow survivors “were railroaded”, given the suffering inflicted on them and the wait they endured by being forced into bankruptcy court. He alluded to how the archdioceses of Upcoming York and Los Angeles agreed to similar settlements without filing for bankruptcy protection, a process that dragged out in New Orleans, Louisiana’s most famous city.

Recent findings suggest that getting payments to the survivors caught up in the New Orleans archdiocese bankruptcy proved more time-consuming than church leaders and attorneys had promised during settlement confirmation hearings in December 2025. Massey even teamed with the Louisiana-based organization Sexual Trauma Awareness and Response to provide survivors with a 24/7 crisis hotline during the settlement payment process.

Recent findings suggest that the archdiocese paid the full $305m into the settlement trust fund shortly after the hearings to confirm the agreement. But a points-based process determining the amount of individual payments – meant to account for the severity and impact of each case – took months to implement over the summer of 2026. And Massey reported in a court filing that the church had been slow in turning over records needed to determine payment amounts.

It has been revealed that another factor in the delay was Massey’s effort to get Medicaid and Medicare to release liens that tried to claw back portions of some survivors’ settlement payments if those survivors received either of those government health benefits. Massey said there are still about 30 Medicare liens the settlement trust is working to get the federal Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) to release.

Sources indicate that the payments under the settlement are for pain and suffering. But the CMS had tried to argue the settlement money should be used to pay Medicaid or Medicare back for mental health care and other ongoing medical treatment that could be associated with the trauma that survivors experienced.

Massey noted he convinced the agency that the archdiocese bankruptcy settlement payments were not subject to those clawbacks.

Sources indicate that upcoming Orleans’ archdiocese is the second-oldest Catholic US diocese. It is among more than 40 Catholic institutions in the US to file for bankruptcy protection amid the financial fallout of the church’s international, decades-old clergy abuse scandal.

Twenty-nine of those organizations had settled their bankruptcy cases as of Friday, according to information from the Penn State University law school.

When the Newly introduced Orleans archdiocese filed its bankruptcy case, then archbishop Gregory Aymond estimated it would cost less than $7m to settle – including compensation for victims.

Yet the case was longer and more expensive than others after Louisiana’s legislature opened a window during which people in the state could seek civil damages for childhood sexual molestation no matter how long ago the abuse had occurred.

Legal and professional fees alone climbed above $50m. And the case became particularly contentious in 2022 when the archdiocese complained about survivors’ attorney Richard Trahant taking steps to have an admitted abuser who went largely unpunished removed from his role as chaplain of a local Catholic high school.

The judge overseeing the bankruptcy imposed a $400,000 fine on Trahant, saying his actions pertaining to the removed chaplain – Paul Hart – amounted to a violation of an order aiming to keep certain information in the case confidential. Trahant unsuccessfully appealed the fine all the way up to the US supreme court.

Then, after Aymond retired in February, his successor archbishop, James Checchio, reopened an internal investigation of all clergy accused of abuse in the bankruptcy.

That process recently resulted in the late Hart being added to a list of clergy that the church considers to be credibly accused of clerical abuse.
