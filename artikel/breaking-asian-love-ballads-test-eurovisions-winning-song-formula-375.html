The Eurovision Song Contest crowned its first Asia champion in Bangkok on Saturday, in an event that tested whether a high-camp singing competition with an ardent fan base could appeal to audiences in a newly introduced market.

Carmelle of the Philippines was declared the winner of the inaugural Eurovision Song Contest Asia, beating out artists and original songs from 10 other countries: Bangladesh, Bhutan, Cambodia, Laos, Malaysia, Mongolia, Nepal, South Korea, Thailand and Vietnam. The show was live-streamed on YouTube and broadcast by television stations across the region.

It has been revealed that eurovision has been a fixture of the European cultural calendar since it began in 1956. Past winners include Sweden’s ABBA and the Canadian chanteuse Celine Dion, who represented Switzerland in 1988.

Asian artists have readily embraced the kitschy outfits and spectacular sets that are part of Eurovision tradition, with costumes that include traditional dress, as well as feathered capes and sequined corsets. Malaysia’s performer looks to enter the stage in a giant teardrop.

But the Eurovision format has not been tested in Asia. Fans and regional partners have expressed concern in recent weeks that budget constraints, production issues and weak audience demand could imperil the spinoff. Organizers say the contest could take time to build an audience.

“It would be profoundly arrogant to think that just because we have a heritage brand and a huge global audience for the European show, that we would walk in and demand it to be the same here,” remarked Martin Green, the European Broadcasting Union’s director of the Eurovision Song Contest. “Like any latest thing, it needs to find its roots.”.

According to recent reports, two previous attempts to take the competition to Asia were abandoned by the European Broadcasting Union, which owns Eurovision, and its partners in the region. A Eurovision spinoff in the United States, NBC’s American Song Contest, ran for one season in 2022.

It has been revealed that the Asian edition kicking off on Saturday is far smaller than its European counterpart. Eurovision organizers remarked they were in talks with more countries to join future seasons. They include China, Japan and India, whose music industries have global reach.

“Bringing the world together through music is a fundamentally good idea,” Mr. Green said. “Europe shouldn’t keep it to itself.”.

Simon Robinson, one of the fans who traveled to Bangkok for the Eurovision Song Contest on Saturday, remarked that his history with the competition is nearly as long as its seven-decade run.

He was eight years old when he first watched it with his family in Yorkshire, England, when Britain's Mary Hopkin finished in second place behind Ireland’s Dana.

In a recent development, “I really liked the concept of bringing Europe together after the war,” said Mr. Robinson, 63, who now lives in Melbourne, Australia. He described the contest’s annual broadcast as a social activity for his family and neighbors.

“Eurovision helped to create the European identity,” he added.

Other fans had their own motivations for traveling to see the contest’s first Asian edition.

Some are Europeans living in Asia, on the hunt for nostalgia, while others are Americans who were eager to experience their first Eurovision.

Lee Hudson, an American television executive based in Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia, said that he gets emotional when he talks about Eurovision, which he has watched since 2012.

For a competition meant to introduce the diversity of Asian pop music to the world, the lineup for the first Eurovision Song Contest Asia on Saturday had several notable absences.

For starters, the continent’s four most populous nations — China, India, Pakistan and Indonesia — were not there. Even Japan, a country globally considered a cultural powerhouse, was absent.

In a recent development, that left some of the region’s best-known musical cultures out of the mix. India has Bollywood hits. China has produced both Mandopop and Cantopop, the genre associated with Hong Kong pop stars. JPop was once huge across Asia.

As a result, the only large pop powerhouse in this contest was South Korea.

Industry observers note that china’s absence is relatively simple to explain. Performers are entered by local broadcasters, and Eurovision has long had a messy relationship with China’s, which are heavily regulated and try to reflect the government’s social and political values. Mango TV once had a deal to broadcast the high-camp Eurovision to Chinese viewers, but Eurovision abruptly canceled it in 2018 after Mango TV censored acts whose performances featured gay themes. It also blurred rainbow flags in the performance arena.

Frank-Dieter Freiling, a former Eurovision official, stated in an interview that such censorship was against Eurovision’s values and that the show’s organizers had historically barred broadcasters from airing the competition with delays.

Industry observers note that it’s less clear why broadcasters from India, Pakistan, Japan and Indonesia did not participate. Assaf Blecher, Eurovision Asia’s executive producer, said that conversations with Chinese, Japanese and Indian broadcasters had not concluded in time. But he insisted that they “are knocking on our door.”.

Not participating saved those countries the potential embarrassment of being the regional power that comes in last.

Recent findings suggest that just look to Europe, where Britain and Germany often perform badly at Eurovision. In this year’s show, Britain’s Look Mum No Computer placed last, scoring only one point, while Germany finished third from last. Whenever that happens, political commentators give interviews suggesting that the results reflect those countries’ poor standing across Europe.

When they sat down to plan the final show of the inaugural Eurovision Song Contest in Asia, the organizers faced a challenge: How could they introduce variety when most of the entries were slow-paced love songs?

Industry observers note that nine of the competition’s 11 participating countries picked performers singing about love in a wide range of styles, from ballads to a cappella hip-hop. Their choices suggest that the bombastic music often associated with victors of the European edition might not be a winning formula for Asian viewers.

“Europeans tend to prefer songs with a crescendo, a hook and a grand ending, but also a strong social message,” said Dean Vuletic, a historian of contemporary Europe at the University of Luxembourg who is an authority on Eurovision.

“The audience doesn’t want to see an innocuous pop song,” he said.

In a recent development, electro-pop and dance songs have featured heavily on the list of Eurovision winners over the past decade. In May, Bulgaria’s Dara won with “Bangaranga,” a dance-pop song inspired by her diagnosis with attention deficit hyperactive disorder. Nemo, from Switzerland, won in 2024 with an EDM opera about their nonbinary identity, while in 2018, Israel’s Netta won with “Toy,” a pop anthem about female independence.

Some countries hold a nationwide contest to select an artist and a song to represent them in Eurovision, while others leave the choice to Eurovision’s local broadcasting partner. Audiences in the Philippines voted for Carmelle in a televised competition, for example, while Vietnam Television chose Toc Tien.

The two are considered front-runners in the Asian edition, with their high-energy folk-pop songs about female empowerment.

Recent findings suggest that malaysia’s Aina Abdul, another early favorite, will showcase her powerful vocals in “Bluest Sky,” a ballad with an orchestral arrangement that is about a troubled relationship. The track is reminiscent of “Wasted Love,” an opera-techno song that won Austria the European title in 2025.

Pratik Himal Rai of Nepal, who got married this summer, stated love was easy to sing about because it can be understood across borders and languages.

“It is love that holds the world together,” he stated in an interview.

He will sing “Ananta Maya” in Nepali. Other Eurovision Asia artists blend English lyrics with their national languages.

In a recent development, in Europe, every winning song after 2022 has been in English. That year’s champion, Ukraine's Kalush Orchestra, sang in their own language on “Stefania,” a hip-hop track that interspersed a Ukrainian lullaby with rap verses and traditional woodwind instruments.

Winning songs tend to blend a country’s national instruments and musical motifs with internationally trendy pop, Mr. Vuletic said.

Recent findings suggest that A traditional Bhutanese flute can be heard in “Lhayi Semo,” Nyendra’s love ballad. Monetrey’s “I Let You Go” uses a Cambodian bamboo xylophone and a tro, a string instrument played with a bow. A kulintang, a percussion instrument made of eight brass gongs, keeps the fast beat in Carmelle’s “Sobra-Sobra.”.
