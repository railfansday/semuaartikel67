According to recent reports, A World for All, edited by Matt Duss (who previously served as a foreign-policy advisor to Sanders), bills itself as a “progressive blueprint” for U.S. foreign policy. It consists of 18 chapters, all written by different authors from the academic and think tank community covering a wide range of foreign-policy topics. At a time when a small but vocal group of Moscow apologists and Hamas enthusiasts has tried to put their stamp on the movement, this book shows that many thoughtful and principled voices still dominate progressive foreign-policy thinking. And as interest grows in the Democratic agenda after the midterms’ blue wave, it also serves as a welcome reminder of how much the entire party has in common when it comes to foreign policy.

Industry observers note that the greatest continuity appears in the case of Russia. Here, Michael Kimmage, a Cold War historian, suggests four pillars for U.S. policy:.

[M]aintaining and expanding meaningful channels of communication with Russia geared toward desired de-confliction and the avoidance of escalatory spirals; aiding Ukraine and containing Russian military power in Europe; monitoring Russia’s global activities and resisting them in a restrained and targeted, not reflexive or messianic fashion; and keeping an open mind as to Russia’s future.

It is hard to imagine most Biden officials finding anything here to disagree with. In fact, they might only object to the extent that this framing implies Biden himself was being reflexive, messianic, or close minded about Russia’s future.

The reason for this congruity, of course, is that the biggest obstacle to a cooperative relationship with Russia is Russia itself. As Kimmage writes, Washington “has been unable to find an enduring partnership, and it has been repeatedly surprised by aggressive Russian acts, from the invasion of Georgia in 2008 to the massive invasion of Ukraine in 2022.”.

Bush initially sought to work with Russian President Vladimir Putin in the so-called war on terror, or that Obama came into office in search of his famous “reset” with Russia. But because of Russia’s actions, even progressives are now considerably more pessimistic about the possibility of a better relationship with Moscow than both their Republican and Democratic predecessors.

Industry observers note that in the case of China, think-tankers Jake Werner and Sandy Shan offer a more pointed contrast with Biden’s approach. With the aim of building a more cooperative relationship with Beijing, they call for the next administration to “[s]et a course to ending economic warfare against China” and normalizing “the presence of China in the US economy.” The two powers, they argue, must work together to “reform global economic governance” and “advance global climate action.”.

Call them the restrainers, the riders, and the racers.

The difference in their recommendations appears less in the stated goals than in what compromises a latest president might make to achieve them. In responding to previous progressive critiques along the same lines, several former Biden officials have insisted that they too sought a cooperative relationship, but the problem came from Beijing. For example, Thomas Wright, who was on Biden’s National Security Council, argued in the Atlantic in July:.

Recent findings suggest that biden did seek to cooperate with China on issues of mutual concern, such as AI. But he insisted that this had to be done without preconditions. That’s because those of us who worked on these policies inside the administration knew the Chinese playbook well. Beijing had little interest in cooperating on climate or anything else. It would stall by demanding that the U.S. make a concession on some matter of strategic importance, such as Taiwan and export controls.

Recent findings suggest that while export controls or other economic measures might not be a concern for Werner and Shan, it is harder to tell how they think progressives should respond to demands for concessions on issues such as Taiwan. Here A World for All seeks to telegraph a different approach without necessarily spelling out the details.

Werner and Shan write that Chinese aggression against Taiwan would be a “cataclysm.” They argue that U.S. “measures to deter Chinese adventurism should therefore continue” but also be “balanced by stronger and more sincere efforts to reas­sure China that the United States is not pursuing permanent sepa­ration.” That is the sole reference to deterring Beijing in the book—which, read alongside repeated criticisms of U.S. militarism and the size of the Pentagon budget, certainly suggests a greatly reduced willingness to go to war in defense of Taiwan. And yet at face value, that line inevitably implies some commitment to using force. The result is nothing if not strategic ambiguity.

According to recent reports, needless to say, it is in the Middle East that the break with traditional Democratic foreign policy is clearest. The Biden administration’s consistent support for Israel’s genocidal policies in Gaza produced the starkest and most emotional division in the party. In the book, analysts Thanassis Cambanis and Zaha Hassan call for Washington to “end blank-check military support for Israel,” which increasingly seems like a demand that Democrats will make of any candidate in 2028.

Elsewhere in this section, though, their recommendations differ from past Democratic administrations more in degree than kind. They call for more diplomacy and fewer reckless wars, as well as supporting human rights over friendly autocrats. More pointedly, they call for reducing Washington’s military footprint in the region to “discourage unreliable partners and allies from starting wars they believe the US will finish for them.”.

Recent findings suggest that rhetorically, at least, these are all ambitions Obama and Biden shared. Obama ran on his opposition to the Iraq War, withdrew U.S. troops from Iraq, and resisted strong pressure to intervene in Syria. He also offered conditional support for the Arab Spring and negotiated a nuclear agreement with Iran, which he hoped would facilitate a “pivot to Asia.” Biden, for his part, campaigned on the promise to restore the Iran deal and make Saudi Arabia “a pariah” for its human rights abuses.

Both men, however, faced constraints in achieving these ambitions. Obama could have done more to push back against the military coup that ended any hope of a democratic transition in Egypt, but this was unlikely to change the ultimate outcome. In a similar vein, Obama was forced to send U.S. troops back to Iraq when the Islamic State upended the region, while Biden ultimately concluded that a fist bump with Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman was the price to pay for the cooperation on oil prices he needed to keep his domestic agenda on track.

A more progressive administration is likely to face some of the same contradictions as their predecessors, even if they ultimately resolve them differently. Indeed, the challenge in evaluating this genre of policy writing, progressive or otherwise, is that, in the abstract, everyone wants their strategy to do everything. Whether you propose competing with rival powers while seeking areas of cooperation where possible or, alternatively, pursuing cooperation while competing where necessary, the real differences only emerge in the way policymakers negotiate the inevitable tradeoffs.

Of course, a constructive conversation about the tradeoffs Biden faced would be easier if members of his administration were more candid about where they fell short. So long as former officials continue to defend their record on Gaza, or proffer weak, equivocal, and mealy mouthed apologies, they will only make it harder to draw useful lessons from his tenure in other areas.

More broadly, A World for All suggests that progressives, no less than other Democrats, are still struggling with two enduring tensions that will plague any future administration.

First, it is clear to everyone in the party that the health of U.S. democracy is intertwined with the fate of democracy worldwide. Yet the last few decades have also made it clear how limited Washington’s tools for democracy promotion can be. One approach that several authors highlight is a renewed focus on fighting corruption at home and abroad.

In a recent development, progressives have led the way in insisting that authoritarianism and corruption are inextricably intertwined. A war against kleptocracy would be smart policy and smart politics, and it would certainly represent a concrete step toward making the world safe for democracy. Yet for the same reason, its impact would be limited so long as many of the countries the United States deals with remain authoritarian. Deploying Magnitsky sanctions against corrupt leaders implicated in bribing Americans or facilitating transnational repression is, in itself, an excellent idea. But it is also hard to imagine rebuilding ties with a upcoming Russian government or pursuing a more cooperative relationship with China while simultaneously sanctioning some of their top officials.

In a recent development, the bigger contradiction is that in the very areas where both the traditional Democratic and progressive approaches to foreign policy differ from those of many Republicans—that is, diplomacy, cooperation, and multilateralism—they are uniquely susceptible to the kind of disruption Donald Trump has created. These all depend on trust and continuity, which is exactly what no one should expect from U.S. foreign policy in the coming decades. Among the many challenges Biden faced in restoring the Iran nuclear deal, for example, was Tehran’s demand for some reassurance that Trump would not simply tear it up again if he was reelected. This, of course, was something Biden could not provide and, as events proved, something that Iran was wise to ask for.

In their chapter on Europe, analysts Rachel Rizzo and Max Bergmann offer one clear-eyed example of what confronting this reality could look like. They argue that, rather than simply try to restore the traditional transatlantic relationship rooted in a strong U.S. commitment to NATO, the next administration should support Europe’s efforts to become “a stronger and more assertive global actor” with more independent defense capabilities.

Can Washington hope to find actors abroad who will support U.S. values more reliably than the United States itself? It’s hardly an ideal solution. But then, if Democrats win the presidency in 2028, they will hardly be inheriting an ideal foreign policy situation.
