Character.AI’s plan to auction off users’ private chats challenged by state AGs

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that what happens to your private conversations when an AI firm goes bankrupt? If it’s Character.ai, apparently, the initiative is to auction them off to the highest bidder.

Now, a coalition of 42 state attorneys general is intervening in Character.ai’s bankruptcy proceedings in an effort to stop the chatbot maker from selling off sensitive user data, which includes the private conversations of millions of people.

Industry observers note that the attorneys general involved in the matter have put out individual press releases, claiming that Character.ai’s dataset includes chat histories from more than 21 million U.S. accounts, more than 2.1 million of which belong to minors. In addition, the sale would involve other personal data, like IP addresses, the statements stated.

Sources indicate that only one anonymous bidder for the chat data has been disclosed so far: Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC (Smirks), which was set up as a Delaware company on November 13, 2026. The following day, Character.ai picked it as the base bid for the auction, scheduled for December 1, California Attorney General Rob Bonta noted. It is unclear what the buyer’s plans are for the chats.

In addition, some states, including California, Colorado, Newly introduced York, and Connecticut, have enacted chatbot safety legislation that will go into effect on January 1, 2027, which could trigger further violations if the sale goes through.

Sources indicate that “Character.AI promised consumers that their data would not be sold. This kind of behavior is illegal under federal and California law and bankruptcy should not be used to sidestep these consumer protections,” Bonta said in a statement.

Connecticut Attorney General William Tong also criticized Character.ai’s business model.

“Their chatbots drove vulnerable youth down a rabbit hole of emotionally volatile and sexually inappropriate chats,” Tong said. “Their reckless misconduct and broken business model drove their manufacturer into the ground. Now they want to abuse the bankruptcy process to do exactly what they said they wouldn’t do — auction off those private chat records and personal details to a shady buyer.”.

Tong added: “We objected to the Disney sale, and we’re calling on the bankruptcy court to reject this sale too as yet another guise to eviscerate legal liability and consumer protection.”.

Industry observers note that the opposition to the Disney sale was led by a similar but not identical coalition of state AGs. They fought against a proposed $20 million licensing deal that would give Disney access to Character.ai’s intellectual property, but not to owners’ chats. The deal has not yet received final court approval. (Disney also agreed to hire most of Character.ai’s remaining staff.).

Industry observers note that character.ai suffered after its co-founders, Noam Shazeer and Daniel De Freitas, returned to Google, when the tech giant signed a non-exclusive license to use the AI startup’s tech. The company also faced lawsuits related to child safety — one involved the suicide of a 14-year-old Florida boy whose mother claimed he had become obsessed with a chatbot on the platform.

California AG Bonta is joined in the effort to oppose the sale of Character.ai user data by attorneys general from Alaska, Arkansas, Colorado, Connecticut, the District of Columbia, Delaware, Florida, Georgia, Hawaii, Idaho, Illinois, Indiana, Kansas, Kentucky, Louisiana, Maine, Maryland, Michigan, Minnesota, Mississippi, Missouri, Nevada, Newly introduced Hampshire, New Jersey, New York, North Carolina, North Dakota, Ohio, Oklahoma, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, South Carolina, South Dakota, Tennessee, Utah, Virginia, Vermont, Washington, Wisconsin, and West Virginia.
