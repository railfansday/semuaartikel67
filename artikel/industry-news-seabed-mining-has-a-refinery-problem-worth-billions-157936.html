Industry News: Seabed Mining Has a Refinery Problem Worth Billions

🌐 Source: Cleantechnica • 📅 26 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 5 min read (1025 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways

  • In a recent development, that gap belongs in the mining economics.
  • Offshore collection gets the attention.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that the Metals Manufacturer wants to recover polymetallic nodules from the seabed and sell the nickel, copper, cobalt and manganese they contain. That business needs an industrial processing system before its revenue projections become sales. TMC’s November 2026 update exposed the timing problem. Its proposed refinery complex in Brownsville, Texas, may not be operating when offshore collection begins. An established Japanese smelter operated by Pacific Metals, or PAMCO, is the prospective initial route, but it needs modifications to accept commercial volumes of nodules.

In a recent development, that gap belongs in the mining economics. TMC’s 2025 prefeasibility study allocated $4.426 billion to two proposed US hydrometallurgical refineries, about 89% of the study’s $4.971 billion of initial project capital. The denominator has a particular financing structure: the offshore amount covers the first collection system after an Allseas capital credit, while later systems are to be supplied through contracts and paid for through operating costs. The newer Brownsville proposal also has a different industrial scope. The 2025 figure reveals the size of the processing business TMC’s economics must support, not a completed financing commitment for Brownsville.

Offshore collection gets the attention. Refineries decide what saleable material a collection system produces and who captures its value. A nodule delivered to port is a complex mineral feedstock, not a sale of four finished metals. One processing route smelts nodules into a nickel-copper-cobalt alloy and manganese silicate, converts the alloy into matte and refines the matte into marketable metals or chemicals. The manganese product needs customers or additional treatment. Each step consumes capital, energy, reagents, skilled labor and working capital.

Sources indicate that TMC has a meaningful metallurgical program. Its third-quarter 2026 SEC filing describes earlier North American tests of about 70 tons of nodules and a 2,000-ton industrial demonstration at PAMCO’s Hachinohe facility. Further work at SGS in Canada tested the refining of intermediate material into battery-grade products. PAMCO has completed feasibility work on treating roughly 1.3 million wet tons per year through a modified existing facility. That is a stronger starting point than a conceptual processing flowsheet.

Industry observers note that the November 12 corporate upgrade nevertheless put the industrial dependency in plain view. TMC’s preferred Brownsville facility will be difficult to complete before the first commercial nodules arrive, and the Japanese facility requires modifications and aligned schedules. Korea Zinc, Glencore, Hatch, Mariana Minerals and other partners provide useful expertise and commercial relationships. Those relationships still leave the construction, commissioning, financing and refining arrangements to complete.

The value gap matters as much as the capital bill. In our 2025 seabed mining techno-economic assessment, metallurgist Lyle Trytten and I calculated a $553-per-ton contained-component reference value for representative Clarion-Clipperton Zone nodules at fixed historical metal prices. Using estimated purchase terms informed by analogous mineral markets, the same assessment gave a modeled raw-feed value of $188–256 per ton, or about 34–46% of that contained reference value.

It has been revealed that the gap pays for real industrial work. Processors bear metal recovery losses, treatment costs, plant capital, financing, product quality and operating risk, and they need a return. The values are estimates on a common reference basis, not bids from refineries or the profit a processor would pocket. Commercial nodule tonnage is often reported wet; TMC’s study assumes 28% moisture. Feed-price comparisons with wet-ton mine costs require the same mass basis.

It has been revealed that the mining company has three main choices. It can sell the raw nodules at a price a processor can afford. It can keep title to the material and pay a plant to process it, provided the contract sets capacity, treatment charges, recoveries and risk allocation. Or it can finance and operate refining facilities and pursue a larger portion of the finished-metal revenue. None transfers the work, capital and risk out of the industrial system.

Sources indicate that TMC’s own operating estimates reinforce that point. The 2025 study projected $13.622 billion of lifetime processing costs and $3.254 billion of refining costs, together about $103 per wet ton, or 42% of its modeled operating expenditure. Those costs sit alongside the proposed refinery capital. The production system may be offshore, but the economic center of gravity reaches well into the onshore industrial chain.

It has been revealed that impossible Metals has a different, less developed processing position. Its published strategy begins with outsourcing to modified plants in allied countries and envisions dedicated facilities later. Its economic model used approximately $791 per ton of contained-metal value under its selected prices and grades, deducted a $230 processing charge, and arrived at roughly $560 per ton delivered to port. Those numbers use different input assumptions from our assessment and cannot be treated as a direct comparison with the $188–256 estimate. The commercial question is whether a processor would accept the feedstock and the risks on the terms in Impossible Metals’ model.

In a recent development, existing nickel-processing projects offer warning enough. Ravensthorpe, Goro and Ambatovy had familiar metallurgical technologies yet encountered expensive commissioning and operational difficulties. An unusual feedstock, high-volume throughput, uncertain manganese outlets and synchronized offshore and onshore ramp-up add challenges for nodule ventures. Demonstrating that the chemistry works settles only one part of the investment case.

According to recent reports, TMC has considerably more testing and more developed partners than Impossible Metals. That distinction matters. Neither has demonstrated a fully operating processing chain that delivers the required metals at its intended scale, recovery, capital cost and commercial terms. Government financing could change who carries part of the risk, but not what must be built or operated.

In a recent development, A seabed mining company cannot book finished-metal economics while making the refinery somebody else’s problem. Investors, taxpayers and prospective processing partners need to know who provides the billions in capital, who carries the ramp-up risk and how much of the metal value the collector retains after every participant has been paid.
