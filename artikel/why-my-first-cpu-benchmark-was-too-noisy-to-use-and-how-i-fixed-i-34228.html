Why my first CPU benchmark was too noisy to use, and how I fixed it

My first benchmark numbers looked reasonable, until I ran the same thing twice and got different answers. This post is about how I found out why, and what I changed before trusting any number.

All numbers come from the repo's README (including its "First attempt (archived)" section) and results/archive_summary.json in cpu-inference-bench.

I started by running ResNet-18 on PyTorch, ONNX Runtime and OpenVINO with default settings on a laptop CPU (Intel i7-12650H). To check repeatability I ran each configuration twice. In 12 of 18 repeated rows, the two identical runs differed by more than 5%, up to 48.27% for one row (PyTorch, 12 threads, batch 1). The results were not usable as a comparison, so I archived them.

In a recent development, A laptop has package power and thermal limits, so the clock changes during a run. Without control over that, a difference of a few percent between frameworks would be within the noise.

Sources indicate that the first attempt used default frequency scaling, so the clock was free to move. This CPU has a sustained package power limit of 45 W and a short-term limit of 115 W, and power and thermal limits like these can change the clock during a run. Background services and other processes sharing the cores can add noise as well.

In a recent development, the data from that attempt is archived locally and is not in the public repo, so I can't show its per-run frequencies or temperatures here. Instead of guessing which factor dominated, I changed the setup so that each one is either controlled or visible: every repeat now records the package temperature, the mean and minimum P-core frequency, and the kernel's thermal-throttle counters.

According to recent reports, I capped the P-cores at 3000 MHz and ran a small pilot. Two of four rows still raised the kernel's thermal-throttle counters, so I chose 2600 MHz as a clock the cores could sustain.

In a recent development, speedup ratios are printed only between two stable rows. Unstable rows are shown and marked, not dropped.

With these controls, ResNet-18 INT8 vs FP32 at batch 1 came out at 2.34× (PyTorch), 2.74× (ONNX Runtime) and 3.48× (OpenVINO, 2.6 ms). Five of 36 BERT rows and 2 of 18 ResNet rows (both FP32) were still unstable, and the README states so.

Thermal counters do not detect power-limit throttling, so I also checked the frequency columns. A VPN client was running during all runs and I did not measure its effect. This is one laptop and one campaign.

The code was written with Claude Code; the methodology, decisions and verification are mine.
