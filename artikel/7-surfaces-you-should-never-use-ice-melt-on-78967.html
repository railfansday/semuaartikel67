Avoid damaging salt-sensitive outdoor surfaces with these expert-backed suggestions.

Ice melt salt is an effective option for preventing and removing ice from sidewalks, driveways and stairs, but it isn’t suitable for every surface. Applying ice melt to certain materials can cause significant damage and should be avoided. Ahead, learn which surfaces are most vulnerable to damage from ice melt, why it happens and what safer alternatives you can use to keep outdoor areas free from ice.

Ice melt salt chemically reacts with certain materials, accelerates freeze-thaw cycles and alters surface moisture levels to cause damage. The type of damage will depend on the surface and how it interacts with ice melt.

Recent findings suggest that ice melt contains rock salt, calcium chloride and/or magnesium chloride to melt ice faster and at lower temperatures. While all melting salt is corrosive and can damage outdoor surfaces, ice melt products can react differently than rock salt alone.

Recent findings suggest that ice melt seeps deeply into porous cement-based materials. The calcium chloride in ice melt can react with elements of concrete or brick to form calcium oxychloride crystals. As the crystals grow, they generate extreme pressure that can cause cracks, surface spalling and crumbling.

De-icing products accelerate freeze-thaw cycles in porous cement-based surfaces, causing them to break down. “It makes the freezing and thawing cycle more aggressive, which is what causes the surface to break up,” Murphy indicates.

Though less damaging than rock salt, ice melt can still contribute to deterioration. Calcium chloride and magnesium chloride attract and retain moisture, increasing the potential for freeze-thaw damage.

According to recent reports, conversely, ice melt draws moisture out of organic materials, causing them to dry out. Over time, this moisture loss can lead to warping, cracking, splitting and other forms of deterioration.

Recent findings suggest that ice melt accelerates metal oxidation to create rust. It can corrode rebar embedded in concrete, potentially causing cracks and structural damage, as well as rusting exposed metal stairs and railings.

It’s best to avoid applying salt to these salt-sensitive surfaces.

“Fresh concrete is the most vulnerable because it takes weeks to fully cure, so salt on the surface will cause the top few millimeters to flake off,” Murphy indicates. To be safe, avoid salting new concrete for the first season.

“Cracked concrete opens up for water to get inside, and salt in the water will only make that worse because it will freeze and thaw more aggressively,” Murphy says.

In a recent development, all ice melt products will damage hard-scape surfaces due to chemical reactions and freeze-thaw cycles. “If you are using them, use them sparingly and remove them as soon as possible to decrease continuous exposure for your hardscape areas,” Zurcher says.

Natural stone like limestone and flagstone can react to ice melt compounds. The porous surface is vulnerable to damaging freeze-thaw cycles as well.

Harsh de-icers can damage dyed, painted or stamped concrete surfaces by breaking down color pigments.

Metal stairs and railings will rapidly rust when salt is applied to them, so it’s best to avoid it.

Ice melt draws moisture from wood, causing cracking, warping and raised fibers. It also degrades paint and stain and leaves behind chalky white salt stains.

Sand: Sand doesn’t melt ice, but does provide traction to prevent slips.

Wood ash: Instead of throwing away wood ash, use it as a salt alternative to melt ice and also provide improved traction.

Electric mats: Installed before cold weather sets in, electric melting mats warm up and prevent ice from ever forming. These can be very effective on stairs and sidewalks.

Weed torch: Weed torches are handy when you need to melt a lot of ice rapidly. Do not use one to melt ice on wood and avoid painted or stained concrete as well.

It has been revealed that “The best way to reduce damage from these chemicals is to shovel snow as soon as possible,” Zurcher says, so you can use less of them. Use ice melt sparingly, and only as directed. A handful is enough to treat three square yards. Instead of using ice melt alone, mix a small amount with sand to treat surfaces.

Recent findings suggest that regular sealing protects cement-based products. “Reseal every three to five years to make sure no water can get in, salty or not,” Murphy indicates. Additionally, use chloride-free de-icing products, or those labelled as “concrete-safe”.

Newly poured concrete is the most vulnerable to salt damage, but all concrete surfaces will experience damage with enough prolonged exposure to salt.

Recent findings suggest that it depends on the extent of the damage. Major cracks and breakage mean concrete needs to be removed and repoured, Zurcher indicates, while surface damage could be skim-coated, with potentially limited results.

Pet-safe products will have similar effects on hardscape areas, Zurcher indicates, as they still contain salt.
