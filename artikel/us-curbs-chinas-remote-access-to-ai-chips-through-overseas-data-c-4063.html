US curbs China’s remote access to AI chips through overseas data centres

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, the US Commerce Department on Monday moved to stop Chinese companies from renting American AI chips through data centres in other countries. Its Bureau of Industry and Security issued an interim final rule on remote access to advanced chips and servers. The bureau remarked the rule was “written by and at the direction of” the Treasury Department and the National Security Council.

In a recent development, the rule has two main parts, according to its text. First, suppliers now need a licence for any data centre, anywhere, that serves companies headquartered in China, Macau or the other countries in the US’s D:5 group. The threshold is more than 12 million units of “aggregate TPP”, a measure of computing power. Data centres already serving such customers have 90 days from the rule’s start to get a licence.

It has been revealed that second, exporters now need a licence to ship advanced chips to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil. The rule calls them countries with “a higher-than-normal risk of diversion”. Companies headquartered in the US are exempt, as long as they file monthly reports.

Industry observers note that recommendation systems used by online shops and social media companies are also exempt. The rule takes effect once it appears in the Federal Register, and the public then has 60 days to comment.

Industry observers note that the rule also tightens checks on chip foundries and packagers that make advanced chips for designers in the countries now facing a licence. It formally scraps the Biden administration’s AI Diffusion Rule. The government never enforced that rule, and the bureau said in May 2025 that it would rescind it.

Commerce said it would watch for countries that tolerate evasion. Possible penalties include import restrictions, fresh licence rules and Entity List designations, according to the rule.

In a recent development, the move follows Jensen Huang’s resignation as Nvidia CEO last week, after he admitted an affair with Alice Huang. She is a former executive of Megaspeed, a Singapore manufacturer that US officials suspect of passing Nvidia chips to China. Megaspeed has denied wrongdoing. In June, Commerce had already moved against Chinese firms’ overseas units buying the chips directly.

Nvidia shares fell as much as 2.9% on Monday. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index dropped as much as 5.5%, Maggie Eastland and Magan Crane reported for Bloomberg.

Recent findings suggest that in a post on X, Chris McGuire of the Council on Foreign Relations called the rule “a major step forward”, CNBC reported. He noted loopholes remain, including some covering activity inside the US.

“I have never seen a BIS rule that was not written by BIS,” McGuire wrote.

Analysts told Fortune that the rule could hit Southeast Asian countries that want to host data centres for Chinese firms.

“For Malaysia, the controls could be disastrous,” Joshua Kurlantzick, a senior fellow at the Council on Foreign Relations, told Fortune.

According to recent reports, on Monday, before leaving for Asia, Donald Trump said he had no intends to replace Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, Bloomberg reported. Lutnick faces criticism from lawmakers and Treasury over enforcement. Trump meets Xi Jinping on Thursday in Shenzhen.
