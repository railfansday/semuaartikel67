How could something so small stress one person out while something more serious barely affects another? For some people, a messy home is stressful. For others, waiting in line at the bank, being around screaming kids, or Christmas shopping. Stress depends not only on what happens, but on what we think it means, and whether we believe we have the resources to deal with it.

Or, according to an acquaintance of mine, pretty much every day at the office.

Lazarus and Folkman’s transactional model suggests that our different reactions to stressors may come down to primary and secondary appraisal. Primary appraisal involves assessing whether an event matters to us and whether it represents harm, loss, threat, or challenge. Secondary appraisal involves assessing whether we have the resources and options to cope with it. An event can threaten anything that matters to us, be it our health, self-esteem, values, commitments, or goals.

The situation itself influences the appraisal process: how we interpret it, and the resources we believe we have.

According to recent reports, the situation itself: Two people might react very differently to the same situation, yet a closer look shows they aren't really in the same situation. Consider romantic breakups. One person may move on quickly, while another may struggle with the emotional consequences for years. What makes some breakups more stressful than others?

Research suggests that events tend to be more stressful when they are sudden, severe, ambiguous, uncontrollable, or long-lasting. For instance, a sudden, unexpected breakup may be different from a mutual breakup that follows a gradual deterioration in the relationship.

Our individual characteristics also influence how we perceive and interpret an event. Namely, personality factors such as higher levels of neuroticism and greater intolerance of uncertainty have been associated with greater stress and distress.

Our values, commitments, and goals also shape how we interpret an event.

Someone who has always imagined a future of marriage and children might experience a potential breakup as a threat not only to their immediate relationship needs, but also to their future and their sense of self. Another person whose identity and goals are strongly focused on career advancement might experience the breakup differently.

It has been revealed that there are different types of resources. Internal resources include skills, experience, physical and mental health, and a sense of agency and control. External resources include practical assistance, social support, money, access to information, and professional help.

Recent findings suggest that what matters for stress is not just whether these resources exist, but whether we perceive them as available and sufficient to help us cope. For example, someone may have supportive friends or family but not feel comfortable confiding in them (or assume they can't help much). Our appraisal of available resources can influence how threatening or manageable an event feels.

Therefore, a breakup may be more challenging for someone who is socially isolated, dealing with financial difficulties, has limited access to mental health services, or lacks confidence in their ability to cope effectively.

Your confidence in your ability to handle it matters; internal and external resources can influence this, including experience, knowledge, social support, health, practical resources, perceived control, and coping skills.

According to recent reports, one final point: comparing your stress response with someone else's can be misleading. We can observe how other people react to events, but we cannot know exactly how they perceive or interpret those events. Just because you experienced more stress than someone else does not mean you are less resilient.

Recent findings suggest that resilience is not the same as stress reactivity. It is about adapting successfully to adversity, not about feeling stress in the first place. A strong stress response is not a judgment on your character. It just says what happened really mattered to you.
