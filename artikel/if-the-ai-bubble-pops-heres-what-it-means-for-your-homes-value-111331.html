If the AI Bubble Pops, Here’s What It Means for Your Home’s Value

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

When artificial intelligence giant Anthropic went public last week, its $1.494 trillion valuation exceeded the combined value of every owner-occupied home in the San Francisco metropolitan area, according to research from Realtor.com®.

Less than a week later, the organization's shares fell below their IPO price, as a renewed sell-off in tech stocks stoked fears that the AI bubble may already be bursting.

“This is how the exuberance ends—not with a whimper but with a bang,” Nobel Prize-winning economist Paul Krugman wrote on Monday.

Krugman, who warned of America's housing bubble as early as 2005, acknowledges he's less certain about AI valuations than he was about home prices two decades ago. But earlier this month, he warned that an AI bust would almost certainly trigger a recession, potentially more painful than the one that followed the dot-com crash.

Others on Wall Street aren't so sure. Many investors remain confident in the industry's fundamentals and see the sell-off as a necessary correction rather than the end of the boom.

But after years of a bull market powered by a handful of AI darlings, newly introduced research from Realtor.com finds evidence that the resulting fortunes have helped shape some of America’s most expensive housing markets.

Now, a reversal of those gains could threaten a source of wealth far more central to the financial lives of middle-class Americans: their homes.

Anthropic's IPO alone was expected to mint around 2,800 millionaires among its current and former employees, according to estimates from private-market research firm Sacra reported by Reuters.

But Americans needn't work in AI or invest in individual tech stocks to have shared in the windfall. Between the launch of ChatGPT in November 2022 and Nov. 3 of this year, the S&P 500 gained nearly $32 trillion in market value—more than half of which came from just 10 AI-linked companies, according to Bloomberg.

In a recent development, about 62% of Americans own stocks, either directly or through investments such as retirement accounts, according to a 2025 Gallup poll. Yet the wealthiest 10% of households hold 88% of the country's household stocks and mutual fund assets.

Industry observers note that in the San Francisco Bay Area's AI hubs, some of that AI-powered stock wealth has translated into an estimated $61,180 in additional down payment on a typical home, according to research from Realtor.com senior economist Jiayi Xu. At the entry-level luxury tier, that premium reached nearly $200,000 in 2025.

The extra cash these buyers have brought to the market has allowed them to bid higher without taking on more debt, potentially displacing competitors into less expensive price brackets and driving up competition further down the market, Xu's earlier research suggests.

And so, what’s a barrier to entry for prospective buyers becomes a source of equity gains for existing homeowners—even those who have never invested in AI.

Annual home price growth now exceeds the national rate in all three markets where Xu identified signs of AI wealth bolstering down payments: Chicago (6.9%), Latest York City (5.8%), and San Francisco (3.7%), compared with 2.1% nationally, according to Tuesday's Case-Shiller release.

It’s an especially key point to make here because real estate wealth is more widely distributed than stock. Households between the 50th and 90th wealth percentiles hold nearly half of the country's household real estate assets, but just 11.4% of its stocks and mutual fund shares, according to second-quarter Federal Reserve data.

Homeowners in markets buoyed by AI wealth have, in effect, acquired a stake in the industry's stock market performance—whether or not they own any shares.

And the potential losses are enormous. To gauge their scale, Krugman looks to the so-called Buffett indicator, which measures the total value of U.S. stocks relative to the country's gross domestic product.

Before the recent sell-off, the ratio had climbed well above its peak during the dot-com bubble. AI-related companies accounted for nearly half of the S&P 500's market value and drove most of the increase in the indicator over the past several years, Krugman explains.

“If the Buffett indicator falls back to what it was in, say, 2019, that would wipe out wealth equal to around 80 percent of GDP: $25 trillion,” he wrote.

According to recent reports, and if his warning seems alarmist, consider what he wrote about the U.S. housing market in August 2005: “This is the way the bubble ends: not with a pop, but with a hiss.”.

More than two years before the onset of the Great Recession, Krugman identified falling sales and rising inventory as the early signs of a housing bust, as sellers struggled to command prices that buyers were no longer willing to pay.

Some of those symptoms are present today. In October, contract signings fell 17% annually while inventory grew 7.4%. Asking prices recorded their 12th consecutive month of annual declines, according to Realtor.com research.

Sources indicate that but the slowdown predates November's sell-off. Elevated mortgage rates and years of home price growth that outpaced wages have driven buyers to the sidelines, while many homeowners can afford to wait for better offers.

“On the seller side, we see more of a pause than an exodus,” Jake Krimmel, senior economist at Realtor.com, remarked earlier this month.

The question is whether an AI bust would produce another localized housing correction or a national downturn.

According to recent reports, “Generally speaking, the stock market and housing market can be connected, but usually not directly,” Krimmel explained amid an earlier bout of AI stock volatility in November 2025. “If they're moving together, that's typically because of some other outside force (could be driven by policy, Fed moves, and credit conditions, or a large macro shock) and/or because of changes in the job market.”.

To his point, when the dot-com bubble burst, the Nasdaq plunged more than 75% from its peak, wiping out trillions in paper wealth. Yet national home values continued to climb through the 2001 recession.

In San Francisco, however, home values fell 6.1% between February 2001 and January 2002, even as they rose 6% nationally, according to an analysis of seasonally adjusted Case-Shiller home price data.

The bigger question hanging over the housing market, though, is what an AI bust would mean for household spending.

According to recent reports, when households lose stock market wealth, they tend to spend less, even if their paychecks haven't changed—a phenomenon economists call the wealth effect. And just as stock market gains have held up demand for homes, they've also helped sustain demand elsewhere in the economy.

According to recent reports, any resulting pullback in spending, therefore, could end up rippling into layoffs in industries far removed from AI. Likewise, a slowdown in AI infrastructure investment could compound those losses, jeopardizing construction and manufacturing jobs in communities distant from Silicon Valley.

“Labor market softness is key for aggregate (and housing) demand,” Krimmel said last year.

And while the destruction of stock market wealth might initially dampen housing demand in a handful of affluent markets, rising unemployment could weaken it nationwide.

According to recent reports, during the dot-com bust, the Federal Reserve cut interest rates aggressively, helping sustain housing demand despite the economic downturn. Persistent inflation could complicate a similar response today.

“Lower interest rates will, in theory, help struggling companies' finances and allow them to keep existing workers, hire more, and/or provide pay raises to top performers,” Krimmel explained last November.

Recent findings suggest that access to credit could also become a problem. Tech companies are relying more heavily on corporate debt and private credit to finance their expansion, according to the European Central Bank's November Financial Stability Review. Losses on those investments could prompt lenders to tighten credit, making financing harder to obtain for homebuyers and builders alike.

The combination of falling incomes and more restrictive lending could depress home values in markets where AI wealth never drove them up in the first place.

But unlike during the Great Recession, a decline in home values would leave far fewer homeowners owing more than their properties are worth, limiting the risk of widespread foreclosures and the additional downward pressure on prices that forced sales can create.

In a recent development, “The typical homeowner today—and the housing market overall—is still well-positioned to withstand a correction without it turning into a crisis,” Krimmel said last November. “Home equity hit an all-time high in 2025Q2; mortgage holders are largely locked into sub-4% rates, giving them lower payments that are increasingly being eaten away by inflation. Very few homeowners are at risk of going underwater, even if prices were to decline 10% to 15%.”.

A year later, homeowner equity cushions remain substantial and mortgage delinquencies are low by historical standards, according to the Fed's November Financial Stability Report.

In a recent development, “Obviously, [a stock market crash] is not something anyone wants to see play out, and undoubtedly, if a stock market correction spilled over into the housing market, many homeowners would feel the pain,” Krimmel stated. “The silver lining of that hypothetical is that the knock-on effects would not be as severe. Luckily, the history of the housing bust and Great Recession would not repeat itself.”.

But for tens of millions of families, home equity is the college fund, the retirement plan, the safety net, and the most crucial source of wealth they'll ever build. The financial protections that could keep them in their homes during a downturn wouldn't necessarily protect that wealth.
