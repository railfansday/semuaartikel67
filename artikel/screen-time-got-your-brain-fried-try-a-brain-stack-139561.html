Screen time got your brain fried? Try a ‘brain stack’

🌐 Source: Fastcompany • 📅 25 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 6 min read (1110 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Every few months, like clockwork, a upcoming analog trend emerges in an effort to get us all off our phones. . .and of course, document the process for social media. The latest is “brain stacking”: keeping a stack of puzzle books, novels, non-fiction and other phone-free activities in a neat pile within arm’s reach, in lieu of doomscrolling. Online, thousands of people have proudly shared their “stacks”. One popular creator, Mandy Rulez, regularly shares timelapse videos of her brain stacks before work, starting her morning with nearby stacks of non-digital activities, like journaling, before moving on to a sudoku, a crossword, and finally a quick ten pages of reading. Other creators are also incorporating timers into the mix. For example, a 12-minute brain dump in a spiral notebook, followed by ten minutes of planning, 15 minutes of puzzles, 20 minutes of coloring. Total brain stack: one hour. Brain stacking is a continuation of the “analog bag” trend earlier this year (otherwise known as, well, simply “doing things.”) But we’re in an era when “brain rot” and “doomscrolling” have become part of our everyday lexicon, so it’s easy to see the appeal. Gen Z, after all, are the ones embracing dumb phones and buying up Bricks in an effort to reduce their screen time. The idea of “habit stacking” has existed in the world of self-help for years, and involves stacking new habits to existing ones. More recently, bedtime stacking, as well as peptide stacking, have emerged as online trends. Then there’s goal stacking and income stacking. Clearly, queuing up back-to-back activities (and, ideally, literally and physically stacking them in a pile to work through) resonates with people on social media. In a year where “maxxing” was crowned Dictionary.com’s word of the year, it’s unsurprising that a single activity alone is not enough—instead, we must stack them one on top of each other to reach our max potential. (There is also the visually pleasing element of the stack when it comes to posting online.)  That does not mean there’s anything inherently wrong with brain stacking. Having a range of activities close at hand means you are far more likely to be intentional with your spare time. And a 2024 study found just five minutes of fiction reading can reduce stress by up to 20%, as well as sharpening concentration and problem-solving skills. Reading is also associated with a 33% lower risk of Alzheimer’s disease. But recent findings from a collaboration among researchers at Clemson University, the Medical University of South Carolina, and Penn State found that “brain-training” activities, such as crosswords, sudoku, memory games and adult coloring books, are not equally effective for everyone. College students, for example, often show only modest benefits from such brain-training activities compared with older adults who live relatively sedentary lifestyles. There’s also no specific research that supports the brain stacking method of working your way through a queue of a certain number of tasks, or spending a specific amount of time on each. If you find yourself absorbed in a book, or reach a flow state in your writing, don’t feel bound to 10-minute sprints and then moving onto the next brain-boosting activity. That mentioned? Any amount of time away from your screens is time well spent.
