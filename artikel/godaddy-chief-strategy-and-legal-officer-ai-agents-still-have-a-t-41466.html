GoDaddy chief strategy and legal officer: AI agents still have a trust problem. The solution is waiting

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that the age of AI assistants is almost here. Soon, we’ll delegate tasks like comparing flight prices or managing routine household purchases to agents acting on our behalf.

Recent findings suggest that that convenience could transform the digital economy. But it also raises a basic question. How will those systems know which agents, services, and businesses they can trust?

The answer will shape whether the next generation of the internet remains open and interoperable—or becomes fragmented across a handful of competing platforms and proprietary ecosystems.

We have solved a problem like this before. In the early days of the internet, navigating online was like trying to find a business in a city with no street names or storefront signs. The destinations existed. But users needed to know the underlying IP address of each server.

Sources indicate that the Domain Name System, or DNS, changed that. It transformed the internet from a confusing network of numerical addresses into a navigable web of trusted identities. Endings like .com, .gov, and .edu helped organize the landscape. More importantly, websites could present stable, human-readable names of their own.

Names like Wikipedia.org and Amazon.com told customers exactly where they were going. These names also gave businesses and institutions a unique, durable, and universally accessible online presence, one which many built into the global brands we recognize today.

In a recent development, DNS also helped establish trust. Without such a system, a name like Amazon.com could never have functioned as a trusted commercial identity. A user might have seen the label in a browser and still had little basis for knowing whether it pointed to Amazon’s servers or to a convincing imitation run by fraudsters.

We are approaching a similar inflection point with AI agents. As these systems increasingly operate autonomously on the web, they will need a shared way to know who—and what—they are dealing with.

Industry observers note that in effect, the internet needs an identity layer for agents. At GoDaddy, we contributed to the open standard—Agent Name Service, or ANS. And since the DNS already does this for web and email services, it can readily adapt to do the same for AI agents.

In practical terms, ANS—now a Linux Foundation project—would help AI agents distinguish legitimate services from convincing imposters before moving money, sharing data, or completing transactions. It would give agents a public way to identify one another, verify who stands behind them, and confirm what they are authorized to do.

Recent findings suggest that that is crucial. An AI travel agent must be able to confirm that it is interacting with an airline’s legitimate booking system, not a polished imitation. An AI shopping agent needs to verify that the merchant, payment processor, and delivery provider are authentic before placing an order.

The risks extend beyond outright fraud. Imagine a firm using an AI procurement agent to evaluate suppliers. A supplier could purchase that agent, keep the software and domain name the same, and nudge the AI to recommend its own products.

To the business using the agent, nothing appears to have changed. But the AI is no longer acting entirely in the customer’s interest.

Without a shared identity and verification layer, businesses may have no reliable way to detect those shifts—and ensure that their agents are doing the work they expect them to.

ANS allows every business to verify who operates an AI agent, whether its software matches its registered version, and whether its ownership or software has changed.

Some companies are already building ways for agents to communicate with one another, especially in payments and logistics. But interoperability inside a handful of commercial ecosystems is not the same thing as a global open standard for machines to follow.

According to recent reports, if machine identity is left to a patchwork of proprietary protocols, the agent economy will grow inside private silos. One organization’s agents will work best with its preferred merchants, payment providers, and service platforms.

In a recent development, the internet we know and use today evolved thanks to shared infrastructure that no one company controlled. The next phase of the internet will require the same openness.

Without a common identity layer for AI agents, the promise of the agent economy will give way to confusion and fraud. And we’ll still be spending hours trying to find the best deal on plane tickets.
