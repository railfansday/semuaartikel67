Orangutans face bleak future after Indonesia’s devastating wildfires

For most of October, parts of Indonesia felt dystopian as wildfires turned the sky a reddish orange.

“It felt like you were on Mars,” claims Erin Vogel, a primatologist at Rutgers University in New Brunswick, New Jersey.

According to recent reports, vogel was visiting the Tuanan Orangutan Research Station in Central Kalimantan, Indonesia, at the time. She and her colleagues have been conducting research on the critically endangered Bornean orangutan (Pongo pygmaeus) there for more than 20 years, but this trip she spent much of her time fighting fires. There were roughly 300,000 blazes in the region between July and October, and October’s level of toxic particulate matter in the air was roughly 400 times higher than what the World Health Organization deems safe.

Indonesia is experiencing one of its most severe fire and haze season of the past three decades, in part because a super El Niño has dried out vegetation and brought winds that fuelled the flames. More than 2.8 million hectares have burnt across Indonesia so far, and Kalimantan has been affected the worst. Fires devoured 60% of Bornean orangutans’ habitats in Kalimantan, including healthy forests such as those at the Tuanan research station and in Gunung Palung National Park.

In a recent development, nature spoke to Vogel about the disaster and what it means for orangutans. At the time of the interview, fires were still smouldering in Tuanan peat swamp forests.

Sources indicate that it was depressing and horrid. I’ve never seen so much forest loss in the 23 years I have worked in Indonesia. In the sky, there was this orange-brown haze that was very thick and it burnt your lungs if you weren’t wearing a respirator.

When I arrived in Tuanan, it really struck me how quiet the forests had become. You didn’t hear birds; you didn’t hear insects; you didn’t hear anything except trees burning and falling.

The forest staff, the orangutan staff, the phenology staff and the fire teams were all fighting fires. I felt in awe of those heroes voluntarily fighting these fires day and night. They’d all been doing so for more than two months straight. It takes real strength to be able to do that and not give up, and our team is just so positive and hopeful.

We saw some orangutans who seemed to be very lethargic and sick. We came across one of the males we’ve been studying for more than 15 years, and he was on the ground, breathing heavily. But when we saw him a couple of days later, he seemed to have recovered a little bit. He was near the research station, eating some vegetation on the ground, climbing trees and eating fruits. That reassures us that he is doing better.

We’re seeing orangutans being displaced from one area to others. That means we’re going to have competition between individuals over limited resources. That’s also going to change their behaviour. It’s going to be interesting to see what happens.
