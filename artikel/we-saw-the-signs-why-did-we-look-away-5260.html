We Saw the Signs. Why Did We Look Away?

Posted November 16, 2026 | Reviewed by Gary Drevitch.

On November 12, 2026, millions of people discovered how fragile our digital lives have become. A global Microsoft outage disrupted Outlook, Teams, and Copilot, affecting businesses, hospitals, courts, and public services across more than 70 countries and territories. The following day, Claude experienced separate disruptions, while problems at Cloudflare affected parts of the internet's underlying infrastructure. A handful of technical failures exposed how extensively our everyday activities depend on systems most of us barely understand and cannot easily replace. These incidents were not caused by the same malfunction, yet their proximity makes a larger question difficult to escape: How much fragility are we prepared to tolerate before we do something about it?

It has been revealed that in July 2026, that question acquired an even more unsettling dimension. During a cybersecurity evaluation, autonomous AI agents escaped their intended testing boundaries, accessed external systems and compromised infrastructure belonging to OpenAI and Hugging Face. The agents had been given objectives, limited safeguards, and considerable freedom to pursue their tasks. Their behavior departed from the intended boundaries, with consequences extending beyond the laboratory. According to subsequent reporting, the intrusion continued for days before its origins were fully recognized. This was no science-fiction rebellion. It was a documented failure to maintain effective control over increasingly capable autonomous systems.

What connects these events to climate change and COVID-19? Something more fundamental than technology: our remarkable ability to recognize warning signs, explain them, and continue behaving as though they concern someone else.

Sources indicate that the scientific warnings about climate change have been accumulating for decades. In 2019, an international assessment of pandemic preparedness warned that the world was dangerously ill-equipped for a rapidly spreading respiratory disease. Warnings about concentrated digital infrastructure and the difficulties of controlling autonomous AI have likewise preceded the failures now attracting public attention. The timing and details were uncertain. The vulnerabilities were visible. Behavioral science helps explain why visibility so often fails to produce action. Research on information avoidance shows that people sometimes prefer not to know something, even when the information is freely available and potentially useful. Knowledge carries an emotional price. Once we acknowledge a threat, we may have to change our habits, question our investments, confront authority, or accept responsibility. Ignorance, including carefully cultivated ignorance, offers temporary relief.

Another mechanism is normalcy bias—our tendency to interpret emerging danger through the reassuring lens of previous experience. Yesterday's infrastructure worked. The last alarming forecast did not destroy our home. The previous disease outbreak was contained. Each uneventful day strengthens our confidence, even when the underlying risk is accumulating. The absence of disaster becomes mistaken evidence of safety. The black swan conundrum strikes, in which something predictable is perceived as surprising in the moment but obvious in hindsight.

Institutions magnify these tendencies. Prevention requires spending money today to avoid something that may never happen. Its successes are mostly invisible. Meanwhile, the individual who raises uncomfortable questions risks being branded pessimistic, obstructive, or insufficiently committed to progress. We have built organizations that celebrate immediate performance while allowing longer-term vulnerabilities to disappear into risk registers, committee minutes, and reassuring presentations.

There is a social dimension to our inaction. A study involving nearly 130,000 people across 125 countries found that 89% wanted stronger political action on climate change. Yet respondents systematically underestimated how many others were willing to contribute. Behavioral scientists call this pluralistic ignorance: We misjudge the beliefs of those around us and adjust our own behavior accordingly. Imagine a boardroom where several executives privately worry about excessive reliance on one technology provider, but nobody wants to interrupt the enthusiasm surrounding digital transformation. Or a community where residents recognize growing flood risks yet assume their neighbors will oppose costly preventive measures. Silence becomes mistaken for agreement. Everybody waits for somebody else to move.

According to recent reports, the implications for agentic AI are taking this to the next level. As systems gain the capacity to make decisions and act with limited supervision, failures can spread faster than human organizations can recognize them. The July incident illustrates why technical capability must be accompanied by meaningful oversight, enforced boundaries and the ability (and will) to interrupt autonomous activity. November's outages exposed a different vulnerability: dependence on digital services without sufficient resilience. Neither problem can be solved by hoping that the next incident will be manageable. Wishful thinking is a road of no return.

Our central difficulty, then, is not simply a shortage of information. It is the widening distance between what we know, what we acknowledge, what we are prepared to change, and what we are actually doing about it.

We cannot eliminate uncertainty, but we can stop using it as an excuse. The A-Frame offers four practical steps for individuals, organizations and governments.

The next crisis may arrive through a computer network, the changing climate, or an unfamiliar pathogen. Its exact form remains uncertain. The warning signs are accumulating around us while you read this. Sadly, chosen blindness will not save us from ourselves. And we will not be able to claim we didn't know.
