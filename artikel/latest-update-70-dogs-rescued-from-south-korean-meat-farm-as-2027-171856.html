Sources indicate that nearly 30 dogs have arrived in New York after being rescued from a dog-meat farm in South Korea. Their journey toward adoption is beginning, but with the country's ban still months away from taking full effect, the work is far from over.

A plane ride to New York probably wasn't on these dogs' bingo cards. Neither, for that matter, was a soft bed, a full bowl of food, or the chance to be someone's beloved family member. After spending their lives confined to cages, even the simplest things may seem exciting and new.

Recent findings suggest that on October 1, nearly 30 dogs were rescued from a dog-meat farm in Gangjin, South Korea, that once bred and slaughtered as many as 1,000 dogs each year. This was part of a larger rescue operation by Humane World for Animals that saved more than 70 dogs from the property.

In a recent development, for the organization, the operation marks its final large-scale dog-meat-farm rescue in South Korea ahead of the country's ban, which takes full effect on February 7, 2027. Humane World for Animals has rescued nearly 3,000 dogs from farms since 2015, making this a significant milestone.

It has been revealed that and yes, it's a reason to celebrate. But before we get too comfortable patting ourselves on the back, there's a much bigger picture to consider. Getting these dogs out was a huge victory, but there are still thousands at risk.

When the rescue team arrived at the farm in Gangjin, in South Korea's South Jeolla Province, they were met with disturbing scenes. Rows of rusting cages, abandoned collars scattered on the ground, and skeletal remains served as a gruesome reminder of the dogs that hadn't been so lucky.

Humane World for Animals has reported finding mixed breeds and dogs of different sizes during its rescue work. The survivors included Jindos, a breed native to South Korea, a husky mix, and smaller dogs, including a spitz mix. In other words, any dog could end up on the menu.

It has been revealed that in this most recent rescue, a concerned citizen alerted a local animal protection group, which contacted Humane World for Animals and the authorities. Officials found that the farm had broken South Korea's Animal Protection Act. The farmer, a man in his 80s, agreed to hand over the dogs and promised not to take part in the dog-meat trade again.

In a recent development, more than 70 dogs were rescued. Nearly 30 travelled to Latest York, another 12 went to a rehabilitation center in Montreal, and some mothers and their puppies remained in South Korea because they were too young to fly safely.

It has been revealed that rescuing dogs from situations like this isn't as simple as just opening a cage and booking the next available flight. International transport requires veterinary checks, vaccinations, documentation, and careful consideration about which animals are fit to travel. The priority is getting them out safely, not rushing them onto a plane for the sake of a happy headline.

South Korea passed its Special Act on the Termination of Breeding, Slaughter, and Distribution of Dogs for Consumption in January 2024. Its key prohibitions will finally take full effect on February 7, 2027, making it illegal to breed, slaughter, or sell dogs or dog meat for human consumption. But the transition is already underway.

In a recent development, in May 2026, South Korea's Ministry of Agriculture, Food and Rural Affairs reported that about 82% of the country's 1,537 registered dog-meat farms had closed. Over a thousand farms closed is something worth celebrating, but it means 272 farms are still operating.
