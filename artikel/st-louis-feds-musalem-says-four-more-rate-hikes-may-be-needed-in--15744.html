St. Louis Fed's Musalem says four more rate hikes may be needed in coming months

St. Louis Federal Reserve President Alberto Musalem indicated Tuesday that the central bank may need as many as four rate hikes in coming months, offering one of the most hawkish views yet by a Fed policymaker about the steps needed to quell inflation.

During a speech delivered in Argentina, Musalem made the case that current policy is not significantly restricting economic activity.

"The economy will likely require several interest rate increases before the spring of 2027 to achieve 2% inflation in a timely manner," he mentioned.

Musalem mentioned the real policy rate, which subtracts the inflation rate from the Fed's target rate, needs to be between 2%-2.5% by next spring, and estimated that the current rate is about a full point below that range. That position suggests the Fed may need to hike at each of the upcoming Fed meetings in December, January, March and April.

Musalem does not get a vote on the rate-setting Federal Open Market Committee over the period where he notes that the rate increases will be needed, though he does get to present views and provide input on the "dot plot" of individual members' expectations. He will next vote in 2028.

To be sure, Musalem noted "an unlikely but still plausible scenario" in which no further hikes would be needed.

"Falling energy prices and lower shipping costs, or an increase in potential output growth driven by higher productivity growth, might ease inflation pressures such that inflation moves toward target in 18 months," he remarked. Another possibility is that demand might decelerate as spending on artificial intelligence slows.

However, he remarked the more likely scenario is that "persistent strong demand and ongoing supply pressures" combine to keep inflation above the Fed's 2% target, thus requiring further increases in the fed funds rate from its current range of 3.75%-4%.

Sources indicate that "It would be preferable for a higher real policy rate to happen in a balanced way, driven both by lower inflation and a higher nominal policy rate," he said. "However, the nominal policy rate may need to do most of the work.".
