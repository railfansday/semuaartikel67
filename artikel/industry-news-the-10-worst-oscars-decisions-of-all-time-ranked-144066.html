Ah, the Academy Awards. Every year, cinephiles tune in for the big event as the Oscars honor the best of the best of the previous year in cinema. And like most viewers, opinions are not only present; everybody has one. While those opinions usually arrive the day nominations are officially revealed, they linger well beyond when the sealed envelope is opened, and the winner is revealed. In the almost century the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences has handed out the gold statuette, a handful of decisions have left us baffled.

In a recent development, from brutal robberies to jaw-dropping shocks, when the Oscars botch it, we make our opinions loud and clear. There are certainly more than ten times that they’ve made the wrong decision; these ten are the absolute worst. Perhaps it was the fact that some of the greatest films of all time were beaten out, or the voters were too prejudiced to make a bold decision; these horrible wins meant deserving projects, performers, and creators lost. Though some individuals earned their award at another time, others are still waiting. While the biggest travesty in Academy Awards history is that Glenn Close doesn’t have a performance trophy, these other decisions continue to have us scratching our heads.

Recent findings suggest that perhaps this category had many people in shock the moment the nominations were announced because it meant that Eurovision Song Contest: Story of Fire Saga was an Oscar nominee. But the truth is, its nomination was well warranted. And it’s not just because I’m a die-hard Eurovision fanatic. Their entry, “Husavik (My Hometown),” is actually a remarkable track. Unfortunately, even with a stunning performance from Molly Sandén “live” on a boat in Iceland with an Icelandic child choir on Oscar night, it was still not enough to sway voters. Instead, the honor was given to “Fight for You” from Judas and the Black Messiah. The song authentically captured the late-1960s soul and funk sound of the film while speaking directly to contemporary themes of racial injustice.

Sources indicate that performed by H.E.R. with music by H.E.R. and Dernst Emile II, with lyrics by H.E.R. and Tiara Thomas, the song addresses racism, police brutality, and state repression, linking Hampton's story and COINTELPRO to modern racial justice movements. Its message aligned closely with broader racial justice conversations and H.E.R.'s recent Grammy Song of the Year win for "I Can't Breathe.” Though a great song, it had momentum to ride on. Though it changed drastically, as we’ll discuss later, the Academy voting trends often favor socially urgent anthems over comedic or parody films. For those familiar with the source material on which Eurovision Song Contest: Story of Fire Saga is based, “Husavik (My Hometown)” likely would have met a victory there. Nevertheless, at least the Will Ferrell comedy got an Oscar nod!

Industry observers note that margaret Thatcher is known for many things. From the infamous “The lady’s not for turning” to earning the nickname “Iron Lady,” she was ripe for a biopic. And if anyone was going to play her, one might think it would be a Brit. But the next best option is Meryl Streep. The legendary actress has a mantel full of Oscars and plenty of nominations to go with them. For her turn as Maggie Thatcher, it should have been simply another nomination as the win should have been given to Viola Davis for The Help. Streep was widely praised for capturing Thatcher's posture, voice, and steely resolve while finding the human vulnerability beneath the political figure. But it was by no means her strongest performance. In fact, a major factor in her victory was the headline: Streep’s third Oscar win ended a 29-year drought after 12 straight defeats.

According to recent reports, while Streep did earn the Golden Globe and BAFTA, a precursor to the Oscar win, the film itself wasn’t well received. Her victory may have stemmed from reverence. It gave voters a long-overdue milestone moment. Distributor Harvey Weinstein ran a heavy awards push for The Iron Lady, and at the time, it carried a lot of weight. Although Davis won the Screen Actors Guild Award and The Help was a box-office hit, the film lacked directing or writing nominations and also had running mate Octavia Spencer competing for acting recognition. Davis finally earned a victory on her third try for Best Supporting Actress for her performance as Rose in Fences.

Recent findings suggest that as will be a trend moving forward, Best Picture wins are quite a touchy topic. One of the most shocking results from the ‘90s was when Shakespeare in Love beat out the war masterpiece Saving Private Ryan. The romantic dramedy reimagines a young, cash-strapped William Shakespeare (Joseph Fiennes) overcoming severe writer's block when he falls passionately in love with Viola de Lesseps (Gwyneth Paltrow), a wealthy noblewoman who disguises herself as a man to audition for his upcoming play, inspiring him to write Romeo and Juliet. It sounds like a lovely film, but was it the best of the year? Once again, producer Harvey Weinstein and Miramax pushed the film with an aggressive, politically inspired campaign.

Sources indicate that shakespeare in Love led all films with 13 Oscar nominations, ahead of Saving Private Ryan’s 11, and won the Screen Actors Guild ensemble award. As was clear, voters favored the lighter, witty romance over a grueling war drama, while Steven Spielberg still took home Best Director for Saving Private Ryan. Timing was also a potential factor, as Shakespeare in Love opened in December 1998 and expanded into early 1999 right as voting began, whereas Saving Private Ryan came out in July 1998. Campaigners reportedly pushed the narrative that Saving Private Ryan peaked in its opening D-Day sequence and dragged afterward. With Spielberg reportedly refusing to engage in similar mudslinging or aggressive counter-campaigning, Shakespeare in Love benefited from winning a campaign war.

It has been revealed that life is like a box of chocolates. Forrest Gump remains one of the most iconic films of the '90s. It's no coincidence that it beat out an equally iconic '90s classic: Pulp Fiction. But one was a safe film, and the other was a daring cinematic risk. Directed by Robert Zemeckis, Forrest Gump stars Tom Hanks as the kind-hearted, slow-witted Alabama man as he recounts his extraordinary life while unknowingly influencing major moments in 20th-century American history and pining for his lifelong love, Jenny Curran (Robin Wright). Forrest Gump was a film that was rooted in nostalgia. And for that, it received extra love. With broad emotional appeal and a less polarizing concept, Forrest Gump stood in great contrast to Pulp Fiction, which featured graphic violence and non-traditional morality that clashed with the mainstream Oscar taste of the time.

Forrest Gump was a massive commercial hit with star power and established pedigree. Coming off his win for Philadelphia, Forrest Gump was a crowd-pleaser. It also, for the time, stood out for its technical innovation in which its invisible CGI seamlessly inserted the performers into archival footage, helping it sweep six Oscars overall. And if it won the minor awards, of course the big one was it as well. The clash between the two films highlighted a great divide between traditional sentimentality and '90s postmodernism. Pulp Fiction's risky storytelling was awarded as Quentin Tarantino won for Best Original Screenplay, but the gritty, non-linear crime story was ahead of its time. Tarantino has argued that the Golden Globes placing the film in the drama category over Musical or Comedy hurt its chances, as it established a specific serious tone for award season despite its dark humor. Nevertheless, when we examine the best films of the decade, Pulp Fiction and even the other big loser, The Shawshank Redemption, tend to be placed above Forrest Gump.

It has been revealed that academy voters have great reverence for industry legends, especially when they've built a steadfast career without a win. So, while Jamie Lee Curtis was arguably in the greatest film of the year, was she actually the best supporting actress in that film? Everything Everywhere All at Once remains one of the most triumphant stories of the 2020s. The daring multi-genre film defied the odds, rightly earning all of the praise it received. At the Oscars, it won seven of the 11 nominations it was up for, but the controversial win was when it beat itself. Curtis won for Best Supporting Actress playing IRS inspector Deidre Beaubeidre, blending comedy, action, and emotional weight, even when she sported a bob and hot dog hands. She defeated her co-star, Stephanie Hsu, and it likely came down to a mix of career-long goodwill, strategic campaigning, and late-season awards momentum.

According to recent reports, as an industry veteran, Curtis' win was a career win for her memorable, vanity-free performance. She was a scene-stealer in a character role compared to Hsu's double parts as Joy Wang and Jobu Tupaki. To some, Tsu was the emotional core and a co-lead. And, unfortunately, she would had no shot against Michelle Yeoh should she have been placed in that category. While Hsu has downplayed and dismissed any rivalry, sharing support and happiness for Curtis' win, many people believed that Hsu's loss undercut the importance of spotlighting Asian visibility that came with the film. Perhaps it came down to some unfortunate vote-splitting, or it was all about Curtis' grassroots championing of the film and her impactful off-the-cuff SAG Award acceptance speech, but Hsu was snubbed for an equally incredible performance.

For many of these entries, we're focusing on a one-on-one race. Except for this one. The King's Speech defeated so many incredible films that we're all still baffled. Let's discuss. In the Tom Hooper film, the story follows Britain's reluctant King George VI (Colin Firth) as he works with an unorthodox Australian speech therapist, Lionel Logue (Geoffrey Rush), to overcome a debilitating stammer before addressing his nation at the start of World War II. Biopics tend to do well at the Oscars. It was no different here. Yet it faced heavy hitters, including Black Swan, Inception, The Social Network, and True Grit. So, how was it victorious? The King's Speech combined broad crowd-pleasing appeal, acclaimed acting performances, typical Oscar-bait storytelling, and preferential treatment.

Many have pointed to The King's Speech's classic prestige as a natural fit for traditional voters. Especially during a ranked-choice voting system, which tends to reward films with broader backing rather than divisive favorites. Yet history has shown that its competitors are the films that truly stood the test of time. As an inspiring, emotionally accessible choice with strong above-the-line wins, it was not enough for the other films to overcome the hurdle. The Social Network emerged as its biggest competitor, having a strong early push, but lost out in crucial categories. Inception rightly achieved four technical Oscars, but its voters were not on board for the high-concept sci-fi thriller. Natalie Portman won for Black Swan, but when Clint Mansell's score was disqualified from Oscar consideration for relying heavily on Tchaikovsky's Swan Lake, the film lost most of its momentum. And True Grit went 0-for-10. Needless to say, the 83rd Academy Awards was a doozy!

It's time to journey back to 1941 when one of the greatest films ever made, Citizen Kane, won the Academy Award for Best Picture. Scratch that. When it LOST the Academy Award for Best Picture. Mind-blowing! One of the biggest shockers in Oscar history came down to a preference for nostalgia, a beloved director, and a media mogul's fervent push to prevent a victory. Citizen Kane failed to win Best Picture, losing to How Green Was My Valley. Based on the book by Richard Llewellyn, the John Ford drama follows youngest son Huw Morgan (Roddy McDowall) as he looks back on his close-knit family's joys, labor struggles, and gradual dissolution in a turn-of-the-century South Wales coal-mining town. How Green Was My Valley was victorious for its combined prestige, studio craftsmanship, and emotional family drama with wartime appeal. Having come off of back-to-back Best Director wins, Ford set the industry standard. Orson Welles made a mark in a very different way.

Recent findings suggest that 25-year-old Welles, a relative newcomer to the industry, faced the ire of newspaper magnate William Randolph Hearst, making voters wary of backing it. Hearst viewed Citizen Kane as a personal attack and used his newspapers to suppress coverage and hurt the film's box office. Welles was seen as a brash outsider disrupting studio norms, using themes of power and isolation to tell his story. His confidence ruffled feathers, resulting in the film's punishment. That remarked, Citizen Kane wasn't viewed as the masterpiece we see it as today. Following Pearl Harbor, How Green Was My Valley was the safer, more comforting option. This defeat remains one of the more peculiar decisions in Oscar history.

Sources indicate that the 2003 Academy Awards were quite controversial. The ceremony was well before cancel culture was the way of the world, so artistic merit outweighed personal conflict. So Roman Polanski's Best Director win for The Pianist wasn't because he didn't deserve it for his work, but the shock came from his troubled past. After widespread critical acclaim, Polanski earned the award through his deep personal connection to the material. Polanski survived the Holocaust as a child, which mirrored the survival themes within the source material. Between his passion behind the project and the belief that he had "paid his dues," Academy voters gave him the win. But he did not attend the ceremony because he remained a fugitive from the U.S. justice system since he pleaded guilty in 1978, ahead of sentencing, to unlawful sexual intercourse with a minor. His victory drew cheers and a standing ovation from parts of the audience, but it sparked a debate over separating an artist's personal crimes from their creative work.

In a recent development, had voters opted to give the win to another director, the options were Martin Scorsese for Gangs of Upcoming York and Rob Marshall for Chicago. It should be noted that the latter won for Best Picture. A major reason these two frontrunners lost was vote splitting. Scorsese won the Golden Globe while Marshall won the DGA Award. This opened the door for Polanski's upset. Despite Chicago's win, some long-time voters were not thrilled at the notion of honoring Marshall for his feature film debut. Gangs of New York also faced campaign backlash over Miramax's promotional tactics. While the overdue sentiment was overwhelmingly felt, Polanski's victory will forever be marred by controversy.

Surely you remember Art Carney's beloved performance as Harry Coombes in Harry and Tonto! Surely it's up to the same standard as Al Pacino as Michael Corleone in The Godfather Part II and Jack Nicholson as J.J. "Jake" Gittes in Chinatown! Yeah, that's a bald-faced lie! And yet, it was Carney who won the Academy Award for Best Actor. Carney was recognized for his warm, understated performance as an evicted elderly widower traveling across America with his cat. Playing a 70-something at 55 with heavy makeup, Carney delivered a low-key, relatable portrayal full of heart and dignity. Yet extreme vote splitting between the Hollywood heavyweights kept the admired contenders off the Oscar stage. It should be noted that they also had to compete against Albert Finney in Murder on the Orient Express and Dustin Hoffman for Lenny, so needless to say, it was a tough year to be any of those men!

According to recent reports, against darker, more intense roles, Carney emerged as the safe, feel-good alternative. It was a win for a TV veteran who made a rare major massive-screen leading turn. But there may be more to the story. At least for Pacino. Some believe his earlier boycott of the 1972 ceremony and The Godfather Part II's major award sweep gave voters the leeway to deny him a trophy. The Godfather dynasty may have worked against Pacino, but why did Nicholson not emerge victorious? It may have come down to Chinatown's bleak, cynical ending, which went against Hollywood standards at the time. Though he may not have won for Chinatown, he did win a year later for One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest.

Not going to lie, this one still stings. Surely Jack Nicholson read the wrong name, like the whole Moonlight-La La Land kerfuffle, right? Unfortunately, that was not the case, as Crash was victorious at the 2004 ceremony. Directed by Paul Haggis, Crash follows the interconnected lives of several strangers from diverse racial, cultural, and social backgrounds in post 9/11 Los Angeles whose paths unexpectedly collide over a volatile 36-hour period, forcing them to confront prejudice, bigotry, and urban disconnect. The film won thanks to strong support from its massive ensemble, an aggressive DVD screener campaign, and cultural pushback against its rival, Brokeback Mountain. Yes, you read that right. The gay cowboy film was too progressive for the time.

Sources indicate that lionsgate sent over 100,0000 screeners to SAG members and other voters. Because actors formed the largest branch of the Academy, Crash was at the forefront of voters' minds. After winning the SAG Award for Best Ensemble, the momentum shifted toward the film in the stretch toward the Oscars. Oprah Winfrey featured the ensemble on her show. Roger Ebert championed the film as the top movie of the year. But Academy Award voters had to reckon with the fact that Ang Lee's brilliant film may have caused some discomfort. Brokeback Mountain was groundbreaking not just for highlighting a gay romance, but also for being set against a lush Western backdrop and disregarding the genre's tropes. There's no denying Crash's cultural impact, as it was essentially a Haggis passion project, but Brokeback Mountain pushed boundaries by celebrating a same-sex romance and focusing on the hardships of a taboo relationship through the lens of identity. Brokeback Mountain didn't leave the ceremony empty-handed, winning three Oscars for Best Director, Best Adapted Screenplay, and Best Original Score, but the supposed anti-gay bias and its subversion of classic Western masculinity became a voter turn-off.
