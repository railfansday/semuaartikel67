Industry News: Scientists tracked kids for 8 years. What they found about screen time was surprising

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

An eight-year study of children in Finland has revealed a surprising connection between screen time and brain function. Researchers found that children who spent more time using screens tended to have better cognitive processing abilities as teenagers, challenging the widespread assumption that screen time is necessarily harmful to developing brains.

According to recent reports, the findings suggest that the amount of time children spend in front of screens may be less important than how they use that time. Activities that encourage learning, creativity, and problem-solving could be particularly valuable. Researchers emphasize that the goal should not be to eliminate screen time, but to balance it with physical activity and mentally stimulating experiences.

Industry observers note that the discovery comes amid growing concerns about insufficient physical activity among teenagers. A sedentary lifestyle has been linked to poorer academic performance, while regular exercise is known to support brain function, particularly in adults. However, scientists still have much to learn about how physical activity, sitting, and screen use during childhood influence thinking abilities later in adolescence.

These questions are especially key because childhood and adolescence are critical periods of brain development. Changes in attention, memory, and learning during these years can have lasting consequences, potentially influencing educational opportunities and career paths well into adulthood.

Industry observers note that to investigate these relationships, Finnish researchers examined how screen time, physical activity, and sedentary behavior from childhood through adolescence were associated with cognitive performance in teenagers. They also explored whether the connections differed between boys and girls and whether the intensity of physical activity made a difference.

More Screen Time Linked to Better Thinking Skills.

One of the study's most unexpected findings was that greater screen time throughout childhood was associated with better cognitive processing in adolescence.

Recent findings suggest that cognitive processing refers to the mental abilities people use to take in information, pay attention, remember details, learn new things, and solve problems. These abilities play an crucial role in school performance and everyday decision-making.

The findings challenge the idea that all screen time should be viewed as harmful. However, the researchers caution that the relationship may depend on what children are actually doing while using digital devices. Watching videos, playing games that require strategic thinking, and engaging in educational activities may involve very different levels of mental effort.

"The findings suggest that screen time can support children's and adolescents' cognitive processing. Presumably, the essential point here is what kind of things they do in their screen time. Teachers and parents should encourage children to use devices and screens in such ways that promote active thinking, problem-solving, creativity, and learning," states Doctoral Researcher Petri Jalanko from the University of Jyväskylä.

"We should not regard screen time solely as harmful but seek balance between physical activity and screen time that promotes active thinking," Jalanko summarizes.

Exercise Affects Boys' and Girls' Brains Differently.

The researchers also uncovered a more complicated picture of how physical activity relates to thinking and memory.

Industry observers note that among girls, spending more time engaged in light-intensity physical activity since childhood was associated with better working memory during adolescence. For boys, greater participation in guided physical activity over the same period was linked to stronger working memory.

It has been revealed that working memory is the brain's ability to temporarily hold and use information. It allows someone to remember instructions while completing a task, keep track of numbers during mental calculations, or follow a conversation while processing newly introduced information.

Recent findings suggest that light-intensity physical activity generally includes relatively gentle movements that do not substantially increase breathing or heart rate. Guided physical activity refers to exercise carried out with some form of instruction or supervision, such as organized sports or structured exercise sessions.

Recent findings suggest that the differences between boys and girls suggest that the relationship between exercise and brain health may depend on more than simply how much a person moves. The type of activity, how it is organized, and the individual's sex could all play a role.

Another Surprising Finding About Physical Activity.

The study produced another counterintuitive result. Teenagers who reported spending less time in unsupervised physical activity tended to perform better on measures of cognitive processing.

Yet when researchers used wearable sensors to objectively measure movement and heart rate, they found no association between overall physical activity or sedentary time and cognitive performance.

One possible explanation is that wearable devices measure movement without revealing what a person is actually doing. For example, a sensor may record periods of sitting or physical activity, but it cannot determine whether someone is solving a difficult problem, studying, participating in an organized activity, or simply resting.

This distinction could help explain why questionnaire responses revealed relationships that were not apparent in the sensor measurements.

According to recent reports, "Our study indicates that the connections of physical activity and sedentary behavior to cognitive processing are complex and depend on the sex, the type and assessment method of physical activity and sedentary time. Moreover, boys and girls may benefit from different types of physical activity in view of brain health," Jalanko adds.

Sources indicate that "However, we need more intervention studies to find out causal relations and sex differences in this respect. Moreover, it is crucial to examine more specifically the effects of the intensity of physical activity on changes taking place in cognitive processing.".

What the Eight-Year Study Revealed.

The findings come from an eight-year follow-up conducted as part of the PANIC study, a long-running Finnish research project investigating children's physical activity and nutrition.

Recent findings suggest that the current analysis included 260 participants, consisting of 124 girls and 136 boys. Their average age at the adolescent assessment was 15.8 years.

In a recent development, researchers collected information about physical activity and sedentary behavior using both questionnaires and wearable devices that combined heart rate monitoring with movement measurements. This approach allowed them to compare participants' reported activities with data collected by sensors.

Sources indicate that to evaluate cognitive abilities, the researchers used the CogState test battery, a collection of computerized assessments designed to measure aspects of mental performance. The tests examined learning, attention, and working memory.

Importantly, the findings show associations rather than cause and effect. They do not establish that increasing screen time will improve a child's thinking abilities or that reducing certain types of exercise will produce cognitive benefits. Intervention studies, in which researchers deliberately change participants' activities and measure the results, are needed to determine whether these relationships are causal.

Nevertheless, the results offer an important perspective on the continuing debate over children's screen use. Rather than treating all time spent with digital devices as equally harmful, the researchers suggest paying closer attention to whether those activities encourage active thinking and learning.

The research also highlights the importance of considering different forms of physical activity rather than focusing exclusively on the total amount of exercise children receive.

Together, the findings suggest that supporting healthy brain development may require a more nuanced approach, one that combines physical activity with opportunities for learning, creativity, and mental engagement, including those that take place on screens.

The study was published in the scientific journal Pediatric Exercise Science.
