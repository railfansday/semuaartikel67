Almost 90% of Europe’s Battery Production at Risk from Franco-German Car Deal

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Europe has huge potential for battery plants but calls to weaken or delay the Industrial Accelerator Act and carmaker demands to weaken the EU’s CO2 standards, risk handing Europe’s battery value chain to foreign competitors.

The Franco-German car deal will secure just 13% of Europe’s planned battery cell production in 2030, new T&E analysis highlights. Weak Made-in-Europe rules, combined with weaker emission reduction targets, risks handing over the market for battery production to Europe’s competitors, indicates T&E.

The IAA introduces local content requirements to ensure scarce public subsidies and corporate tax incentives anchor homegrown EU manufacturing. 100 GWh of cells and 250 GWh of cathodes could leak abroad because of weak Made-in-Europe rules, the analysis shows — equivalent to 1.5 million battery packs.

At the same time, Europe’s battery industry faces a double blow from the EU Parliament’s proposals to weaken emission reduction rules which will cut cell demand by 247 GWh or approximately 3.7 million EU made EVs between 2027 and 2032 — almost three times the annual capacity of Europe’s largest battery plant, LG Energy Solution in Poland.

According to recent reports, franziska Grüning, Raw Materials Officer at T&E, mentioned: “The Franco-German deal is a disaster for Europe’s battery industry. Securing a full European battery value chain, from mining to production, and the high value jobs it will provide, should be a top priority for Europe’s leaders. Instead, they are debating how far to weaken rules that would secure domestically produced batteries. Weak local content incentives for batteries in the IAA will send a terrible political signal for the future of Europe’s battery industry. Homegrown companies struggling to cross the valley of death should benefit from an IAA demand boost.”.

Sources indicate that with many cell factories only just beginning to ramp up, most EU production expected by 2030 comes from plants that have not yet entered production. The future of factories such as Lyten (formerly Northvolt) in Sweden, PowerCo Salzgitter in Germany, or Verkor Dunkirk in France, could be jeopardised by this deal.

In a recent development, the EU’s battery industry must not go the same way as its solar panels industry, claims T&E, which calls on the EU to reject the Franco-German deal proposal on batteries, and stick to the timelines and battery requirements of the EU Commission’s proposal. In addition, a strict Made-in-EU scope, or at most a narrow list of trusted partners of the EU27 plus the European Economic Area (EEA) and the UK, must be introduced, and there should be no further weakening of the car CO2 standards.
