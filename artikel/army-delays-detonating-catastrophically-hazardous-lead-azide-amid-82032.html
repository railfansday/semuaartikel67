Army delays detonating "catastrophically hazardous" lead azide amid fear, anger

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a recent development, in a swirl of questions, fears, and outrage, the US Army declared Thursday that it would delay a strategy to blow up 68 deteriorating barrels of lead azide, which would launch toxic gas and lead into the air. A federal appeals court further halted aims later on Thursday with an emergency order. The plume, containing neurotoxic lead, could potentially drift 100 miles north of the Army installation housing it in Middletown, Iowa, according to modeling released by the Army earlier this week.

The Army had planned to carry out two controlled detonations, set off simultaneously around noon Friday, to destroy the barrels where they lie, which is in earth-covered concrete containment structures (called earth-covered magazines or ECMs). Prior to the court stay, the Army rescheduled the blast until November 23 to provide more time to communicate about the situation with Iowans, who have reported being unaware of the detonations until earlier this week.

“Public communication is essential to public trust,” Acting Secretary of the Army Adam Telle mentioned in a post on social media at 7:31 pm ET Thursday. “Therefore, the Army will continue to actively engage with state and local officials throughout the region to distribute accurate, up-to-date information.”.

Industry observers note that telle claimed that the Army could not delay the detonation any further than Monday due to the state of deterioration of the compound, which is extremely explosive and sensitive. “This will delay the detonation until the latest possible moment prior to a forecasted temperature drop, which would risk further destabilization of the material.”.

According to recent reports, lead azide is a compound used in detonators for munitions and is stockpiled by the US military. In March, the US Army identified that the stockpile in the Iowa facility—the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant (IAAAP)—had deteriorated. The lead azide in the 68 barrels, reported to total around 9,700 pounds (4.4 tons), is suspended in a mixture of water and ethanol within the ECMs, but has degraded and crystallized over time. Temperature fluctuations can cause lead azide to dissolve into the water/alcohol solution and recrystallize into needles or platelets, the Army mentioned. In crystallized forms, lead azide becomes extremely sensitive to any physical shock, friction, or heat. The Army determined the current state is a “catastrophically hazardous condition.”.

In a recent development, in an environmental assessment released Wednesday, the Army said that trying to move the lead azide from its current position for any reason was ruled out. Any sloshing or physical shock from loading, transporting, or burying the barrels for safer detonation would create “an unacceptable safety risk and a high probability of accidental, uncontrolled detonation.”.

The military considered neutralizing the lead azide using a chemical reagent, but again ruled it out because it remarked the process would require handling the lead azide over an extended period, with barrels neutralized at a rate of 3 to 10 per day. “The chemical neutralization process requires significantly more ‘hands-on’ time and physical manipulation of the drums by personnel over a prolonged 7- to 22-day period, drastically increasing the probability of accidental, fatal detonation during handling,” the Army wrote in its assessment.

In all, the Army considered a controlled, mitigated “Blow-in-Place” strategy the best option, and it specifically planned for a fall detonation. The Army mentioned the fall weather would “take advantage of periods of reduced thermal cycling and to ensure the lowest impact to facility operations and local agriculture (harvesting).”.

According to an emergency permit for the detonation from the Environmental Protection Agency, the plan is to place explosive charges and an accelerant on or near each of the ECMs.

In a recent development, the Army intends to try to mitigate the detonation’s impact with a few simple methods. Those include lime-fill and sand-fill barriers around the ECMs, water cannons and misters to control lead dust in the wake of the explosion, and timing the detonation with ideal weather conditions—avoiding high winds that could increase the area exposed to the plume or, conversely, a thermal inversion that could trap the plume in one place.

Sources indicate that modeling released Wednesday—based on the conditions for the scheduled detonation on Friday—suggested the plume could deposit lead dust at an amount between 5 and 100 micrograms per square foot in a stretch up to 25 miles north, nearly to Wapello, Iowa. Those levels are above those considered hazardous for floors and windows, respectively. Meanwhile, airborne lead, at a level of 0.18 micrograms per cubic meter, would reach nearly 100 miles north, toward Cedar Rapids, Iowa. The EPA standard for lead levels in the air is 0.15 micrograms per cubic meter.

Industry observers note that during the detonation, the Army advised residents in the immediate area to consider leaving or at least staying indoors with windows closed. It also recommended people wash their cars if they park outside. Similarly, local fire departments reportedly planned to hose down children’s playgrounds to remove any coating of lead dust in the aftermath, and schools planned closures.

The Army’s plan was to communicate clearly with the public about the aims, including holding an open-house event on October 15. But while Middletown residents may have been aware of the detonations, Iowans in the projected path of the plume were surprised by the news this week.

In its environmental risk assessment, the Army noted it planned to mitigate the lead fallout with “rapid excavation” of soil and dust and treat it with a “stabilization agent.” This was to prevent it from remaining in the environment of the military facility, from which it could drift or make its way into groundwater. But the efforts would not mitigate the airborne plume, and lead does not break down in the environment. Children and pregnant people are particularly vulnerable to the neurotoxic effects.

Amid health and environmental fears, state leaders and residents raised a range of questions Wednesday and Thursday, from how the lead azide was allowed to deteriorate to its current hazardous condition and whether the Army had truly exhausted safer options—options that wouldn’t launch a toxic cloud over a swath of the state.

Iowa’s governor-elect, Rob Sand, wrote on social media that an “unacceptable lack of communication and clarity has made Iowans understandably concerned and angry about this situation.”.

Political leaders in nearby Illinois also chimed in, sending a letter to Telle and EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin, expressing “grave concerns” over the initiative.

“Alongside our neighbors in Iowa, we are also demanding answers about how this could affect our people’s health and safety,” Illinois Governor JB Pritzker wrote on social media.

Industry observers note that on Thursday, prior to the announcement of a delay, environmental groups filed a lawsuit seeking a court-ordered pause of the detonation. The groups alleged, in part, that the EPA approved the strategy “without full consideration of less dangerous alternatives that would avoid widespread dispersal and deposition of toxic lead; and inadequate public notice of the plan to open-detonate hazardous waste due to the extraordinarily rushed timeline set by the Army.” Late Thursday, a federal appeals court issued an emergency stay of the Army’s EPA permit for the demolition, halting prepares until it can review the case.

Friday morning, the Army, Department of Health and Human Services, and EPA released a joint statement saying:.

It has been revealed that the entire interagency is working together to ensure that the controlled detonation in Middletown, Iowa is conducted in the safest fashion possible. Officials have been meticulously planning for this event since March, and all agencies involved are prepared to take the most extreme mitigation measures available to ensure there is no risk to the public and any disruptions are minimized. The entire Trump administration will continue to engage with state and local officials, as well as the broader community, to keep all apprised of updates as they arise.

Notably, in June, Zeldin listed the EPA’s work with Iowa’s lead azide as one of the top “100 environmental victories that showcase EPA’s unwavering dedication to protecting American families.” The work was number 60 on the list, described as providing “critical emergency support to Iowa Army Ammunition Plant, deploying technical experts for 68 deteriorating drums of explosive lead azide.”.

This post was updated November 20 to include that the federal appeals court granted a stay in the case brought by the environmental groups.
