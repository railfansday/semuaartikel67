Recent findings suggest that GUAYNABO, Puerto Rico, Nov. 24, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- Job security affects everything from career decisions to financial planning, yet workers don't experience it equally.

According to recent reports, zetyÂ®, a leading resume builder, has released its 2026 Job Security Index, the latest research from the Zety Workforce Hub. The index evaluates job security scores using millions of employee-reported reviews and worker sentiment metrics. While the scores shed light on how secure workers feel in their own jobs, they don't explain what drives those perceptions.

To better understand what distinguishes the highest- and lowest-rated occupations, each one was grouped into 50 standardized job categories and cross-referenced with O*NET data. The analysis identified recurring workplace and job characteristics associated with higher and lower job security scores.

The Average Job Security Scores in this study are based on Zety's proprietary data, which aggregates millions of employee-reported ratings from major employment and workplace review platforms.

In a recent development, to standardize the analysis, similar job titles were grouped and matched to their corresponding O*NET occupations. Each occupation was then compared across several O*NET categories, including Essential Skills, Abilities, Knowledge, Work Activities, Work Styles, and Job Zone.

The results show notable differences in the types of work, preparation, and workplace characteristics represented among the highest- and lowest-rated occupations.

Top 10 Occupations for Job Security.

Human Resource Managers top the list with a Job Security Score of 3.89, and the occupations that follow it share several defining characteristics. These jobs are more likely to involve interpreting information, applying specialized knowledge, using digital tools, and making informed decisions.

Postsecondary Teaching Assistants.

Data Engineers and Database Architects.

Business Intelligence and Data Analysts.

Management Analysts and Consultants.

Financial and Investment Analysts.

*Job Zones classify occupations based on the level of education, experience, and training typically required to perform the job.

Bottom 10 Occupations for Job Security.

Industry observers note that where the top 10 occupations are defined by analysis and expertise, the bottom 10 are defined by physical presence. They also tended to require less formal preparation and were more likely to center on execution rather than problem-solving.

Light Truck and Delivery Drivers.

Warehouse and Material-Moving Workers.

Heavy and Tractor-Trailer Truck Drivers.

General Maintenance and Repair Workers.

Baristas and Counter-Service Workers.

What Shapes Employee Perceptions of Job Security?

The clearest differences in the data were associated with the nature of the work itself, more than the industry it falls under. Employees feel most secure in roles involving analysis, technology, and specialized expertise, and least secure in roles involving physical labor, routine tasks, and location-dependent duties.

According to recent reports, preparation plays a role, too: nine of the bottom 10 occupations fall within O*NET Job Zones 2 or 3, compared to none of the top 10. But it isn't the whole story. Mental Health Counselors stand out as the notable exception. Despite requiring graduate-level education and falling within Job Zone 5, they received the lowest Job Security Score in the study, suggesting that employee perceptions of security are influenced by more than education requirements alone.

Recent findings suggest that "Job security is often discussed in terms of economic conditions or hiring trends, but employees also experience it through the realities of their own work," said Jasmine Escalera, career expert at Zety. "While education and training matter, they don't produce the same sense of security across every occupation. The skills employees rely on, the decisions they make, and the way they contribute each day all influence how secure a role feels.".

In a recent development, using Zety's proprietary data, millions of employee-reported job security scores and worker sentiment metrics were aggregated and analyzed. To ensure sufficient sample size, the analysis included only occupations with at least 5,000 employee reviews.

Industry observers note that because employee-reported job titles vary widely, similar titles were consolidated into 50 standardized occupational categories. Generic or ambiguous titles that could not be reliably matched to a specific occupation, such as "Intern" or "Manager," were removed. Each standardized category was then mapped to a representative O*NET occupation using the closest official occupational classification. Each occupational category was assigned an average Job Security Score calculated across all matched job titles, weighted by employee review count.

Comparisons focused on O*NET measures of Essential Skills, Abilities, Knowledge, Work Activities, Work Styles, and Job Zone to identify patterns associated with employee-rated job security.
