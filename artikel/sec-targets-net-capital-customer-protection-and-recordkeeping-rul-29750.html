SEC Targets Net Capital, Customer Protection and Recordkeeping Rules for Crypto Brokers

AI-assisted summary, reviewed by NullTX editors.

According to recent reports, the SEC's crypto broker-dealer proposal, RIN 3235-AN48, is under White House review with no public text. Here is what the four named rules require today and what review means.

Four rules written decades before Bitcoin existed are now sitting at the White House for a crypto rewrite, and nobody outside the SEC has seen a word of the draft. What is known is the list of rules the agency wants to change, and that list states a lot.

Sources indicate that the White House's regulatory review office logged the proposal on November 3. According to the Unified Agenda entry for RIN 3235-AN48, the item is titled "Amendments to Broker-Dealer Financial Responsibility and Recordkeeping and Reporting Rules Regarding Crypto Assets." It sits at the proposed rule stage and carries an economically significant designation. The cited legal authority is Sections 15(c) and 17(a) of the Exchange Act, and the entry lists no legal deadline.

The abstract is short. The SEC's Division of Trading and Markets claims it is considering recommending that the Commission amend Rules 15c3-1 and 15c3-3 and other financial responsibility rules, along with Rules 17a-3 and 17a-4, to address how they apply to crypto assets. The CFR citation is listed as not yet determined. The entry's timetable also targeted a proposed rule in July 2026, so the package has run later than the agency planned.

That delay is worth a second look. It hints that the drafting proved harder than expected, which fits the subject, since these rules were built for a world of paper certificates and central clearing agents.

Recent findings suggest that review by the Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs is not an approval. It is an interagency check on a draft the agency intends to propose, and it comes before the Commission votes to publish anything. The SEC, as an independent agency, now submits significant rules to that office under a 2025 executive order, which explains why a securities rule highlights up in a White House queue.

Industry observers note that "Economically significant" is a defined term. It generally covers a rule expected to have an annual economic effect of $100 million or more, or one that materially affects a sector of the economy. The label signals expected scale, not content or direction. After review, the process continues with a Commission vote, publication of a proposal, a public comment period and, only then, a final rule.

Nothing in the record notes what the rules would change. Anyone claiming the rewrite will loosen or tighten requirements is speculating.

Industry observers note that rule 15c3-1 is the net capital rule. It requires broker-dealers to hold a minimum level of liquid capital, and it applies haircuts to the value of proprietary positions so a firm can absorb losses and still meet its obligations to customers.

Industry observers note that the SEC staff's FAQ on crypto asset activities already makes clear that broker-dealers can't ignore crypto. When a firm takes a proprietary position in crypto assets, that exposure must be counted in its net capital computation, and digital assets get no carve-out. The open question, and the likeliest subject of rulemaking, is how a volatile asset with no traditional ready market should be valued and haircut. That design choice carries real consequences for how much capital a crypto-active broker has to hold.

Rule 15c3-3 is the customer protection rule. It requires broker-dealers to promptly obtain and maintain physical possession or control of customers' fully paid and excess margin securities, and it calls for periodic reserve computations to safeguard customer cash. It applies only to securities, so crypto assets that aren't securities fall outside it, even when a broker-dealer holds them.

Industry observers note that existing staff guidance has tackled both prongs. The FAQ claims a broker-dealer may establish "control" of a crypto asset security through a control location specified in paragraph (c) of the rule. A December 17, 2025 Division statement took up "physical possession," saying the staff wouldn't object if a firm deems itself to have possession under five conditions. Those conditions cover direct access and transfer capability, written policies assessing the underlying ledger, no custody where the firm knows of material security or operational weaknesses, protection of private keys, and contingency prepares for events such as 51% attacks, hard forks and airdrops.

In a recent development, commissioner Peirce's statement on that release urged the Division to move swiftly on recommendations for amending Rule 15c3-3 to cover crypto custody. The OIRA package looks like the follow-up. Because staff statements are non-binding, a formal rule would give firms something sturdier to build on.

Rule 17a-3 lists the books and records a broker-dealer must make, including ledgers, blotters and customer account information. Rule 17a-4 governs how long records must be kept and in what form, with electronic records stored in a non-rewriteable, non-erasable format or under an audit-trail alternative.

Sources indicate that crypto complicates both. A transaction's record lives on a public ledger the firm doesn't control, so a rule would need to settle whether onchain data counts as the firm's own record, how wallet addresses and key-custody events should be documented, and how reconciliation against the ledger should be preserved. The Division will likely address those practical points, given that recordkeeping failures rank among the most common triggers for enforcement actions.

Industry observers note that the sequence is predictable even if the content isn't. The first signal would be the release of the draft text or a Commission vote to propose. A comment period follows, in which broker-dealers, custodians, exchanges and investor advocates weigh in, and then comes a possible final rule.

The package matters less for any single provision than for what it signals. The SEC appears to be moving from staff FAQs and non-binding statements toward enforceable rules for the plumbing that crypto-active brokers depend on. Net capital, customer asset protection and recordkeeping determine whether a firm can safely hold customer assets, and they set the cost of doing so.

Until the text appears, the useful questions are narrow ones. Will the SEC differentiate between crypto asset securities and other crypto assets? How will it treat custody through third parties? And will capital charges be calibrated to volatility or set conservatively? The answers will show whether broker-dealers become a practical route to crypto custody or remain a niche.
