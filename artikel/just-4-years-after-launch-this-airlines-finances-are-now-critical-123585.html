According to recent reports, when Norse Atlantic Airways commenced operations four years ago, in June 2022, a upcoming dawn for transatlantic travel appeared to have arrived. The carrier promised low-cost long-haul flights using a modern fleet of Boeing 787 Dreamliners, operating from Oslo Gardermoen Airport (OSL) in Norway and, via its UK division, Gatwick Airport (LGW) in London. Now, however, the airline is on the brink.

Sources indicate that this morning, Norse issued a statement about its financial situation and the strategic review it is undertaking as it looks to return to more stable economic ground. The carrier described its financial footing as 'critical' and underlined the need to secure financing to remain operationally viable.

Norse did not mince its words in Wednesday's statement, explaining outright that "the organization’s financial situation is critical" and "dependent on securing financing to meet its short-term liquidity needs and to continue its operations." Given the carrier's well-documented financial struggles, Norse is exploring various options for its future.

In a recent development, in this regard, the airline explained on Wednesday morning that it continues to "entertain discussions for a potential takeover." Elsewhere, other options include placing greater emphasis on ACMI (wet lease) contractsto make up for its financial shortfall. The news will concern the airline's flyers and staff, and it has committed to "providing further information as and when appropriate." The airline also remarked it is in "discussions" with agencies to help with its finances.

It has been revealed that "Norse is also engaged in discussions with selected parties on short-term financing. However, there can be no assurances that such discussions will result in a viable solution.".

Industry observers note that when Norse commenced operations back in June 2022, its first route linked Oslo with John F. Kennedy International Airport (JFK) in New York City. Certain flights on this route operated via London, giving passengers flying between the UK and Norway the rare chance to fly short-haul on a 787. Norse then set up a UK subsidiary, allowing it to serve London without the Norway leg.

Industry observers note that today, Asian leisure destinations also play an crucial role in Norse's winter network. According to Cirium, an aviation analytics company, Norse itself is operating scheduled flights from Oslo and Stockholm to Bangkok and Phuket in Thailand this month. Norse UK is operating flights from London Gatwick to Bangkok and Cape Town, with Orlando being its only US destination.

Recent findings suggest that fleet-wise, data from ch-aviation highlights that Norse right now has a total of 12 787-9s. Of these, seven are registered in Norway, and five are registered in the UK. Interestingly, three of Norse Atlantic's five UK-registered Boeing 787s are already being used on ACMI contracts, with Pakistan International Airlines listed as their wet-lease customer.

The fact that Norse has described its financial situation as critical casts serious doubt on the long-term economic viability of its operations. Unfortunately, the low-cost long-haul market is a difficult nut to crack, as has been proven time and again over the years. Another notable example of a failure on this front involved Norwegian Air Shuttle and its 787s.

These Dreamliners operated low-cost intercontinental flights using both Boeing 787-8s (Norwegian Long Haul) and 787-9s (Norwegian Long-Haul and Norwegian Air UK). Much like Norse Atlantic, the carrier promised big things when it came to revolutionizing the long-haul scene for budget-conscious flyers. Unfortunately, the onset of the coronavirus pandemic brought these aims screeching to a sudden halt.
