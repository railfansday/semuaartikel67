We took our sweet time starting out this morning. Like true “hiker trash,” we were determined to take full advantage of the complimentary lobby perks at our hotel: free coffees, teas, and an automated orange squeezer that freshly crushed OJ right in front of you.

By 10:00 AM, we finally walked out into the busy streets of Millandoiro to begin the day’s trek. Unlike yesterday’s intimate trail options, this morning started on one main route heavily packed with pilgrims.

Industry observers note that the twist? The entire throng was walking northbound toward Santiago de Compostela—meaning we were walking directly into the oncoming crowd.

When you hike in the same direction as everyone else, you rarely make direct eye contact with fellow walkers. Heading south against the traffic created a completely different dynamic.

For the pilgrims walking toward Santiago, encountering someone going the opposite direction was a rare novelty, so almost every single person looked up, made eye contact, and wanted to interact.

Sources indicate that at first, we greeted everyone with a friendly “Hola,” “Buen Camino,” or “Bom Camino” (since we were on the Portuguese route). But after saying it every two minutes for an hour straight, Knockerz and I were mentally exhausted.

Recent findings suggest that you simply cannot engage with hundreds—or thousands—of people every single day on a trail. We quickly vowed to drop our gaze, lock our eyes on the ground, and hike on like zombies.

Recent findings suggest that it was a gorgeous, sunny day with temperatures in the low 60s. Even with our heads down, we kept physically bumping into people.

Because northbound pilgrims never expected anyone to be walking south, they rarely saw us coming. We spent half the morning doing an awkward “side-step shuffle dance” at every pass until we decided on a strict rule: always stick to the far right edge of the path.

To a long-distance hiker accustomed to a single marked trail, walking backward through a network like this takes constant focus.

Our route wound through narrow paved roads connecting medieval hamlets tucked into the hillsides. It felt like an endless labyrinth of cobblestone alleyways.

Because every arrow blaze was painted for northbound travelers, we constantly had to stop at intersections, turn around, and hunt for marks hidden behind stone walls.

To make matters busier, this section serves as the final 100 km for pilgrims on the Portuguese route seeking their Compostela certificates:.

After our peaceful, quiet walk to Finisterre, jumping right back into the final 100 km circus was a shock to the system. We passed over a thousand people in the first two hours alone.

At one point, we took a single-track dirt path leading through a lovely forest. Noticeable red arrow blazes had replaced the blue ones, so we decided to follow them.

The path grew narrower and narrower, and soon we began spotting piles of toilet paper left behind by crowds unfamiliar with Leave No Trace (LNT) principles. Realizing we had strayed off the main route, we turned around and backtracked to the original junction.

We resumed our meander through ancient hamlets before spilling out onto a busy highway near A Escravitude, passing its grand yet worn-down cathedral along the roadside.

As the warm sun heated up the afternoon, we pushed through an eclectic mix of industrial zones, busy highways, railroad tracks, and quiet medieval villages.

Industry observers note that with three miles left, the oncoming pilgrim traffic finally thinned out. But right on cue, we got turned around again inside a tiny medieval village with narrow pathways.

Rather than backtracking, we decided to test Google Maps to navigate through the alleyways back to the official trail:.

Taking these brief detours through the “back country” turned out to be a great way to avoid the crowds, escape the trail litter, and explore off-the-beaten-path villages.

It has been revealed that before long, we reached Padrón. Because this town marks the starting point for many 100 km section-hikers heading north, we expect the heavy trail traffic to drop off significantly starting tomorrow.

Sources indicate that after dumping our packs in our room, we wandered the narrow alleyways of this small medieval town. Under heavy renovation, the main cathedral was covered in scaffolding. A river walk led to a large old Roman bridge framed by a large convent on a hill.

We picked up dinner ingredients across the street, cooked in our shared kitchen, and settled in for the night. Walking the Camino Portuguese in reverse is proving to be vastly different from the Camino Francés—and heading the “wrong way” makes every mile a unique adventure.
