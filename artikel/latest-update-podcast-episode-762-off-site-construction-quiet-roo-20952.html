Make it Easier to Build More Modular Housing.

I’m an owner-builder in Bay City, Oregon, in Marine Climate Zone 4C. I’m planning a single-slope roof with a 3:12 pitch and a 24-ft. clear span using parallel-chord trusses. My assembly plan consists of standing-seam aluminum roofing over purlins, 4 in. of continuous polyiso, taped Zip sheathing as the primary air barrier, and a truss cavity filled with 8 in. of mineral wool or fiberglass batts, for a target total insulation value of R-56. The interior will be acoustic fabric (a black muslin theater fabric) and then wood slats with gaps to form an acoustic ceiling. I’m not planning for an interior membrane planned so the acoustic function works as designed.

Sources indicate that my question is: With 4 in. of exterior polyiso and Zip sheathing serving as the primary air barrier, what building science function would an additional interior air or vapor-control layer serve? Would an acoustically open ceiling be compatible with this assembly, or am I missing a key risk? If so, do you have any ideas how I might make this work?

As a fellow Oregonian, I know the 4C climate zone well. In a lot of ways, its a Goldilocks zone—not too humid, not too dry. The climate tends to be pretty forgiving, with the exception of our frequent rains.

Industry observers note that as I evaluate assemblies, I always define and break out the control layers to see if there are any problems. Beyond water, air, vapor, and thermal, it is good to consider wind and fire control layers. The most glaring omission I see is a well-defined water control layer. I consider roofing to be a water-shedding layer, as water can get through via penetrations, mistakes, etc. You blueprint to use polyiso, but you didn’t specify the facer type. In our area, the most commonly used facer in the roofing community is fiberglass faced, but aluminum facer is easily sourced, too. The former would make a terrible water control layer and the latter would be okay, except you would have to tape all the seams, and aluminum isn’t the most durable. I would start by installing a self-adhered WRB or roof underlayment directly on top of the polyiso. The permeability doesn’t really matter (more on that to come). This would be your primary bulk-water control layer. The next most vital layer is the air control layer. As long as you tape the seams of the Zip sheathing, you should be good there. Pay attention to how  the roof air barrier connects to the wall air barrier to maintain continuity.

Sources indicate that for vapor control, of all the materials in this assembly, foil-faced polyiso would be the least permeable, as it is a Class 1 vapor barrier. If the polyiso is fiberglass-faced, it is a Class 3 semi-permeable vapor retarder, with a permeance of roughly 1 to 4 perms. The OSB in the Zip is roughly the same at 3 perms. In one sense, vapor control becomes less of a concern because you have created a vented assembly with the purlins below the metal roofing. In another sense, it may be a moot point because the thickness of the insulation should keep the Zip sheathing warm enough to prevent condensation. At approximately R-20, the exterior insulation would also meet the code requirement in our area. If vapor were to pass through, it would simply get whisked away via the vented gap. If you were to put a vapor-closed roof underlayment on top of the fiberglass-faced polyiso, the combination would essentially a layer similar to foil facing. In both cases, you are fine because the insulation is thick enough to keep condensation from forming on the bottom side of the Zip. If anything, a vapor-open membrane would be slightly more resilient, but we are splitting hairs in the weeds at this point.

In a recent development, for the thermal control layer, the main change I would make is to switch to two layers of 2-in. polyiso boards in lieu of one 4-in. layer. Rigid insulation is prone to shrink, and by offsetting the seams you will avoid numerous thermal breaks throughout the assembly. On the interior, pick whichever fluffy insulation you like. Eight inches should work well, but adding a few more Rs wouldn’t hurt, either. From a wind-washing control perspective, mineral wool and wood fiber are denser than fiberglass, so I would consider those. One wants to avoid the thermal degradation from wind washing– think about a sweater on a windy day.

Patrick’s point about the fire control is valid. If you were to install a layer of Sheetrock and fire-tape it, its vapor-open nature means it wouldn’t affect the assembly at all. From a sound-control perspective, you might consider applying an elastomeric acoustic sealant and/or installing resilient channels before hanging the Sheetrock. From that point, adding the black muslin fabric and wood slats would work well.

I’m reaching out for some guidance. Earlier this summer during a storm, we lost a large tree in front of our house. But without that tree blocking much of the afternoon and setting sun, the AC has clearly been working harder this summer, making it obvious that more than a new tree is needed.
