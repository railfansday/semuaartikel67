Recent findings suggest that A map app can spot a traffic jam before drivers reach it. Here’s how phones detect slowdowns, predict delays, and sometimes get it wrong.

Most drivers know this experience. They’re en route somewhere when their smartphone map app starts pushing back their ETA. Soon, brake lights appear ahead as traffic slows to a crawl.

But how did the navigation app know about the traffic before the driver encountered it? Popular navigation apps like Google Maps, Apple Maps, and Waze use real-time location data from drivers’ phones to detect slowdowns and estimate traffic conditions.

Learn what’s happening and why the estimates aren’t always right.

Map apps combine location data from many phones to understand traffic conditions on the road. Google explains that it uses combined location data for this, and Waze says drivers share real-time information just by driving with the app open. When enough drivers slow down in the same area, the app can show a backup on the map.

Location information plays a major role in this process, although apps also take steps to protect consumers’ privacy. Apple Maps’s privacy statement reads, “Use of your location is required for most Maps features to function optimally. Because your location can give away your identity, we convert precise locations to less-exact locations within 24 hours. When you use Maps for navigation, we convert precise locations from the beginning of your route to a less exact area.”.

Sources indicate that apps also use past traffic information to predict delays before drivers encounter them. “To predict what traffic will look like in the near future, Google Maps analyzes historical traffic patterns for roads over time,” Google’s wevsite states.

These predictions help apps estimate arrival times and suggest alternate routes when traffic starts building.

In a recent development, “Our predictive traffic models are also a key part of how Google Maps determines driving routes,” the Google website continues. “If we predict that traffic is likely to become heavy in one direction, we’ll automatically find you a lower-traffic alternative. We also look at a number of other factors, like road quality. Is the road paved or unpaved, or covered in gravel, dirt or mud?”.

Drivers can also report problems such as accidents and hazards through Google Maps, Apple Maps, and Waze, though the options vary by app and region. These reports help explain why traffic has slowed down. Google notes it also uses data from local governments, which can flag road restrictions such as construction.

The colored lines on the map show where traffic is building. In most navigational systems, green means no traffic delays, orange means medium traffic, and red means delays, with darker red indicating slower speeds.

It has been revealed that traffic updates are generally useful, but they aren’t always accurate. Apple, for example, claims it displays incident markers only when it has a high level of confidence in the reports it receives, so a fresh incident may take time to appear. Coverage also varies depending on how much information the apps receive. On less-traveled roads, fewer drivers may contribute location data, making it harder for apps to detect slowdowns in real time.

Arrival times are estimates, too. Google states its predictions combine historical patterns with live conditions, but it also notes that historical patterns aren’t always reliable indicators of what the road will look like. Conditions can also change unexpectedly because of events like snowstorms.

Map apps can help drivers avoid delays, but their estimates aren’t guaranteed. Drivers still need to follow road signs and pay attention to the conditions around them.
