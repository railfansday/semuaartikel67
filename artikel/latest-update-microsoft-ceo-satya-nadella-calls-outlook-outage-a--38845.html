Latest Update: Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella calls Outlook outage a 'perfect storm.' Here's what went wrong

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Microsoft could have done more to prevent the Outlook outage last week that brought swaths of corporate America to a standstill, CEO Satya Nadella acknowledged in an interview with CNBC's Jim Cramer.

Sources indicate that "It was one of those crazy perfect storms where there was a latent bug that got sort of found only during a particular traffic pattern," Nadella said on "Mad Money" on Wednesday evening. "Of course, we should have done the testing before. We should have figured out how to have the bulkheads such that you didn't have a global outage.".

The Outlook disruption last Thursday was the first domino to fall in a rough week for the technology sector, as a string of major outages disrupted everything from corporate communications to airline operations and banking transactions. The incidents underscored just how dependent everyday life has become on technology — and how indispensable Microsoft is to corporate America. That role is why its stock has long been a cornerstone of investors' technology exposure.

Nadella mentioned the incident highlighted a broader challenge facing corporate America: as AI helps companies uncover software bugs faster, the growing number of updates needed to fix them can introduce instability into older systems.

It has been revealed that "The thing that AI has done is it allowed us to find these bugs and fix these bugs. But that means you've got to then patch them, and that creates instability," he noted, explaining that there's been an increase in how quickly companies develop, test and deploy fixes, known as "patch velocity." He added, "And I think a lot of what you're seeing is a little bit of, maybe, old infrastructure with upcoming patches and instability.".

In a recent development, despite Microsoft's critical role in the business world, its stock had a rough start to the year as investors worried that advancements in artificial intelligence could upend seat-based software businesses. But the narrative started to shift over the summer, supported by Microsoft's strong cloud growth and relative spending discipline versus its hyperscaler peers.

Shares bottomed out for the year in late June, and since the company's late-July earnings report, the stock has climbed 30%. The momentum has stalled a bit of late, though. The stock has pulled back nearly 9% over the past two weeks since its all-time closing high on Nov. 4, the day after the midterm elections.

Sources indicate that nevertheless, Microsoft's latest earnings on Oct. 28 reinforced its business momentum. Azure cloud revenue surged 48% to $33.2 billion, highlighting robust demand for AI computing power. At its Ignite conference Tuesday, Microsoft also laid out its vision for Copilot as the central hub for workplace productivity, making the case that AI could strengthen its dominance in enterprise software rather than undermine it.

Copilot has faced criticism for lagging behind the AI offerings from Anthropic and OpenAI, in which Microsoft was an early investor. Nadella highlighted Microsoft's September overhaul of Copilot, which brought its AI tools into a single app and introduced consumption-based pricing for more advanced features. By charging customers for usage, Microsoft is helping to defend against a decline in the number of paid seat licenses, which had been at the heart of the AI disruption thesis earlier this year.

In a recent development, "So the consumption will go up, and people will have to have new business models. So, for example, in Dynamics now, a lot of our business is not about seats; it's usage," Nadella said. Dynamics is Microsoft's customer relationship management and enterprise resource planning application.

The Copilot update also reflected Microsoft's push to become less reliant on OpenAI's models in its own applications as their partnership evolves.

"You want Claude, you want OpenAI, or our own models? You can route across all of them," Nadella remarked.

Sources indicate that another one of the debates surrounding Microsoft and other cloud operators is whether their AI buildout plans will be derailed by growing opposition to data center construction. Some on Wall Street, including CNBC's Jim Cramer, argue that established tech giants like Microsoft stand to benefit, at the expense of smaller players such as the neoclouds.

Nadella acknowledged the challenge, emphasizing that companies must demonstrate the tangible benefits of their investments to the communities hosting their facilities.

Industry observers note that "We have to win the hearts and minds and the votes one community at a time," Nadella said. "It's not about even a narrative that I can tell you, but it is about the reality that communities feel.".

He pointed to Quincy, Washington, where Microsoft has operated a data center for roughly two decades, as an example of how such investments can benefit local economies through jobs, tax revenue and infrastructure improvements.

"The overall tax benefits that have come in have all gone to building a new school, a new city center, lots of the facilities that the people in the community love, and that and oh by the way the wages, the average wages of the people who are employed there is twice the average wage before," he said. "So we have created long term employment.".

It has been revealed that nadella also addressed Jensen Huang's departure from Nvidia, which has dominated headlines from Silicon Valley to Wall Street and Washington. Microsoft is a big buyer of Nvidia chips for its Azure cloud.

Recent findings suggest that "Jensen, let's face it, was a singular founder," he said. "He did a fantastic job building a great manufacturer. And by the way, I know [interim CEO Colette Kress]. She worked at Microsoft for a long time. She's a terrific person. Ian Buck, Nick Parker, who worked with us, who's there. So [these] are all fantastic people at Nvidia, and we are very thrilled to be partnering with the company Jensen built with the people that are at Nvidia. And then, of course, it's the Nvidia board will decide what happens next.".
