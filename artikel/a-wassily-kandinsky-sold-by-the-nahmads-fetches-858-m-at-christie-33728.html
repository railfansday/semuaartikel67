A Wassily Kandinsky Sold by the Nahmads Fetches $85.8 M. at Christie’s, Notching a Latest Auction High

At Christie’s 20th-century art sale on Tuesday night, a painting by Russian-born pioneer of abstraction Wassily Kandinsky notched a new high at auction when it hammered for $74 million; with the house’s fees, the final sale amount was $85.8 million.

Betonte Ecken (1922–23) carried a $60–$80 million estimate and was guaranteed to sell by the house, financed in full or in part by a third party, as noted in the sale catalogue. Christie’s confirmed to ARTnews ahead of the sale that it was bound to sell at a record-setting level. It was tied for the highest-estimated lot in the house’s marquee November sales with a landscape by Austrian market titan Gustav Klimt, also on Tuesday. According to recent reports, the work sold after a short bidding war between Christie’s global president Alex Rotter, Sara Friedlander, chairman of post-war & contemporary art, Americas at Christie’s, and art advisor Ralph De Luca. Rotter came away the victor, with the winning $74 million bid.

The seller was the prominent Lebanese-American Nahmad family, which acquired the canvas at Sotheby’s Latest York in 1986, ARTnews revealed in October; according to Sotheby’s, it hammered at that sale for $1 million. (It had also come to market at the same house in 1964, when it hammered for $61,000.) The art-dealing dynasty’s holdings are reputed to include as many as 5,000 artworks, collectively worth upwards of $4 billion.

It has been revealed that kandinsky’s previous high was $44.8 million, paid for the artist’s 1910 painting Murnau mit Kirche II (Murnau with Church II) at Sotheby’s London in 2023. Some 14 Kandinsky canvases have fetched prices north of $20 million since 1990, according to the Artnet auction price database; all but one has sold since 2012. His third-highest price, $41.8 million, was achieved in October, when Christie’s London sold his 1907 painting Das Bunte Leben (La vie mélangée).

Betonte Ecken measures just over four feet square and is marked by dynamic, abstract geometric shapes; the four corners are each pointed to with strong shapes and lines, in keeping with its title, which translates as Accentuated Corners.

Sources indicate that the painting has changed hands a number of times. Christie’s provenance shows it first coming to auction at the Jacob Hecht Auction House in Berlin in 1926, from the Schwarz collection, then going to that city’s Galerie Nierendorf, which then sold it, probably in 1929, to Hilla Rebay. She was then the principal adviser to museum founder Solomon R. Guggenheim, an early supporter of abstract art in the US. Guggenheim later acquired it from Rebay, and it remained in the museum’s collection until 1964, when the museum sold it at Sotheby’s London to Fresh York art dealer Stephen Hahn. At some point, Hahn sold it to Boston collectors Sandra and David Bakalar, who put it up for auction at Sotheby’s in 1986, when it went to the Nahmads.

Born in Moscow in 1866, Kandinsky published the book On the Spiritual in Art in 1911 and was part of the influential Blaue Reiter group. After his first solo exhibition, at Der Sturm gallery in Berlin in 1912, one of his works was included in the groundbreaking 1913 Armory Show exhibition in Newly introduced York. He lived in Russia from 1914 to 1921, mainly in Moscow, where he maintained a position at the People’s Commissariat of Education, until moving to Weimar in 1922. His first New York solo was in 1923, organized by Société Anonyme, the organization founded in 1920 by Marcel Duchamp, Katherine Dreier, and Man Ray.

Kandinsky became a German citizen in 1928, but would relocate to France when the Nazi government closed the Bauhaus in 1933, becoming a French citizen in 1939. The Nazis seized dozens of his works in the party’s campaign against “degenerate art.” Kandinsky died in France in December 1944, three days short of his 78th birthday and six months shy of Germany’s surrender to Allied forces.
