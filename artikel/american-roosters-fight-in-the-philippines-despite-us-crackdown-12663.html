By Francesca Regalado and Mark Walker.

Francesca Regalado reported from Bacolod City in the Philippines.

Jerry Jalandoni navigated hundreds of chickens running loose on his farm in the Philippines before stopping at a row of tall cages that held his latest acquisitions: a dozen cockfighting roosters from the United States.

Sources indicate that on top of one cage was a cardboard box, poked with holes that had allowed a hen to breathe during her solitary journey from Texas to Bacolod City. “She’s been through a lot,” Mr. Jalandoni said.

The hen arrived alone, he mentioned, because her mate had been held back by exporters wary of attracting the attention of animal rights groups. Such birds come from game fowl breeds developed in the United States that are renowned internationally for their sturdiness and aggression — qualities valued in cockfighting, a legal and highly profitable industry in the Philippines.

Industry observers note that those birds were not supposed to be on Mr. Jalandoni’s farm, according to U.S. federal laws. But they are part of a trade in which breeders in the United States send fighting roosters to the Philippines.

While the trade has long been an open secret, the channels through which American fighting birds arrive in Asia are drawing fresh scrutiny from U.S. officials.

Industry observers note that cockfighting — the practice of tying metal blades, or gaffs, to the legs of roosters that fight each other to the death — has been illegal in the United States since 2008, when Louisiana became the last state to outlaw it. Transporting animals across state lines or out of the country for the purpose of fighting was banned in 1976.
