Two Cheers for the Supreme Court - Though Not More

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that in  a much-discussed recent New York Times article, University of Chicago law Prof. (and former VC blogger) Will Baude praises the Supreme Court and its independence:.

Industry observers note that with their sweeping midterm victory, Democrats are exploring ideas for a package of reforms to the Supreme Court. And it's not just Democrats: In recent months, the court has managed to invite calls for reform from both sides of the aisle. Some conservatives are even willing to discuss expanding the number of justices.

Industry observers note that still, these calls do not reflect a bipartisan agreement on what is wrong with the court. The calls for reform simply reflect the fact that the court has managed to offend people on the left and right. Defying the Trump administration on key agenda items while also enforcing colorblindness and overruling Roe v. Wade has left it with few true fans.

Sources indicate that the left, understandably, strongly opposes the court's recent rulings on abortion, the Voting Rights Act, the unitary executive and so on. These decisions are a substantial deal. But court reformers still suffer from a failure of perspective. If Chief Justice John Roberts could write an opinion essay, I would imagine him saying: "What? Ruling against the administration's misuse of the National Guard, sweeping emergency tariffs and attack on birthright citizenship, plus preserving the independence of the Federal Reserve and the integrity of the midterm elections, wasn't enough for you?".

The court has ruled against the administration in high-stakes cases on those complications all in the past year. And it has done so even though six of the justices were appointed by members of the president's party — three by President Trump himself….

Recent findings suggest that under the circumstances of the second Trump term, the Supreme Court is doing amazingly well. If anything, it deserves our celebration, not our condemnation.

I agree with much of this. I made similar points in two articles critiquing proposals for court-packing (see here and here), emphasizing the importance of the Court's rulings against Trump, and how they were not simple gimmes. In each case, there were at least minimally plausible arguments the other way, such that a Court intent on helping Trump and the GOP at all costs could have found a way to do it:.

Over the last 18 months, the Supreme Court has played a key role in thwarting several major Trump power grabs. The most recent example is the birthright citizenship case, which prevented him from stripping citizen status from hundreds of thousands of children of undocumented migrants and temporary visa holders, thereby subjecting them to potential deportation.

Industry observers note that in Learning Resources v. Trump (which I helped litigate), the court struck down Trump's attempt to use the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) to impose the highest tariff schedule since the Hoover-era Smoot-Hawley tariffs that notoriously exacerbated the Great Depression. Trump's tariffs would have imposed some $1.7 trillion of new taxes on Americans. The court's reasoning was not limited to the specifics of IEEPA. All six justices in the majority made clear that they rejected the notion that the president could have a virtually unlimited power to impose tariffs. Thus, the precedent is likely to help block his efforts to twist other statutes into authorization for unconstrained presidential tariff authority….

In lesser-known cases, the court blocked Trump's effort to use the National Guard against blue states (thereby causing him to abandon the effort) and twice used brief "shadow docket" decisions to block deportations under the Alien Enemies Act of 1798 and signal that courts could review Trump's invocation of that law….

It has been revealed that [Some critics] acknowledg… these second-term defeats are real yet insist… that they are just "a floor." But protecting people from massive power grabs that gravely endangered the liberty and well-being of millions is no small matter. The same goes for blocking executive use of the military to suppress domestic opposition. And, in each of these cases, the Trump administration had at least minimally plausible arguments for their positions, sometimes backed by prominent conservative legal scholars and commentators. A court inclined to give Trump and the GOP whatever they want could easily have gone the other way on some or all of them. Even if the court could and should have done more to constrain Trump, these rulings are crucial safeguards for liberty and democracy.

Unlike Josh Blackman, I think the conservative justices' willingness to rule against Trump and GOP priorities is a notable measure of their independence. It's a strong sign that they are not simply partisan hacks, and not beholden to any president or party. That doesn't prove that any time a conservative judge rules against a Republican president, they are necessarily right to do so. Being independent and being right are not the same thing. But both are important.

It has been revealed that at the same time, I am less of a fan of the current Supreme Court majority than Will Baude is. In my view, they have made several very bad decisions:.

Recent findings suggest that still, it is true that the conservative majority has issued some very bad rulings. I agree with Craig that Trump v. United States—the 2024 presidential immunity ruling—is one of them. But that decision does not give the president blanket immunity from all criminal prosecution for actions taken while in office or even categorical immunity for all "official acts." It's a bad decision but not one that totally eliminates criminal prosecution as a check on presidential abuses of power…. [I also have ambivalent views on Trump v. Slaughter and Louisiana v. Callais].

Sources indicate that in my view, the Roberts Court has made several decisions that are worse than any of the above. Most notable are Trump v. Hawaii, the awful 2018 anti-Muslim "travel ban" case, and the similar recent decision in Mullin v. Doe, the Haitian TPS case. In both of these cases, the court's conservative justices turned a blind eye to blatantly unconstitutional discrimination in the immigration context (in one case based on religion, in the other on race). This is part of the court's broader pattern of indefensible double standards when it comes to immigration restrictions.

On top of that, the Roberts Court recently gave us the Supreme Court's worst Takings Clause ruling in many years, undermining its previously generally good record on constitutional property rights.

Thus, I am only willing to offer two cheers for the Court. To earn three, the justices would need to give us fewer stinkers!

That noted, the Court's record in checking Trump should give pause to advocates of court-packing and other "reform" measures that would have the effect of destroying judicial review. As explained more fully in my two recent articles critiquing court-packing (here and here) the proper baseline of comparison is not an ideal Supreme Court, but one that would have little or no ability to check the party in power, especially if that party controls both the White House and Congress. The latter is the likely result of court-packing. By that standard, the Roberts Court has had a massive beneficial impact. Between them, the birthright citizenship, tariff, and National Guard decisions have saved millions of people from severe threats to their livelihoods and civil liberties (most notably hundreds of thousands of children saved from potential deportation under Trump's birthright citizenship executive order).

It has been revealed that like Baude, I am not opposed to all possible Supreme Court reforms. For example, I am a long-time supporter of Supreme Court term limits. I am also open to imposing tighter ethics constraints on the justices, and have criticized Justice Alito's claim that Congress lacks power to regulate the Court on such matters. Again, like Baude, I highly doubt that ethics reforms would have any significant impact on the Court's rulings. But it may still be desirable to constrain outside gifts and the like, if only to prevent more serious ethics concerns from potentially arising in the future.

I also think some proposals to better manage and constrain the "shadow docket" may be desirable, though I will not try to cover this difficult matter here.

Industry observers note that finally, Baude is right that the Court's relative unpopularity is a potential danger. It is, as he suggests, caught between progressives who believe it's way too conservative, and right-wingers who blame it for constraining Trump too much.

At the same time, it's worth remembering that the Supreme Court is less unpopular than either of the other branches of government, and that most of its major recent decisions enjoy majority public support. As I always emphasize, a decision's popularity is far from a good indication of its correctness. But such (relative) popularity can potentially protect the Court against political attack.

Recent findings suggest that finally, I would note this: As prominent conservative legal commentator Ed Whelan points out, the results of the recent midterm election make it highly likely that Justice Alito, Justice Thomas, or both will ultimately be replaced by a liberal Democratic nominee. Democrats considering court-packing should ask themselves whether they really rather destroy judicial review, or see it wielded by what may well soon be a liberal court.

Industry observers note that for my part, I have plenty of differences with the current conservative majority. And if it is replaced by a more liberal one, I expect to have many disagreements with that majority, as well. But either is far preferable to just getting rid of judicial review entirely. The Trump era has provided plenty of additional evidence for that proposition.
