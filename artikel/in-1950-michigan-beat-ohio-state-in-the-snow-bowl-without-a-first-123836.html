In 1950, Michigan beat Ohio State in the Snow Bowl without a first down, and the blizzard behind it dumped up to 62 inches of snow

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The Snow Bowl storm, later called the Great Appalachian Storm, contributed to at least 160 deaths and is the worst Ohio Valley snowstorm NOAA has analyzed.

It has been revealed that on the Saturday after Thanksgiving in 1950, Ohio State and Michigan played their annual game in Columbus in a blizzard. At noon, the temperature at the city’s airport, a few miles from Ohio Stadium, was 10°F, and visibility was half a mile.

Michigan won 9-3. It did so without a single first down and without completing a pass, according to the National Weather Service. The game became known as the Snow Bowl, and some call it the Blizzard Bowl.

Industry observers note that the storm that buried the field was much bigger than one football game. Over the following days, it dropped as much as 62 inches of snow in West Virginia, sent temperatures down to 3°F in Atlanta, and pushed hurricane-force gusts into the Northeast.

It ranks as the worst Ohio Valley snowstorm analyzed by scientists at the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA).

The game’s place in history has little to do with the quality of the football, the weather service’s office in Wilmington, Ohio, states.

It is remembered for “the weather, as the game was played in a significant blizzard unlike any that either school had played in before, or since.”.

Neither team could move the ball, so they kept punting it away. Michigan punted 24 times, and its total offense for the whole game was 27 yards. Ohio State punted 21 times, and four of those punts were blocked by the Wolverines.

In a recent development, all of the scoring came from blocked kicks. Ohio State blocked a punt that led to a field goal. Two of Michigan’s blocks led to a safety and a touchdown.

“It speaks to the severity of the storm, the remarkable conditions, and just how debilitating extreme weather can be,” the Wilmington office wrote.

Sources indicate that the weather service has analyzed the afternoon’s hourly observations at Port Columbus Airport. The temperature climbed only from 10°F at noon to 16°F at 4 p.m.

Sustained winds blew at 17 to 22 mph, with gusts of more than 35 mph, so wind chills stayed between -8°F and -1°F. Visibility dropped as low as a quarter of a mile in snow and blowing snow.

Sources indicate that two days earlier, the weather had been very different. On Thursday evening, November 23, which was Thanksgiving Day, it was 39°F in Columbus at 8 p.m.

Industry observers note that an arctic cold front then moved through, and by midnight it was 20°F. By 7 a.m. on Friday, the temperature had fallen to 8°F.

Industry observers note that on the day of the game, 7.5 inches of snow fell on Columbus. The weather service says that is still the city’s record for a single November day.

It has been revealed that from Thursday night to the following Tuesday, the city got 14.9 inches. Its November 1950 total of 15.2 inches remains the snowiest November on record there.

The cold front that arrived on Thanksgiving night was the precursor to the Great Appalachian Storm of 1950.

According to the weather service office in Jackson, Kentucky, the arctic air behind it sent temperatures to all-time November lows across the Upper Midwest and the Ohio Valley.

Chicago dropped to -2°F on November 24, tying its lowest November reading, and the next day Louisville hit -1°F and Lexington -3°F, both records for the month.

In a recent development, on November 25, low pressure formed over the Carolinas, the Jackson office says. It moved northwest into Ohio by the middle of the next day, spreading heavy snow as it went, then spun over Lake Erie before it weakened and left for Canada on November 29 and 30.

The snow piled up from western Pennsylvania deep into West Virginia. Several places received more than 50 inches, and Coburn Creek, West Virginia, reported the largest total, 62 inches, according to NOAA’s National Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI). Pittsburgh got about 30 inches.

Recent findings suggest that the cold reached far into the South. Temperatures fell to 22°F in Pensacola, Florida, 5°F in Birmingham, Alabama, 3°F in Atlanta and 1°F in Asheville, North Carolina, NCEI notes, and the record cold damaged crops in Georgia and South Carolina.

The storm drew arctic air in on one side and warm Atlantic air in on the other. While Pittsburgh was buried under snow at single-digit temperatures, Buffalo, Fresh York, about 200 miles to the north, had temperatures in the 40s and no snow at all, the Jackson office states.

In a recent development, in the Northeast, the problem was wind. New York City recorded a gust of 94 mph, and Newark, New Jersey, 108 mph. Bear Mountain, just north of New York City, had a 140 mph gust, and Mount Washington in New Hampshire reached 160 mph.

The winds caused extensive tree damage and power outages, and coastal flooding that broke through dikes at LaGuardia Airport and flooded its runways.

Fierce winds are still a feature of significant East Coast storms, as earth.com saw when blizzard winds battered the coast in a more recent storm.

Heavy rain on the warm side of the storm brought near-record flooding to eastern Pennsylvania. Then, in the first week of December, unusually warm weather melted the snow quickly. The Ohio River rose to 28.5 feet in Pittsburgh and 56 feet in Cincinnati, 4 feet above flood stage in both cities.

Recent findings suggest that the storm contributed to at least 160 deaths, NCEI states. Insurance companies paid out more for its damage than for any earlier storm or hurricane, according to the Jackson office. They call it the costliest storm on record up to that time. NCEI describes it as one of the costliest storms on record.

NCEI rates major snowstorms with its Regional Snowfall Index, which combines how much snow fell, how large an area it covered and how many people lived there.

The 1950 storm scored 34.7 in the Ohio Valley, the highest of the 232 storms analyzed there. The second-worst, in March 1993, scored 24.6. In that region, more than 6.1 million people lived where at least 18 inches of snow fell.

In the Northeast, it ranks ninth of 230 storms, just behind the storms of February 2003, February 2010 and January 2016.

Scientists are still studying how storms like this work. NASA-backed scientists have flown research planes straight into snowstorms to learn how their heaviest bands of snow form.

In a recent development, the information was obtained from the National Weather Service’s pages Snow Bowl – November 25, 1950 (Wilmington, Ohio) and The Great Appalachian Storm of 1950 (Jackson, Kentucky), from NOAA NCEI’s article The Great Appalachian Storm in Historical Context, and the Michigan vs. Ohio State team game records of the University of Michigan’s Bentley Historical Library and the Ohio State University Archives.
