TESTOSTERONE GOES BEYOND building muscle. T helps you maintain muscle mass, facial and body hair, sex drive, and red blood cell production. So when your T levels start to decline—which happens naturally in your thirties—there’s a whole range of symptoms like low libido or just feeling blue.

Testosterone replacement therapy (TRT) can help raise T levels. After a couple of weeks to a month, you’ll start to see some changes, some big and small. Some pleasant and not so pleasant. Below, urologists explain what happens to your body a month after taking testosterone.

Sources indicate that IF YOU HAVE low T, your doctor will conduct some tests to see if you’re a good candidate for the therapy. Ryan Smith, MD, urologist specializing in men’s health at the University of Virginia Health, claims a doctor will perform two blood tests in the morning (on different days) when testosterone is at its highest.

Low is considered under 300 nanograms per deciliter (ng/dL) by some accounts and under 264 ng/dL by others. Doctors also factor in any symptoms you’re having, like trouble getting erections or orgasms, fatigue, or just feeling blah.

Nowadays, testosterone is more accessible without a prescription, with many people only having to complete an online assessment. Leaving out a doctor’s approval and supervision, however, can be harmful, states Justin Dubin, MD, urologist at Baptist Health.

In a 2022 study that Dr. Dubin co-authored, researchers found that many online platforms are not providing testosterone therapy in accordance with the American Urological Association and the Endocrine Society guidelines. Using a secret shopper, the researchers found that the platforms offered therapy to men who didn’t meet the guidelines and didn’t mention the risks or benefits of testosterone therapy.

Working with a doctor ensures that you’re prescribed testosterone only when you need it and that you’re taking the right dose. Then, they can work with you to make sure your testosterone levels respond appropriately. Doctors like to recheck everything every six months to make sure you’re not having any negative side effects.

A number of testosterone options are available. You can choose between injectables, topical gels, creams, under-the-skin pellet therapy, pills, and nasal sprays. You can learn more about them here.

It has been revealed that each comes with its own set of side effects, so it all comes down to your personal preference, health situation, and what your insurance covers. You can read about the real-life experiences of guys of different ages and body types and their experiences with taking testosterone.

In a recent development, IT CAN TAKE a few weeks to a month or two to see your symptoms improve after starting testosterone replacement therapy, says Dr. Smith. Here are some of the side effects—some obvious and some not—that come with testosterone therapy.

It has been revealed that when you’re low on testosterone, you might notice your sex drive dip. But when you take TRT, the treatment reactivates the androgen receptors in the part of your brain that controls desire, notes Abraham Morgentaler, MD, associate clinical professor of urology at Harvard Medical School and author of Testosterone for Life. Regaining a healthy sex drive is one of the biggest benefits of testosterone replacement therapy, he says.

In a recent development, TRT may also make your erections more satisfying, though this isn’t the only piece of the puzzle. Erections also rely on healthy nerves and blood flow. That means that testosterone therapy by itself won't always help erectile dysfunction.

Muscles respond to testosterone and starting TRT can increase muscle mass, notes Dr. Morgentaler. That’s because testosterone activates the androgen receptors in muscle tissue to stimulate growth.

The effect of a testosterone replacement dose on your muscles, however, is fairly minimal. To make the most of this muscle-building benefit, you’ll need to be doing your part through strength training and possibly raising the amount of protein you consume (depending on your current intake). Some men also report fat loss. While testosterone doesn’t directly incite fat loss itself, part of it may be thanks to the uptick in muscle mass. The more muscle you have, the higher your basal metabolic rate (BMR). That means your body will burn more calories at rest.

Another indirect way testosterone can help with fat loss is motivation to keep pushing once you see results. Jed Kaminetsky, MD, urologist at NYU Langone Medical Center, testosterone can increase your energy levels, making it easier to get up and sweat it out.

Sources indicate that fatigue is a common symptom of low T. “When we treat them, a lot of men will say that their energy has improved,” indicates Dr. Morgentaler.

Scientists studying testosterone aren't sure exactly how the hormone plays a role in energy—just that it does. One of the prevailing hypotheses Dr. Morgentaler can get behind is that testosterone somehow affects your mitochondria (that organelle you learned in high school is the powerhouse of your cells). The theory is that “testosterone turns them on, so they're more productive in terms of creating the energy that the cells need.”.

Sources indicate that ronald Tamler, MD, PhD, associate professor of medicine at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai, says changes in energy may also be tied back to androgen receptors. “If they don't get sufficient input, that can cause fatigue,” he says. Bringing testosterone levels back up can help reverse this.

Experts don't have a definitive answer to why testosterone impacts mood so deeply—after all, “the brain is a complicated thing,” claims Dr. Tamler. But the positive effect of TRT on mood is a potential life-changing benefit.

It has been revealed that an old but highly referenced clinical trial in 2003 looked at how testosterone gels affected middle-aged men with treatment-resistant depression and low T. After 8 weeks, the men indicated greater improvements in the psychological aspects of depression (sadness, guilt, and anxiety) than those given a placebo. The men taking testosterone gels also showed nearly the same rate of improvement in somatic aspects of depression (improvements in sleep, appetite, and libido). A more recent review of 27 clinical trials involving 1,890 men with low T found that TRT provided a significant reduction in depressive symptoms, especially at higher doses.

One of the most notable things to know before starting TRT is that the treatment decreases sperm production and may cause infertility.

According to recent reports, testosterone might affect the size of your testicles, too. "Most of the size of the testicle is dedicated to making sperm, so when you're making less sperm, the testicles get smaller," says Dr. Morgentaler.

Sperm production may or may not go back to normal even after stopping treatment, states Dr. Kaminetsky. One study found increasing age and longer TRT linked to lower chances of normal sperm recovery.

It has been revealed that some people notice a little swelling in their feet and ankles because testosterone can encourage your body to hold onto excess fluid, says Dr. Morgentaler.

This isn't a big deal for most people, he notes, and swelling is more common if you're taking a non-daily treatment like an injection, when you're getting a higher dose of T in one sitting.

Testosterone replacement therapy may change your skin type—for better or for worse, says Dr. Morgentaler.

Testosterone can increase oil production, which isn't necessarily a bad thing. Oil production is crucial for healthy skin, so you may actually end up with a better complexion. However, too much oily buildup can lead to breakouts.

While Dr. Morgentaler notes testosterone-induced breakouts aren’t common, they are more likely to occur in men with a history of acne. Skin changes may also be more common if you’re taking testosterone injections versus other modes of administration since you're getting a higher dose all at once.

According to recent reports, in all men—whether you’re taking T or not—some testosterone is converted into the hormone estradiol, a form of estrogen. In men who naturally have more breast tissue, testosterone can be converted into estradiol, which may stimulate breast tissue to grow.

This is called gynecomastia, states Dr. Morgentaler, and it's not as common as other side effects. If gynecomastia does happen, your doctor will likely stop treatment for a month or two to allow your breast tissue to go back to normal. Then, you’ll resume T along with a drug that blocks the conversion of testosterone to estradiol.

If you’re going the topical testosterone therapy route, one issue to watch out for is transference with women and children.

Darshan Patel, MD, assistant professor of urology at the University of California San Diego’s Men’s Health Center, indicates testosterone from gels or creams can be transferred through skin-to-skin contact or from clothing and absorbed by someone else.

“You really have to be careful to wash your hands well and not expose others,” adds Dr. Smith. Contact with excess testosterone can affect children’s genitalia and cause premature pubic hair development and aggressive behavior.
