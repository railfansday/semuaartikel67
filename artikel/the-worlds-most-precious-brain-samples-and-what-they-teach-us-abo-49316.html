The world’s most precious brain samples — and what they teach us about being human

Recent findings suggest that across the road from her laboratory, at the Royal Melbourne Hospital in Australia, surgeons are removing brain tissue from a person with brain cancer. They set some aside for Palmer to collect and study. It is about the size of a shelled peanut.

A colleague of hers takes the sample, places it in a tub of artificial cerebrospinal fluid and rushes it back to the lab, securing it in a chamber filled with bubbling carbon dioxide and oxygen to keep it alive.

In a recent development, these precious samples can survive for only a day or so before they start to deteriorate, so Palmer’s team wastes no time. Within half an hour of receiving the tissue, they are recording the electrical current passing between neurons as they fire.

Palmer’s group at the Florey Institute in Melbourne is using live brain tissue to understand more about brain cancers and how the activity of neurons influences tumour growth. Her lab, and others using such samples, are doing something rare — measuring how live, working human neurons behave.

Sources indicate that most studies of brain-cell function rely on tissue taken from people who have died or from living animals. Post-mortem human brains, however, preserve only the structure and not the activity of cells, and the brains of other animals might function differently. “As much as we like mice, I don’t want to understand the mouse brain. I want to understand the human brain,” Palmer states.

Slices from a sample of living human brain ready for study.Credit: Emmalyn P. Leonard.

Sources indicate that working with living brain tissue from another person is both humbling and a great privilege, she notes. “Every sample is precious.” It’s also an artisanal skill: attaching recording equipment to individual neurons requires the neuroscientific equivalent of a gardener’s green fingers. It relies on mastery of a decades-old technique called patch-clamping, which has fallen out of fashion as flashier recording methods have become available.

Despite the barriers, there is increasing interest in this rare resource. The number of studies published about this tissue has increased from just a handful two decades ago to upwards of 40 a year, according to data from Scopus, with the bulk attributed to about ten research teams. Neuroscientists around the world are working with surgeons to access the tissue and keep it alive in their labs for periods of time ranging from 24 hours to a few weeks, investigating the intrinsic electrical properties of cells and how they communicate with each other during both health and disease.

“When looking into a disease, being able to look directly at the human tissue is absolutely vital,” says Palmer.

In a recent development, neuroscientists have many ways to study the human brain: non-invasive scanning, indirect measures such as blood samples and, after death, in slices under the microscope. They can also study brain function directly in animal models and, very rarely, in people who have electrodes implanted while they are undergoing surgery. But none of those approaches allows scientists to measure and manipulate the activity of individual human brain cells in their natural environment.

According to recent reports, that has changed in the past ten years, notes Scott Owen, a neuroscientist at Stanford University in California. Although people have been studying live human tissue since the 1970s, he says, the field has finally reached a point at which enough is known about the mammalian brain to understand how human brains differ from those of other animals.

Recent findings suggest that his team works with paediatric neurosurgeons to acquire tissue taken from children with epilepsy, cancer or abnormal tangles of blood vessels in their brains. By comparing samples from people with different diseases, the researchers can look for features common to all donors and deduce which are conserved properties of the human brain, rather than artefacts of any one condition.

Sources indicate that in a preprint published in September, for instance, Owen and his colleagues found distinct differences in how human pyramidal neurons — significant, bushy brain cells that live in the cortex — mature compared with those of mice1. The team analysed neocortex samples from 24 children and teenagers, ranging in age from 9 months to 18 years, and mouse samples from equivalent developmental stages. The study, which is yet to be peer reviewed, looked at patterns in how neurons fire, or spike. It’s known that human neurons are quicker to spike after a stimulus than are rodent neurons and that this might correlate with intelligence. Owen and his colleagues found that human neurons got faster at responding over the course of development, with neurons from teens spiking more promptly than those from children; the opposite happened in mice.

Slices from a sample of living human brain, ready for study.Credit: Rennie Kendrick.

Sources indicate that when the researchers looked at the genes that might explain how this happens, they found two that increased in expression over time in people, but not in mice. These genes help to create ion channels in cell membranes, which allows signals to travel between neurons. Faults in these genes have been linked to several neurodevelopmental conditions2.

According to recent reports, karri Lämsä, a biophysicist at the Hungarian Centre of Excellence for Molecular Medicine in Szeged, is also interested in how human neurons fire and how these patterns might change with time and new experiences. His team studies centimetre-wide cubes of the cortex that are discarded during surgeries to enable access to deeper parts of the brain. In a study published last year, they looked at a type of neuron that inhibits others. In these cells, the axon — the main trunk — transmits signals faster and spikes at lower thresholds than in the same cells in mice3. Fast-acting neurons of this type are thought to have a crucial role in complex brain functions.

Sources indicate that lämsä’s team has also been studying how networks of these fast-spiking neurons might change with age. In samples taken from 106 people ranging in age from 11 to 84 years, the researchers found that these circuits become weaker over time and the spikes in electricity travelling across the cell membrane become smaller and slower4. These changes could explain why the human brain becomes less capable of learning and processing information as people age.

Industry observers note that other work using live human tissue has challenged a key assumption about the human brain: that it is a scaled-up version of the mouse brain. Peter Jonas, a neuroscientist at the Institute of Science and Technology Austria in Klosterneuburg, is using live human tissue to understand how brain cells function alone and in networks. Jonas had worked with animal models for more than 30 years when, in 2022, he began collaborating with a neurosurgeon at the Medical University of Vienna. He would receive tiny samples of human brain tissue — mainly from the hippocampus — measuring about one-fifth of a teaspoon.

Sources indicate that his lab’s work suggests that some properties of the human brain, such as the number of dendritic spines (tiny outgrowths at the end of synapses that connect neurons), follow different scaling rules from those of the mouse. Neurons are also more densely connected in the human neocortex — the area of the brain thought to control higher functions such as decision-making and planning — than in the mouse one, but have fewer connections in the hippocampus. Jonas states that this kind of information can be gleaned only from living tissue.

In a 2025 Cell study examining tissue taken from the hippocampus and cortex, his team also found that the electrical signals transmitted between neurons were more reliable and precise than they were in mouse brains5. The synapses in human brains successfully triggered the receiving cell to fire 90% of the time, compared with a 62% success rate in mice. Computer modelling by Jonas’s team suggests that reliable transmission could set the human brain apart from those of other species. “We are at the beginning of understanding this,” states Jonas.

Meanwhile, Palmer has observed that the excitability of brain cells differs between humans and rodents6, which could be because human dendrites are twice as wide as those of mice. Differences in size also affect how the cells handle information, she indicates. Larger neurons with longer, bushier branches can form widespread networks, whereas smaller neurons restrict a network to their close neighbours.

Recent findings suggest that palmer also finds human tissue invaluable for studying disease. Cancers in mouse brains are generally composed of a limited number of cell types, whereas every single cell in a human brain tumour is different. Furthermore, most brain cancer research in mice has focused on one particular type of cancer, called glioblastoma.

Recent findings suggest that using slices of human brain tissue, Palmer and her team have found that cancer cells taken from aggressive tumours were more active than those in slower growing tumours7, boosting signalling and forming more synapses with neurons. The researchers also found that neurons were generally more active in people with aggressive tumours and that this increased activity leads to greater spread of cancer cells, confirming results from animal models.

Recent findings suggest that in a study testing the efficacy of a drug for brain cancer, Palmer’s team also saw changes to excitability and signalling in neurons8. Together, the findings could explain how the drug slows down tumour growth, or halts it entirely.

According to recent reports, one of the biggest challenges of working with living human brain tissue is a specialist technique that scientists need to learn. Called patch-clamping, the technique involves pressing the tip of a glass micropipette against the membrane surrounding a neuron and using suction to make a tight seal. A cell’s electrical activity can then be measured through one or several ion channels in the membrane. These measurements are used to study how a neuron behaves in response to different stimuli.

In a recent development, patch-clamping was invented in the 1970s and is an indispensable tool in electrophysiology. It stands in stark contrast to techniques such as calcium imaging and optical imaging, which allow scientists to measure electrical activity in thousands of neurons at once. These techniques are more expensive but less technically challenging, states Palmer. In comparison, “patch-clamping takes a year to learn and years to understand,” she says. “You never master it.”.
