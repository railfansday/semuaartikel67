Military History Museum in Dresden Agrees to Return Contested Nazi-Era Family Archive to Jehovah’s Witnesses

In a recent development, five years after the Jehovah’s Witnesses sued the German government over ownership of an archive tracing one family’s persecution by the Nazi regime, the files have been returned to the religious organization, reports the New York Times. Jehovah’s Witnesses were targeted by the Nazis specifically for their refusal to salute Hitler, display the swastika flag, or serve in the military; 4,500 were sent to concentration camps, and 1,800 died during the Nazi era.

The more than 1,000 documents, photographs, drawings, letters, and reports that make up the archive were collected and saved for decades by Annemarie Kusserow, one of 11 children from a Jehovah’s Witness family that lived in the town of Bad Lippspringe in North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany. According to an oral history published by the U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum, the Kusserow home was searched by the Gestapo 20 times, and the Kusserow parents, Hilda and Franz, were the first to be arrested, in the mid-1930s. The children eventually ended up in prisons, concentration camps, or training schools. Two brothers, Wilhelm and Wolfgang, were murdered by the Nazis, and another survived the war but died shortly after from tuberculosis contracted at Dachau. Industry observers note that at the time of her death in 2005, Annemarie Kusserow’s will stated that her family’s archive should go to the Jehovah’s Witnesses. But one of her brothers, Hans Werner Kusserow, had the archive in his possession at the time, and in 2009 he sold it to the Bundeswehr Military History Museum in Dresden. This was “especially troubling,” the Jehovah’s Witnesses’ official website notes, given that her brothers lost their lives due to their pacifism.

The parties came to an “amicable agreement” on November 19, which was formalized the following day by the Higher Regional Court of Cologne. The museum and the Jehovah’s Witnesses agreed to keep the details of the settlement private. A Kusserow relative told the New York Times it is “a great relief that at last we will have these precious documents back where Annemarie always wanted them.”.
