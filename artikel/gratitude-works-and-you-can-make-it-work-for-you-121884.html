Gratitude Works and You Can Make It Work for You

“Tis the time of year to be grateful (of course), and this might elicit many an eye roll at many Thanksgiving dinner tables. But there’s something to be said about gratitude’s ability to alter our mental and even physical well-being. Before we all tuck into our helpings of turkey this year, here are five surprising benefits gratitude confers, as well as some simple ways to be grateful—even when it feels antithetical to our current state of mind.

People who exhibit more dispositional gratitude have been shown to sleep better. This may be because gratitude, when compared with rumination, is a much more calming way to engage our minds as we wind down. Rumination can ratchet up our emotions and keep us wired. One study found that having individuals with a neuromuscular condition count their blessings each night for three weeks improved their sleep quantity and quality.

Industry observers note that in a 2016 study, 70 patients with heart failure were randomly assigned either to journal about gratitude for eight weeks or to receive treatment as usual. The researchers found statistically significant reductions in inflammatory biomarkers among participants assigned to the gratitude journal condition. Other studies suggest that gratitude may tamp down inflammation by encouraging prosocial activities, like care and support giving, which can downregulate our body’s stress response.

Both dispositional gratitude and momentary (situational) gratitude can tamp down the green monster of envy, eliminating it altogether or at least morphing it from malicious to benign.

Recent findings suggest that A 2020 study assessed athletic performance across several sports and found that a gratitude workshop enhanced performance, improved stress management, and a more positive attitude towards training. Additional studies have found that gratitude can help protect athletes from burnout and increase motivation on and off the field as a function of hope.

An attitude of gratitude can make or break a marriage or long-term partnership. A 2012 study found that partners who were more appreciative of each other were more likely to respond to one another’s needs, demonstrate more commitment, and engage in more relationship maintenance behaviors, like initiating conflict resolution.

If you’re sold enough on gratitude by the above, here are some ways you can practice it in your everyday life. Even when things feel terrible.

Recent findings suggest that on your morning commute, in your daily shower, or when you’re lying in bed trying to sleep, slowly tabulate as many things as you can in your life at present that are good, neutral, or at least not terrible. This is particularly important if you tend to hold grudges, ruminate over wrongs done to you, or generally focus on the negative. As you do this, you help unhook your attention from the things going wrong in your life and interrupt the rumination cycle.

This classic gratitude exercise entails reflecting at the end of each day on three good things that happened from when you woke to when you sat down to journal or quietly reflect. Even on the worst days, this exercise prompts you to seek at least three good things amid the stress. These things needn’t be enormous or life-changing. They can be as simple as a pretty sunset, a strong cup of coffee, or that you have a roof over your head. Once you’ve come up with three good things, go back to the moments in which you experienced them and reflect in depth on what made them possible, who was involved, and how you felt during them.

Write an email, letter, or record a voice or video note thanking someone who’s done something special for you or someone who just lets you be yourself. Better yet, arrange a "thank you" meet-up over coffee or an activity. The thanks doesn’t have to be for something huge, like saving you from a house fire (though this would certainly be something you would want to thank someone for profusely). It can be for something seemingly insignificant, like checking in on you during a busy time. Be detailed and specific in your thanks. Not just “thanks, that was cool.” But, “I so appreciated how you did _____ because _____; it makes me feel ______.” The more detailed we are in our gratitude, the more (positive) effect it has on the recipient and us.

If you genuinely struggle to see the good in everyday life or you’ve gone through a particularly long stretch of misery, it’s worth reaching out to a mental health professional. What we focus on becomes our reality, and many of us can get caught up in the bad news that seems to be coming at us from every corner of the internet or possibly even our social circles. A therapist can guide you through cognitive behavioral techniques to loosen negativity’s hold on your brain so that you can feel more regulated, safer, and connected—even in an era where that may seem like quite the stretch.
