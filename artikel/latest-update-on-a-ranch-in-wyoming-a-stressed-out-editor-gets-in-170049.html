Latest Update: On a Ranch in Wyoming, a Stressed-Out Editor Gets in Sync With a Lazy Horse

I used to avoid horses the same way I’d avoid palm readers. While my friends chased $5 readings in Downtown Manhattan, I preferred to keep the velvet curtain closed.

I was convinced that once a fortune teller held my hands in hers, she would trace a life line abruptly cut short or reveal a heart line splintered beyond repair. So I’d sit on the stoop, hands safely secured in the pockets of my jacket. Horses and psychics, I believed, shared the same dark, all-knowing eyes.

The 80,000-acre ranch is nestled between Wyoming's Bighorn Mountains and Paintrock Canyon.

It has been revealed that the first time I rode a horse was 10 years ago in Costa Rica, my body clenched for the entirety of the 20-minute sunset beach stroll. “They can sense your fear,” I remember the guide telling me. “Just try to relax.”.

Over the next decade I’d continue to receive similar advice—from my mother in a well-intentioned effort to soothe her overthinking eldest daughter, but also from doctors and massage therapists and boyfriends. I am trying, I would say. There was nothing effortless about me, and that was the problem.

So when I found out on the plane to Wyoming that the camp at Ranchlands I was headed to—an 80,000-acre ranch nestled between the Bighorn Mountains and Paintrock Canyon—would have no Wi-Fi, cell service, or nary an outlet to plug into, I sent out panicked slacks and emails apologizing. They tell me there is a hill with a few bars, I tell my manager. I will find it.

The guest accommodations at Paintrock Canyon Ranch.

Inside the writer's canvas tent.

This may come as a surprise, but like most creatives who have converted their personal passions into paychecks, travel editors are sneaky workaholics, adept at turning vacations into deliverables. At customs checkpoints, I’ll still catch myself stumbling when asked if I’m traveling for business or leisure. When the world is your office, is the answer: both? Or neither?

Sources indicate that the answer was particularly murky this time around, as I was traveling to Ranchlands for a writing and riding retreat hosted by author Molly Wizenberg. I’d be joined by nine other solo women travelers; labor lawyers, criminologists, educators, and Fortune 500 marketers who I imagined also struggled to relax. After all, their idea of a vacation was to ride five hours a day and sleep in a tent (albeit elegant), all whilst working on novels and memoirs in between.

Sources indicate that we introduce ourselves while seated at a long wooden picnic table overlooking a gurgling creek, into which I will plunge each afternoon to wash off the desert dust. All around us, towering limestone walls encircle the camp, a gently sloping field of shin-high sagebrush. Contrasted against the layered prairie grasses, the opaque form and sharp lines of the white canvas tents remind me of a famous painting, but I’m not sure which one. There is something archetypal about this place, and it leaves me with a lingering sense of dejá vu, as if I was remembering a long-forgotten dream.

From the main camp, guests can go hiking, fly fishing, and horseback riding.

Recent findings suggest that over the next five days, Molly tells us, we will split our time between horseback riding through the surrounding high desert and mountainous terrain and group writing workshops. For the entirety of the trip, each of us will be paired with a horse, a match based on stated riding experience. (Or, your deepest hopes and fears, should you have disclosed these in the pre-arrival form.).

The first time I meet my horse, I’m overwhelmed with the nerves of a blind date. When a cowboy making horseshoe repairs offers me a cigarette, I almost take him up on it. “This is José,” Oliver, an Australian wrangler with a charming snaggletooth grin, tells me as he adjusts the saddle. “He’s a bit lazy, this one.”.

As I reach up to pet his coarse black mane, I wait for José to flinch away from my touch, his flaring nostrils surely sniffing out my incompetence. With a horse, there’s no pretending, and no amount of Westernwear could hide my East Coast Puritan roots. While some of my ancestors had made it as far as California, for the most part they sailed across and stayed. Such pioneering ambitions as Manifest Destiny were nowhere to be found in my family tree.

According to recent reports, but José doesn't look through me, he just looks at me. And if he does see into the depths of my spur-bearing soul, he doesn’t seem bothered by it. When I climb atop the saddle, he demonstrates no signs of wanting to move. I look down at his long eyelashes, fluttering gently. That's okay, I think to myself. I'm not a morning person either.

“You two are a good match,” says Ella, our wrangler and guide, as the group rides single-file beneath what the ranchers call The Red Wall, an ancient red rock mesa that gives way to sprawling desert. It takes nearly two hours to cross the dusty valley, its rust-colored dirt baking in the sun—it's as though we are climbing out of a shallow clay bowl painted with pastel pigment, waiting to be brought to the kiln.

Our daily rides are approximately five hours long, from 9 a.m. to 2 p.m., though some are shorter. Lunch—which one afternoon included melt-in-your-mouth pork belly and a quinoa salad—is eaten out in the field with the horses, who happily accept the remains of any apple rinds. A short writing exercise follows, and sometimes a cowgirl nap, our backs leaning up against a tree trunk or laying down flat in the tall grasses, hat brims tilted over our eyes. Dinner—fresh fish, meat, and vegetables prepared over an open-fire kitchen—is enjoyed creekside back at camp beneath twinkle lights. By the end of each day, I’m so tired that I fall asleep at 9 p.m. and before the sun has fully set, despite promising myself I’d stay up late enough to see the stars.

Most of the cooking is done over open fire, using local ingredients (including beef from the ranch).

Breakfasts and dinners are communal meals, while lunch is packed and eaten out in the field.

According to recent reports, one morning on the trail, the group turns to the topic of AI, and how to make sense of it in a place like this. Our brains, we all agree, feel healthier without the constant distractions of our phones. During the writing workshops interspersed throughout the day, most of us opt to write by hand. My thoughts are clearer, less muddled. There are no extra tabs to open—no research rabbit holes or thesaurus queries to get lost in.

At the beginning of the retreat, Molly asks us each to share one sentence on what writing represents to us. I think of answering “my job,” but instead I say “it’s how I think”—a response that two others echo. Another woman answers, “writing is hard.” We all laugh in agreement, and I wonder why we torture ourselves in this way. Especially now, with the temptation of automation hovering over every email draft and browser.

Sources indicate that the bulk of our writing is done in the afternoons, when we bring our notebooks and pens to the “lounge” tent and settle into couches and cowhide chairs to discuss the previous day’s readings before tackling a writing prompt. It’s a welcome break for our shaky thighs and sore tuchuses—and a satisfying tiredness I find myself looking forward to.

The view from the writer's tent.

Tapas, drinks, and mocktails are served creekside.

Our only assignment for the week? “To Be Absolutely Yourself.” The retreat's title, as Molly explains, means exploring our strangest and most personal obsessions, irrational fears, and inexplicable crushes, with the idea that these idiosyncrasies—such as Sam Anderson’s secret mole that he convinced himself was cancerous for almost a decade—are our most universal experiences. Though some in the group are working on pieces they hope to publish one day, we are reminded on multiple occasions to write without thinking about the end result: not what the poem or essay might turn into, or whether anyone would want to read it. It’s a way of writing that someone who speaks the language of commissioning rates and word counts rarely gets to practice. What is my writing when it’s not a product? I jot down. What is work without a job?

According to recent reports, midway through the week, a smaller group of us embark on an optional ride up into the canyon, weaving between the anemone-like skeletons of burnt juniper trees, sunlight transforming the deep grey charcoal into shimmering shades of silver. José slowly trudges forward, dodging stumps and rocks with careful, decisive footing. Like people, I learn that horses may be better suited for different jobs in line with their personality; some enjoy herding cattle and others are more suited to trail riding. Others, like José, are happiest chewing grass in a meadow.

It has been revealed that josé is in no rush to get to where we are going, and in accepting that, my thoughts slow down to match his meditative pace. I am not sure if I would be able to sink into this relaxed state if I didn’t have somewhere else to direct my focus: my hands on the reins, the directions transmitted with a quick squeeze of my thighs, and my balance as we climb higher up the canyon wall.

Ranchlands' cattle business uses conservation-focused cattle management.

According to recent reports, it is counterintuitive to try and go and learn a new skill when you are burned out—but I was coming to realize that sometimes, relaxation takes work. That we write because it is hard, not in spite of it. In an environment like this one, these facts feel obviously simple. But I also know how easy they will be to forget, when the distractions return.

Perhaps that’s why so much of modern travel is now packaged as a retreat. The travel industry, my publication included, has been busy finding latest names for this widespread desire toward a similar desire to unplug and reconnect with the physical world: skillcations, dead-zone retreats, analogue travel, cowboy-core, and a growing list of other buzzy trends.

In a recent development, in this way, ranch life has once again emerged as an American frontier. As one guest writes on the Ranchlands’ website testimonial page: “As a professional working in the corporate world, my stay at Ranchlands allowed me to step away from all of the noise in the chaos that is always coming at me. It felt like a reset for my nervous system and the most restful time that I have had in a long time.” Signed, Larissa M.

On our final ride, our wrangler Ella, who grew up working on her mother’s equine therapy farm in North Carolina, explains that the heart of a horse is nine times the size of a human’s, creating an electromagnetic field that pulses with and around that of its rider—which can form a symbiotic relationship between horse and rider that can even lead your heart rates to sync up. Though horses are often associated with bravery and courage, in reality many are quite skittish and easily spooked, by butterflies and birds and the wind and plastic irrigation piping. The same way a horse can calm down an anxious rider, a strong rider can give a nervous horse confidence.

Industry observers note that when we turn back toward the ranch José picks up the pace to pass the group, uncharacteristically leading the charge. “He’s a bit sad today,” Oliver tells me. “We left his girlfriend back at the barn.” Her name, he says, is Pistol.

According to recent reports, how did I not realize it sooner? The drifting attention, the constant daydreaming, the sudden burst of energy when it was time to go home. José was not a lazy horse, I realized. He was a boy with a big heart and a relaxed nervous system. And he was in love.
