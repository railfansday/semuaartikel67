Rockwell Adds NVIDIA Omniverse to Emulate3D for Factory-Scale Simulation

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Rockwell Automation has integrated NVIDIA Omniverse, NVIDIA RTX rendering, and OpenUSD technologies into Emulate3D, expanding its industrial simulation software toward larger, more complex factory environments. The integration combines Rockwell’s simulation and controls-testing capabilities with NVIDIA technologies for visualization and connected 3D workflows.

The aim is to help manufacturers model production systems, test control behavior, and evaluate design changes before equipment is installed. Rockwell also positions the expanded simulation environment as a foundation for AI systems to learn, validate decisions, and optimize performance before physical deployment.

“To accelerate AI adoption across engineering and operations, manufacturers are looking beyond traditional simulation,” said Matheus Bulho, senior vice president, Software & Control, Rockwell Automation.

Recent findings suggest that virtual commissioning lets engineers connect control logic to a simulated production environment and test how the system responds before commissioning the physical equipment. That creates an opportunity to find sequencing problems, unexpected interactions, and design issues while changes can still be made in software.

In a recent development, rockwell’s FactoryTalk Twin Studio documentation describes Emulate3D as a way to verify connected control systems under different operating conditions. Its established applications include throughput analysis, machine prototyping, virtual commissioning, and operator training. The software sits alongside control-engineering tools, connecting the behavior of a virtual machine or line to the logic intended to run it.

Extending that approach across a factory makes interactions between systems more key. A change to one station’s cycle time, for example, can alter the flow of work through downstream equipment. Modeling connected systems gives engineering teams a way to investigate those dependencies before committing to a layout or operating strategy.

Industry observers note that “The ability to explore, test and validate ideas virtually is becoming increasingly valuable as manufacturing systems grow in complexity,” stated Lora Green, Senior Manager for Advanced Manufacturing Simulation at GE Appliances. She said advances in simulation help teams evaluate ideas earlier and approach deployment with greater confidence.

Recent findings suggest that the three NVIDIA technologies play related roles in the workflow. OpenUSD, short for Universal Scene Description, provides an extensible framework for describing and composing 3D scenes. Its schemas and layered data model let teams combine assets and make changes across a scene without rebuilding every part of the underlying workflow.

For a factory model, that shared representation can help connect equipment geometry and scene information across design, engineering, and simulation tools. It becomes more useful as the environment grows from a single machine into many interacting assets assembled by different teams.

NVIDIA describes Omniverse libraries as modular components that software developers can integrate into existing applications. Their capabilities span rendering, physics, storage, and the preparation of simulation-ready assets. This gives industrial software vendors building blocks for extending established engineering tools while retaining their application-specific workflows.

RTX contributes physically based rendering and sensor-simulation capabilities within the broader Omniverse stack. More realistic lighting and materials can improve how teams inspect a virtual environment and, in supported workflows, how simulated sensors observe it. Rockwell’s announcement specifically identifies RTX rendering as part of the Emulate3D integration; the broader NVIDIA ecosystem also supports physics and synthetic-data workflows.

In a recent development, rockwell’s product page describes an existing factory-scale testing framework called Emulate3D Factory Test. Its capabilities include synchronizing multiple system models, version-controlled engineering workflows, automated test execution through Test Runner, and a Fault Framework for assessing how systems respond to fault conditions.

Those features connect larger visual environments to practical engineering work. Teams can repeat a test after changing control logic, compare results, and investigate how a connected production system responds when something goes wrong. Factory-scale visualization helps engineers review those interactions in the context of the surrounding equipment.

Industry observers note that rockwell also highlights deterministic outcomes as a foundation for AI-driven engineering and operational decisions. Repeatable simulation is valuable when evaluating a proposed change because it helps teams compare alternatives under controlled conditions. Confidence in the result depends on the model’s assumptions, physical properties, and representation of the control system.

Recent findings suggest that simulation gives industrial AI a place to explore decisions before those decisions affect production. NVIDIA’s broader physical AI workflows include testing robot behavior and producing synthetic sensor data in virtual environments. Rockwell frames this integration around giving manufacturers a stronger digital foundation for testing designs, operating strategies, and AI-enabled workflows.

Industry observers note that that foundation depends on simulation-ready 3D assets with the physical properties and other information needed by the intended test. An equipment model’s geometry, behavior, and relationship to the surrounding system all influence what engineers and AI systems can learn from it.

It has been revealed that rockwell’s product page describes advanced full-factory visualization powered by Omniverse APIs as initially available through private preview. The November announcement expands the integration story without specifying a general-availability timeline for that visualization capability.

Sources indicate that the significance of the integration is the tighter connection between factory design, control-system testing, and the environments used to develop industrial AI. For manufacturers, the practical opportunity is to evaluate more of a production system together, identify problems earlier, and carry better-tested engineering decisions into physical commissioning.
