Moms may be burned out and lonely right now – but we don’t have to accept it.

That was the theme of Motherly’s State of Motherhood event on Monday, which brought together some of the most powerful women in business, media and advocacy to diagnose how moms have fared during the last tumultuous decade, and detail what we need going forward.

Sources indicate that the conversation was driven by an in-depth analysis of Motherly’s own research. Every year since 2018, we’ve polled thousands of moms (more than 60,000 in fact!) for our annual State of Motherhood survey. And every year, moms have told us they’re scaling back at work and having fewer children than they’d prefer, but they’re still feeling isolated and overwhelmed. In 2025, 70% of moms said motherhood is lonelier than expected and in 2026, 42% felt more burned out than in the previous year.

Industry observers note that “The truth is that our expectations of what it means to be a good mom changed faster than the systems built around to support her, and Gen Z moms are running into that gap even earlier,” said Motherly’s CEO Sharon Yi in her opening remarks. “But none of us should have to sacrifice our sanity, our careers, our identities to have children, and no mother should have to carry it alone.”.

Leaders from organizations at the Clinton Foundation, the Andrew Carnegie Foundation, the ACLU, and the Vote Mama Foundation, joined together with executives at JP Morgan, Pfizer, Coterie, IKEA, and Paramount, to hear from women who are working tirelessly to create a world where moms feel supported, connected, and respected.

That included actress Jennifer Garner, who talked about her work as an ambassador for Save the Children and as co-founder of organic children’s nutrition company Once Upon a Farm.

Garner kicked off the event in a fireside chat that touched on everything from finding community as a mom to the importance of advocating for children and families.

“Every kid deserves healthy, real food, an education, and parents who have a roof over their heads, food in their bellies, and time and space and energy and support to love their kids,” she noted. Traveling around the country for her work with Save the Children, she described seeing families stretch food-bank, WIC, and SNAP resources to feed their children.

In a recent development, that’s why Once Upon a Farm worked hard to become the first WIC-certified tech giant offering organic refrigerated baby food, she mentioned. The company is now WIC-certified in 22 states. For Garner, it’s about dignity and choice: families using public benefits should be able to access high-quality food.

In a recent development, it’s all baked in, pun intended, to the organization’s social mission to “drive systemic change in childhood nutrition for a happier, healthier, more equitable world,” Garner states. In 2021, the company transitioned its corporate structure from an LLC to a Public Benefit Corporation (PBC), which legally requires it to balance profitability with its social and environmental impact. It’s a challenging path for a business to pursue, which is why there are fewer than 20 publicly-traded PBCs, and Once Upon a Farm is the only one focused on kids. Towards those goals, Garner noted, the company has donated a million meals to kids in rural America, and it pays a premium for produce “so that the people who are growing and picking our food are recruited ethically, and have a say in their safety and what their workday looks like.”.

Industry observers note that when it comes to balancing the demands of her multi-hyphenite life with motherhood, Garner didn’t hold back. On her non-negotiables, she joked, “I work out every day… unless I don’t. I read every night until I fall asleep… one page or none. I go to church… when I can. My kids come first… except for weeks where it’s crazy and I tell them they come first from afar.”.

Finding the right balance changes day by day, Garner mentioned. She assesses who needs her most in a given moment, including herself. “You get to choose moment to moment what you do and what your family needs,” she said to the moms in the room.

She also encouraged moms to come together to help defeat the loneliness that Motherly’s data revealed. “We’re literally meant to be in community,” she described. “When my second was born, I joined a baby group for second-time moms, and that is the most active group chat on my phone to this day.”.

Plus, she added, small groups of committed moms have the power to make real change.

Recent findings suggest that “We all love our babies the same. And when you realize that, that every mother in the world loves their baby the way you love yours, which seems impossible, but it is true, that also connects you to every other mother,” she mentioned. “And then you feel like, how can I help every other mother who doesn’t have the advantages I have?”.

Next up was a panel about the state of motherhood in 2026, breaking down Motherly’s data showing that moms are increasingly burned out, and figuring out fixes to help. Featuring some of the most influential women working to make the United States a better place for families, the panelists pointed out that burnout is a structural problem — not an individual failure — and that collective action can turn broad public support for family policies into change.

“I think motherhood in America is impossible by design,” stated Reshma Saujani, the founder and CEO of Moms First, a national non-profit organization working to advance paid leave and affordable childcare for working families. “Why does the school day end at 2:30 and the workday end at six? Why do we pay more for our childcare than our mortgage? Why do one out of four women go back to work two weeks after having a baby?”.

Lauren Smith Brody, the founder of The Fifth Trimester and co-founder of Chamber of Mothers, said she’s optimistic that federal lawmakers will soon advance legislation to help moms across the country, including paid family leave, affordable childcare for all, and measures to improve maternal health outcomes. “I feel a lot of hope as we walk into these offices on Capitol Hill and we make a lot of behind the scenes connections between Democratic offices and Republican offices, because guess what? They actually do want many of the same things,” she said. “Moms are top of the list. They know that our voting power is unmatched. They know that they need us. They know that we voted them in and can vote them out.”.

“I am telling you standing right here, [affordable] childcare and paid leave will get passed in 2029 within the first hundred days,” Saujani said. “I don’t care who is elected president because we are getting this done.”.

It’s all about moms coming together to hold leaders accountable, said Ailen Arreaza, the executive director of the national nonprofit parents organization ParentsTogether. “Moms often feel it’s our sole responsibility to protect our kids, and feel shame when we think we’ve failed — especially when it comes to technology,” she said. “One of the best ways that we can hold companies accountable is by sharing our stories. We do a lot of work with parents who have lost children to significant tech, and their stories are so powerful,” she said. “That also helps with the shame. When we talk to each other about what we are struggling with, what we are facing, that shame evaporates.”.

Sources indicate that michelle Jawando, the CEO of Omidyar Network, a philanthropy organization working to foster responsible technology and AI development, remarked she’s encouraged by the fact that the mistakes of the “move-fast-and-break-things” era in Silicon Valley are providing a roadmap for more responsible tech development going forward. “I believe that everything that happened in that proceeding era gives us an opportunity to get AI right,” she said, pointing to California, which recently passed the strongest AI companion chatbot legislation in the country.

The morning’s final panel, moderated by the host of Motherly’s upcoming podcast, Daniela Gysler-Wood, provided practical ways mothers can protect their physical and mental well-being.

Dr. Bayo Curry-Winchell, a board certified family medicine physician and the author of the upcoming book How to Talk to Your Doctor, encouraged moms to keep seeking answers if they’re feeling unwell. Her advice? If a clinician claims you are fine but you do not feel fine, say, “I understand that you think I’m fine, but I’m not. What are the next steps?”.

She also suggested asking, “What three to five possibilities are on your working list?” This helps you understand the range of possibilities if your doctor is focused on just one diagnosis.

When it comes to getting enough rest, celebrate the small wins, mentioned Megan Holm, the women’s sleep research lead at sleep technology manufacturer Eight Sleep. “Sleep when you can, and you don’t need to check off everything on your to-do list in order to put your head on the pillow. You can take a 30-minute nap,” she said.

According to recent reports, “I think we’ve all just accepted that exhaustion is part of motherhood, and I don’t think that’s where we should be,” noted Victoria Thain Gioia, the co-founder and CEO of Perelel, an OB/GYN-founded and doctor-formulated vitamin and supplement brand. “To get through the day and stay sane, and to take care of small children, and do your job, you need to be nutritionally sufficient, not depleted. So while it can sometimes be overhyped, the conversation about protein, collagen, lactoferrin and nutrients that can actually support your energy [is important]. We really need to focus on care for mom beyond just pregnancy and postpartum.”.

Sources indicate that when it comes to protecting our mental well-being, the panelists encouraged moms to let go of guilt and connect with other parents. “Check in on your friends, nurture your friendships, don’t ask everything of your partner or your spouse, and ask for help,” remarked Elise Hu, the host of TED Talks Daily, a flagship podcast from TED Conferences. “Asking for help doesn’t just benefit you, the person who needs the help, it also benefits the helper because think about all the times that you have been asked for advice or counsel. That’s such a tribute to you, right? Because somebody is saying to you, ‘I trust you and I trust your advice and I trust your counsel.’ It nurtures the connection on both ends, not only for the helpee, but also the helper.”.

The moms in the room didn’t just leave with actionable advice and encouragement – although there was plenty on tap – they also took home a Happ Paige Mini full of goodies, including Once Upon a Farm snacks, Perelel Collagen+ Defense Supplements, Shiseido Sun Protector Clear Stick Sunscreen, Then I Met You Cleansing Balm, Ursa Major Golden Hour Recovery Cream, Merit Shade Slick, and the latest Bentgo Lift Chug Lid water bottle.

Recent findings suggest that guests also got a chance to try out a mini version of Eight Sleep’s pod, an intelligent mattress cover that uses built-in biometric sensors to track your sleep and automatically adjust its temperature. “In May, we released pregnancy mode, which is automatic temperature support to support women as they go through pregnancy and up to 24 weeks postpartum,” mentioned Holm, Eight Sleep’s women’s research lead, during the second panel. “As you grow a child, the environment around you feels really hot and uncomfortable. So we automatically make sure that when you can rest, and even if it’s not for eight hours every night, you’re comfortable.”.

And bestselling author Loryn Brantz greeted guests as they walked into the room, signing free copies of her upcoming release, I Am a Spicy Nugget, as well as copies of her New York Times bestseller, Poems of Parenting. Some lucky guests also received a signed print.

Motherly’s CEO Sharon Yi closed out the day with a call to action for all of the “incredible, powerful, influential” women in the room: “From all these conversations, I heard over and over that we need community, and that community is power, big or small,” she began. “We need advocates, people who fight relentlessly for the care, the policies and the respect that moms deserve. And we need storytellers, people who show the real picture of this journey at every stage without judgment.”.

That’s where Motherly can play a powerful role going forward, she noted.

“Motherly is growing from a trusted resource into an active community online and off,” she explained. “What does that mean in practice? It means we’re showing up in her group chat. We’re in her feed with a slate of new vertical video demonstrates that I’m really excited about. We’re in her earbuds with a new podcast. And we’re popping up in her neighborhood with our new hyper local event series, coming to a neighborhood near you so that moms can find the people where they are.”.
