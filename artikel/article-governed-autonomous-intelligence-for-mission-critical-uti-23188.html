Utility operations teams - the IT and platform teams responsible for supporting enterprise applications, integrations, and infrastructure already have more monitoring data than they can comfortably consume.

Application logs, infrastructure metrics, API telemetry, security events, job failures, service-management tickets, and business alerts all describe pieces of the same operating environment. The hard problem is not collecting another signal. It is connecting those signals swiftly enough to understand what is happening, what it may affect, and what should happen next.

Agentic AI is attractive because it can reason across that fragmented evidence, retrieve operational knowledge, call tools, and coordinate multi-step work. But a utility organization cannot treat an agent the same way it treats a conversational assistant. A wrong summary is inconvenient. A wrong production action can affect a customer-facing service, a safety-related workflow, regulatory reporting, or a system that participates in reliable energy delivery.

According to recent reports, this article describes the architecture pattern we used for an agentic operations platform in a regulated utility environment. The reference implementation uses Amazon Bedrock AgentCore, Amazon Bedrock, retrieval over enterprise runbooks and incident history, and Model Context Protocol (MCP) tool interfaces.

The model can propose anything. It can execute only what the policy gate authorizes, through the governed tool path, with human approval wherever policy requires it. Authority never lives inside the model.

Some capabilities, especially prediction and autonomous remediation, are intentionally enabled incrementally rather than all at once. That is not a limitation to hide. In a regulated environment, the ability to expand autonomy gradually and revoke it quickly is part of the architecture.

Recent findings suggest that A large utility estate spans custom applications, SaaS products, cloud platforms, integration layers, databases, observability tools, field and operational systems, and third-party services. Each domain can be monitored well in isolation while the end-to-end service remains difficult to reason about.

Recent findings suggest that the recurring operational gaps are familiar. Related signals appear in separate tools and are correlated manually. Technical severity is visible, but the business consequence is not. Runbooks and lessons learned exist, but they are spread across wikis, ticket histories, shared drives, and the memory of experienced engineers. Thresholds tell teams when a known condition has already occurred; weaker precursors across dependent systems are easy to miss. When action is finally taken, the evidence tying the change to its rationale, authorization, approver, and outcome may be assembled after the fact.

This is especially notable in utilities because the governing context is not uniform. Regulatory requirements vary across utility sectors, operational environments, and jurisdictions. For example, North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC) Critical Infrastructure Protection (CIP) requirements apply to the Bulk Electric System (BES) and defined in-scope BES Cyber Systems. At the same time, customer-facing services, billing, field operations, and other utility programs may be subject to applicable industry-specific, federal, state, or local regulatory requirements.

The architecture therefore has to do more than produce a better dashboard. It must correlate across domains, reason with trusted operational knowledge, map technical events to business impact, support prediction without pretending prediction is certain, and preserve an execution model that security, compliance, operators, and auditors can understand.

The organizing idea is an AI control plane. The term is borrowed deliberately from networking and cloud infrastructure, where the control plane decides what should happen and a separate plane carries it out. The separation is useful here for the same reasons: safety, auditability, and blast-radius containment.

Figure 2. The AI control plane separates reasoning authority from execution authority. The model can propose broadly; only policy-authorized actions can cross into the execution plane. (Image source: created by the author).

In the reasoning plane, the orchestrator and domain agents can analyze events, retrieve context, compare an incident with historical patterns, estimate business impact, predict likely failure modes, and propose remediation. None of those components holds authority to change a production system directly.

Industry observers note that A proposed action crosses into the execution plane only through a deterministic policy gate and a governed tool gateway. The gate decides whether the action is permitted and what approval is required. The gateway enforces the final authorization, obtains narrowly scoped credentials, invokes the target system, records the result, and performs or requests post-action validation.

It has been revealed that this distinction matters when the model is wrong. A hallucinated root cause in a conventional autonomous design can become a bad action. In this design, it becomes a bad proposal. Policy can reject the proposal, a human can review it, or the gateway can deny authorization. The model’s error still matters, but its cost is limited by construction.

The reference implementation runs on Amazon Bedrock AgentCore. AgentCore supplies managed agent runtime, memory, gateway, identity, and observability integrations with Amazon Bedrock for model access and Bedrock Knowledge Bases for retrieval over enterprise knowledge. AgentCore Policy, generally available since March 2026, covers the boundary this pattern relies on: rules authored in natural language or policy-as-code are compiled to Cedar, AWS’s open-source authorization policy language, and attached to an AgentCore Gateway, which evaluates every agent-tool request before allowing or denying it. That is deterministic, action-level authorization enforced at the gateway rather than inside agent logic, and teams starting today should evaluate it before building an equivalent. In this design, AgentCore Gateway is the AWS implementation of the tool gateway. AgentCore Evaluations supports continuous and on-demand evaluation of agent behavior.

In a recent development, MCP is used at the tool boundary to standardize how tools are described and invoked. A schema-defined tool boundary gives the platform one place to constrain operations, validate arguments, enforce policy, and record evidence.

Sources indicate that the "one place" property is notable. A tool gateway is only a governance boundary if agents cannot bypass it. In our design, that means no target-system credentials in the reasoning runtime, deny-by-default identity policies, tightly restricted network paths, and credentials exposed only to the tool implementation that needs them. The principle is simple: one door to production, with every control attached to that door.

It has been revealed that the pattern is not tied to AWS. It needs a managed agent runtime, a retrieval layer, and a policy-enforcing tool gateway that agents cannot bypass. On Microsoft Azure, Microsoft Foundry Agent Service provides the runtime, Microsoft Entra supplies agent identities, and Azure AI Search handles retrieval, with Azure API Management serving as the tool gateway and an AI gateway for model and MCP traffic (some of these governance features are still in preview). On Google Cloud, the Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform provides Agent Runtime and Agent Identity, with Agent Gateway as the tool gateway, enforcing IAM access policies and Model Armor screening for prompt injection.

The boundary that matters sits between the agentic layer and the tool gateway; everything above it reasons, and nothing above it can act.

Sources indicate that the reasoning plane uses a hierarchical multi-agent structure rather than one large agent with broad access. A narrow agent with a bounded responsibility is easier to test, constrain, evaluate, and explain than a monolithic agent that knows every domain and can call every tool.

The Orchestrator Agent is the entry point for normalized operational events. It determines the analysis required, routes work to the appropriate domain agents, and assembles their findings. It coordinates but does not execute.

Recent findings suggest that the Application Reliability Agent analyzes application failures and degradations against known patterns and recent history. The Platform Intelligence Agent looks at API and interface failures, latency, partial degradation, and upstream/downstream dependencies. The Business Impact Agent translates technical state into business consequences — for example, a risk to customer service, billing, a safety-related program, a reliability commitment, or a reporting deadline. The Remediation Agent is the only reasoning component allowed to propose a production-touching action, and its proposal still has no authority until it passes the policy and approval path.

Figure 4. The orchestrator decomposes each event and routes analysis to bounded domain agents; only the Remediation Agent proposes production actions, and those proposals still pass through policy and the tool gateway (Image source: created by the author).

All agents use shared operational context retrieved from enterprise knowledge. The useful corpus is the organization’s runbooks, SOPs, architecture documentation, incident history, business rules, dependency maps, and known recovery procedures. Grounding a recommendation in the documented procedure for this environment produces a very different artifact from a plausible answer based only on general model training.

According to recent reports, the quality of this layer depends heavily on knowledge hygiene. If a runbook is outdated, contradictory, or missing a rollback step, RAG will faithfully retrieve an outdated, contradictory, or incomplete instruction. Curation, ownership, versioning, and source traceability are therefore part of the operational design, not a content-management side project.

In a recent development, in practice, every runbook carries a named owner and a last-review date, and retrieval returns both with each passage. Runbooks past their review interval are flagged to the owner, and operator rejections citing a faulty runbook are routed to the runbook’s owner for correction.

It has been revealed that A common event contract is just as important as the knowledge base. We normalize incoming signals into a small operational schema that includes the affected service or component, environment, event type, timestamp, source, technical severity, dependency identifiers, correlation keys, business-service mapping, and a reference back to the original evidence. The raw payload is preserved, but deterministic parsing and source adapters, not the LLM, establish basic facts such as which system emitted the event and whether two identifiers refer to the same service. A model can help interpret an unfamiliar log message, but those foundational facts are too important to leave to probabilistic extraction.

Consider a common integration-operations pattern. An API has not failed, but its latency begins to rise. At roughly the same time, a downstream integration starts retrying more frequently, and a processing queue grows. Each signal on its own may still be below a page-worthy threshold. Together they suggest that a customer-facing workflow could degrade if the pattern continues.

It has been revealed that the ingestion layer normalizes the three signals and preserves their source evidence. The orchestrator recognizes that the affected components share a dependency chain and asks the Platform Intelligence Agent to analyze the interface behavior. The Application Reliability Agent checks whether the application has shown a similar resource or connection pattern in prior incidents. The Business Impact Agent maps the dependency to the business service that relies on it.

Industry observers note that retrieval then pulls the current runbook for this failure class and the most relevant historical incidents. The agents don't merely summarize alerts; they produce a structured operational hypothesis: the likely failure mode, supporting evidence, dependent services that may be affected, and documented remediation options. If the evidence is weak or contradictory, that uncertainty is carried forward rather than hidden.

Suppose the runbook permits two next steps: gather additional diagnostics and, if a specific condition is confirmed, restart a stateless component. The action catalog may classify the diagnostic tool as low risk and allow it to run automatically. In this deployment, the catalog maps the restart to a human-approval rule. The Remediation Agent can propose it, but it cannot decide to downgrade its risk because the model is confident.

Sources indicate that when an operator approves the restart, the tool gateway verifies the approval reference and policy, obtains the scoped execution identity, invokes only the permitted operation, and captures the response. The workflow then checks the signals that motivated the action: latency, retry behavior, queue trend, and service health. If those conditions do not recover, the workflow does not mark the remediation successful simply because the restart command completed.

The architecture removes manual correlation and runbook hunting from a familiar operational problem while preserving the same controls an experienced engineer would expect before touching production.

Prediction is where the platform moves beyond faster incident summarization. The goal is to identify combinations of weak signals that historically precede a failure: rising latency, queue growth, retry behavior, resource saturation, repeated warning patterns, or anomalies that become meaningful only when correlated across a dependency chain.

The prediction layer can use historical incidents, time-series trends, dependency information, and semantic analysis of unstructured logs to produce a forward-looking statement: what may fail, on what horizon, through which mechanism, with what confidence, and with what likely business consequence.

According to recent reports, the important design decision is what prediction is not allowed to do. A confidence score is not an authorization token. Predictions enter the same governed path as observed incidents. They carry evidence, confidence, and business-weighted risk so operators can decide how to respond.

In a recent development, false positives are expected. They should be measured, reviewed, and reduced over time. Overselling prediction is one of the fastest ways to lose operator trust.

Human control should not be a sentence in a governance document. It should sit directly on the execution path.

The platform maps every candidate action to a predefined risk class outside the model. An approved action catalog drives that classification, not the model deciding whether its own proposal is dangerous. The catalog can consider the operation, target environment, blast radius, reversibility, data impact, and dependency sensitivity, but the resulting policy is fixed, versioned, testable, and reviewable.

Recent findings suggest that this is intentionally conservative. In the early stages of deployment, even some low-risk actions may remain approval-only until the team has enough evidence to allow automatic execution.

The policy record should be concrete enough to test without an LLM. For each permitted operation, we keep the tool identifier, target environment, allowed scope, risk class, required approval type, execution timeout, key preconditions, and expected postconditions, plus a link to the rollback procedure where rollback is meaningful. The agent may supply parameters such as a service name or requested capacity, but policy decides whether they fall inside the authorized envelope. The risk-driving facts should come from controlled configuration, inventory, or independently verified context, because asking the model to label its own proposal low, medium, or high leaves the authority decision with the model.

Post-action validation is easy to underestimate. "API returned 200" is not the same as "the system is healthy," so a remediation workflow should validate the outcome it was meant to produce and escalate if the expected state is not restored.

The gateway also provides defense in depth. It scopes least-privilege credentials to specific tools, authorizes each agent-and-action combination independently, and rechecks required approvals at execution time instead of trusting an upstream component’s claim that they happened. It also validates tool arguments against their schemas. Every invocation records the requesting identity, policy decision, approval reference, inputs, result, and post-action health status.

Typed tools constrain what a manipulated agent can request, but they do not make prompt injection disappear, because an agent can still misuse an operation within its allowed scope. The control therefore comes from layers: treating ingested content as untrusted data, narrow tools, argument validation, least privilege, policy enforcement, approval requirements, network constraints, and post-action validation.

Industry observers note that content inspection adds one more layer at the same boundary; since June 2026, AgentCore Policy can evaluate Bedrock Guardrails to screen tool gateway inputs and outputs for prompt injection and sensitive-data exposure (initially in a subset of AWS Regions). Treat that as one more control on the door rather than a solution to injection, and keep the bounded-authority layers regardless of how good the detection gets.

For regulated operations, governance is not a section added after the architecture. It is why the architecture has this shape.

Teams should manage model and agent changes with the same discipline as other production software: version-controlled prompts and agent definitions, reviewed changes, evaluation before promotion, and rollback capability. Historical incidents and adversarial cases provide useful regression tests. Teams should sample production traces and evaluate them for task completion, tool selection, groundedness, safety, and policy adherence. They should also monitor confidence and recommendation quality for drift after deployment.

Teams should also minimize operational data. Logs and tickets can contain customer, employee, or security-sensitive information irrelevant to the reasoning task. Before broad ingestion, teams should establish data classification, domain isolation, encryption in transit and at rest, and retention rules. OWASP’s Top 10 for LLM Applications identifies prompt injection and excessive agency as distinct risks, and its agentic security guidance extends that thinking to goal hijacking, tool misuse, identity and privilege abuse, and multi-agent interactions.

Audit evidence should answer more than "what did the system do?" For every consequential action, the organization should be able to reconstruct the originating signal, retrieved evidence, recommendation, applicable policy, human decision if required, executed operation, identity used, and validated result. Whether you can call that record immutable depends on the actual storage control; at minimum, it should be durable, access-controlled, tamper-evident, or otherwise protected according to enterprise evidence requirements.

In a recent development, the compliance mapping must be equally precise. NERC CIP does not apply to every application operated by an electric utility. For in-scope BES Cyber Systems, CIP-010-4 addresses configuration change management and vulnerability assessments; CIP-004-7 includes personnel and access-management requirements; CIP-011-3 addresses protection of BES Cyber System Information; and CIP-008-6 covers cyber-security incident reporting and response planning. An agentic platform can support the evidence needed for applicable requirements, but the platform itself does not create compliance.

Industry observers note that for customer-facing and regulated programs, evidence and reporting obligations may vary based on applicable jurisdictional requirements. Corporate applications outside CIP scope may instead fall under internal change controls, privacy requirements, or financial controls. The practical rule is to map evidence to the regime governing the specific system rather than labeling the entire utility estate "CIP compliant".

Frameworks such as NIST AI Risk Management Framework (Govern, Map, Measure, Manage) and ISO/IEC 42001:2023 can organize the broader AI governance program, but they complement rather than replace industry-specific requirements.

Recent findings suggest that in regulated environments, organizations should measure agentic AI value against operational baselines, not unsupported improvement percentages. The signals we tracked included detection time from the earliest useful signal to an actionable alert; response time to validated resolution; prediction precision, false-positive rate, lead time, and operator-rated usefulness; engineering effort spent on manual correlation, after-hours paging, and repeated analysis; and governance measures such as approval latency, policy denials, blocked unauthorized actions, evidence completeness, and audit reconstruction time.
