Five major challenges that may still make it onto this term’s oral argument docket

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that time is running out for the justices to add cases to this term’s oral argument docket. Under the Supreme Court’s typical briefing schedule, a case must be granted by mid-January to be briefed in time for the final argument session, which begins this term on April 19.

Industry observers note that but despite being short on time, the justices are not short on major petitions for review. Over the next two months, they will likely consider petitions addressing such high-profile problems as the Trump administration’s ban on transgender servicemembers, state laws requiring Ten Commandments displays in public school classrooms, and the regulation of prediction markets like Kalshi.

Before we explore some of the major issues pending before the Supreme Court, let me first offer a brief refresher on how a petition for review makes it to the oral argument docket. (For more on this process, read law professor Stephen Wermiel’s SCOTUSblog column from last week.).

Litigants file a petition for review, also known as a petition for certiorari, at the Supreme Court when they want the justices to review a lower court ruling against them. In their petition, they lay out, among other things, their views on why the ruling was wrong and why the issue is worthy of the justices’ attention.

Industry observers note that after a petition is filed, the litigants that prevailed before the lower court (known as the respondents) have 30 days to respond, although they may ask for additional time. Sometimes, they file a brief in which they agree that the Supreme Court should take up the matter, but it’s far more common for respondents to file a brief in opposition, in which they explain why they believe the lower court’s ruling was correct and why the dispute does not warrant Supreme Court review.

Recent findings suggest that once this brief has been filed – or once the respondent has formally waived its right to file such a brief (although the court may request one) – the court will schedule the petition for consideration at one of the justices’ private conferences. There are at least two such conferences every month during the term, and the court reveals in advance when these meetings will take place and when it will distribute the petitions assigned to each conference to the justices’ chambers.

The court typically places a petition on a conference list at least 14 days after the response brief was filed. By counting 14 days from the response deadline and then consulting the distribution schedule, one can then (usually) predict the conference at which the justices will first consider a petition. (It should be noted, however, that the court sometimes delays consideration of a petition without explanation, interfering with court watchers’ predictive powers.).

Recent findings suggest that for a petition to make it to the oral argument docket, at least four of the nine justices need to vote to grant review. But the court rarely announces that a petition has been granted after the first conference at which it’s considered, likely because the court needs extra time to verify the details of the case. Instead, successful petitions are almost always relisted at least once, which means the justices take a second look at them during a successive conference. For this reason, the earliest a court watcher can expect to learn that a petition has been granted is generally after the second conference to which it’s assigned.

It has been revealed that once a case is granted, litigants must file briefs on the merits, a process that takes at least three months under the court’s standard briefing schedule. That’s why a case must be granted by mid-January in order to be argued during the current term (unless the court adopts an expedited briefing schedule, which is a story for another day).

Without further ado, here’s a look at five high-profile issues that have a shot at being taken up by the justices before that mid-January deadline.

Ban on transgender servicemembers.

One week after President Donald Trump returned to the White House in January 2025, he signed an executive order that required Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth to impose a ban “on individuals with gender dysphoria” – that is, distress based on a mismatch between an individual’s gender identity and biological sex. The next month, the Department of Defense issued a policy that generally disqualifies anyone who either has gender dysphoria or has undergone medical interventions to treat gender dysphoria from serving in the military.

This ban on transgender servicemembers led to a number of legal challenges, including a dispute that arrived on the Supreme Court’s interim docket in April 2025, United States v. Shilling. On May 6, 2025, the court granted the Trump administration’s request to pause an order from a federal judge in Washington state that had prevented implementation of the policy anywhere in the country.

Shilling is now pending before the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 9th Circuit. A separate but related case, United States v. Talbott, has been appealed to the Supreme Court. Talbott involves a group of transgender people who either right now serve or want to serve in the U.S. military. After the Trump administration put the service ban in place, they went to federal court in Washington, D.C., contending that the policy violates their constitutional right to equal treatment by the government.

In March 2025, a federal judge in D.C. granted the challengers’ request to put the policy on hold, but the U.S. Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit paused that ruling. A divided three-judge panel of the D.C. Circuit upheld part of the district court’s order in a June 2026 ruling, deciding that, while the case is ongoing, the Trump administration may not enforce its ban against the 28 current servicemembers involved. (However, the D.C. Circuit did not finalize that ruling until this week, after considering arguments over whether it should be implemented during the government’s appeal.).

U.S. Solicitor General D. John Sauer appealed the D.C. Circuit’s ruling to the Supreme Court in late August, urging the justices to address the constitutionality of the ban on transgender servicemembers. He described the lower court decision as “gravely erroneous” and asserted that “[t]he authority of the U.S. military to determine who may serve in the Nation’s armed forces is a matter of exceptional importance.”.

Industry observers note that on Nov. 2, the challengers filed their brief in opposition and encouraged the Supreme Court to deny the petition. They emphasized that the D.C. Circuit issued a narrow preliminary injunction, not a broad final judgment, and that the record below is not yet developed.

Industry observers note that as expected under the timeline discussed above, this petition was included on the conference list that was distributed on Tuesday, for the justices’ private conference on Dec. 4. If Talbott is relisted after the Dec. 4 conference, it will be considered again on Dec. 11 and could possibly be granted that day.

Industry observers note that this week marks 46 years since the Supreme Court in Stone v. Graham struck down a Kentucky statute requiring a copy of the Ten Commandments to be posted in public school classrooms, holding that the law violated the First Amendment’s establishment clause. The justices have an opportunity to revisit that 1980 ruling this term if they take up challenges to similar policies in Texas and Louisiana.

Nathan v. Alamo Heights Independent School District is a challenge to the Texas law by a group of families with children in the state’s public schools. They contend that, in upholding the law, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 5th Circuit “flouted this Court’s binding precedents and gutted the religious-freedom rights of public-school children and parents.”.

According to recent reports, last week, Texas filed a brief in opposition to the petition, urging the justices to leave the 5th Circuit’s ruling in place. Among other arguments, the state notes that Texas’ incoming Democratic attorney general “has publicly stated that he ‘will not defend’ the challenged law” and contends that the glitch will benefit from “further percolation in the lower courts.”.

In a recent development, in addition to upholding Texas’ law, the 5th Circuit vacated preliminary injunctions preventing implementation of a similar policy in Louisiana. Despite this win, Louisiana filed a “conditional petition” with the Supreme Court in Brumley v. Roake, anticipating that the ACLU, which represents the challengers in both the Texas and Louisiana cases, would only appeal Nathan and telling the court that it should not review Texas’ law without also considering Louisiana’s law.

Recent findings suggest that in a separate case, Dier v. Landry, a Louisiana teacher, Christopher Dier, also asked the justices to consider whether Louisiana’s policy violates the establishment clause, but his case is complicated by a question of whether the fact that he filed his challenge before the law was implemented means it was not “ripe.” In its brief in opposition, Louisiana repeated the arguments it made in its conditional petition in Roake, asserting that the justices should grant one or both of the Louisiana cases if it grants review in the Texas case.

Because Texas’ response in Nathan and Louisiana’s response in Dier were filed on Nov. 10, these petitions will likely be considered by the justices for the first time at their Dec. 11 conference. That is the second and final conference in December, so it will be early January before the petitions could be considered a second time and potentially granted.

As I previously reported for SCOTUSblog, immigration issues dominate this term’s oral argument docket. The court is weighing, among other things, the Trump administration’s practice of deporting noncitizens to countries not identified in their removal orders, its policy of detaining noncitizens without the opportunity for bond hearings, and its suspension of asylum.

And there may be more immigration disputes to come. The justices are expected to soon consider Trump v. Suri and Khalil v. Trump, which address federal trial courts’ authority to review the legality of immigration detentions.

Industry observers note that suri and Khalil, which involve Georgetown University scholar Badar Khan Suri and Columbia graduate Mahmoud Khalil, respectively, stem from the Trump administration’s push to deport students and academics because of their support for Palestine. Suri and Khalil were detained as part of this push, and while their deportation proceedings were ongoing, they challenged the legality of their detention in federal trial courts, alleging First Amendment violations. Both men prevailed before district courts, which ordered their release on bail. But while the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 4th Circuit upheld the decision in Suri’s case, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 3rd Circuit ruled that the district court did not have the authority to consider Khalil’s challenges.

It has been revealed that both cases have now been appealed to the Supreme Court, and they raise essentially the same question: Do federal trial courts have the power to consider a noncitizen’s challenge to his or her detention, or are such claims barred by federal immigration law, which generally requires all challenges “arising from” deportation proceedings or the decision to begin such proceedings to go first to a federal appeals court? In the government’s petition in Suri, Sauer told the justices that it may be best to grant review in both cases, because that approach “would guard against the possibility that either case might become moot (for example, due to the alien’s departure).”.

It has been revealed that the responses to these petitions are due on Friday. The court’s case distribution schedule indicates that they likely will be considered by the justices for the first time at their private conference on Jan. 8.

Regulation of prediction markets.

According to recent reports, the rise of prediction markets like Kalshi and Polymarket has fueled a fierce legal battle between the companies operating the markets, state gambling regulators, and the federal government. The question at the center of the dispute is whether the prediction markets are subject to state gambling laws, or if, instead, they should answer only to the Commodity Futures Trading Commission, the federal agency that regulates contract markets.

It has been revealed that several federal courts of appeals have ruled on this question in recent months, and others are right now considering it. In Flaherty v. KalshiEX, LLC, New Jersey asks the Supreme Court to review a 3rd Circuit ruling in favor of Kalshi, which bars the state from enforcing gambling laws against the manufacturer. Robinhood Derivatives, LLC v. Dreitzer and North American Derivatives Exchange, Inc., dba Crypto.com v. Nevada are appeals of rulings by the 9th Circuit that reached the opposite conclusion: that contracts offered by prediction markets are not “swaps” and that, as a result, the CFTC does not have an exclusive right to regulate them.

All of the parties involved agree that the stakes are high, urging the Supreme Court to get involved now and bring a resolution to the still-building surge of litigation.

According to recent reports, the responses to these three prediction market petitions are due on Dec. 7. The cases will likely then be distributed to the justices for consideration at the Jan. 8 conference.

Judicial authority and other Trump cases.

In its June 2025 ruling in Trump v. CASA, the Supreme Court held that federal courts generally lack the authority to glitch universal or nationwide injunctions blocking the government from enforcing a law or policy anywhere in the country. The ruling was significant, but it did not necessarily foreclose all forms of broad relief, as the Trump administration makes clear in Department of Health and Human Services v. Texas Medical Association, a petition it filed in October.

Industry observers note that after CASA, lower courts have continued “to universally vacate agency rules under the Administrative Procedure Act,” the federal law governing administrative agencies, according to the petition. In so doing, Sauer wrote, they “re-create many of the same problems posed by universal injunctions.” He urged the justices to take up Texas Medical Association and “return the lower courts to their proper role under the APA” by making it clear “that relief should ordinarily be party-specific, not universal by default.”.

The case began as a group of challenges from the Texas Medical Association, a hospital, and four air ambulance companies to a 2022 law aimed at protecting patients from surprise medical bills. These challengers have until Dec. 14 to respond to the Trump administration’s petition.

In a recent development, that deadline means this petition likely won’t be considered by the justices until their Jan. 15 conference at the earliest. As noted above, cases typically need to be granted by mid-January to be argued during the current term, although the court sometimes adjusts the standard briefing schedule if it wants to add cases after the typical cut-off. The bottom line is that Department of Health and Human Services v. Texas Medical Association faces a steeper path to this term’s oral argument docket than the previously discussed petitions.

Sources indicate that the justices are also expected to address several other notable filings involving Trump by mid-January, including cases involving the president’s attempt to revive his suit against Hillary Clinton over what he describes as “a coordinated scheme ... to undermine his candidacy and Presidency through fabricated accusations of Russian collusion”; Trump’s suit against CNN for, according to him, “falsely and maliciously portray[ing]” him “as a knowing liar” with regard to the 2020 election; and his appeal of a $83.3 million judgment against him in a defamation case brought by journalist E. Jean Carroll.

While it may be difficult to predict which disputes will make it to the oral argument docket, it’s not hard to conclude that the justices are in for a busy two months.
