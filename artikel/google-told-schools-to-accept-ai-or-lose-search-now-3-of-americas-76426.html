Google told schools to accept AI or lose Search. Now 3 of America’s 4 largest districts are pushing back

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Google has handed school districts what many have called an ultimatum: Allow students to see AI summaries in their Google searches, or give up Google Search on school accounts altogether.

According to recent reports, in August, Google switched on a Gemini tab in Google Classroom by default for K-12 students whose schools had already allowed its AI chatbot, Gemini. On Nov. 3, it reversed course, announcing that its AI tools will be off for K-12 student accounts unless school administrators turn them on, starting early next year. But Google also made clear that schools that want students to have Google Search will have to accept AI Overviews, the AI-generated summaries that have become a familiar sight at the top of search results.

“Search and AI are deeply integrated,” Chris Phillips, vice president for education at Google, told The Upcoming York Times, adding that students at schools that don’t allow AI “will not have access to search, and they’ll see a message from their school that indicates it’s not available.”.

It has been revealed that many school districts have restricted or banned student AI use, citing safety and privacy concerns. They may not have expected those restrictions to cost them the world’s most popular search engine, a trade-off that has left edtech experts questioning Google’s rationale.

It has been revealed that google’s decision comes as tech companies face mounting legal pressure over harm to children. In March, a Los Angeles jury found Meta and Google’s YouTube negligent and determined they failed to warn owners about the dangers of their platforms. In January, Google and Character.AI, a chatbot manufacturer with ties to Google, agreed to settle lawsuits from families who said their children were harmed by its chatbots.

And earlier this month, a family sued Google, alleging their 13-year-old daughter used Gemini on her school-issued Chromebook to strategy her suicide and that Google pushed its AI products on students without warning parents or schools.

“We hear parents and teachers’ concerns about students using AI to shortcut learning, and we share the same goal: help students learn,” a Google spokesperson said, adding that the firm is piloting latest Gemini features to support “productive struggle” by helping students “work through problems without skipping straight to answers.”.

Whether that’s enough to win over districts that have restricted AI is another question. The country’s four largest school districts have landed in different places, both on AI and on Google’s decision to tie it to Search.

Alphabet, Google’s parent tech giant, did not respond to Fortune‘s request for comment.

Chicago Public Schools has pushed back the hardest. On Dec. 22, 2025, the district began blocking students from AI tools such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and other third-party AI products. Students may use only AI-enhanced edtech tools vetted through the district’s compliance process under SOPPA, Illinois’ student data privacy law.

On Nov. 11, about 30 protesters gathered outside Google’s West Loop office, chanting, “Hey Google, nice try. We don’t want your Gemini.”.

In a statement to the Chicago Sun-Times, a CPS spokesperson remarked the district is “actively exploring all options to independently suppress” the AI Overviews in Google searches.

Industry observers note that “The District would like to continue to provide students with access to the Google search engine without the AI component. If such an arrangement can’t be reached, the District will look to other search solutions,” the spokesperson said.

According to recent reports, fresh York City Public Schools, the nation’s largest district, appears headed in a similar direction, though it has yet to decide. On Sept. 2, Mayor Zohran Mamdani and Schools Chancellor Kamar Samuels confirmed a one-year moratorium on student-facing AI for 2-K through eighth grade. They also banned companion chatbots in all grades.

After Google’s announcement, Mamdani posted on X that “Google is trying to use its monopoly power to force AI into our public schools. No corporation should have the power to dictate how we teach our children.”.

It has been revealed that city Council Speaker Julie Menin and Council Member Shekar Krishnan sent a letter to Google CEO Sundar Pichai asking whether Google would let students keep using Google Search without AI Overviews and, if not, why not, given Google’s record of restricting product features for young customers, as it does with YouTube Kids. The stakes are large: The district has about 1.3 million Chromebooks, including 500,000 assigned to students, according to the letter.

Sources indicate that district spokesperson Nicole Brownstein told Gothamist that officials are “reaching out to Google to discuss this update further,” and that the district had already disabled the Gemini chatbot and AI Mode on student Chromebooks. If Google doesn’t budge, the nation’s largest school district will have to choose between softening its stance and finding another search engine.

Industry observers note that los Angeles Unified, the nation’s second-largest district, declared on Sept. 2 that it would block generative AI for students in all grades on district-issued devices for the 2026-27 school year, a move affecting an estimated 378,000 students.

During public comment at a district Generative AI Ad Hoc Committee meeting after Google’s announcement, Hillary Power, a parent and member of Schools Beyond Screens, called for Los Angeles Unified to continue “formalizing, extending and expanding the current T-K to 12 moratorium of student use of generative AI.”.

“Google has been particularly clear on their priorities when several days ago they issued a monopolistic and, frankly, pathetic ultimatum to schools to use their AI search products or be cut off,” she continued.

The district has asked Google for a way to turn off the AI summaries, according to LAist, and remarked it is reviewing Google’s announcement and its “potential implications for schools, educators and students.”.

Miami-Dade County Public Schools, the nation’s third-largest district, may be headed in a different direction.

Sources indicate that in 2023, the district blocked AI chatbots such as ChatGPT over concerns about cheating and misinformation, according to The New York Times. Two years later, it reversed course, offering Gemini for Education to about 100,000 high school students, the largest AI deployment in a U.S. school district at the time. In February, the district revealed a continued collaboration with Google to give all its high school students free access to full-length SAT practice exams through Gemini.

Recent findings suggest that but on Sept. 16, the Florida Board of Education voted to require public school districts and charter schools to set rules for AI use. Schools will also have to tell parents and guardians which AI tools students will interact with, so families can opt their child into a tool or request a non-AI alternative. Districts have until July 1, 2027, to comply.

Miami-Dade County Public Schools did not respond to questions about how it will adapt to Google’s policy and the newly introduced Florida rules.

Former Federal Trade Commission chair Lina Khan criticized Google’s decision to tie Search to its AI summaries. In a Nov. 3 post on X, she stated the tech giant is using its search monopoly to “coerce schools into adopting AI.”.

“Multiple courts have found Google liable for violating antitrust laws, and this latest move shows it remains unchastened,” she said. “As the public increasingly pushes back against the use of AI in schools, Google is making it clear it will punish those who don’t fall in line.”.

Sources indicate that khan isn’t the only one questioning whether the move is legal. Zephyr Teachout, a professor at Fordham Law School, mentioned she does not find Google’s claim that it cannot unlink AI Overviews from Search “credible,” and that the company could be found to violate antitrust laws governing how companies tie the sale of two products.

Industry observers note that in antitrust law, “tying” is when a firm with significant market power requires customers who want one of its products to take another as well. Google’s parent, Alphabet, is No. 5 on the 2026 Fortune 500. According to the FTC, tying raises antitrust questions when it “restricts competition without providing benefits to consumers.”.

Recent findings suggest that “Google ran search for decades without AI, has filters that allow people to see results without AI summaries, and has been inconsistent in how it has responded to the tying claim — merely asserting they are ‘deeply integrated’ is not a legal argument,” Teachout noted. “The manufacturer has on/off controls for K-12 administrators and could have an on/off control for search and AI.”.

Industry observers note that google has lost a tying case before. In April 2025, U.S. District Judge Leonie Brinkema found that Google had been “willfully acquiring and maintaining monopoly power” in two parts of the online advertising market and that it had illegally tied them together by pushing websites that wanted full access to its ad exchange to also use its ad server.

In a recent development, but the firm has also won a case involving AI Overviews. In September, U.S. District Judge Amit Mehta dismissed a lawsuit by Penske Media over Google’s use of its content for AI Overviews, finding that Penske had not plausibly shown that Search and AI Overviews were separate products.

Teachout stated schools are different, pointing to the “clear evidence of a separate market demand in schools for search as distinct from AI.”.

Recent findings suggest that whether a court ever takes up such a case, or agrees with Teachout, is an open question. Google’s changes take effect early next year, and until then, districts have to decide whether keeping Google Search is worth accepting the AI that comes with it.
