Newly introduced species of flying frog discovered in a mountain pond called Coca-Cola Lake

In a recent development, scientists identified a new flying frog species in Indonesia's mountains. DNA, body features, and calls helped confirm the discovery.

A seasonal pond sits high in the mountains of Sulawesi in Indonesia. Climbers know it as Coca-Cola Lake.

A upcoming species of flying frog has been collected there and it has been named Rhacophorus kurniatiae.

Body measurements barely separate this frog from its closest relative. The difference demonstrates up when the animals breed. The relative builds a nest of foam.

The newly introduced species leaves its eggs in a loose mass of jelly instead.

No other flying frog on Sulawesi has been documented laying its eggs in a loose, irregular mass of jelly.

The frogs were collected on November 18, 2011, by Wahyu Trilaksono. Trilaksono is a herpetologist with Indonesia’s National Research and Innovation Agency.

Four colleagues from Indonesia and the United States joined Trilaksono in describing the fresh species.

Recent findings suggest that thirty-one adults were collected in total. Every one of them came from that single pond.

Males measure 1.7 to 1.8 inches (43 to 47 millimeters) from snout to rump. Females are larger at 1.9 to 2.2 inches (49 to 57 millimeters). That is roughly the length of an AA battery.

In a recent development, the pond is found 6,155 feet (1,876 meters) up in the Mekongga Mountains. This range lies on the southeastern arm of Sulawesi.

The pond is not natural. It was created by a logging organization as a place to store logs.

It has been revealed that rain and seepage fill the pond for part of the year. No stream runs into it or out of it. During the dry season, it shrinks to a few pools and exposed moss.

Loggers worked these mountains before they became a protected forest in 1994.

The 2011 trip belonged to a series of expeditions run for the ICBG Indonesia Project.

Recent findings suggest that the project was a biological inventory of the mountains. Its Indonesian and American partners included the Museum of Wildlife and Fish Biology at the University of California, Davis.

According to recent reports, the field team was led by Alan Hitch, who is a research affiliate at that museum. Hitch recalled reaching the pond on the way up the mountain. Green frogs were all over it.

“You don’t really see frogs in major congregations like that,” Hitch stated.

Biologists describe an event like this as explosive breeding. Adults gather suddenly along the edge of the water for a brief burst of mating. Heavy rain appears to trigger it at Coca-Cola Lake.

Asian flying frogs are tree frogs. Their name comes from gliding, which is done on large webbed feet. A foam nest is where the typical flying frog lays its eggs.

Rhacophorus boeadii is the closest relative of the newly introduced frog, and it breeds that way. Scientists named it last year from mountains in central Sulawesi.

It has been revealed that coca-Cola Lake is shared with a third flying frog. Its eggs are packed into compact balls of gel.

According to recent reports, R. kurniatiae does neither of these things. Its eggs are laid straight into an irregular mass of jelly that contains no foam. The mass hangs above the water or is loosely attached to whatever is nearby.

It has been revealed that the frogs showed no preference for any plant species or leaf shape. The embryos stay inside the jelly until they become tadpoles.

The authors wrote that the habit may reflect an adaptation to water that comes and goes.

It has been revealed that the team needed to check whether the pond frogs were a species of their own. Twenty-eight body measurements were taken on preserved specimens for that purpose.

The 81 specimens came from six flying frog species found on Sulawesi and nearby islands.

Industry observers note that eighteen of them were the fresh frog. Its females were compared with R. boeadii females. None of the 28 measurements differed reliably between them.

Recent findings suggest that three measurements did differ among males. These were eye width, the length of the first toe and the width of the pad on the fourth toe.

Recent findings suggest that the DNA was close as well. One stretch of mitochondrial DNA differed by 2.19% to 2.43% between the two species. The gap between the fresh frog and the other four species was about 4% to 5.7%.

Andrew Engilis Jr. is the curator of the UC Davis museum and a co-author of the study.

Species can sometimes “look the same, but their behavior is different and their genetics are different,” Engilis stated.

Animals like these are known to biologists as cryptic species.

Recent findings suggest that A few features do set the latest frog apart on close inspection. A small flap of skin is present on each heel. A fold of skin runs along its limbs and its third finger carries more webbing.

The call of one male was recorded on a digital camera at 6:40 p.m. It lasted about 1.4 seconds.

Four notes made up the call. A single short pulse opened it. Three notes of three to five pulses each came next. The call of R. boeadii is a single note.

The team also wanted to compare the call with that of the third flying frog at the lake.

Call measurements published in 2015 by Hellen Kurniati were used for this. She was a long-serving herpetologist at Indonesia’s Museum Zoologicum Bogoriense.

It has been revealed that the latest species has been named in honor of Kurniati. She died before it was described.

In a recent development, only one recording from one male was good enough to be analyzed. The authors wrote that the opening note may be that male’s own variation. It may not be a trait of the whole species.

R. boeadii is known from two mountain regions in central Sulawesi. R. kurniatiae has been found only on the southeastern arm.

In a recent development, two major faults run close to the line between those ranges. Faults are long breaks in Earth’s crust where blocks of land slide past each other.

The authors suggested that the faults may have acted as barriers, splitting an ancestral frog population in two.

A similar pattern is seen in the flying lizards and macaques of Sulawesi.

Industry observers note that closely related species live on different arms of the island. The idea has not been tested for the frogs. More genetic analysis will be needed for that, the authors wrote.

So far, researchers have found the frog only at Coca-Cola Lake.

According to recent reports, this raises potential conservation concerns, the authors wrote. They pointed to pressure from farming, deforestation and climate variability across Sulawesi.

The amphibians of Sulawesi have been studied less than those of Java, Sumatra and Borneo.

Thirty-three amphibian species are known from the region and six of them are flying frogs. The researchers expect the true number to be higher.

In a recent development, several species have been added by recent fieldwork. One of them is a fanged frog that nests on land.

It has been revealed that engilis said the region is a candidate for national park status. Continued species discovery could help speed that process, Engilis added.

The authors called for targeted surveys of the island’s mountain habitats.

According to recent reports, other high ponds in the Mekongga Mountains could be surveyed in the future. Such work could show whether Coca-Cola Lake is the frog’s only home.

The full study was published in the journal Zootaxa.
