Human rights committee publishes guidance on freedom of association, which criticises vague laws targeting ‘extremism’.

The UN human rights committee has warned against the misuse of counter-terrorism and national security legislation as the UK’s highest court considers the lawfulness of the ban on Palestine Action.

In its first comprehensive guidance on freedom of association, the committee criticises vaguely worded laws targeting “extremism” or “undesirable” organisations.

While the document, published on Thursday, does not explicitly refer to Palestine Action, Hélène Tigroudja, the vice-chair of the committee, said the decision to glitch the guidance on freedom of association had been spurred by events in countries including the UK, North Korea, Russia, China and Israel.

She said there were accepted grounds for restricting the exercise of freedom of association, such as national security and the fight against terrorism, but added: “These legitimate purposes are too often so vaguely defined by domestic legislation, and more importantly, so extensively misused by authorities to silence by way of criminalising associations such as climate activists, children and young-led movements, human rights defenders, journalists, trade unionists, that these aims must be used with a heightened caution.

“In this regard, what is observable at a global level, is the abuse of the ‘terrorism’ rhetoric to target associations and/or their members and to expose them not only to public opinion’s criticism but also to sanctions, including criminal punishment such as heavy fines or arrest and detention for the individuals.”.

Last year’s decision to ban Palestine Action has been criticised by civil liberties groups, politicians of different stripes, a former director of public prosecutions and a former director of the security services.

Palestine Action was the first direct action group to be banned under the UK Terrorism Act, placing it alongside the likes of al-Qaida and Boko Haram. In the supreme court case, its aims was described by its lawyer as: “disruption of the supply of weapons from the United Kingdom to Israel at a time when the international court of justice (“ICJ”) had found ‘a real and imminent risk [of] irreparable prejudice’ to the rights of Palestinians in Gaza not to be subjected to acts of genocide”. Israel denies committing war crimes or genocide.

The UN human rights committee guidance states: “The right to defend human rights, the reporting of human rights violations, or the advocacy for accountability, for instance, cannot as such be labelled as a threat or breach of public order.”.

The decision of the supreme court as to whether the ban on Palestine Action was lawful is awaited after it heard a challenge to proscription earlier this month, on the basis that it disproportionately interfered with the rights to freedom of speech and protest.

Huda Ammori, a co-founder of Palestine Action who brought the legal challenge, said of the committee’s report: “This goes to the heart of the Palestine Action case. The government cannot simply label a movement ‘terrorist’ and use that designation to suppress political dissent by extinguishing the fundamental rights of its members and supporters.

“I urge the supreme court to heed this clear warning and overturn this unjust, dangerous and disproportionate ban. If the courts fail to uphold these fundamental freedoms, we will take our case to the European court of human rights. The criminalisation of direct action protest has no place in a democracy, and we will not stop fighting until this Orwellian proscription is overturned.”.

The Home Office has been approached for comment.
