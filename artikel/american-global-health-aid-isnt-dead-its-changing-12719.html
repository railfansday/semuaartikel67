📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that americans might have assumed that their country was out of the business of promoting global health. On his first day in office, President Trump announced the United States’ withdrawal from the World Health Organization and abruptly “paused” foreign aid. Over the following weeks, Elon Musk took a chain saw to the U.S. Agency for International Development.

According to recent reports, in the wake of these actions, reporters documented shortages of tuberculosis, malaria and H.I.V. drugs, widespread clinic closures and the abandonment of patients enrolled in medical trials. Many programs never resumed, and researchers predicted that hundreds of thousands of avoidable deaths would occur in the first year alone. Assessing whether this came true is complicated by the fact that programs tracking health outcomes were among those the U.S. ended.

It has been revealed that congress had originally allocated about $10 billion to the government’s main global health programs, or roughly $30 per U.S. resident, to run one of the world’s most effective foreign aid efforts. But even after the wave of staff terminations and grant cancellations, Congress still wanted that money to be spent. To direct the funds, last September, the U.S. State Department released a fresh initiative: the America First Global Health Strategy.

This strategy endorsed some goals that experts have long championed regardless of political fealty, such as having recipient countries take more responsibility for running and funding the programs.

But it also had a distinctly Trumpian element that seemed shocking to observers of the foreign aid world: The money had significant — and unusual — strings attached. Countries that receive aid were invited to negotiate “America First” agreements with the United States “that promote our national interests while saving millions of lives,” according to a letter from Secretary of State Marco Rubio.
