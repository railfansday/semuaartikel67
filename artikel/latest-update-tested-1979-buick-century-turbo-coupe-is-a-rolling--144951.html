From the June 1979 matter of Car and Driver.

According to recent reports, A paradox, according to a well­-thumbed copy of Webster’s Fresh World, is a statement that seems contradictory, but may be true. Which brings us imme­diately to the Buick Century Turbo Coupe. On one hand it most definitely is a Buick, which is automatically a state­ment. As most of us were growing up, the Buicks in our lives belonged to the family doctor or other such community pillars. Buicks were sedate, relatively ex­pensive cars—stop-offs on the way up the GM ladder for the man with the growing bank account, or the highest reasonable aspirations for the workaday man who longed to rise out of Ford and Chevy land.

On the other hand we have this Tur­bo Coupe posturing about in its rackish, spoilered finery, squatting on its we­-mean-business fat tires, its twin pipes burbling, acting more Trans Am than Buick. A contradiction.

Buick started making out-of-character performance noises a few years ago when its chief engineer, Lloyd Reuss, and a band of enthusiastic young engi­neers decided to crank up a turbocharg­ing project for the V-6 engine. Their ef­forts were first seen in the 1975 Indy pace car, which also sported some very un-Buick-like suspension. In 1977 Buick offered a slick Sport Coupe variation on the LeSabre and Regal theme. The Le­Sabre version we tested was so good it elicited comments like “a breath of fresh air for people who’ve hungered to own a good-handling American car.” And one exuberant staffer stated all it needed was a three-pointed star on the hood to make it a Mercedes-class piece. In 1978 the turbo motor went into pro­duction, and the stage was set for this year's Turbo Coupe.

Bruce Robinson is a project engineer at Buick’s Flint, Michigan, headquar­ters, and one of those enthusiastic types Reuss brought in to spice up Buick’s slide-rule department. Reuss is gone now—he’s chief engineer over at Chev­rolet—but Robinson and the Reuss en­thusiasm still exist. Robinson was as­signed the Turbo Coupe. He didn’t build it single-handedly, but as much as any car that pops off the line can be said to be one man’s baby, the Turbo Coupe is his.

Recent findings suggest that “We wanted to go from GT, like the LeSabre and Regal Sport Coupes, to All Out,” Robinson explains. “We wanted to make it go, and handle, and stop like no other Buick.”.

He did it, he explains, by creating what Buick now calls the FE1 package, a giant step up from the existing FE2, which is Buick’s equivalent of Chevro­let’s famous F41 handling upgrade. The FE1, available only on the Turbo Coupe, is basically stiffer springs, steer­ing changes, and wider wheels. The front spring rates have been increased from the regular Sport Coupe specs of 365 pounds per inch to a giganto 440. Rear springs are up to 140 lbs/in from 125. (For further comparison, the base V-6 Century coupe has spring rates, front/rear, of 300/115.) Anti-roll bars are the same for FE1 and FE2: 32 mm front, 22 mm rear; but another massive up­grade for the Turbo came in the rear upper-control-arm bushings, little de­vices we don’t think much about but which have a lot to do with transient re­sponse. The base and Sport Coupe ver­sions have bushings rated at 20,000 lbs/in; the Turbo Coupe is fitted with 35,000-lbs/in bushings. Rounding out the FE2 package is a quicker steering ra­tio, 14:1 instead of 15.13:1, and P205/70R-14 radials on seven-inch rims.

In a recent development, but as much as we wanted to know all that good spring and tire stuff, we were even more curious about the exhaust system. It is, in a word, loud. We spent a lot of time playing with the throttle and letting off in low and second just to hear that sound-from-the-past, that Golden Oldie exhaust noise. How'd Robinson do it?

The sound, he reveals, was a specifi­cally planned part of the overall image. They got it by the simple expedient of routing the exhaust into a single con­verter and then out into a pair of ex­haust pipes that carry a resonator each. No mufflers.

“A delightful byproduct,” Robinson gleefully reveals, “was an extra ten horsepower due to the reduced back pressure.” The odds on finding ten horsepower these days without spend­ing ’leventeen trillion dollars in devel­opment are about the same as Bianca and her pals dropping by your place for dinner tonight: slim to none.

That throaty exhaust note, by the way, is more evident inside the Turbo Coupe than outside. The body acts as a sound chamber to increase the effect. “The noise level passed the EPA tests,” Robinson says, “but we’re not about to ask them to check it again to make sure.” Right.

So Robinson and his associates have built a Buick with all the right suspen­sion bits and pieces, they’ve come up with a creditable 175 horses to move it around, they’ve given it an exhaust sys­tem to warm the cockles of an old hot rodder’s heart, and they’ve dressed it up with spoilers and flat-black paint. Why, we had to wonder, after doing all that, did they leave out some traditional, mandatory even, performance-car parts, like a manual transmission and an in­strument panel with some gauges?

“Simple,” Robinson indicates. “Money.”.

Adding a manual transmission would have required a complete 50,000-mile EPA durability test, and that costs hun­dreds of thousands. Ditto a different in­strument panel. Buick didn’t have any­thing on the shelf that would have bolt­ed in. Designing a fresh panel and tool­ing up to produce it would also have cost hundreds of thousands.

“Anything you spend on building a latest model, or a new version of an exist­ing car,” Robinson explains, “you have to figure you can recover in sales.” And Buick doesn’t figure to sell too many Turbo Coupes, it turns out. It's an image car, a showroom-traffic build­er, so if you see one on the floor of your local Buick dealer, you’re probably looking at his allotment for the year. Should you jump at it?

The Turbo Coupe is easily the best-­looking of all the “aero” designs, which Buick and Olds offer in two- and four­-door versions. The improvement in looks seems to come from the simple addition of the rear-deck spoiler, which also happens to reduce overall drag, Buick reports, by a healthy 18 percent. (The two-door aero design, by the way, will be around at least through 1980; the ugly four-door is going away.).

Recent findings suggest that inside, as we’ve hinted, there is little to distinguish the Turbo Coupe from its lesser brethren. The handsome, but un­informative, instrument panel contains a tricolor turbo-boost indicator that reads out in one, A Little, and Whoosh. Our test car had vinyl buckets, and while they provide pretty good sup­port in all the right places, they are as slick as greased owl potty. Cloth is avail­able, however, and we recommend it.

In a recent development, over the road, the Turbo’s yes-I-am, no-I’m-not nature surfaces again. De­spite all the good things Robinson and his merry band have wrought, the car’s manners are still rather Buick-like. Which isn’t necessarily all bad. Your teeth aren’t jarred by every tar strip, and the car is very quiet at cruise. But there is an unexpected numbness in the steering that should have been factored out. Response is good, but that all-vital feel is missing.

Despite the suspension modifications, the Turbo Coupe is still an understeer­er. That’s to be expected, so no points lost there. General Motors is a firm be­liever that understeer is safer than over­steer. Better, the reasoning goes, the in­competents plow off the road nose-first, scrubbing speed all the way, than have the tail leading the procession at prodi­gious speed. Fair enough, because the knowledgeable Trans Am or Z28 driver can put the boot into his mount at the proper time and induce some very pleasant oversteer. Not so with the Tur­bo Coupe; the little engine-that-would­-be-a-V-8 just doesn’t produce enough torque rapidly enough to let you play the tail-out game with much success. The boost gauge may be up there in Whoosh, but the tail will be dutifully following the furrow the front end is plowing. We’re talking about out there at the cop-baiting, wife-scaring, dog­-cowering limit, of course. In normal brisk motoring, the suspension does a good job. There’s no willow-wallow here, thank you.

The Coupe also does other good things. You’ll note that our comparison graphs show it at the top in acceleration ­and braking. Straight-line numbers will not disappoint.

The Turbo Coupe certainly has the flash and dash Buick wants for its show­rooms, and it’s sure to draw its share of peekers and pokers and hood lifters. The traditional Buick buyer will peek and poke and then head off to the Elec­tra or LeSabre four-doors. The curious that stopped off on their way to price Trans Am and Z28s will poke and peek with a good bit more interest. And when they learn they can have a bare-bones Turbo Coupe for a little more than $6000, they may even give it some seri­ous thought. But our best guess is they’ll eventually find their way back to those other showrooms. The Turbo Coupe, as good a Buick as it is, doesn’t seem to be right for either group.

Sources indicate that what we want is Bruce Robinson and his boys to turn their attention to a newly introduced Skylark Sport Coupe. Turbo that little honey, slap the FE1 package on it, and stand back. When we suggested that to Robinson, his eyes lit up with what could only be engineer-type lust. So maybe it’ll happen. And maybe it’ll even have a tachometer.

I must admit, the first night I drove the Turbo Coupe I kept putting my foot into it and running up through the gears, then backing off and doing it again and again, all the way home. The pumped-up little six, all snarling and willing, was just too inviting for me to ignore. Sometimes I’d even drop it into low and back off just to listen to the popping of the exhaust as it simmered down. I acted as silly as a newly licensed adolescent that night, but you just can’t resist playing in the Turbo Coupe. It’s that kind of car.

Recent findings suggest that but what turned me on the most was the message hidden within its sophisticat­ed mechanicals: a fresh generation of mus­cle cars, one that pays its respects to both ecology and our tight petroleum supply, is not only possible, it’s just around the corner. All Buick has to do is work its tur­bocharging wizardry on the half-ton-­lighter Skylark front-driver and we’ll have the first of that new breed. Let us pray. —Rich Ceppos.

I happen to be a firm believer in turbo­charging and all the wonderful things it can do for our cars—David E. Davis, Jr., to the contrary—but this Turbo Coupe from Buick stretches the very heartstrings of my enthusiasm. Past the limit. It’s a horrible fate for any engine as spunky as Buick’s turbo V-6. This motor should be zapping around a 2000-pound sports car or burning the tires off Buick’s chic little Skyhawk. Instead, it’s sentenced to the homeliest hunchback sedan Buick has stamped out in recent history. Grown men wince at the sight of this TR7 garage on wheels. And I wince at the thought of such an eager engine straining its heart out against a 3500-pound curb weight and an automatic transmission, without so much as a tachometer or a boost gauge to register the effort. To finish off the in­sult, this Century’s all gussied up in what I assume is Buick’s idea of a performance disguise. It looks like something the local sheriff would use to sneak up on the kids down at Shakey's. They wouldn’t be con­vinced, and neither am I. —Don Sherman.

Normally, when you dip the accelerator on a six-cylinder American car, it gives you the feeling you’re saving gas. Saving gas is a noble pastime but the feeling of saving gas is drudgery, right up there with Cometing out the sink.

According to recent reports, that is why I think this Buick stands to give the American six-cylinder a good name. When you dip the pedal, things hap­pen. First off, the whole dashboard glows orange. (Knepper stated the illumination comes from the boost light, but it’s hidden behind so many spokes and switches that I never actually saw it.) Then the exhaust turns mean and the bucket seat gets all pushy. Instead of saving gas, you get the feeling you’re hurrying.

It has been revealed that really, except for a count of the spark-­plug wires, there’s nothing six-cylinder about this car. It feels more like a high schooler’s hot rod, glass packs and all, and I had the urge to head on down to the drive-in and burn some rubber. I haven’t been in that mood for years. —Patrick Bedard.
