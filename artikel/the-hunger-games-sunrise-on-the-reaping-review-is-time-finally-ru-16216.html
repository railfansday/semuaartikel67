Sources indicate that not so long ago, the old it’s-funny-because-it’s-true joke about soap operas was that people in soap operas never seemed to watch soap operas. But now that reality demonstrates have all but taken over that space, the function of soaps has evaporated almost entirely, while “unscripted TV” has absorbed all of its clichés. When it first appeared in 2012, The Hunger Games seemed fresh and even satirical; now, though, it just feels tired, and this sixth lackluster offering raises only one serious question: Hasn’t anyone forced to take part in a violent dystopic game show ever seen a violent dystopic game show before?

Sources indicate that aha, you might say. This is a prequel, taking place a full 24 years before the original. True, the citizens of Panem have only had to put up with 49 editions of the Hunger Games thus far, but, leaving aside the ancient art of combat-for-entertainment explored in both Gladiator movies, in our world the idea of films pitting humans against humans has been around since 1932’s The Most Dangerous Game — and we’ve seen it all before, even if they haven’t. True, the target audience for this wasn’t alive for Logan’s Run (1976), or The Running Man (1987), and would have certainly missed Bong Joon Ho’s little-seen Snowpiercer (2013). But most would have been around for Edgar Wright’s recent Running Man remake, which this film most closely resembles. Watch on Deadline.

In a recent development, it begins in District 12 in almost Hillbilly Elegy levels of poverty-porn, with the first of several irrelevant chapter headings: “The Birthday.” As we’ve already gathered, it is the 16th birthday of Haymitch Abernathy (Joseph Zada), and, once again, the reaping is the dark shadow that falls across his special day. When one of the “tributes” tries to do a runner (and is killed in the process), Haymitch is forced to take his place, along with local girls Maysilee Donner (McKenna Grace, who also plays her twin sister Merrilee) and Louella McCoy (Molly McCann), plus unpopular oddsmaker Wyatt Callow (Ben Wang).

In a recent development, they are all duly whisked off to the Capitol, where Haymitch is given a makeover by the lascivious fashionista Effie Trinket (Elle Fanning) and built up as the show’s bad boy by overacting show host Caesar Flickerman (Kieran Culkin) — who, admittedly, does have some good one-liners (“Look alive, children, while you still can!”). But the tributes’ first outing during the opening parade goes horribly wrong when Louella is killed in a chariot crash, not that you’d notice much difference. She is rapidly replaced by a lookalike, the equally inconsequential Lou Lou, but not before Haymitch upsets the vindictive, all-seeing, all-powerful President Coriolanus Snow (Ralph Fiennes), who sadistically does… Well, nothing at all, or at least not just yet. Just you wait, Haymitch Abernathy!!!!

As he trains for the games and prepares to enter The Arena, Haymitch gets involved in a fiendish plot to sabotage the whole enterprise, something that involves a young newbie called Ampert and his father Beetee, who is being cruelly forced to mentor his son before watching his untimely death. “The Arena is a killing machine, but all machines can be broken,” says Beetee, demonstrating this epochal moment by using a potato to turn a little fairy light on. In the meantime, Haymitch has found an unlikely potential ally in cameraman Plutarch Heavensbee (Jesse Plemons), who drops heavy hints that he is playing both ends against the middle and offers to turn a blind eye to the blueprint.

Playing Haymitch as nothing at all like the adult Woody Harrelson he will later grow up into, Zada has a naïve, doe-eyed charm that helps suspend disbelief for at least two hours of the film’s increasingly tedious running time. That indicates a lot considering The Arena itself is quite ridiculously unscary, with its prospect of poisonous plants, electric butterflies and man-eating beavers — or maybe rats, it was hard to tell even in Imax — about as terrifying as being mauled to death by The Wonderful World of Disney. Worse, though, there is no sense of peril at all this time, even when Snow finally gets round to getting his drawn-out revenge: The Hunger Games has reached its High School Musical moment where everyone gets a participation prize, and any significant death is obviously just an unfortunate accident. The cast of the original film appear briefly at the end; one can’t shake the feeling that it’s to pay their last respects.
