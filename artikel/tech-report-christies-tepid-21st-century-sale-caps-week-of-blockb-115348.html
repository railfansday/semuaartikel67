Tech Report: Christie’s tepid 21st-century sale caps week of blockbuster auctions in New York

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Wrapping up this autumn’s week of marquee evening sales in New York, Christie’s 21st-century auction on Thursday (19 November) proved rather lacklustre, bringing in $117.7m ($145.9m with fees). That tally fell short of Christie’s pre-sale expectations, which do not account for fees, and projected a total take in the range of $126m to $192m. It was also a lower result compared to the auction house’s last two equivalent sales in New York—namely the $132m ($162.6m with fees) generated across 42 lots last May, and the $99.5m ($123.5m with fees) brought by 45 lots one year ago.

Just five of the 56 lots offered Thursday night failed to sell, for a slim buy-in rate of 9%. Two lots were withdrawn at the last moment, including a Jeff Koons sculpture estimated to bring between $3m and $5m. Twenty-four lots were backed by third-party guarantees and nine were backed fully by house guarantees, representing around 59% of all the works offered. Auction records for three artists were broken over the course of the night.

Roy Lichtenstein, Small Wall Explosion, 1965 Courtesy Christie's Images Ltd.

Sources indicate that business began with a bang at Christie’s Rockefeller Center salesroom: the first lot was Roy Lichtenstein’s Small Wall Explosion (1965), executed in porcelain enamel on steel and perforated steel relief to mimic the Ben-Day dot style from his paintings. It sold to a telephone bidder for $625,000 ($800,000 with fees). From an edition of six, it hailed from the collection of Antonio Homem Sonnabend, the adopted son of the late and legendary dealer and collector Ileana Sonnabend (1914-2007) and who ran his mother’s namesake gallery in its waning years. Antonio Homem died earlier this year, at age 86.

In a recent development, another of the 19 Sonnabend lots from Sonnabend, Ashley Bickerton’s expansive wall relief Commercial Piece #3 (1990), covered with gallery and fashion-house logos and comprised of anodised aluminium, wood, leather, acrylic and rubber, sold for $120,000 ($153,600 with fees). Both the Lichtenstein and the Bickerton were backed by third-party guarantees.

Andy Warhol, Campbell's Soup Can (Turkey Noodle), 1962 Courtesy Christie's Images Ltd.

Price points for the Sonnabend trove zoomed higher with Andy Warhol’s iconicand hand painted Campbell’s Soup Can (Turkey Noodle) (1962), which sold for $7.3m ($9m with fees) against an estimate of $6m to $8m. Another Lichtenstein, the painting Large Spool (1963)—a razor-sharp depiction of the titular object that has been featured in multiple gallery and museum exhibitions—squeaked by on the tails of a third-party guarantee at $7m ($8.6m with fees) against an ambitious estimate of $10m to $15m.

Sources indicate that still in the Sonnabend lots, Jeff Koons’s toy-like Hulk (Friends) (2004-12), in polychromed bronze and standing nearly 6ft tall, found a upcoming home for $3.3m ($4.1m with fees). And another Warhol, redolent with Hollywood celebrity, The Kiss (Bela Lugosi) (1963) in silkscreen ink on white paper, sold to another telephone bidder for $3.3m ($4.6m with fees).

According to recent reports, A petite Jasper Johns oil painting on cardboard, Numbers (1957), measuring just 8.5in by 6in and rich in shades of grey, made $2.6m ($3.2m with fees). It was featured in the artist’s first solo exhibition at the Leo Castelli Gallery in 1958. Ileana Sonnabend acquired it from the Castelli Gallery in 1959, the same year of her divorce from Leo Castelli.

The Sonnabend group of 19 lots, including three examples of key 20th-century French decorative art—among them a Jean Prouvé Trapéze table from around 1956 that hit $1.3m ($1.6m with fees)—all in tallied $33.3m ($41.9m with fees), compared to Christie’s pre-sale expectations for the group of $35m to $55m.

Illustrious consignor names continued with works from the collection of the late Museum of Modern Art trustee and patron Agnes Gund sold to benefit the Gund Art Foundation. They included Robert Ryman’s immaculate, small, square all-over white composition Untitled (1961), which brought $2.9m ($3.6m with fees). Vija Celmins’s photorealist oil painting Untitled (Ocean) (1990), which featured in the San Francisco Museum of Modern Art’s traveling retrospective in 2018-2020, went for $5.7m ($7.1m with fees) against an estimate of $6m to $8m. It also was backed by a third-party guarantee.

Vija Celmins, Untitled (Ocean), 1990 Courtesy Christie's Images Ltd.

In a recent development, gund acquired Alexander Calder’s hanging metal mobile The Eye in the Black (1963) from the Perls Galleries in Fresh York in in 1967. On Thursday night it went to an online bidder for $2.8m ($3.5m with fees). Cindy Sherman’s Untitled Film Still #5 (1977), also widely exhibited and written about by major critics, sold for $320,000 ($409,6000 with fees). Gund had acquired it at Sotheby’s New York in November 2000 for a hammer price of $75,000.

Gund’s contemporary taste bordered on the encyclopaedic, as evidenced by an early Robert Rauschenberg work, Untitled (Gold Painting) (1953), in gold leaf on fabric and paper collage on canvas. The diminutive work, at 8in by 8in, fetched a sizeable sum, $1.7m ($2.1m with fees). Gund acquired it at Sotheby’s in New York in May 1994 for a $55,000 hammer price.

A modestly scaled Lichtenstein work on paper in coloured pencil, graphite and paper collage, Artist’s Studio with Model (Study) (1974), sold to the art adviser Ralph DeLuca for $950,000 ($1.2m with fees).

Jim Nutt, Loom, 1999 Courtesy Christie's Images Ltd.

Industry observers note that A standout from the Gund trove, a work she described as “my Mona Lisa”, was another modestly-scaled work, by Jim Nutt, founder of Chicago’s Hairy Who group. Titled Loom (1999) and measuring 15in by 14in, the portrait of a woman with a penetrating gaze set a latest auction record for Nutt’s work when it more than doubled its high estimate of $350,000 and ultimately sold for $850,000 ($1m with fees). The ten lots sold to benefit Gund’s charitable foundation tallied $20.3m ($25.7m with fees), squarely within Christie’s pre-sale estimate of $16m to $24m.

Sources indicate that though not part of the Gund Art Foundation offerings, Alice Neel’s double portrait David and Catherine Saalfield (1982) hailed from Agnes Gund’s family and brought $1.6m ($2m with fees). Gund acquired it directly from the artist the year it was painted—not surprising since the sitters were her young adult children.

Alice Neel, David and Catherine Saalfield, 1982 Courtesy Christie's Images Ltd.

The appeal of buying a piece of the legacy of storied dealers and collectors did not fare so well for a work that had belonged to the late gallerist Marian Goodman. Bidding on Gerhard Richter’s Abstraktes Bild (1990) stalled at $5.7m, against an estimate of $6m to $8m, and the lot failed to sell. As Richter’s longtime New York dealer, Goodman acquired the lushly surfaced squeegee painting directly from the artist and exhibited the painting in a gallery group show the year it was made. (A second Richter from another consignor fared better, with that somewhat larger Abstraktes Bild from 1991 bringing $7.6m, or $9.4m with fees.).

Recent findings suggest that among the works from more anonymous consignors, Richard Prince’s famous Nurse series made a splash when Everglade Nurse (2003), executed in acrylic and inkjet print on canvas, sold for $3.1m ($3.9m with fees). It last sold at Sotheby’s Newly introduced York in November 2008 for $3.4m with fees, a rather flat return after 18 years.

Recent findings suggest that large-scale paintings destined to test living room wall space were also in evidence with Christopher Wool’s untitled black-and-white abstraction from 2009, measuring more than 8ft tall. It brought $2m ($2.5m with fees). George Condo’s more than 9ft-wide Double Heads in Blue and Silver (2014), realised $2.4m ($3m with fees). The Condo hailed from the collection of Stefan Edlis and his wife Gael Neeson, who donated 44 works cumulatively valued at more than $400m to the Art Institute of Chicago in 2015.

The top lot of the evening, unsurprisingly, was Jean-Michel Basquiat’s quirky and text-rich composition, Untitled (Leonardo and his Five Grotesque Heads) (1983), which hammered to another anonymous telephone bidder for $28m ($32.8m with fees) against a sky-high estimate of $30m to $50m.

Jean-Michel Basquiat, Untitled (Leonardo and His Five Grotesque Heads), 1983 Courtesy Christie's Images Ltd.

Evidently inspired by Leonardo da Vinci’s 1493 pen-and-ink drawing on paper A man tricked by Gypsies (also known as Five grotesque heads), from the Royal Collection Trust at Windsor Castle in London, Basquiat’s freestyle interpretation in oil stick and acrylic against a burnt orange background crackles with the artist’s terse way with wordplay and actually features eight heads in all.

Measuring 80in by 80in, the painting has no exhibition history, at least none acknowledged in Christie’s online catalogue entry—though it was included in the Whitney Museum of American Art’s 1992 Basquiat retrospective. The catalogue entry for the Whitney exhibition listed the owner as Zurich’s Bruno Bischofberger, an early and key champion of the artist. It last sold at auction at Sotheby’s London in October 2005, when it was the top lot at £736,000 ($1.3m) including fees against an estimate of £400,000 to £600,000. Sotheby’s at the time identified the buyer as “US Trade” and the current consignor acquired it from that entity in 2006.

Julia Jo, One Fell Swoop, 2026 Courtesy Christie's Images Ltd.

The evening ended on a sweet note with Julia Jo’s large-scale abstraction One Fell Swoop (2026), donated to benefit the New York-based Coalition for the Homeless. It set a new auction record for Jo’s work, fetching $320,000 ($409,600 with fees). (The night’s other new auction record was for the work of the Spanish artist Antonio Santín.).

In a recent development, apart from that feeling, the outlook was not as rosy. “It’s been a very long week, with a lot of art, and I think people are tired,” DeLuca said. “The theme of the week is that estimates are a little aspirational.”.

The long week’s last burst of activity will be a pair of day sales at Christie’s on Friday (20 November), after which the US art industry will mark a pause to observe Thanksgiving before migrating south for Art Basel Miami Beach.
