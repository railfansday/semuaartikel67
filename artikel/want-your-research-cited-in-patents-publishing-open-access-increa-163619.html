Want your research cited in patents? Publishing open access increases your chances

It has been revealed that open-access science publishing is having an impact far beyond the academic sphere. Industries that rely heavily on scientific discoveries, such as biotechnology and pharmaceuticals, disproportionately cite open-access work in their patents over paywalled research, a study has found.

An analysis of almost 500,000 patents, and the research papers they cite, has shown that open-access papers were cited 1.38 times more often than would be expected on the basis of their prevalence in scientific papers1.

In a recent development, when the study authors expanded the definition of open access to include papers available on Sci-Hub, a website that hosts pirated copies of scientific articles, this over-representation was even stronger. Papers that were freely available (either through the journal publishers or Sci-Hub) were 3.41 times more prevalent in patent citations than they were in the wider scientific literature.

According to recent reports, the findings, published in Scientometrics, show how heavily inventors lean on open-access research, says study co-author Abdelghani Maddi, a scientometrics researcher at Sorbonne University in Paris. When you include platforms such as Sci-Hub, “the numbers are striking”, he says. “Open science is not only about who can read science, it’s about who can build on it.”.

For their analysis, Maddi and his colleagues leveraged the ‘Reliance on Science’ data set, a publicly available tool that identifies citation links between patents and journal publications2. They found that research-intensive industries, such as biotechnology and pharmaceuticals, cite the scientific literature (both open and pay-walled) in their patents much more heavily than do more engineering-based industries, such as vehicle- and construction-equipment manufacturing.

The researchers also showed that patents from countries with a high proportion of high-tech industries, including South Korea, Singapore and Denmark, cite more scientific publications than do those from countries with more heavy manufacturing industries, such as South Africa, Brazil and Italy.

To track patent citations of open-access papers specifically, the team cross-referenced research publications in the Reliance on Science patent database against those in OpenAlex, an index that catalogues hundreds of millions of scientific documents. They then used a normalization metric to account for differences in open-access publishing practices across research disciplines. Open-access publishing is much more prevalent in the health sciences than it is in computer science, for example, states Maddi.

According to recent reports, seeing how open-access papers were over-represented in patent citations suggests that making research freely accessible does more than just help people to read and share scientific findings, states study co-author Francesco De Pretis, an applied mathematician at the University of Modena and Reggio Emilia in Italy. It also makes that knowledge more likely to be used in developing new technologies and inventions, he adds. “Our findings show that open science is not just a matter for academia, it’s also the business of industry and of national economies.”.

According to recent reports, matt Marx, an innovation and entrepreneurship researcher at Cornell University in Ithaca, New York, whose team developed the Reliance on Science database, states the finding that patents tend to cite open-access papers “is especially intriguing”. But he cautions against assuming that ease of access is the only factor at play. “Perhaps inventors are more likely to cite papers they can read because those are open access, or it could be that prominent or crucial papers are more likely to be open-source,” he says.

According to recent reports, regardless of the exact causes, the access barrier is real, says Marin Dacos, who heads the open-science taskforce in France’s Ministry of Higher Education, Research and Space. Negotiating contracts with thousands of journal publishers worldwide requires specialist library staff — a resource that is generally only available to large companies and universities, he says.
