The same fundamental physics that holds atomic nuclei together may also help explain why the Universe is expanding at an accelerating rate. Researchers at the University of British Columbia (UBC) and the University of Sheffield have developed a theoretical model that connects dark energy to the strong nuclear force, potentially explaining cosmic acceleration without introducing undiscovered particles or fresh fundamental fields.

Dark energy accounts for approximately 70 percent of the Universe’s total energy budget, yet its physical origin has remained unknown since scientists discovered cosmic acceleration nearly three decades ago. Many proposed explanations rely on hypothetical particles or fields that have never been detected. The fresh research, published in The Astrophysical Journal, instead draws on established principles of the Standard Model of particle physics.

The proposed explanation centers on Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD), the theory describing the strong nuclear force that binds quarks inside protons and neutrons. According to QCD, even apparently empty space contains quantum fields with complex properties. The researchers suggest that the vacuum’s underlying structure could produce a small energy shift as the Universe expands, potentially accounting for the acceleration observed across cosmic distances.

Recent findings suggest that A key feature of the model involves the vacuum’s topological properties, which describe aspects of its quantum structure that can influence physics over large distances. The researchers propose that this structure responds to cosmic expansion in a way that generates an effective dark energy contribution through its interaction with gravity.

“Our theoretical framework suggests no upcoming particles may be required at all,” says Professor Ariel Zhitnitsky of UBC’s Department of Physics and Astronomy, whose earlier theoretical work provided the foundation for the study. “The characteristic energy scale predicted from the QCD vacuum and gravity comes out close to the observed dark-energy scale today without having to introduce an ad-hoc new field nor new parameter.”.

Rather than introducing an adjustable parameter to reproduce the observed strength of dark energy, the framework derives its characteristic energy scale from established physical constants.

“The most striking result is that the observed dark-energy scale emerges naturally from known physical constants, without the need for any additional fitting parameters,” states UBC Professor Ludovic Van Waerbeke.

In a recent development, the researchers also investigated whether the framework could reproduce measurements of the Universe’s expansion history. Using astronomical observations, including light from distant exploding stars (supernovae) and radiation left over from the early Universe, they found that the model performed comparably to standard cosmological models in fitting the data.

“The idea that the quantum vacuum as described by QCD could have the right properties to account for the accelerated expansion of the universe is very intriguing,” indicates Dong Ha Lee, a PhD researcher at the University of Sheffield and lead author of the paper.

The analysis also indicates that the framework can reproduce the changing dark energy behavior suggested by current cosmological observations. Unlike a simple cosmological constant, which maintains a constant energy density over time, evolving dark energy models allow that density to change as the Universe expands.

Sources indicate that “It raises the possibility that dark energy could be connected to the established theory of the strong nuclear force, without introducing new fundamental fields. Testing models based on this idea against cosmological data is an essential step. Our analysis shows that such a model can reproduce the evolving dark energy behavior suggested by current observations. This motivates further theoretical work to better understand how the QCD vacuum responds to cosmic expansion and to develop predictions that can be tested with future observations.”.

Sources indicate that although the model agrees with existing astronomical measurements, that agreement does not establish that the QCD vacuum is responsible for dark energy. Further theoretical work is needed to clarify how its quantum structure interacts with cosmic expansion and whether the framework produces predictions that distinguish it from competing explanations.

The team aims to refine its calculations and compare the results with upcoming observations from next-generation space telescopes. More precise measurements of how the Universe has expanded over billions of years could help determine whether dark energy is connected to the same quantum physics that governs matter inside atomic nuclei.

Reference: “Evolving Dark Energy Is Vacuum Energy After All” by Dong Ha Lee, Carsten van de Bruck, Eleonora Di Valentino, Ludovic Van Waerbeke and Ariel Zhitnitsky, 20 November 2026, The Astrophysical Journal. DOI: 10.3847/1538-4357/aeb9cc.
