The App Economy Is Changing: What Happens When Anyone Can Build Their Own Software?

On October 13th 2026, Google announced that Android people can now build their own apps with Google AI Studio and run them privately on their own phones through Google Play, with no public store listing required. The apps are “vibe-coded”: you describe what you want in plain language, and the AI writes it. The first release is limited to the US and to users aged 18 and over, and sharing with a small circle of trusted contacts is planned for the months ahead.

The shift is simple to say. For fifteen years, the question was “Which app should I download?” Now it can be “What do I want my phone to do?” Sam Bright, Google’s VP and GM for Google Play and Developer Ecosystem, put it as a move from “there’s an app for that” to “there’s an app for me”.

According to recent reports, think of a parent who wants one screen showing this week’s school pickup rota, the football schedule and the grocery list. No store has that app, because no store would ever build it. Today, that parent can describe it and have it on their phone by the evening.

I welcome this. It puts real creative power in the hands of people who never had it. But it also invites a question worth answering clearly: is a personal app the same thing as software? In my view, it is not.

A personal app might only need to work for one person. Software used by customers, employees or the public has to work reliably for people who depend on it. Both are software, but the responsibility changes when other people rely on what you build.

Industry observers note that A useful test: ask what happens at 3 a.m. when it fails. For a personal app, you might be able to fix it in the morning. For a service that people rely on, someone may need to respond immediately.

Not every app needs the same level of engineering, and not every task should be entrusted to an untested app. A gym-timer you built for yourself is a personal app. A system that holds your members’ payments, health data and contracts is software, and it carries obligations that a prompt cannot replace.

According to recent reports, personal apps do not appear in Play search or listings and are not shared with anyone except their creator. Sharing with trusted contacts is still to come. A popular app can later be moved to a public listing, but only after trust-and-safety reviews, and apps must still follow Google Play’s policies. Google’s own announcement frames the update around Play.

That is a design for individuals, families and small circles. It is not a design for a company rolling out a system to its staff and customers.

Sources indicate that these are meaningful, useful things, and until now most of them were never built because the cost was too high. That is the real gift here: the cost of building something small and personal has dropped to almost nothing.

Personalised apps will take some ground, and it is worth being clear about where.

It has been revealed that where they compete. Small, single-purpose utilities that people used to download because nothing else fitted: a custom unit converter, a simple expense log, a workout timer, a to-do list shaped exactly your way, a utility tool that you envisioned but didn’t know how to build. . Why pay a subscription for a tool that does 5% of what you need, when you can build one that does 100% of it?

Industry observers note that where they do not. Anything where trust, scale or accountability is the product: banking, payroll, healthcare records, logistics, identity. Nobody wants a one-off, unreviewed app holding their salary or medical history. Those systems need shared data, audit trails, integrations and people accountable when something goes wrong.

So the honest picture is not “apps replace software.” It is that the long tail of tiny needs finally gets served, while serious software is judged on what it was always meant to deliver: reliability, security and support. Incumbents who rely on being merely convenient should pay attention. Those who rely on being dependable have a clearer story than ever.

AI makes writing code fast. It does not make code correct. The 2025 Stack Overflow Developer Survey found that 84% of developers use or plan to use AI tools, yet 46% say they distrust the accuracy of AI output, against 33% who trust it. Even the people using these tools every day check the results.

According to recent reports, for a simple personal app, the consequences may be manageable, although privacy and security still matter. If your habit tracker miscounts a day, you fix it. For software that serves other people, the same slip can mean a leaked record or a wrong payment.

That is why serious teams treat quality as part of how software is made, not a final polish. The NIST Secure Software Development Framework describes this: practices for preparing the organisation, protecting the code, producing well-secured software and responding to vulnerabilities.

Sources indicate that the challenge grows as an app changes. A new feature can affect existing functions, data structures or access permissions. Without testing and version control, a small change can break something that previously worked.

An everyday comparison: a home cook can improvise a dinner for the family. A restaurant needs hygiene checks, supplier records and a process that works the same way every night. Both are cooking, but only one is held to a standard.

When you build something, “ownership” means three different things. It helps to ask all three questions before you commit.

According to recent reports, A personal app that is easy to build but hard to leave can become a quiet trap. Google’s private distribution model limits who can discover these apps, but privacy also depends on how the app handles data and permissions. The wider lesson holds for any AI builder: understand what you can export, what depends on the platform and what happens if you want to leave. Choose tools that let you keep your code, your data and your options.

Recent findings suggest that it is tempting to think that if AI writes the code, engineers become less crucial. The evidence points the other way. Google’s DORA 2025 research describes AI’s primary role as an amplifier: it magnifies an organisation’s existing strengths and weaknesses.

In a recent development, A team with good testing, clear ownership and sound design gets faster and better. A team without them gets faster at making mistakes. The biggest returns come from the surrounding system, not the tool itself.

Industry observers note that picture two teams given the same AI assistant. One reviews every change, tests it and monitors it in production. The other ships whatever the AI produces. Within a few months, the first has a product customers trust, and the second has a pile of problems nobody understands.

So the engineer’s job is moving from typing code to judging it: deciding what to build, checking what the AI produced, and keeping the whole system safe. That is a more valuable skill, not a lesser one.

Google’s announcement is good news. Anyone with an idea can now turn it into a working app on their own phone without any prior experience or knowledge of coding, and only by specifying the intent of what they want to build, without hiring a crew of human specialists. That widens who gets to build, and it will produce many small, useful things that never existed before.

According to recent reports, the lines worth keeping clear are these. A personal app can serve one person’s needs. Software that other people rely on carries greater expectations of reliability, security and support. Both deserve to exist, and each needs a different level of care.
