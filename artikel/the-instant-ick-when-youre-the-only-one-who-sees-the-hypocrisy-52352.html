Can you spot inauthenticity in a flash?

Industry observers note that I don't mean the obvious kind, where someone needs to be better than everyone else. That kind is easy to feel. They talk a little louder, push a little harder, and take up more space than the moment calls for. Like a black hole, they absorb the energy in the room and give nothing back.

Sources indicate that there is a quieter kind of inauthenticity that often goes unnoticed, because it arrives dressed in humility and perfection. You probably know the type: the person who can do no wrong, who seems to have a tender heart for everyone, and who is praised by all for being so good.

Both kinds of inauthenticity are about status. Alfred Adler, one of the earliest psychologists to study power, observed that the striving for superiority can also take the form of a striving for perfection, or "godlikeness." Superiority usually plays out over worldly things, like success or winning an argument. Godlikeness plays out on a level ordinary mortals can never reach.

Those of us who are deeply connected to our own humanness often feel this second kind most keenly. When someone seems a little too pious or a little too perfect, we feel an instant ick. Some part of us knows they are hiding their human nature.

According to recent reports, that reaction is moral disgust. Like physical disgust, it is visceral: a feeling of repulsion designed to protect us from what could harm us. It is why such people can be so hard to be around, and why we often feel an urgent need to tell someone what we saw. Disgust makes us pull away from what feels contaminated and reach for what feels clean and true.

The frustration is that few people believe us. To most, this person is a role model. We all want to overcome our weaknesses, and they seem to have done it.

In a recent development, there is another reason we may not be believed. Someone who has earned a reputation for being good, without paying the price of actually being good, is often on the lookout for signs they are about to be exposed. And moral disgust, like physical disgust, shows on the face. Research suggests it activates the same facial muscle we use when we taste something bitter: the upper lip rises and the nose wrinkles.

In a recent development, so they may work, quietly, to discredit the person who saw through them. A hint dropped here. A flicker of alarm there. All while appearing to be above it all. Nobody questions their intentions, precisely because they seem so good.

Suddenly you are the judgmental one. The difficult one. The one who needs to change.

This is the one-two punch of hypocrisy: First you witness it, then you are made to pay for seeing it. It can shake your sense of self-worth, because we are social animals. As researchers Romin Tafarodi and William Swann Jr. write, "We cannot refrain from peering into the looking glass that others hold up to us, as much as we distrust the images we see there.".

Sources indicate that I have had my share of these experiences, and they have been especially painful because I care deeply about connection. I like being someone others can depend on, not because I need to feel valued, but because connection is part of what makes us human.

It has been revealed that as I've grown older, I have tried to look past the hypocrisy. Behind the piety or perfection, there is often a frightened child who never felt acceptable as she was. The idealised image masks her pain, and she cannot afford to drop it.

It has been revealed that empathy is an important first step. It unhooks us from the reactive stance of disgust, and from its urge to make sure everyone else knows.

Recent findings suggest that but empathy is neither an end in itself nor a one-way street. Used only outward, it can become spiritual bypassing: staying silent in the name of being a good person, even when bringing the hypocrisy to light would be key or healing. When we direct empathy toward ourselves, too, and toward our own hurt, it becomes a means to an end: finding the response that is right for us.

Industry observers note that when we rest in a place where everything we feel is allowed, without labels or judgment, we begin to hear the inner voice that tells us how to respond. It isn't easy, but this is the work of authenticity: expanding our capacity to be with what is, and trusting the guidance that is always in conversation with the present moment.

Every situation is different, and no single answer fits them all. Sometimes hypocrisy needs to be named, especially when it is harming someone. Sometimes it is best left alone, especially when other people's relationships are involved. You might not tell your partner that their mother is a hypocrite, because it could shake their whole sense of self. Unless her hypocrisy is harming you, in which case you may need to raise it with kindness, or set boundaries that protect you.

Industry observers note that no one can tell you what is right for you, because no one else is living your experience. But when you listen inward, you find not only the answer but the courage to act on it. You will need that courage. There are no guarantees. People may criticise you, and things may not go as planned. Yet trusting your inner guidance lets you look yourself in the mirror and say, "I did what felt right at the time.".

Recognising inauthenticity is, in many ways, simply our wiring, designed to keep us safe from other people's hidden motives.

Responding authentically is power.

In a recent development, think of someone who gives you that instant ick. Notice where you feel it in your body, without judging yourself. Then ask: If I weren't triggered at all, what would I see? Perhaps a frightened child behind the mask. Perhaps someone causing real harm. From that place, what does the situation call for: letting it be, speaking up with kindness, or a boundary that protects you?
