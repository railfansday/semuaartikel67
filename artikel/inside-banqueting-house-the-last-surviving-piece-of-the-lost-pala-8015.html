Inside Banqueting House, the Last Surviving Piece of the Lost Palace of Whitehall

In central London, the 400-year-old Banqueting House is keeping a piece of Britain’s history alive. As the last surviving structure of the former Palace of Whitehall, the landmark highlights royal legacy, classical architecture, and art with its crowning feature: a massive ceiling painted by Flemish Baroque artist Peter Paul Rubens almost four centuries ago.

While Banqueting House indicates how proportion, decoration, and fine art can come together in grandeur, it also stands as a reminder of the Stuart era and how quickly power and glory can fade.

According to Historic Royal Palaces, the Palace of Whitehall started out as York Place, which was the London home of the Archbishops of York. When Cardinal Thomas Wolsey lost power, Henry VIII took over the property and turned it into a royal palace, calling it Whitehall. It became a primary location for Tudor and Stuart court life, where royal ceremonies, political drama, and entertainment took place.

Recent findings suggest that the building that stands today was completed in 1622 for James I, who wanted a grand setting for court celebrations. It was the third banqueting house built on the site, replacing an earlier structure whose columns blocked people’s views of the festivities.

A 1698 fire destroyed most of the complex, leaving Banqueting House as its sole remnant.

In a recent development, architect Inigo Jones brought Palladian ideas, which center around classical symmetry and proportion, into English royal architecture. He traveled in France and Italy to study ancient Roman and Renaissance buildings, then used what he learned at Whitehall.

The exterior originally combined honey-hued and pinkish-brown stone with decorative details in white Portland stone. It took two phases of work to give the facade its more current, uniform pale appearance: partial refacing in 1774, followed by a larger restoration starting in 1829, led by architect John Soane.

Industry observers note that inside, the main hall is a so-called “double cube,” meaning it’s twice as long as it is wide and tall. These proportions make the space feel open and balanced, encouraging visitors to look up.

Jones designed the hall for masques—elaborate indicates with music, dance, poetry, and costumes—which honored the monarch and supported Stuart rule. Under the main hall, a vaulted undercroft served as a drinking den for James I and his friends.

The most famous part of Banqueting House is its nine-panel ceiling, painted by Rubens. Conversations about the paintings started under King James I’s rule, but his son Charles I officially ordered them in 1629 to honor his father as well as the Stuart family. Rubens finished the paintings in Antwerp in 1634, and they were installed two years later. The artist made £3,000 and recieved a gold chain for his work.

Together, the works cover more than 2,400 square feet. The three main paintings depict James I joining the English and Scottish crowns, ruling with wisdom, and rising toward the heavens. The other scenes use classical myths to support the idea that the Stuart monarchy was chosen by divine right.

It has been revealed that jones designed the ceiling to accommodate the giant canvases, setting them into a frame of carved and gilded wood. The classical architectural details help balance Rubens’s dramatic figures and movement.

Banqueting House is also linked to the fall of the very monarchy it was meant to celebrate. On January 30, 1649, Charles I walked through the building and out to a scaffold set up outside, where he was executed after the English Civil War. Less than 15 years after Rubens’s paintings were put up, the king passed under the ceiling he had commissioned in honor of his father as he went to his death.

Sources indicate that the building later saw the return of the monarchy and was used for other royal events, including an old healing ritual called the “King’s Evil,” where monarchs touched people thought to have a skin disease, hoping to cure them. The hall was also used to give out Maundy money to the poor before Easter.
