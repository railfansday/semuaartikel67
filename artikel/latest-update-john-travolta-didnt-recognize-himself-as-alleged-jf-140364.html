John Travolta rarely connects with all his characters. Sometimes, he even loves to hate them.

According to recent reports, “They say you have to find something you like,” the actor explains. “But I’ve never felt that. I think you have to like the result, pulling it off. That’s where the joy comes from. To become these people, you don’t have to identify with them or enjoy being them. Even so, Travolta faced one of the biggest challenges of his career playing a rather literal executioner in Roland Joffé’s “November 1963,” which opened the Torino Film Festival with its international premiere.

On paper, Johnny Roselli seems right in Travolta’s wheelhouse: a real-life wiseguy turned showbiz fixer, an Al Capone protégé whose career took him from Hollywood to Las Vegas to the Bay of Pigs, leaving a trail of bodies along the way.

“He was an odd mix,” says Travolta. “He was the guy who asked Sinatra to connect Kennedy with the mob. He hooked Kennedy up with Marilyn Monroe. He was a Hollywood icon of sorts, and had this other, darker side as a hitman. That’s complex. I’ve done dark subject matter before, but this was real. It actually happened, and that’s the difference.”.

Roselli was purportedly a CIA go-between and sharpshooter. “November 1963” takes that history a step further, imagining him as a key triggerman on the Grassy Knoll, directly responsible for the killing that shook a nine-year-old boy in Englewood, Newly introduced Jersey, and still haunts the actor to this day.

In a recent development, “It was the biggest crime of the century,” notes Travolta, instantly transported back to second grade. “The announcement came at 11 a.m. Eastern Standard Time that the President had been shot, and then three hours later, at 2 p.m., they confirmed that he had passed. We all went to our homes, and all I can remember is being home with my father, glued to the television, sitting on his lap and trying to make sense of it all.”.

Travolta carried that weight onto the set, where, he says, he was asked “to play a very evil man with moments of humanity.”.

“The actors were all suffering because everybody who was hired was a very decent person, and we all had to play, to some degree, people who were not,” he continues. “So it was harder to go home every night. It took a glass of wine and a nice dinner to snap out of the zone.”.

That feeling returned in full force when Travolta finally watched his finished performance at Torino’s opening night, prompting a kind of out-of-body experience.

“I thought, ‘Wow, I can’t believe that’s me,’” he says. “I’ve never seen myself lose my soul in a film, and [here] I was soulless. I was very proud that I could portray that, because it’s against my very essence. When I looked at it on screen, I went, ‘Oh my god, he’s the devil.’”.

Written and produced by family members of Windy City crime boss Sam Giancana, “November 1963” foregrounds the Chicago Outfit’s role in the assassination plot, leaving CIA and government involvement for a possible follow-up.

“This is not history; it’s about human beings,” Travolta says. “There will be lots more to tell, but you can’t do it all at the same time.”.

That deliberately incomplete picture appealed to Joffé, who translates the murky history into a mise-en-scène of skewed angles and funhouse reflections.

In a recent development, “That’s how history comes to us,” he notes. “I’d say this story is probably 90% true, insofar as no one will ever know what really happened. What’s most important to me is whether it’s credible. And has that mindset changed? The transactional mindset, the mafia mindset, the idea that power is all that counts and loyalty has to be bought at the point of a gun. My feeling is that it doesn’t disappear. It transmutes. It may become a political party. It may become the way business acts.”.

In one telling revision, the film casts slain Dallas policeman J.D. Tippit, whose murder was attributed to Lee Harvey Oswald, as a key conspirator. Joffé uses the twist to further muddy the waters while dismissing the notion of a neatly orchestrated plot.

It has been revealed that “I reject the conspiracy theory,” he notes. “If it were a conspiracy, we would have known by now, and we absolutely do not. What it was is collusion, and collusion is very different. Collusion is subtle. It happens in the shadows. It’s a nod, a wink, a knowing glance. It’s putting a gun in the right place.”.

Industry observers note that the distinction, he argues, is that no grand design is required. The CIA and organized crime had already worked together on plots to kill Fidel Castro, but that didn’t mean anyone had to convene a committee to strategy Kennedy’s assassination.

It has been revealed that “It’s not about control,” Joffé says. “It’s about the fact that life is chaotic, and the killing of the President comes out of that. We made a film about chaos.”.
