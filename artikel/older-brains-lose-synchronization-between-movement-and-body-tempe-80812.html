Older Brains Lose Synchronization Between Movement and Body Temperature

A chronobiology study in mice reveals that while the brain’s 24-hour circadian rhythms persist into old age, shorter multi-hour “ultradian” rhythms in physical activity and core body temperature significantly weaken. Simultaneously, aging decouples movement from internal temperature regulation and triggers neuroinflammatory gene shifts, glial remodeling, and neuronal loss inside the brain’s master circadian clock, the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN).

Sources indicate that throughout every 24-hour day, mammalian physiology follows an intricate temporal choreography. Governed by the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), a tiny cluster of neurons in the hypothalamus that acts as the brain’s master biological clock, core body temperature, hormone release, and physical activity rise and fall in predictable ~24-hour circadian rhythms.

Layered atop this overarching 24-hour cycle are faster, multi-hour biological oscillations known as ultradian rhythms, which pulse every few hours to coordinate bursts of activity, metabolic demand, and thermoregulation.

While age-related sleep fragmentation and impaired temperature regulation are well-documented clinical hallmarks of aging, precisely how ultradian rhythms evolve across the lifespan, and how they relate to cellular changes inside the SCN, has remained poorly understood.

Now, a comprehensive chronobiology investigation examining mice across the adult lifespan (from 3 to 21 months of age) reveals that while the 24-hour circadian framework remains detectable in old age, the faster 2- to 6-hour ultradian rhythms deteriorate, accompanied by a breakdown in coordination between physical activity and internal body temperature.

To separate externally driven daily routines from internally generated biological timing, the researchers continuously recorded locomotor activity and core body temperature in mice across multiple age brackets under both standard 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycles and constant darkness, an experimental condition that removes environmental light cues to expose the brain’s intrinsic pacemaker.

By demonstrating that shorter ultradian rhythms and temperature-activity coupling falter even when overall 24-hour circadian rhythmicity persists, the findings offer a fresh lens through which to view age-related sleep disturbances and vulnerability to heat or cold stress.

Further experimental work will determine whether targeting neuroinflammation or supporting glial-neuronal communication inside the SCN can restore ultradian stability, potentially paving the way for interventions that alleviate sleep fragmentation and thermoregulatory decline in older adults.

What is the difference between circadian rhythms and ultradian rhythms?

According to recent reports, circadian rhythms are biological cycles that repeat roughly every 24 hours, such as the daily sleep-wake cycle. Ultradian rhythms are shorter biological cycles that repeat multiple times within a single day, typically every 2 to 6 hours, regulating smaller bursts of physical activity, alertness, and core body temperature fluctuations.

How does aging affect these biological rhythms?

It has been revealed that researchers found that while older mice kept their basic 24-hour rhythms and core clock gene cycles, their shorter 2- to 6-hour ultradian rhythms weakened significantly. Furthermore, physical movement and internal body temperature became disconnected, no longer rising and falling together as closely as in younger animals.

What changes happen inside the brain’s master clock during aging?

Inside the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), the brain’s master clock, aging was linked to increased activity of immune-related genes, changes in supportive glial cells, and a reduction in neuron density, which may impair how the brain coordinates fine-tuned daily bodily rhythms.

Aging remodels the suprachiasmatic nucleus and weakens ultradian rhythms and activity–temperature coupling.

Aging alters daily physiology, but how these changes relate to remodeling of the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) remains incompletely understood.

Here, we examined time-resolved SCN transcriptomic profiles alongside longitudinal recordings of core body temperature and locomotor activity, using separate cohorts for the molecular and physiological analyses.

Aging was associated with extensive SCN transcriptomic remodeling, including enrichment of innate immune, interferon-related, complement-associated, and glial programs, while selected canonical clock genes retained detectable rhythmic expression.

Industry observers note that the composition and temporal profiles of broader rhythmic transcriptional programs also differed between young and aged SCN samples. Aged mice retained detectable circadian rhythms but revealed reduced 2–6 h ultradian wavelet power in locomotor activity and body temperature, lower activity–temperature correlations, and altered optimal integration windows and time-of-day-dependent relationships. Histological analyses revealed glial remodeling and reduced neuronal marker-positive cell density in the aged SCN.

These findings identify parallel age-associated changes in SCN molecular and cellular organization and in physiological rhythmic power and activity–temperature associations.
