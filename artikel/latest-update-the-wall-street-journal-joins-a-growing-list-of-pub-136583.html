Many publishers are responding to a fresh reality on the open web: Bot traffic is rivaling traffic from human readers.

One of the world’s largest business publishers is shutting the door on unwelcome bots. In May, Dow Jones overhauled its bot strategy and now disallows all bots by default, the tech giant told Nieman Lab. The latest bot permissions apply to all of its free and paid consumer news sites, including The Wall Street Journal, Barron’s, and MarketWatch.

As part of the shift, the organization revised each site’s robots.txt file — a webpage that tells bots which parts of a site they can and can’t access. Rather than reactively blacklisting bots through robots.txt, Dow Jones now operates with a whitelist for select crawlers and scrapers.

“As bot traffic has shifted from discovery-oriented use cases toward systems that can ingest, summarize, or otherwise benefit from our content without a relationship in place, we have moved to a more deliberate signal,” said Nolly Evans, chief development officer at Dow Jones. “[This] signals to third parties that they must negotiate and partner directly with us for content access.”.

The Wall Street Journal’s whitelist contains more than 60 bots, including search engine crawlers and three scrapers operated by OpenAI, which has a $250 million-plus licensing deal with Dow Jones’ parent manufacturer, News Corp.

“We’re seeing astronomical growth in bot traffic while human referrals and traffic to news sites are being curtailed,” noted Evans. “That’s a clear signal to publishers that the old norms are no longer working, and that something needs to change.”.

Robots.txt files are voluntary guidance for all bots, including those deployed by AI companies. They’re not hard technical blocks or legally binding contracts. Some AI companies, like Perplexity, have used “stealth crawlers” to bypass robots.txt files while evading detection. Still, The Journal isn’t alone in using robots.txt to disallow bots by default, whether to create friction for scrapers, as leverage to negotiate for content licensing deals, or to minimize the burden on their servers.

An analysis of the robots.txt files of 1,000 leading English-language publishers websites by the bot analytics tech giant Known Agents shows that about 2% of those sites now disallow all bots by default, including Reuters, Bloomberg Law, Publishers Weekly, and the British technology news site The Register.

Many of these publications are owned by News Corp, including several of the company’s Australian newspapers (The Australian, The Daily Telegraph in Sydney, the Herald Sun in Melbourne) and its marquee U.K. papers (The Times, The Sun, The Scottish Sun).

“One of the most important issues for us is making sure that the IP journalists create is respected by the AI companies,” News Corp CEO Robert Thomson noted in an interview with CNBC last week. “The principled companies want to have an agreement, and the unprincipled companies don’t. We’ll try to convince you to be transactional and pay us for our IP and pay us for our journalism, or we’ll sue you.”.

Sources indicate that more and more publishers are opting to restrict bots using whitelists. Late last year, The Atlantic told Digiday that it only allows bots associated with its licensing deals or those that drive significant traffic to the website. In June, Digiday first reported that Reuters and Time had started issuing whitelists of their own.

People Inc. has been vocal about its decision to go beyond robots.txt files and hard-block bots by default. When the company made the switch at the beginning of this year, it went from blocking 2,000 unique bots to more than 30,000.

Many of these publishers are responding to a upcoming reality on the open web: Bot traffic is rivaling traffic from human readers. Over the past 90 days, around 34% of pageviews across more than 5,000 randomly selected websites came from bots, according to an index maintained by Known Agents. About 29% of those bots were “AI-related” (e.g. AI agents, AI chatbots, and AI training scrapers), as opposed to more traditional bots like search engine crawlers and archivers operated by the Wayback Machine.

Despite some publishers wanting to restrict AI-related bot traffic, many aren’t making full use of their robots.txt files to do so, according to Gavin King, the CEO of Known Agents. The organization started as a free crowdsourced database to help identify bots, but now offers publishers analytic tools and other products to monitor and control bot traffic.

Known Agents right now tracks more than 2,000 AI-related bots, but the majority of English-language news publishers only restrict a fraction of those on their robots.txt files.

In a recent development, “Robots.txt is the easiest first line of defense,” stated King. “The fact that people are reaching out to [internet infrastructure companies to sign] six-figure year contracts instead of updating their robots.txt for free is such a miss.”.

King acknowledged that robots.txt alone can’t stop content from being scraped without permission or compensation. But he also pointed to clients who have reduced traffic from known AI-related bots by more than 90% by updating their robots.txt file with a more comprehensive list.

According to recent reports, “It’s been such a manual process,” noted King. “Everyone is doing it individually, in-house instead of automating the process.”.

Slate is one of the publications that has leaned on Known Agents’ “Automated Robots.txt” to try to stem the tide of AI bots to its site. Slate’s small product team uses the free version of the tool to access Known Agents’ API weekly and review any newly discovered bots or renamed agents that have been added to the company’s database. The tool automatically revises its robots.txt file based on that review.

“We have almost 30 years of content, which is a long history of being online,” Greg Lavallee, Slate’s VP of technology, said. “We’re seeing no value from bots and it costs us bandwidth fees, so we decided to tell them not to bother.”.

It has been revealed that lavallee noted that since September, preliminary data demonstrates that several bots have ignored the publication’s robots.txt and continue to attempt to crawl its website, though he declined to name the exact bots. Slate is exploring “server-side paywall strategies that will make it more difficult for scrapers to access full content,” he noted.

Slate presently has no licensing deals with major AI companies, despite active outreach to buyers in the past.

It has been revealed that “It’s very easy for us to take that robots.txt list and go look at the [server] logs and say, ‘We told you not to access and then you did,” noted Lavallee. “I feel like you have to [update your Robots.txt file], but it’s definitely not enough. Something more needs to happen than asking nicely.”.
