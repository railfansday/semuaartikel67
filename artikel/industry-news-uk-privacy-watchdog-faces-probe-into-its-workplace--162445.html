Independent review will examine leadership, accountability, and how staff concerns were handled before former commissioner John Edwards resigned.

Britain's privacy watchdog is getting a taste of its own medicine, with an independent review set to examine the culture, governance, and accountability surrounding the events that led to former Information Commissioner John Edwards' resignation.

Karon Monaghan KC will examine whether the regulator's culture and governance allowed problems to develop or made concerns harder to raise.

Edwards quit in June after an independent workplace investigation found there was a "case to answer" over his behavior and that his conduct fell short of what was expected of a public official. He disagreed with some of its findings, but ultimately conceded that he had shown "poor judgment.".

It has been revealed that that investigation, however, focused on Edwards alone. According to the terms of reference published on Thursday, it "did not consider the wider conditions in which that conduct occurred, and it was not designed to.".

Recent findings suggest that the upcoming review will cast a wider net. Monaghan wants to know whether staff could call out bad behavior further up the food chain, and what awaited those who tried.

It will also poke around the regulator's leadership and governance arrangements and its relationship with its sponsoring government department, including whether there were adequate ways for concerns to be escalated and those at the top to be challenged.

Current and former staff can give evidence confidentially and voluntarily, while Monaghan will be given access to relevant documents and existing material, including staff surveys and data on bullying, harassment and discrimination.

The ICO has confirmed it won't use confidentiality clauses in settlement agreements to prevent people from contributing to the review.

Monaghan decides which witnesses and documents are relevant, rather than the ICO or DCMS. Access remains subject to legal restrictions, and she must be told when material is withheld and why. She can speak to whoever she thinks is relevant and ask for the complaints, correspondence, and other paperwork she needs.

It has been revealed that and if that leads her somewhere unexpected, she can ask to widen the investigation. DCMS can refuse, but it won't be able to quietly brush the request aside, and any decision not to expand the scope has to make it into the final report.

The review won't reopen the investigation into Edwards, make findings about the conduct of named individuals, or delve into the merits of the regulator's enforcement decisions.

Its arrival comes amid a broader shake-up at Britain's data watchdog. The old Information Commissioner's Office was formally replaced by the Information Commission on September 30, bringing with it a latest board-led governance model.

The government first promised the wider review in July, shortly after Edwards' departure, but it didn't appoint its reviewer or nail down the terms until late November.

"We need to understand what is working, where things are falling short and, crucially, what needs to change," remarked DCMS Secretary Lisa Nandy.

It has been revealed that the watchdog won't have to wait long for its report card. Monaghan is expected to deliver her findings in the first quarter of 2027, after which they will be published and handed to Parliament. ®.
