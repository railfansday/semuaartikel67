A brief summit between the leaders of China, the United States, and Russia was held in Shenzhen, China, on Nov. 19. Critics saw little substantive achievement, arguing that the three had merely taken advantage of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation forum to meet for a photo opportunity. Some American observers faulted U.S. President Donald Trump in particular for yielding to China and Russia rather than showing leadership.

In my view, the summit, short as it was, did matter. In great-power politics, the format can sometimes matter more than the concrete results. The leaders of the three countries had never sat down together in this format; that they managed to do so says something about the kind of era that we are entering.

In a recent development, in an era of great-power politics, one of the keys to avoiding military conflict is for the powers to hear one another out. They did so in Shenzhen. They also exceeded expectations by issuing a joint peace declaration with four points of agreement. And for Chinese President Xi Jinping in particular, the summit offered a first chance to try out an approach to great-power relations that he had originally proposed to Washington, this time with both the United States and Russia at the table.

Yet while Xi hosted, the biggest winner was clearly Russian President Vladimir Putin. The war in Ukraine has left Russia diplomatically isolated from much of the West. Shenzhen could not end that isolation, but it revealed that Russia could not simply be shut out of great-power diplomacy. Russia is a major military power and rich in natural resources, but its overall strength still falls well short of that of the United States or China. By sitting down with Putin as an equal, the Chinese and American leaders acknowledged that Russia remains a power that they cannot bypass on questions of global strategic security.

Industry observers note that xi was a winner, too. Beijing revealed that it could bring two adversaries, the United States and Russia, together on Chinese soil and secure a joint declaration. The format itself put China back at the center of the international stage. A decade ago, it would have been hard to imagine China convening such a meeting. That also helped Xi’s standing at home.

Trump had just suffered a crushing defeat in the midterm elections. Yet both China and Russia accorded him the respect due an American president with whom they could still discuss strategic matters. China, in particular, gave him a lavish welcome rather than treating him as a lame duck.

Recent findings suggest that but the three leaders did not sit around a table simply for the sake of appearances, and Xi in particular made some meaningful gains here. The Chinese president raised the question of how the three powers should deal with one another from here on. His answer: with mutual respect, peaceful coexistence, mutually beneficial cooperation, and dialogue and consultation.

Sources indicate that these are principles that Beijing has long pressed in its dealings with Washington, rooted in Xi’s proposal for a “newly introduced type of great-power relations” between China and the United States. As host, Xi set the tone by extending that framework to all three countries. The summit was a chance for Beijing to put it into practice. In effect, Xi brought Putin into the room to press Trump on an approach that he had long advocated for U.S.-China relations.

Sources indicate that that, from Beijing’s perspective, was the summit’s most key achievement. Rivalry between Washington and Beijing, and outright hostility between Washington and Moscow, did not prevent the three countries from coordinating on strategic stability.

Sources indicate that this takes a minimum of trust. None of the three countries has to believe that its rivals will give up competing. Each has to believe that the others also want to prevent a miscalculation from triggering a conflict, that they are willing to explain their intentions, and that they will listen when another country spells out its red lines. Sitting down together, rather than meeting only in pairs, means letting down their guard enough to acknowledge that some risks involve all three and cannot necessarily be managed through bilateral talks alone.

If regular coordination takes hold, then Shenzhen will matter even more to Beijing. After the summit, Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi said that China would stay in touch with Russia and the United States at the working level. That is a start. Whether the leaders’ dialogue lasts will depend on whether they meet a second or third time, and whether communication holds up when relations come under strain.

In a recent development, those gains stem largely from the format. The declaration itself offers further clues to what Beijing wanted to accomplish. The first, third, and fourth points are particularly revealing.

Industry observers note that the first concerns U.N. reform. The three countries agreed to work toward reforming the United Nations so it could respond more effectively to global challenges and better fulfill its basic mission of maintaining international peace and security.

In a recent development, my reading is that Beijing pushed for this language to reassure the global south. Beijing knows that some countries in the global south are uneasy about China sitting down with the United States and Russia at a summit of its own making. Putting the U.N. reform first sent a related message: The three powers had met to help the United Nations fulfill its peace and security mandate and had no intention of making newly introduced rules over the heads of the global south. Wang’s insistence after the summit that countries large and small are equal was meant to convey the same point.

Sources indicate that the third point in the declaration names the International Criminal Court. The leaders emphasized the importance of state consent and respect for the immunity of state officials, and they mentioned the court and other similar institutions must not abuse their jurisdiction. Moscow likely pushed for this provision in response to the court’s arrest warrant for Putin, backed by Trump due to the U.S. far right’s hostility toward the court. But the words “and other similar bodies” were most likely added at Beijing’s insistence. They give China a joint statement that it can cite in future disputes over jurisdiction, such as the South China Sea arbitration.

Recent findings suggest that those words also have implications for Taiwan. If mainland China were to attack Taiwan and Washington were to push for Chinese leaders to be held criminally accountable for alleged war crimes, Beijing could point to a declaration the United States helped problem and ask: What did you say in Shenzhen about state consent and the immunity of officials?

In a recent development, the fourth point can be read as a gain for Beijing on nuclear challenges. The three countries reaffirmed that a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought—language that originated with U.S. President Ronald Reagan and Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev, but that has been taken up by China. They also agreed to hold talks on reducing nuclear risks and preventing proliferation.

Industry observers note that trump wanted trilateral nuclear disarmament talks. What made it into the declaration was language on risk reduction and nonproliferation. Beijing succeeded in redirecting U.S. pressure to join disarmament talks toward issues that all three countries might be willing to keep discussing.

China also gained a basis for putting diplomatic pressure on Japan, where changes to—or even the abandonment of—the three non-nuclear principles are under consideration. If Tokyo goes down that path, particularly if the debate turns to Japan acquiring nuclear weapons of its own, Beijing can cite the trilateral agreement and press Washington to say where it stands on those risks.

Xi showed at Shenzhen that China could convene two adversaries, set a framework for discussing strategic stability, and get them to agree on some common positions. As much criticism as the meeting drew, it also raised hopes that trilateral talks might become a regular fixture. Xi’s remarks suggest that he would like to see that happen. The real test will come when the three countries face a crisis and their interests collide. Will they still be willing to talk the way that they did in Shenzhen?

Deng Yuwen is a Chinese writer and scholar.
