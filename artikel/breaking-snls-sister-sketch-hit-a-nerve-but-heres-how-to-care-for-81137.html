Breaking: SNL’s ‘Sister’ sketch hit a nerve, but here’s how to care for elderly parents without upending your life

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In its 50 years on the air, Saturday Night Live has tackled more than its fair share of social issues. But rarely has an SNL sketch resonated as deeply—and prompted as many national conversations—as the Sister commercial parody that aired on November 14, 2026. This sketch, starring host-for-the-night John Mulaney, promotes Sister™ as the solution to caring for aging parents. The parody claims that simply texting the words “Dad fell” to your sister will prompt her to quit her job, break her lease, and completely upend her life to come take care of him—for free. Clearly, the all-male writing team of Streeter Seidell (who dedicated the sketch to his sister, Heidi), Mikey Day, Simon Rich, and Chase Rosenberg are onto something. AARP found that as of 2025, 59 million Americans were providing care for an adult loved one, and women made up 61% of those caregivers. And it’s taking a toll. AARP reports that caregivers of adults over 50 have been caregiving for 5 years and spend 26 hours per week providing care—up from an average of 3.9 years and 22 hours per week as of 2020. About 20% of those caregivers rate themselves as having fair or poor health, and they report an average of 7 poor mental health days per month. These unpaid caregivers spend an average of $11,508 per year on caretaking, which is equal to 31% of the median household income. Women across the country may have laughed through their rage at this painfully accurate comedy—but they deserve more than the mere satisfaction of simply feeling seen by an incisive satire. Cameron Huddleston, author of Mom and Dad, We Need to Talk, and Dr. Darla Bishop, financial wellness expert, public health researcher, and author of How to Afford Everything, offer the following practical suggestions for how America’s siblings can take care of aging parents without upending their lives, bankrupting themselves, or emptying their retirement accounts. Don’t minimize the costs of caregiving The humor of the SNL sketch relies on the family’s ignoring and downplaying the very real cost the sister pays when she becomes the caregiver. As Dr. Bishop puts it, “If you’re cutting back your hours, turning down promotions, or dipping into your retirement savings, caregiving has a price tag, even when no money changes hands.” But the sister faces more than just financial costs. We see her clash with her father and brother and argue with her husband. “In my work, I teach people to think about their three currencies: time, energy, and money,” Dr. Bishop notes. “Caregiving requires all three.” So rather than the sister being a free resource, as Mulaney claims in the ad, she’s using up all of her currencies while her brother pays nothing. Recognizing the heavy costs of caregiving is the first step to breaking the dynamic illustrated by SNL. “I want families to understand that just because you can afford to help your parents today doesn’t mean you can afford the long-term consequences of doing it alone,” Dr. Bishop says. Don’t ignore available resources “When a parent needs care, families assume they have two choices: do it themselves or pay someone else,” Dr. Bishop says. “But there are resources in between, and finding them could save your family thousands of dollars and countless hours.” Specifically, Dr. Bishop recommends starting with your local Area Agency on Aging, which can help you find services like transportation, meal delivery, respite care, and caregiver support. She also suggests exploring the Eldercare Locator at eldercare.acl.gov to help find affordable caregiving near you. You also don’t have to figure out these services on your own. According to Huddleston, an aging life care professional can help. (These people were previously known as geriatric care managers.) “These professionals will be familiar with resources in your area—from adult day care centers to government programs and community assistance,” Huddleston says. To find an aging life care professional, check out the Aging Life Care Association in your area. And don’t forget other, potentially overlooked resources available to you. Your parents may be eligible for Medicaid, depending on their income, health, and location. The VA’s Aid and Attendance program provides benefits for veterans and their spouses that help cover the cost of assistance with activities of daily living. Even your own employer benefits may help. “Some workplaces offer caregiver support, flexible scheduling, or access to care coordinators,” Dr. Bishop says. Don’t go it alone Huddleston was a caregiver for her mom, who had Alzheimer’s disease. One of her regrets about that time was not asking for more help from her family. “If I had asked, I would’ve gotten more help,” she says, “but I didn’t want to be a burden on others.” This gets to a common mindset the SNL skit illustrates: A lot of women are afraid to ask for help. “We assume it’s our responsibility, and we don’t want to appear like we can’t manage that responsibility,” Huddleston says. While it’s true that sometimes women ask for help and don’t receive it, Huddleston believes that many families assume the caregiver has it covered. “My husband was great about helping with my mom, doing so much to support me and her,” she says. “I’d love to see more men respond that way. But if they’re not seeing the toll it takes on the caregiver, as my husband saw daily with me, they probably aren’t even aware of how much of a sacrifice it is to care for a parent.” When asking for help, Dr. Bishop’s three currencies framework can help you figure out who can do what. You can divide responsibilities based on the time, energy, and money you each bring to the table. “Maybe one sibling lives nearby and can take Mom to appointments while another has more financial flexibility and can contribute toward paid care,” Dr. Bishop suggests. “A third might be great at managing paperwork, coordinating benefits, or researching resources.” Huddleston recommends keeping a running list of the types of support you need so that you’re prepared to make specific requests. “You might ask your brother to find an aging life care professional and cover the cost, to line up a housekeeper or lawn maintenance for your parents’ house, to pay for grocery delivery, to come stay with your parent in the evenings or weekends to give you a break,” she says. She also wants caregivers to let go of any guilt they have about asking for specific help. “It would be easier if men stepped up without being asked to help,” she says. “But if you’re not getting the help you need, don’t feel bad about asking for it.” Don’t wait to make decisions It’s easy to put off thinking about Mom and Dad’s health. None of us like to consider our parents’ mortality, and the idea of losing independence can be terrifying to an aging parent. But it’s easier to make good decisions before Dad falls, when no one is panicked or stressed. That’s why both Huddleston and Dr. Bishop both recommend having a family meeting well before there’s a crisis. “Put everything on the table,” Dr. Bishop says. “Ask yourselves, ‘What does our parent need? What resources do they already have? What can each of us realistically contribute without jeopardizing our own financial security?’” If your family tends to avoid such conversations, Huddleston suggests using the SNL skit as a jumping-off point to start the conversation. Don’t neglect your own long-term care planning Another way to break the cycle of relying on women’s unpaid caregiving is by planning for our own future care needs—and much earlier than you might think. “By the time you’re 50 years old, you should be thinking about long-term care and how you could pay for professional care so that you don’t have to rely entirely on a family member for support,” Huddleston says. “At that age (and into your early 60s if you’re healthy), you can still qualify for long-term care insurance or a hybrid life insurance policy with long-term care benefits.” Dr. Bishop also recommends getting your affairs in order—and that doesn’t just mean having a will in place. “One of the greatest gifts you can give your adult children is not making them play detective during a family crisis,” she says. Specifically, your kids should know where to find your crucial documents, who manages your money, what insurance you have, and your wishes in the event that you can’t make medical or financial decisions for yourself. So funny it hurts It’s unlikely that Saturday Night Live expected its three-minute parody to trigger a national reckoning about women’s disproportionate caregiving burden. But it struck a nerve for a very good reason. As Dr. Bishop puts it, “women aren’t the backup proposal. We are THE PLAN.” But that doesn’t mean we’re stuck with this dynamic forever. We can end our reliance on women’s unpaid labor by recognizing the heavy time, energy, and money cost of caregiving; exploring all available resources; refusing to let one person handle all caregiving; making proactive decisions; and planning ahead for our own long-term care needs. “The answer isn’t for women to stop caring about their families,” Dr. Bishop says. “It’s for families to stop assuming that caring automatically equals sacrificing your career, your finances, or your own well-being.”.
