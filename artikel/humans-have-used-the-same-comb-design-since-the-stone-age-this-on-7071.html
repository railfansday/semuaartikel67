Humans Have Used the Same Comb Design Since the Stone Age. This One Might Finally Beat Baldness.

The oldest-known comb, fashioned from animal bone, dates back nearly 8,000 years to mesolithic Syria. But in those intervening 10,000 years, the comb hasn’t seen much innovation. Sure, plastics have long since replaced bone as the material of choice for this particular piece of grooming gear (though animal bone combs are still a thing). But the comb’s overall shape and function—individual tines and all—has remained remarkably unchanged.

In a recent development, “The basic design evolved from the fingers of the human hand,” the Pitt Rivers Museum at the University of Oxford writes on its website. “From Bronze-Age man to Cleopatra, to Napoleon's wife, Josephine, all would recognize these items as such.”.

But just because something’s remained largely unchanged for millennia doesn’t mean there isn’t room for innovation. Take, for instance, the “Detangle,” an experimental comb developed by Beihang University in China as a tool for tackling androgenetic alopecia (AGA), or just simply baldness. At first glance, the comb looks much like any other hand-inspired comb—there’s a long handle and the recognizable tines. However, that’s where the similarities end, because this comb has teeth packed with embedded electrodes as well as small compartments for delivering molecules designed to stimulate hair growth. Researchers published a study detailing the efficacy of this experimental comb in the journal Nature Communications.

“Androgenetic alopecia affects over 1.5 billion individuals, causing progressive hair loss with psychological and quality-of-life burdens,” the authors write. “This system features electronic teeth embedded with height-differentiated electrodes and integrated drug-loading modules, which generate an enhanced electric field penetrating up to ~9 times deeper into hair follicle regions than conventional external application.”.

This “Detangle” device, whose name the authors admit was inspired by the Disney animated film Tangled, isn’t a newly introduced hair-loss treatment so much as a revolutionary way to deliver one. One of its most high-tech features is a control circuit that creates pulsed electric fields across two types of electronic teeth. These teeth have different heights that generate a stronger electric field than if the teeth were aligned. That generates a positive environment for the transmembrane delivery of a therapeutic molecule into the impacted follicular cells, increasing uptake by more than three times, the authors report.

According to recent reports, the authors tested the comb on mouse models and found hair growth recovered near 100 percent within a month, a steep increase from the approximate 70 percent recovery rate of typical minoxidil treatments—one of the go-to FDA-approved treatments for hair loss. To achieve this impressive recovery rate, the authors relied on the SRD5A2 siRNA molecule and a VEGFA plasmid. The first molecule inhibits dihydrotestosterone (DHT), a hormone that causes hair follicles to shrink, while the latter boosts blood vessel growth around the impacted follicles.

“By co‑delivering VEGFA plasmid and SRD5A2 siRNA, the Detangle system promotes hair follicle regeneration via a dual mechanism that synergizes electrostimulation with molecular regulation,” the authors write. “Given the multifaceted signaling abnormalities in AGA, the Detangle system presents a versatile platform for co‑delivering therapeutic agents beyond metabolic modulation.”.

After 10,000 years, the comb finally gets a serious glow up.
