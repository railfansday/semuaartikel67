Scientists discover a surprising twist in how the body burns calories

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, unlike ordinary body fat, brown fat burns calories to generate heat, making it an attractive target for future obesity treatments. Researchers at the University of Basel and University Hospital Basel have now investigated whether medication can switch on this energy-burning tissue in humans. Their surprising findings suggest that harnessing brown fat with drugs may be more challenging than anticipated.

While white fat primarily serves as an energy reserve, brown adipose tissue uses energy to keep the body warm. People with more active brown fat also tend to be leaner and have better metabolic health.

In a recent development, exposure to cold naturally activates brown fat, prompting scientists to search for medications that could mimic this response. A research team led by Professor Matthias Betz at the University of Basel and University Hospital Basel examined whether activating a specific receptor, known as the beta2 adrenergic receptor, could stimulate brown fat in humans. Their findings were published in the journal Cell Metabolism.

Drug Boosts Calorie Burning Without Clear Brown Fat Activation.

In a recent development, to investigate, the researchers conducted two experiments involving 11 healthy volunteers. They compared the effects of mild cold exposure with those of fenoterol, a medication that stimulates beta2 adrenergic receptors. The scientists measured how much energy the participants expended and used PET/CT scans to monitor brown fat activity.

Recent findings suggest that both interventions caused the body to burn more energy. However, the scans revealed an key difference. Only cold exposure produced clear evidence of brown fat activation, with the tissue absorbing considerably more glucose than it did following fenoterol administration.

Recent findings suggest that "We were surprised that energy expenditure increased strongly with fenoterol even though we could not detect a comparable activation of brown fat," states Betz. "Our study demonstrates that an increase in energy expenditure does not automatically mean that brown fat has been activated.".

It has been revealed that exactly where the additional energy is being consumed remains a mystery. The researchers suspect that fenoterol may increase energy consumption in other tissues, potentially including skeletal muscle or white fat.

Implications for Future Weight Loss Drugs.

According to recent reports, the findings also suggest that the biological mechanisms controlling brown fat in humans are more complex than previously thought. Analysis of human tissue indicates that fully activating brown fat may require the combined action of multiple receptors or biological signals rather than stimulating a single receptor.

Understanding these mechanisms could open upcoming possibilities for treating obesity. Current weight loss medications work by reducing appetite. However, as people shed pounds, their bodies often compensate by burning fewer calories.

A treatment that simultaneously reduces appetite and increases calorie burning could help counteract this metabolic adaptation.

According to recent reports, "Ideally, we could combine a reduction in appetite with an increase in energy expenditure," notes Betz. "But first we need a better understanding of how brown adipose tissue is regulated.".
