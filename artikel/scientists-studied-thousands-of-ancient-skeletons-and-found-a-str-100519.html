Scientists Studied Thousands of Ancient Skeletons and Found a Strange Truth About Human Progress

According to recent reports, the dairy industry’s “Milk. It does a body good” slogan has some historical backing, at least in Europe. That’s one of the more surprising takeaways from a large study that used ancient skeletons to track how well people lived across Europe and Asia over the past 7,000 years. The researchers based their analysis on one telling measurement: human height.

In a recent development, “When children and adolescents have access to sufficient, high-quality food, they grow taller, within the constraints of genetic factors,” Manon Stephan, an economic historian at Germany’s University of Tübingen, said in a statement. “Therefore, height enables us to draw conclusions about living standards.”.

To test that idea, Stephan and colleagues at Tübingen and the Max Planck Institute of Geoanthropology examined 18,457 bones from 783 sites in 45 countries. In the study, published in Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, the team estimated people’s heights from their long bones. They then tracked how average height changed in each region and compared those changes with shifts in population density and technology.

In China, men were taller 7,000 years ago, averaging 5 feet 10 inches. Over time, however, their height steadily declined, reaching a low of just 5 feet 3 inches on average in the 19th century. Chinese women’s height also declined, though less drastically, falling from an average of 5 feet 2 inches 7,000 years ago to 4 feet 10 inches in more recent times.

Trends like these would be nearly impossible to trace through written records alone. “In many parts of the world, any written sources that would allow us to draw conclusions about economic development appear quite late on our timeline and are patchy in their distribution,” Stephan said. “That’s why we rely on bones.” The bones in question came from 22 Asian and 23 European countries and were taken from the graves of commoners in both densely populated and rural regions.

In Europe, by contrast, the trends went in a different direction. Around 5,000 years ago, men in northern Europe averaged just 5 feet 4 inches. However, the study authors wrote, as pastoralist peoples migrated from the steppes of Eastern Europe and western Siberia, they brought with them more advanced dairy farming and the genetic trait of lactose tolerance. This led to greater consumption of dairy products, which the study identifies as a leading factor in increased height. Average heights in northern Europe continued to climb into the early Middle Ages, but sometime between 500 and 1000 C.E., they began to decline, a trend that lasted until the end of the 19th century.

Sources indicate that women’s heights followed a similar pattern. China and Japan experienced the steepest declines, while Europe saw a rise, though a less steep one than among men.

The authors wrote that “human stature is a valuable indicator of nutritional status and health.” Reconstructing this indicator offers insight into which societies provided better health and nutrition to their people. Surprisingly, technological progress did not prove to be a major factor in improving either.

“Our study also confirmed that technological progress does not automatically lead to greater height,” mentioned Jörg Baten, a professor of economic history at the University of Tübingen. “In China, for example, newly introduced, higher-yielding rice varieties and other innovations were introduced in the 11th century [C.E.]. While the higher yields enabled the empire to remain the dominant power, they did not subsequently lead to a better standard of living for the general population. Instead, population density increased: People had more children because this was seen as a form of security in old age. This meant, however, that each individual had not more to eat, but less. And importantly the quality declined, for example, in terms of high-quality protein.”.

Europe’s findings on technology mirrored Asia’s. Improved technology helped support denser populations, but it didn’t increase average height or necessarily improve the nutritional value of food.

Going forward, the authors hope to link more of these changes in height to shifts in social conditions or developments in agriculture. “This requires further research,” mentioned Mark Hudson, an archaeologist at the Max Planck Institute of Geoanthropology. “However, the statistical data reveal trends and provide clues as to when these changes occurred.”.
