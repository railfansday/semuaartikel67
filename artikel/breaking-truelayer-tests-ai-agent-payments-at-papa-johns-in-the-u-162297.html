Industry observers note that trueLayer announced on November 27, 2026, that a Papa John’s AI agent had completed what it described as the first agentic Pay by Bank payment to a named UK retailer. The pilot involved UK retail banks including Lloyds and NatWest and ran through the Financial Conduct Authority’s Supercharged Sandbox.

According to recent reports, the agent helped with the customer’s order and then paid directly from the bank account linked to the customer’s Papa John’s account. Pay by Bank uses open-banking technology to link a bank account rather than a card, bringing the payment step into the AI-assisted ordering process. At a showcase on November 26, 2026, TrueLayer presented its findings to the FCA and industry participants.

It has been revealed that A customer creates one mandate with the bank, setting limits for a particular merchant or over a chosen period that the agent cannot exceed. Each attempted payment is checked against those limits, so an instruction above them is declined by the bank rather than relying on the agent to police itself. Payment approval remains inside the bank app, keeping banking credentials and passwords away from the agent. Customers can revoke the arrangement through either the merchant or their banking app at any time.

Recent findings suggest that this separates the agent’s ability to initiate a purchase from the bank’s enforcement of the customer’s payment authority. TrueLayer also described the pilot as the first agentic use of the variable recurring payment APIs at Lloyds and NatWest, bank interfaces for payments authorized under an ongoing customer mandate.

Recent findings suggest that trueLayer argues that bank mandates let consumers delegate purchases without sharing card details, that non-card payments can preserve competition in payment acceptance as agents handle more shopping, and that the showcase can inform the conditions and standards needed for safe adoption at scale. The work remains a sandbox pilot rather than a general consumer launch.

In a recent development, the retailer pilot followed TrueLayer’s July 22, 2026 announcement of its selection for the sandbox. The FCA received 199 applications and selected 21 companies for a cohort running from July 13, 2026, through December 31, 2026, with a particular focus on agentic payments. The sandbox, part of the FCA’s AI Lab, provides a controlled setting and regulatory support for moving advanced AI use cases toward commercial deployment.

According to recent reports, an earlier prototype kept a person in the loop while completing a transaction inside an AI shopping agent. It supported a one-off immediate payment and Bank on File, which establishes a recurring mandate for future purchases. The Papa John’s work extends those mechanics into a named retailer’s ordering flow with participating UK banks.

The FCA’s expectations for agentic AI in financial services, published on November 24, 2026, add regulatory context to the demonstration. The regulator said firms must identify which agent activities require authorization and which rules apply, particularly when software can initiate transactions, move money, arrange borrowing or make investment decisions.

In a recent development, using an external AI provider does not transfer compliance responsibility away from a regulated firm or its senior managers. They are expected to understand how autonomously their systems act, the risks created and the controls needed in real time and under stress. When customers’ agents approach a firm, it should have processes to recognize the agent, verify consent and permitted actions, and provide help if something goes wrong.

According to recent reports, payments are a specific case. Before accepting an agent’s instructions, firms should assess their systems, controls and potential liabilities, including whether to accept a payment initiated by an AI agent or from a wallet under its control. The pilot’s bank-held mandate and in-app approval address the spending-control portion of that assessment; they do not by themselves settle broader questions of regulatory authorization, liability and senior-management oversight.

According to recent reports, the pilot establishes a concrete division of authority between agent and bank. Wider adoption depends on pairing that payment mechanism with the FCA’s broader expectations for accountability, consent, liability and customer support, which extend beyond whether an individual transaction stays within its limit.
