Breaking: How to Improve Brain Health with 5 Daily Exercises

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Brain health isn’t just an concern for the elderly. Maybe you've experienced the first subtle signs of decline. Sometimes it’s a name that’s on the tip of your tongue. Or perhaps you've read a news story in the morning that you can't remember by the evening. It could be that you're feeling increasingly mentally fatigued after a typical day spent dealing with emails, text messages, phone calls, and constant interruptions.

The good news is that you can take steps to keep your mind sharp. “Our brain needs to be stimulated, at any age," says Giuseppe Alfredo Iannoccari, a neuropsychologist, professor at the University of Milan, and president of Assomensana, an association founded in Monza, Italy in 2004 to promote cognitive health. Routines help conserve mental energy, while fresh experiences force it to work,” says Giuseppe Alfredo Iannoccari, a neuropsychologist, professor at the University of Milan. While working with people suffering from neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer’s, he came up with the idea to create a gym for the brain. “I asked myself what could be done earlier, when people are still healthy,” he said. His studies led him to develop a protocol that anyone can integrate into their daily routine.

The method is based on the principle of neuroplasticity—that is, the brain’s ability to modify its connections in response to learning and experience. This ability persists throughout one’s entire life, although it changes with age. Today, Iannocari's courses consist of weekly 90-minute sessions in groups of up to fifteen participants. Each session features about ten exercises focused on memory, attention, language, reasoning, creativity, and other cognitive skills. At the beginning and end of the program, twelve mental functions are assessed to measure their progress. “In our tests, we observe an average improvement in the overall cognitive efficiency index of 25–30%," states Iannoccari. "Some functions, such as attention and language, respond particularly well. Others, such as logical reasoning and prose memory, require more intensive work.” These results are based on the association’s experience and should not be interpreted as a percentage reduction in the risk of dementia. The World Health Organization’s guidelines, updated in 2026, acknowledge the potential benefits of cognitive training, while emphasizing that evidence regarding the long-term prevention of cognitive decline remains limited.

Industry observers note that when it comes to our ability to concentrate, Iannoccari points to one culprit at the center of our modern lives. “Sometimes we feel tired not because we’ve done too many things, but because we’ve switched what we were doing too many times," he says. “Constantly switching from one task to another is more tiring than focusing on the same activity for an extended period.” A stream of notifications, information to process, and the need to respond promptly to various requests put our attention to the test above all else. “It’s the foundation of all cognitive processes. When it’s fragmented, even the quality of the information we’re able to acquire deteriorates.”.

And then there’s AI, which promises to free us from an ever-increasing number of tasks. What are the consequences for our brains? “If we ask artificial intelligence to compare two tables, perform an analysis, and write a report, we get the work done in a few minutes that would previously have taken an entire day," notes Iannoccari. “Saving work also means reducing some mental activity. But it depends on how we use these tools.”.

For Iannoccari, the key is to preserve two abilities: critical thinking and analysis. “We must know how to formulate questions, and this requires familiarity with the subject,” he states. “Then we must evaluate the answers, recognize any inconsistencies, and verify the information. Only a well-trained mind can spot errors.” This glitch also applies to seemingly beneficial habits, such as reading books or solving crossword puzzles.“ Reading is invaluable, especially for the emotions it evokes and the worlds it allows us to discover," he says. “But reading doesn’t automatically mean memorizing. If we want to remember what we’ve read, we must do so with the intention of being able to recount it to someone else.” As for puzzle-solving pastimes, “Sudoku trains certain concentration and planning skills, while crossword puzzles stimulate the retrieval of information from memory,” he says. “These are useful activities, but a structured program allows you to work on different skills and gradually increase the difficulty of the exercises.”.

Recent findings suggest that in addition to guided sessions, Iannoccari suggests short daily sessions he calls “Brain Breaks”— five-minute breaks for small exercises that counteract brain aging. “If we wait to find five free minutes, we’ll probably never find them.” “We need to make it a habit, just like having a cup of coffee.” Here are five exercises you can try:.

Choose a topic—preferably a positive one—read it carefully, and try to recount it to three different people without constantly referring back to the text. “The first time we repeat a piece of information, we’re often uncertain,” indicates Iannoccari. “The second time, we become clearer and more fluent. The third time, we have another chance to reinforce it in our memory.” It’s an exercise that stimulates what’s known as “prose memory”—the ability to recall and process information we’ve read or heard. “Let’s imagine learning one piece of news a day for 365 days: how much knowledge will we have accumulated?”.

Sources indicate that for five minutes, write down as many words as possible that start with the letter A, trying to be quick. Repeat the exercise the next day, avoiding words you’ve already used. On the third day, move on to B, and so on. “It’s a way to train verbal fluency and lexical retrieval, particularly useful when we start to experience that ‘word on the tip of my tongue’ phenomenon,” indicates Iannoccari. The goal is to gradually make the search more challenging by striving to find words that aren’t immediately obvious.

It has been revealed that right-handed people can try performing some simple tasks with their left hand, and vice versa. Open a drawer, move small objects, draw, and gradually try writing. “This new challenge forces the brain to break out of its automatic routines and engage resources we normally use less," he says. “Surprising results can be achieved: people who initially can’t even draw a line gradually learn to write with their other hand.” Of course, it’s best to choose safe activities and proceed without rushing.

How many phone numbers do we still remember? And how often do we rely on the GPS even to get to relatively familiar places? “Before calling someone, I try to remember their number," notes Iannoccari. “Before using a calculator, I do the math in my head. Before turning on the GPS, I try to figure out the route.” The same goes for the grocery list. “I write it down so I can be sure I won’t forget anything, but at the supermarket I first try to recall the items. Only then do I check the list.” These are small opportunities to keep practicing skills that we increasingly tend to delegate to digital tools.

Once in bed, instead of focusing on the next day’s commitments, try to mentally retrace the day that just passed. What time did you wake up? What did you eat? Who did you meet? What did you talk about? What details stood out to us? “You have to try to be very detailed,” notes Iannoccari. “Often, people who have trouble falling asleep find that this exercise also helps them drift off.”.

But there’s another aspect that’s particularly close to his heart. “I find there’s something romantic about it. We live one day after another and often retain almost nothing of them. Reflecting on it means recognizing its value. Every day leaves something behind, like a geological layer deposited on top of the one before it.” This little ritual offers an opportunity to recall the experiences we wish to preserve.

When is the right time to start? “As children,” says Iannoccari. “We encourage them to play sports, but we rarely think about the importance of training mental abilities just as intentionally.” In adult life, of course, exercise, sleep, nutrition, stress management, and social relationships also matter. “But if I had to name three drivers of the mind, I’d choose curiosity, creativity, and emotions."he says. "Curiosity opens us up to fresh knowledge, creativity allows us to connect that knowledge in original ways, and emotions make experiences more memorable.”.

Industry observers note that he also notes that, though older age is associated with worse brain function, it's also how we collect the wisdom of lived experiences. “We think an efficient brain is the one we had at twenty, and we lose sight of what we’ve gained by the time we’re sixty or seventy," he states. "When we were young, we may have been faster, but over time we’ve accumulated skills, knowledge, and experience. We possess a much richer worldview.”.

The real challenge is to remain present to ourselves and others, valuing who we’ve become.” And perhaps, as the years go by, one of the most important skills is precisely learning to choose where to invest our mental energy. “We meet people who, now that they’re retired, are busier than when they were working. We’ve come to associate efficiency with the number of things we can do, but in doing so, we lose the opportunity to delve deeper, reflect, and let experiences settle. I always say that I don’t pursue nice ideas—I pursue great ideas. Because there are so many nice ideas, and they risk scattering our energy in a thousand different directions.” “With age,” concludes Iannoccari, “we have a great privilege: the ability to choose.”.
