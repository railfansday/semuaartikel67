If you want a recession-resistant career, look for work people need in any economy, like health care, utilities, public safety, and teaching. Those fields grew or held steady in the 2007-2009 recession while the economy shed more than 7.5 million jobs. None is layoff-proof, so keep an emergency fund whatever you choose.

Recent findings suggest that picture a nurse who kept full hours through 2009 while her spouse’s construction work dried up. The same split demonstrated up across the economy.

In the 18-month recession that ended in June 2009, construction lost nearly a fifth of its jobs, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS). Health care added 428,000 jobs over the same months, even as the country shed more than 7.5 million overall, a BLS study of health care employment found.

The jobs that hold up share two traits. People need the work whatever the economy does, and insurers, taxpayers, or utility customers pay for it rather than shoppers who can put a purchase off. A reputation for steadiness isn’t proof, though. Accounting and bookkeeping firms cut 6% of their jobs in the recession, a BLS review of professional services found, while total employment fell 5.4%.

Pay is each job’s BLS median for May 2025, so half its workers earn more and half earn less. Training is what each job typically takes to start. If you’re choosing a field for the next decade rather than the next downturn, weigh these against the career fields expected to grow fastest too.

Industry observers note that health care kept adding jobs throughout the recession, and the BLS study found the biggest gains in outpatient care, led by home health services. People still get sick and grow older in a downturn.

Utility employment rose 0.6% during the recession while total employment fell 5.4%. Homes and businesses use power and water in any economy, and someone has to keep both running.

In a recent development, government employment grew 0.8% during the recession, and most public safety workers are on government payrolls. Local governments did start trimming staff after September 2008 to cover budget shortfalls, so a long downturn can slow hiring.

Schools and colleges added nearly 180,000 jobs from December 2007 to December 2009, a BLS analysis found. The gain came mainly in state and private education, where colleges and universities employ most workers. Public schools depend on state and local budgets, though, and deficits there can bring teacher layoffs.

A steady field lowers your risk without removing it. Public safety and teaching jobs depend on budgets that shrink in a long downturn, and a household’s other paycheck can stop, as it did for the nurse’s spouse.
