Can Updating Drug Memories Help Treat Cocaine Addiction?

Posted November 25, 2026 | Reviewed by Ekua Hagan.

Many people recover from addiction. One obstacle along the way is exposure to drug cues—reminders of past use such as paraphernalia, places, or people. These cues can evoke vivid memories of the drug experience, triggering craving and relapse even after long periods of abstinence. Weakening such learned responses is therefore a recovery target, especially in cocaine addiction, for which no medication has been approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration.

Sources indicate that cue-exposure therapies pursue this goal through extinction, or presenting a drug cue again and again without the drug to form a new, competing memory. Extinction does not overwrite the original drug memory, so the learned responses tend to return over time or when the drug is encountered again. Accordingly, extinction-based therapies have had limited success in preventing relapse.

Sources indicate that extinction learning is guided by the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC), a frontal region that tracks value, and by the amygdala, a region central to emotional learning. In cocaine addiction, we previously reported lower vmPFC activity than in healthy individuals, both during drug-cue extinction and when this learning was tested the next day. This impairment may limit therapies that rely on the region.

In our 2025 PNAS study, led by Ahmet Ceceli, we merged our expertise in addiction neuroscience with our co-author Daniela Schiller’s expertise in learning and memory. We combined two windows of opportunity, one behavioral and one pharmacological, timed to coincide.

The behavioral window is memory reconsolidation. When a stored memory is recalled, it becomes unstable for a short time and can be weakened or strengthened before it is stored again. Retrieval-extinction uses this window, presenting a brief reminder of a drug cue 10 minutes before extinction to update the original memory: The cue no longer predicts the drug.

Recent findings suggest that in healthy volunteers, Schiller and colleagues indicated that retrieval-extinction kept learned fear from returning, with less prefrontal involvement, as if the updated memory no longer required prefrontal control. In people with heroin or nicotine addiction, retrieval-extinction has also been associated with reduced cue-induced craving.

The pharmacological window is provided by methylphenidate, best known as Ritalin and commonly prescribed for attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder. Like cocaine, it increases the brain messengers dopamine and norepinephrine. Taken by mouth, however, it acts more slowly, consistent with a lower potential for abuse than cocaine. It nonetheless remains a controlled stimulant with risks of misuse and addiction. In rodents, it facilitates learning-related brain plasticity and extinction.

In a recent development, in people with cocaine use disorder, a single low dose taken by mouth normalized prefrontal function and reduced impulsivity during a task involving drug-related words. We therefore proposed using methylphenidate not as a stand-alone treatment, but as a cognitive enhancer alongside behavioral therapies that strengthen self-control and weaken drug-related associations. Here, it was timed to peak during the reminder.

Sources indicate that our small proof-of-concept clinical trial included 18 adults with cocaine use disorder, 12 men and 6 women. Each took part in two identical sessions about a week apart, once after taking 20 mg of methylphenidate and once after an identical-looking placebo pill. The order was random, and neither participants nor researchers knew which pill was given. Most participants were also receiving methadone or buprenorphine for opioid use disorder.

In a recent development, participants first learned to link neutral cartoon images with a brief video of someone smoking a crack pipe. A few hours later, they took the pill. One hour later, at the medication’s expected peak and inside an MRI scanner, they briefly saw some of the drug-linked images again as reminders. Ten minutes later, all images were shown repeatedly without the videos. Reminded images thus underwent retrieval-extinction, and the other drug-linked images underwent standard extinction.

It has been revealed that the next day, participants completed a validated picture-choice task. How often they chose to view drug over pleasant pictures served as a laboratory measure of drug seeking. In our earlier work, this choice tracked current and future cocaine use.

Under placebo, vmPFC activity decreased over the course of standard extinction, replicating the impairment we previously reported in cocaine addiction. By contrast, in healthy people the vmPFC becomes increasingly engaged as standard extinction progresses, which is thought to reflect updating of the cue’s value.

On their own, the medication and the reminder each engaged the vmPFC, with the reminder only as a trend. We interpret these modest effects as a compensatory overreliance on an already compromised region.

Industry observers note that crucially, combining the two yielded a different pattern. When the reminder was given under methylphenidate, vmPFC activity decreased over the course of extinction, as did its communication with the amygdala. This disengagement resembles the effects of retrieval-extinction on fear memories in people without addiction, where prefrontal control may no longer be needed. It does not, however, show that the drug memory was weakened or erased.

Sources indicate that under placebo only, the more the vmPFC was engaged during retrieval-extinction, the more often participants chose to view drug over pleasant pictures the next day. This pattern suggests that the learned drug response persisted; it was absent under methylphenidate.

Sources indicate that next-day drug-picture choice did not differ significantly between methylphenidate and placebo, nor did craving or cue ratings. The findings therefore concern brain function and its relationship with behavior; they do not yet show reduced drug seeking or a treatment benefit.

Industry observers note that to our knowledge, these results are the first to describe the brain activity underlying a combined medication-behavioral approach to drug-cue memories in human addiction. They extend memory-updating research from fear to drug memories, from animals to humans, and from behavior to the brain, while repurposing a medication already in clinical use as a cognitive enhancer.

In our impaired Response Inhibition and Salience Attribution (iRISA) model of addiction, drug cues capture excessive attention and value at the expense of other rewards, while self-control is compromised. Promoting the “forgetting” of disruptive memories may reduce drug seeking by both lowering the pull of drug cues and enhancing self-control. Similar principles may apply to other conditions marked by harmful responses to powerful memories, such as posttraumatic stress disorder.

As a proof of concept, this trial was small, tested a single dose, included no healthy comparison group, and measured behavior only on the following day. Its results await replication in larger samples that allow comparisons by sex, drug use history, and substance. Longer-term studies should also test whether repeated sessions lead to lasting normalization of prefrontal function and, ultimately, to less craving, drug seeking, and relapse.

By precisely targeting compromised prefrontal circuits, medication-enhanced cognitive training may ultimately support recovery from addiction.
