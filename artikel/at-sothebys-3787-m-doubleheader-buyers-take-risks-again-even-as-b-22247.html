At Sotheby’s $378.7 M. Doubleheader, Buyers Take Risks Again, Even as Bidding Drags

🌐 Source: Artnews • 📅 17 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 9 min read (1845 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sotheby’s kicked off the November marquee sales with a doubleheader on Monday evening that generated $378.7 million across 74 lots. The top-line results look solid, but a sluggish second half seemed to further illustrate the choosy mood among collectors right now.

The nearly three-hour marathon started strong with the dedicated sale from the collection of cable television magnate Ted Baum and interior designer Ruth Baum. It was a white-glove result, with all 24 works sold for a hammer total of $90.9 million. With fees, the sale landed at $110 million, just a shade above the presale high estimate of $109 million. Recent findings suggest that the Baum sale was a lively affair from the start. After the first lot—Roy Lichtenstein’s 1983 cut-paper work Two Paintings: Sleeping Muse (Study)—sparked a four-minute contest among three specialists and a bidder in the room, CEO Charles Stewart strode over to Gareth Jones, the house’s chief marketing officer, and remarked of the $3.8 million total, “Well, that’s a good start.” Three lots later, a Lucio Fontana copper canvas with a glorious cut down the middle ignited another bidding war, the bids ricocheting among three bidders in the room. It hammered at $3.5 million, or $4.4 million with fees, well above the $3 million high estimate.

Oliver Barker, chairman of Sotheby’s Europe, handled the proceedings with panache, making even a fairly ordinary stretch of bidding feel brisk. Amid the bidding for Lot 7, Andy Warhol’s 1984 painting The Scream (After Munch), he teased Jacqueline Wachter, chairwoman of private sales, for taking a bit too long with her phone bidder, asking whether she was “doing her shopping or doing her conversions.” Then he quipped to the audience, “Amazon is always open. As is Sotheby’s Dot Com.” It was a little sharp, perhaps, but it got a laugh and helped lighten the mood as the sale began to drag.

The crowded salesroom was a who’s who of the art trade: Dominique Lévy, Brett Gorvy, Amalia Dayan, David Schrader, Emmanuel Di Donna, Christophe Van de Weghe, and Gagosian Art Advisory’s Bernard Lagrange were just a few of the heavies in attendance.

In a recent development, in the end, six works hammered above their estimates and another 10 landed in range, with spirited bidding throughout, both in the room and on the phone. Joan Mitchell’s Green Book of Barbara Guest led the sale at $19.3 million, followed by Mark Rothko’s Untitled at $17.7 million and Ed Ruscha’s Dimple at $11.6 million. The Baums had bought the Mitchell privately in 1986 for just $65,000, making its sale price nearly 300 times what they paid. The Rothko, which they acquired for $115,000 in 1983, brought more than 150 times its purchase price. Not bad for a few decades of collecting. The final lot in the sale, Mario Merz’s neon sculpture Iguana, sold well below its estimate, but that did little to dampen the enthusiasm, as the room erupted in applause.

The Now and Contemporary sale that followed was considerably less celebratory. Of the 53 lots originally offered, three were withdrawn before the sale, and another five went unsold: works by Kerry James Marshall, Ellsworth Kelly, Roy Lichtenstein, and Keith Haring, and, curiously, a Formula One race car. Tom Eddison, head of contemporary art at Sotheby’s London, who took over auctioneering duties for the sale, noted the car was a first for the auction house, and it required a lengthy preamble explaining that the sale was subject to the terms of service of RM Sotheby’s, the house’s classic-car affiliate. The car garnered a handful of bids but failed to reach its low estimate before Eddison declared it unsold. It may be some time before Sotheby’s tries that experiment again.

That’s not to say the results for the contemporary sale were lackluster. When all was said and done, the sale garnered a hammer total of $224.8 million. With fees, the total climbed to $268.8 million, within the presale aggregate estimate of $226.2 million to $323 million, and well above the equivalent sale in November 2025, which generated $178.5 million. Of course, we should note that last year’s sale followed the trove of works from the late cosmetics heir Leonard Lauder, which generated an eye-watering $527.5 million. Alas, not every season sees three Klimts hit the block.

Of the 45 lots that sold, a full 14 hammered above their presale estimates, and another 24 landed in range. Still, while there always seemed to be a handful of bidders for each sold lot, with Eddison darting his hands left, right, and center, most works hammered after only a minute or two.

It has been revealed that the most protracted bidding wars came somewhat early. A six-minute contest among four specialists for Jeff Koons’s 1991 sculpture Bob-Tail ended with contemporary art specialist Bame Fierro March winning the lot for a hammer price of $9 million. At $10.7 million with fees, that’s good for just over three times the high estimate. (Is Koons’s market trending back up, or do collectors just really like an adorable English sheepdog?) Seven lots later, Agnes Martin’s cream canvas Desert, from the collection of Lenore and Bernard Greenberg, sold after a six-minute duel between contemporary art specialist Alya Sama and Lisa Dennison, chairman of the Americas. Dennison won on a $9.2 million hammer bid, or $11.3 million with fees, comfortably above the $8 million high estimate. And Andy Warhol’s 1964–65 painting Flowers fetched $18.2 million with fees against a $10 million to $15 million estimate.

Mark the Warhol and Koons results as green shoots for a market that’s spent much of the past two years playing it safe. Neither artist has had the easiest time in the secondary market lately, which makes those results particularly interesting. Something to watch going forward, especially for Koons.

After the sale, Dennison pointed to the continued strength of early Martin paintings, which have been outperforming expectations for several seasons.

Recent findings suggest that art adviser Ed Dolman similarly found the night’s results encouraging. In a text message toward the end of the sale, he said, “The best works always do fine, [but] buyers were taking some risks again.”.

Dolman also pointed to the depth of bidding. While 29 of the 45 sold lots in the contemporary sale had guarantees—as did every work in the Baum sale—those guarantors were often outbid on Monday night. Buyers, it seemed, were willing to go a little further.

In a recent development, not that everything was suddenly flying off the walls. Lichtenstein’s Reflections on ‘Painter and Model,’ the top lot of the contemporary sale at $24.7 million with fees, came in well short of the high end of its $20 million to $30 million estimate. Willem de Kooning’s Water…Soft Banks of a Window (Untitled VIII) brought $20.3 million, just above its $20 million low estimate, while Sam Francis’s Towards Disappearance I made $17.1 million against a $15 million to $20 million estimate.

The buzziest result of the night was no doubt for Yayoi Kusama’s 1960 painting No. 3P.B., which hammered for $12.5 million after 10 bids spread across four minutes. With fees, the result came to $15.1 million, just above the $15 million high estimate, and good for a new record for the artist, who died in August. About 10 lots later, another of her “Infinity Nets” paintings, Infinity Nets (OZEH), sold for a hammer price of $2.5 million, or $3.1 million with fees, just a shade above the $3 million high estimate. That work, a white canvas measuring roughly 6½ by 8½ feet, is far more recent, dating to 2011. And if you missed out on those two Kusamas, have no fear: Another 10 works of hers will hit the block over the rest of marquee week. Five are from her long-running “Infinity Nets” series.

Yet, for all the strong prices, the second half of the contemporary sale was a slog. With Eddison at the rostrum, the pace slowed considerably. Bids were drawn out, pauses stretched on, and even works that eventually fetched impressive prices seemed to take an age to get there. A few market watchers wondered whether such a sluggish tempo might discourage bidders, particularly on works that didn’t have much competition to begin with. It was an odd contrast: a sale that sometimes felt as though it was struggling to find its rhythm, even as buyers were spending millions more than expected.

According to recent reports, to Nina Del Rio, an art adviser and auction house veteran, the results looked remarkably strong, particularly given the subdued energy in the second half of the sale. Still, she mentioned, there hasn’t been much evidence of a shift in the market. At least not a fundamental one. Buyers appear to be behaving much as they did in May, when the best works attracted serious money but enthusiasm for younger artists remained muted. A few years ago, the Now sale was where collectors went to get their hands on artists whose waiting lists seemed impossible to crack. That sense of urgency has largely disappeared.

What collectors do seem willing to pay for is scarcity. Del Rio pointed to the modern masterpieces coming up later in the week, the sort of works that rarely appear at auction and may not come around again for decades. Among them is Wednesday’s sale of the Blaquier Collection, a group of Impressionist and Post-Impressionist masterpieces remarked to be worth around $450 million, and a $180 million Van Gogh suddenly subject to a last-minute restitution claim.

Recent findings suggest that dennison warned against reading too much into a single evening. Given the current mood, starting the week with contemporary art rather than a mix of modern and contemporary masterpieces makes it harder to judge the market as a whole. And while works in the $10 million to $20 million range performed well, the sale lacked the $30 million, $40 million, and $50 million trophies that drive enthusiasm across the whole week.

It has been revealed that as for that Formula One car, Dennison said the result left her scratching her head. The last bid offered, $3.9 million, was just under the $4 million low estimate. Did the interested buyer really think the $100,000 gap was too wide to bridge?

Industry observers note that there is a risk to putting something unexpected into a contemporary art sale. But, as Dennison pointed out, sometimes it works, as when Christie’s sold the Tyrannosaurus rex skeleton “Stan” during its 20th Century Evening Sale in 2020 for $31.8 million, nearly quadruple the high estimate.

According to recent reports, sotheby’s shouldn’t give up on the idea. There’s something appealing about an evening sale that doesn’t draw a firm boundary around paintings and sculptures. Not every experiment works the first time, or even the second. And if the object is truly one-of-a-kind, buyers are ready to shell out the cash.
