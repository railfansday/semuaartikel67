In the weeks since that video was streamed on Facebook, Chris and Geraldine Taylor, who run animal rescue NGO Luna and Furry Siblings (LAFS), say they have uncovered 15 dumping sites in the suburbs of Bulacan, near the Philippines' capital of Manila.

Sources indicate that in total they've unearthed the remains of some 170 dead pets. Belgian Malinois, golden retrievers, pugs, puppies and cats have been found dumped off service roads, sealed in septic tanks, floating in rivers and buried in vacant lots.

Many of these animals are thought to have been sent to cremation businesses in the expectation they would be turned to ash.

Now, many grieving owners are having to confront a distressing reality: their beloved pets may not have received the send-off they paid for. The urn in their home may not contain their pet at all. And they may in fact see their so-called fur child again, jettisoned like common waste on the side of a highway.

One saw the livestream and wrote on Facebook: "I think one of those pets is mine." Below, they attached a photo of a beaming Alsatian.

A string of grisly discoveries in the suburbs near Manila has put thousands of grieving pet owners on edge.

When Chris arrived on that back road three weeks ago, he couldn't have known he was uncorking a scandal that would see at least one business reportedly shut, four people placed under investigation, and a personal invite to sit down with a senator to discuss animal-cruelty laws.

Chris, a former police officer, and his wife are retired, but only formally. For the past three years, they've been running an animal rescue shelter out of their home in San Jose del Monte, 30km (18.6 miles) north-east of central Manila.

On 30 October, he'd driven into town to report a case of animal neglect at the local district office. When he arrived, he was told the official in charge had just left to respond to another complaint: a report of dumped animals on a nearby roadside.

Chris had seen this once before: 14 dogs that were found and soon reburied. This time, he followed the official to the scene and started documenting â taking photos, scanning the bodies for microchips and uploading footage to his Facebook page.

It has been revealed that "I figured there's got to be more to this," he explains. "So we put out word: if anybody knows anything, any other dump locations... just report it. Let us know.".

By crowdsourcing intel in this way, and collaborating with authorities, LAFS has so far uncovered a total of 15 dumpsites, containing an estimated 170 animals. "Some of them were just bags of bones," Chris says. "Others were liquefied.".

It has been revealed that LAFS has meticulously documented the crime scenes. Often wearing little more for protection than an N95 mask and rubber gloves, Chris crouches in the underbrush and slices open discarded plastic bags, picking through the contents while Geraldine holds the camera.

According to recent reports, their Facebook videos have racked up more than 30 million views. Commenters ask Chris if he has seen their pet among the bodies, or if he can turn a carcass over so they can check for identifying marks.

Some are successful. On that first night in October, all 25 bodies were returned to their owners.

There's a question which hangs uncomfortably over every dumpsite: who would do something like this?

As more owners came forward, a common denominator emerged. Many stated they'd taken the animals to be incinerated at the same place: a crematorium called Ahjay Paws in the city of Bacoor, not far from Manila.

It has been revealed that "So I posted: anybody that's had Ahjay's services... these might be your animals," notes Chris. "And man, it just flooded in.".

Ahjay Paws, the firm that was supposed to cremate Olaf, is under investigation.

The Philippines has long been a dog-loving nation, but since the Covid-19 pandemic the "fur parenting" community has exploded.

Industry observers note that A 2021 study found the country had the highest rate of dog ownership in the Asia-Pacific. Since then, the pet dog population has increased 4.7% year on year, according to another study, exceeding 21 million in 2025.

As the numbers soar, businesses have stepped up to address an inevitable implication: more deaths.

"Cremation here in the Philippines has become quite a major thing with the boom of the pet industry," says Anna Cabrera, director at the Philippine Animal Welfare Society (Paws). "There's even a ceremony where they hold wakes for the pets.".

These events offer grieving owners a last chance to see their beloved pets before they're taken to a crematorium and transmuted into an urnful of ashes.

Pet care - including for those that have died - has become a booming industry in the Philippines.
