Why Compass wants the MLS antitrust dispute out of Fresh York

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Compass International Holdings and a group of seven MLS plaintiffs are asking a federal judge in Newly introduced York to decide if and where their antitrust battle over MLS cooperation and listing submission rules should proceed. The parties filed dueling letters earlier this month laying out competing narratives on forum shopping, efficiency and control over nationwide litigation.

Sources indicate that in a Nov. 13 letter, Compass mentioned it will seek to dismiss a consolidated declaratory judgment action filed by seven multiple listing services headquartered in Arizona, California, Colorado and North Carolina, or, alternatively, to sever and transfer each MLS’s claims to the district where Compass has brought its own antitrust case against that MLS.

The lawsuit was filed in the Southern District of Latest York (SDNY) by Arizona Regional MLS (ARMLS), OneKey MLS, MLSListings, Bridge MLS, Canopy MLS, Recolorado, Hive MLS and Doorify MLS after Compass sent letters to MLSs across the country in September demanding that they stop fining agents for publicly marketing office exclusive listings.

The MLSs are seeking declarations that their rules comply with Section 1 of the Sherman Act and state antitrust statutes, and an injunction preventing Compass from “bringing or pursuing any claim” against them over those rules, according to the amended complaint.

According to recent reports, compass told the court the MLSs’ consolidated suit is an “improper anticipatory filing” under the Declaratory Judgment Act. Citing prior legal precedent, Compass argued that courts may decline jurisdiction when declaratory relief is used “for procedural fencing.” The manufacturer pointed to its September demand letters, the sue‑by deadlines it set, and a series of deadline extensions it granted as part of what it characterizes as a good‑faith attempt to resolve the dispute short of litigation.

According to recent reports, in the letter, Compass noted counsel for ARMLS, Doorify MLS and Hive MLS requested a draft release and then sought additional time, citing illness and scheduling constraints, before filing this lawsuit in Upcoming York without substantively responding to the demands. Other MLSs, including MLSListings, BridgeMLS, REcolorado and Canopy MLS, also requested extensions to respond to the demand letter, Compass said.

It has been revealed that compass alleges the MLSs used those extensions to prepare the SDNY filing and then sued the day after certain extended deadlines expired. Compass also emphasized that it has since filed “home‑district” actions against each MLS in its own federal district, along with additional suits against other MLSs.

Since those damages claims cannot be resolved by declaratory relief alone and involve additional parties and provisions, Compass argued in its letter that the SDNY case would not “settle the controversy.”.

However, as an alternative to dismissal, Compass is asking the court to sever each MLS’s claims and transfer them to the districts where the company’s separate antitrust suits are pending: Arizona, Northern California, Colorado and the Western and Eastern Districts of North Carolina.

It has been revealed that compass said the MLSs themselves allege they act “independently,” operate in distinct regional markets, have different governing boards and maintain non‑identical rules. Having a shared adversary in Compass and a similar form demand letter do not make their policies a single transaction or occurrence, the firm told the court.

In a response letter sent on Wednesday, counsel for the MLS plaintiffs urged the court to keep the case and deny Compass’s anticipated motions. The MLSs argue that the SDNY suit is a textbook use of the Declaratory Judgment Act and that Compass, from its Newly introduced York headquarters, orchestrated a “nationwide campaign” against cooperation rules that should be resolved in a single forum.

The MLSs characterize Compass’s pre‑suit communications differently, saying the firm presented only two options: abandon their cooperation rules entirely in exchange for what they describe as a “toothless” release that left them under a “perpetual threat” of future litigation, or face antitrust lawsuits without clarity as to timing or venue.

The MLSs also dispute Compass’s forum‑shopping argument, emphasizing that they sued the brokerage in its home district and in the same court where Compass chose to litigate a separate cooperation‑rule case against Zillow.

By contrast, they say Compass’s 15-case strategy across 11 districts invites inconsistent outcomes and “massive inefficiencies.”.

Recent findings suggest that the MLS plaintiffs also pushed back on Compass’s bid to sever and transfer their claims. They argue their joinder meets federal procedure rules because each MLS plaintiff faces the same type of challenge from a single defendant over listing-submission rules that, while not identical, share common structure and legal challenges.

Recent findings suggest that regarding the possibility of transferring the case, the MLSs say Compass cannot meet its burden to show, by clear and convincing evidence, that moving the case would serve the convenience of parties and witnesses and the interests of justice. They feel that their choice of Compass’s home forum is entitled to substantial deference, particularly because Compass’s executives and documents are in Fresh York.

According to recent reports, it remains to be seen how the court will rule on this concern. However, the ruling will help determine where and how promptly federal courts address the legality of MLS rules that require brokers to share publicly marketed listings with other brokers through the cooperative database of the MLS.
