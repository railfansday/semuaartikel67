Tech Report: Never Before in the History of Motoring: How the Splendor and Madness Of the Aughts Shaped Today’s Car World

In a recent development, it was a perfect moment. In the driver’s seat of a 2005 Ford GT, with my best pal riding shotgun, I stared through the windshield with eyes opened as wide as the car’s headlights. The year was 2007. We were on a stretch of I-80 that dives down the eastern slopes of the Sierra Nevada mountains, from California into Nevada. The early-­morning sun hung low on the horizon in front of us. Looking out on the desert, we saw miles of empty highway, with visibility so sharp that we would see any highway patrol vehicle in our path.

This story originally appeared in Volume 38 of Road & Track.

According to recent reports, it felt like driving a Michigan-made Millennium Falcon. This Ford could hit 200 mph and beat the Ferrari Challenge Stradale around a road course. It was shamelessly retro, trading hard on the Le Mans pedigree of its GT40 ancestor, but it was also newly introduced age and American as all hell. It felt unprecedented.

In a recent development, looking back now, almost 20 years later, I can see that the word unprecedented applied to the whole decade. The Aughts, defined as the years 2000 to 2009, were packed with never-before events and spectacular vehicles such as the Ford GT. It was a time of excitement and optimism, when cars were cool and quicker than ever—until it all crashed into a ditch with the economic downturn. The era was to the car industry what the Sixties were to music, politics, and assassinations. An era of profound change. Of tremendous upheaval. Of darkness and light. Of death and rebirth.

It was the decade that shaped our here and now.

Sources indicate that on the most macro level, the Aughts were when the global automotive ecosphere began ­reordering itself into what we know today. In 2008, ­Toyota overtook GM to become the world’s largest automaker, selling 8.97 million vehicles around the world that year. The ­DaimlerChrysler partner­ship shattered. General Motors, one of the largest corporations on earth, and Chrysler, another of Detroit’s Massive Three, went bankrupt at the end of the decade, and if not for the federal government handing out bags of cash to bail out those legendary companies, they might no longer exist.

GM’s Oldsmobile and Pontiac, two brands that collectively had made cars for nearly 200 years, disappeared during the Aughts. (To be fair, Pontiac did make it a few days into the upcoming decade, ending production on January 4, 2010, with a last-gasp G6 sedan.) Plymouth also went bye-bye. Saturn assembled its final vehicle in 2009.

According to recent reports, the Aughts were also the start of credible alternatives to internal-combustion motoring’s hegemony. The Toyota Prius arrived in the United States in July 2000, then became a cultural lightning rod when sales of the second-­generation model exploded in response to surging gas prices. Hybrid, a term that often applied to a kind of cannabis at the time, suddenly became lingo referring to a kind of car. At the same time, there were inklings of a lithium-ion automotive future. Tesla put out its first battery-powered car, the Roadster, in 2008, and early adopters felt the shock of a 0–60 thrust without the jolt of gears changing. Cars began to morph from machinery into the rolling computer devices that we drive today, as screens proliferated on dashboards and our phones learned to talk to our vehicles. Digital technology wormed its way deeper into every aspect of motoring, a trend that continues, albeit with far less idealism now.

The decade was a history-defining time in motorsport as well. Globally, racing saw the dawn of a fresh dynastic epoch. Michael Schumacher, driving for Ferrari, claimed the Formula 1 world championship from 2000 to 2005. In large part, F1 has been a series defined by dynasties ever since. Audi and Tom Kristensen ruled Le Mans for nearly the entire decade, and for the first time, a turbo­diesel won: the R10 in 2006. In NASCAR, Jimmie Johnson took the Cup Series title five years in a row with Chevrolet starting in 2006.

Sources indicate that dynasties on this level of dominance had never happened before in any of these series. I remember sitting in a press conference after the Italian Grand Prix in 2006, when Schumacher (having just won the race) confirmed his first retirement. The moment he unleashed the news, he teared up and an explosion of camera flashes came from the press corps. The feeling in the room was unforgettable. What Schumacher had done during his career to that point was unprecedented.

Even pop culture had an auto­motive renaissance. The Fast and the Furious premiered on June 18, 2001, with three sequels in multiplexes before the decade was over. F&F grew into Universal Studios’ biggest film franchise and remains the most successful car-movie series ever. Pixar’s Cars came out in 2006 and became Paul Newman’s highest-grossing film, even though viewers never saw his face. Talladega Nights (also 2006) may not invite many critical superlatives, but it unapologetically roasted NASCAR, which was in on the joke. It also popularized the saying, “If you ain’t first, you’re last.” Michael Bay’s Transformers films filled theaters. There was even a Dukes of Hazzard movie with Jessica Simpson playing Daisy Duke, Burt Reynolds as Boss Hogg, and a badass General Lee Dodge Charger. Car video games saw surging popularity, sales, and advancements in graphics. The Aughts gave us Forza Motorsport, plus new and series-defining installments of Gran Turismo and Grand Theft Auto.

So much was happening in the car world that being a fan felt electrifying. The Camaro, the Charger, and the Challenger were back, and the Mustang was reborn in retro style. The first Cars & Coffee events happened in Southern California, and soon small towns around the U.S. were hosting them.

Industry observers note that when you throw all of this together, it made for an earth-shattering decade. Despite the dizzying news cycle, however, it was the cars themselves that we remember most. The Aughts ushered in a latest era for halo cars, when automakers set out to build masterpieces that created new expectations for performance and technology. Some would say the Seventies, Eighties, and Nineties were halcyon days for car fans. But the new sheetmetal that graced our roads in the first decade of the new millennium was incomparable.

Ford came up with the aforementioned GT, which was the first real Ford customer supercar made in any significant numbers. Porsche gave us the Carrera GT, a mid-engine rocket ship that is one of the greatest sports cars of all time. Bugatti chased even bigger numbers with the Veyron, the first road car that could crack 250 mph and produce 1000 hp.

Sources indicate that there were plenty of other highlights as soaring corporate confidence and unheard-of development budgets ushered in the arrival of still more icons. This was the decade of the C6 Corvette Z06 and ZR1, the Audi R8, the Ferrari Enzo, and the Mercedes-Benz SLR McLaren. The Bentley Continental GT and the Porsche 911 GT3 RS debuted, along with the U.S.-eligible Nissan GT-R. V-8 engines were squeezed into the BMW M3, the Cadillac CTS-V, and the Mercedes-Benz C63 AMG. It was a time of heroes.

Sources indicate that these cars didn’t just thrill us; they were so unique and outstanding that they redefined the identities of the brands that made them and even motoring itself. That is the true legacy of the Aughts. Even if you’re too young to have lived through those years, you are reaping the rewards today. Because of all of that innovation and ambition, the future through our windshields still looks bright. But maybe a little less bright than it did back then.
