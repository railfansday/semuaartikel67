According to recent reports, the Vera C. Rubin Observatory discovered 2026 ED138, a distant dwarf planet candidate beyond Neptune whose size and shape remain unconfirmed.

Recent findings suggest that A distant object named 2026 ED138 may be a dwarf planet between 370 and 930 miles (600 and 1,500 kilometers) wide, the Vera C. Rubin Observatory announced on October 27, 2026.

Archival images show that another instrument, the Dark Energy Camera, had recorded it since February 25, 2016, without anyone noticing.

The object remains a candidate because its size has been estimated, not measured.

Sources indicate that matthew Holman, a senior astrophysicist at the Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian, found the object on the evening of October 13. He was running Rubin data collected earlier in 2026 through a search code developed with colleague Kevin Napier.

“My first reaction was one of excited disbelief,” Holman said.

“Objects like this are very rare, so I immediately started checking whether I or the code had done something wrong,” he added.

The code first detected a cluster of ten observations on eight nights. Mario Jurić, Rubin’s solar system lead scientist at the University of Washington, then found 20 more in Rubin’s catalogs and images.

In all, the observatory’s telescope saw the object 30 times on 20 nights between March 4 and July 11. The results were presented at a planetary science meeting in Spokane, Washington.

“It’s the largest trans-Neptunian object found in about 20 years,” Jurić noted.

The object is right now about 46 astronomical units from the Sun, or roughly 4.3 billion miles (6.9 billion kilometers). Pluto averages 39 of those units, each equal to Earth’s distance from the Sun, by NASA‘s figures.

Its orbit, which comes no closer to the Sun than about 37 astronomical units, is tilted almost 25 degrees from the solar system’s plane. The announcement suggests that gravitational interactions with Neptune disturbed the object’s original path.

According to recent reports, earlier surveys missed 2026 ED138 because it lies far south and near the crowded regions of the Galactic plane, the discovery paper states. In April, Rubin had already submitted observations of more than 11,000 fresh asteroids.

Industry observers note that given the orbit computed from Rubin data alone, an archive search service returned older candidate detections. The object appears in 45 Dark Energy Camera exposures on 14 nights, the last on April 7, 2023.

“Once we identify a latest object, we can search back months, and sometimes even decades, finding it in images where nobody knew to look,” Jurić explained.

According to recent reports, A century-long archival search, posted on November 2 and not yet peer-reviewed, pushes the record back further. It reports the object on 12 photographic plates taken between 1954 and 2000, plus a marginal detection on a 1937 plate.

Industry observers note that the diameter is calculated from the object’s brightness and an assumed albedo, the fraction of sunlight its surface reflects. That albedo has not been measured.

It has been revealed that the upper end of the declared range requires a very dark surface, one that reflects only four percent of the light. A diameter between 380 and 600 miles (612 and 968 kilometers) is more probable, by the discoverers’ own assessment.

According to recent reports, pluto, Eris and Makemake, whose surfaces are rich in methane ice, reflect about 60 to 90 percent of sunlight. So bright a surface is considered less likely for 2026 ED138, although its composition is still unknown.

In a recent development, “Thermal photometry or a stellar occultation is needed to decide between these sizes,” the authors wrote. An occultation occurs when an object passes in front of a star and briefly blocks its light.

The archival study predicts 15 such events between 2027 and 2030, six of them over populated land. It also reports a tentative rotation signal with a period of 10.4 hours.

By intrinsic brightness, the object ranks ninth or tenth among known bodies beyond Neptune, depending on which measurements are compared.

According to recent reports, under the 2006 definition of the International Astronomical Union (IAU), a dwarf planet orbits the Sun and is not a satellite. Its own gravity must pull it into a nearly round shape, and it has not cleared the neighborhood around its orbit.

It has been revealed that the IAU has so far recognized five: Ceres, Pluto, Haumea, Makemake and Eris. “If confirmed as a dwarf planet, ED138 would join a very small family of worlds,” Holman stated.

Icy bodies as large as 2026 ED138 are expected to be round, the Rubin release states. Whether this one is compact and round is less certain, its discoverers cautioned in their paper.

A 2019 study of a distant binary suggested that objects 250 to 620 miles (400 to 1,000 kilometers) wide mark a transition.

Smaller bodies stay porous, while larger ones have collapsed their internal voids under much higher pressures and temperatures.

The discovery team estimates that about three more objects this bright, and probably more, remain undiscovered away from the Galactic plane. Rubin’s ten-year survey of the southern sky began in June 2026.

The study is available as a preprint on arXiv.
