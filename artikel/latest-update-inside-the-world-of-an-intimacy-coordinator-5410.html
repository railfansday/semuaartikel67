Recently, after seeing a stage play, I spoke to a lead actor about how she is able to feel safe during physically intimate scenes in front of an audience. She told me that there is an intimacy coordinator who comes onto the set to make sure the actors’ feelings about intimacy with other actors are taken into account. She has also worked on film sets with an intimacy coordinator when she felt uneasy and vulnerable in front of the camera and crew and her lover in the film.

Industry observers note that I wanted to find out more about this and, unexpectedly, I met intimacy coordinator Christina McHugh at an event in Latest Mexico a few weeks later. I asked her to define what it is that she does when she is hired to work on films. “Old-fashioned doctors went house to house, a mail person goes door to door, and an intimacy coordinator goes set to set.” The geographical areas she covered were Albuquerque, Santa Fe, and movie ranches. She goes from one set to another.

She explained that making actors feel psychologically and physically safe was mostly prep work, before the scenes were shot. She had extensive conversations with directors and then worked with the actors to find out what their boundaries were and to get their consent. “Then I would go back to the director and impart information like the fact that the actor wanted less nudity. I would ask the director if it could be filmed a different way.”.

For filming sex scenes, she used things like a “half-deflated Pilates ball to put between the actors’ crotches. There were often three layers of barriers between the actors for simulated sex. If the woman is on top of the man, her bouncing is done with a 9-inch Pilates ball that makes it look like simulated sex.”.

McHugh stated that her job entails negotiating boundaries with individual actors, the director, and trying to get all sides to agree. This is especially true in building trust on the set of a film with hyper-exposure, that includes nudity, simulated sex, and sexual violence.

Some of the sets she worked on include "Better Call Saul," "Pluribus," "Love Lies Bleeding," "Obliterated," "Duster," "The Cleaning Lady," "Waco," and "Dead for a Dollar.".

Recent findings suggest that I inquired where she learned her profession, and she replied that she trained with the Intimacy Professionals Association. “In about 2017, it was the first time it revealed up in a credit line. It started with an HBO series, "The Deuce," which was about the porn industry. The actress felt she was being treated as a porn star and not an actress.”.

I believed her when McHugh stated you need a spine made of titanium to deal with producers and directors. She is not flippant about the challenges actors have when she is negotiating with them. She said that young female actors tell her about being victims of sexual predation, and she takes care of them. This is an vital part of her job, which she takes very seriously.

Industry observers note that entertainment writer Liz Konkel had this to say in a recent post on Cinema Blend: “The rules have certainly changed in regard to how sex scenes are filmed, thanks to the rise of intimacy coordinators on sets. Now, this doesn't mean we don't get plenty of steamy content. However, what it does mean is that more technical work goes into creating such scenes. In a post #MeToo Movement industry, these coordinators also help ensure actors feel comfortable and protected during intimate scenes.”.

Recent findings suggest that in the same article, she also addressed how actors’ boundaries and trust concerns were dealt with before intimacy coordinators. “Back in the early aughts, scenes were seemingly required of the actors if it was in the script. However, some performers have since shared stories where trust was taken advantage of on set. For instance, "Basic Instinct’s" Sharon Stone has made claims involving inappropriate requests for nudity amid production. With that, many now consider intimacy coordinators to be essential.”.
