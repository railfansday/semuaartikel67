How to Make a Pot, a newly introduced book by Maggie Boyd, a ceramic artist in New York, is a how-to book that works for clay newbies and veterans, as well as those who are only vaguely interested in clay, given that, at its heart, the book is a meditation on life and art.

It is broken into two parts. Part one explores what a pot is and what it means to make one—reminding you that art is a way to interact with the world—while part two, covering the technical side, gets into how every pot is an experience, and that expertise is the result of many well-intentioned but imprecise turns. It’s not a spoiler to say that Boyd’s book is about how we hold the world and care for it, a future hidden in every piece of fresh wet clay.

How to Make a Pot is very smart and fun and serious, as was its author, who—when Vogue talked to her the other day in her studio, in Bushwick—was wearing a vintage 1930s baseball jersey and making pots.

Vogue: Why write a book on ceramics?

Sources indicate that maggie Boyd: I was teaching a class for friends in my studio—it was a secret little speakeasy class—because I had a lot of friends who wanted to learn ceramics. One of the people who ended up taking the class, who kind of instigated it, was my friend Caleb, who said, “Listen, you should write a book.” And I said, “You know, I’m not sure, but I think I have a book that I wrote already in my brain.” I’ve been teaching for about 15 years and I’ve been working with clay for over 20 years, so everything that is in there is these collected observations from trying to figure out how to explain a process that is so lengthy and cumbersome and dependent on timing and pressure and a sensitivity to the material. There’ve been so many attempts to try to clarify that process for people.

Industry observers note that part one of the book had me thinking of that great line by M.C. Richards, the Black Mountain College ceramicist and writer, who remarked: “Poets are not the only poets.” It is a series of drawings with very few words, and I am wondering about what made you want to start a how-to book with drawings as opposed to, say, technical diagrams and step-by-step instructions?

Well, I regularly speak in metaphor. I explain things with drawings, all that stuff, and the way that I teach ceramics is through drawing and basically interpretive dance moves! And I hadn’t seen a book that approached teaching ceramics in this way. So the first part is almost like a prayer or meditation or primer—a spiritual primer before you actually even touch the clay. And the point of that is to resensitize the reader, because I think that especially with—well, I hate to name any of the social media platforms, but I feel as if that one platform has made it so that people are like, “Oh, you can make clay really fast! You can make money at it—it’s a business!” There are all these things that it has sort of taken away from the magic of pushing a material that moves.

So part one is a kind of reset?

In a recent development, yes, because we are so inundated with the search for the ultimate point of productivity. We’re a grind culture. We’re like: How do we turn the most beautiful, calm, mystical experience of interacting with material into profitable business immediately? And that shifts the way we relate to the material. And I wanted to slow us back down.

Because everything tells us that easier is the best way, which reminds me of a conversation I overheard the other day: two ceramicists were talking about the world being taken over by AI slop. It’s a discussion being had these days by so many people making things: drawings, films, clothing, or even sentences.

I’ve been thinking about this non-stop lately because everyone’s always saying, “Oh, AI just makes everything easy. It streamlines everything and it takes away all this effort.” And to hear this makes me so sad because that is the point for me, the effort. I like doing this. I want to do it. AI would take away that experience with the material. There’s so much magic when you interact with the material or a person, and you can’t do that with AI. That’s not the effort I want removed from my life.

Recent findings suggest that part of the effort, it seems to me, is learning the hard way that, for example, at around 212 degrees, if there is moisture in a piece it will, no matter how potentially wonderful, explode. Right?

It’s a circle of failing and trying. Clay is a rigorous material to understand and you need a lot of help and a lot of tips and a lot of support and a lot of pre-discovered knowledge. So part two is just me telling you what I’ve learned and what worked for me, knowing that it will be imperfectly described and not everything will apply to you. You take whatever you can from everything you read. And what I’ve learned from doing ceramics for so long is every time I go and I talk with a new artist about a new thing, I learn something from them. Because we’re all approaching this material and it has so many forking paths of possible approaches that I’m always like, “Oh, that’s how you do it.” And it always shifts. So in that way, I don’t feel like I need to know everything because I just know what I know. And hopefully that’s helpful.

Was it difficult to describe, say, how to turn a pot on a wheel? Something that is so seemingly simple but complicated? What was the real triumph for you in, I guess, achieving an explanation?

The triumph is when you have a skill that is so innate and just in your body and then you translate that into words, step by step. It’s such a wild writing exercise. Try it at home! Write how it is to take a fork and bring food to your mouth. It was such an eye-opening experience to understand what how-to manuals do. I had to step outside of myself. Sometimes I would even video myself doing something so I could watch and realize, Oh, that’s the order I do it in!

Do you remember the first ceramic piece that you ever made in your life?

Sources indicate that in grade two—that’s what you say in Canada, not “second grade”—we got to work with clay, real clay, for the first time. Not Play-Doh, but clay that was going to go in a kiln. And I made a dragon. And I remember I made a super long neck and these, like, Bart Simpson-looking eyes and pinch spikes all the way around along its tail. And I remember I was like: Maybe I’m kind of good at this? Like, I had this sense really early that I understood working with this material.

This tall dragon that was so beautiful, and then I remember that I glazed the whole thing in a forest green, and I loved that it had green eyeballs that matched its skin! Later on, it got lost, so I actually remade it from memory when I was 23, for my dad. I remember because when I made it before, it said: “Maggie, age 6.” And then the second time I wrote: “Maggie, age 23.”.

I’ve read about the “Projects Class” at the Nova Scotia College of Art and Design, where you attended; it involved artists from all over—everyone from Lucy Lippard to Dan Graham to Sonic Youth—collaborating with the school, or even sending up proposals for the students to execute.

I showed up and I completely threw ceramics out the window. Because I was like, okay, well, obviously I’m going to be a performance and sound installation artist who lives in a gallery and then maybe secretly sleeps at school and, you know, does all the stuff that you need to do to get your art school yah-yahs out. I took every possible class and had no intention of graduating. I just wanted to stay forever.

Industry observers note that luckily, I got hired to do the after-school programming at this place, the George Dixon Center, a public housing project in Halifax. So I kind of pulled this skill out of retirement and started teaching. And then people in the neighborhood wanted to take classes, and then I was teaching kids that were about four years old. The teacher before me was saying, “Kids are not allowed to touch the wheel until they’re 11 years old,” or whatever. And then as soon as she left, I noted, “All right, kids, get on the wheel!” And it was totally crazy and so amazing. I had something that people wanted to learn that I could share. From that point on I was always teaching ceramics.

How did your art school theoretical studies and your pot-making studio skills connect? Did the two skill sets blend together at some point?

I think for a long time they felt separate, and it’s taken until kind of recently for them to fuse comfortably. I thought of [ceramics] as sort of my trade skill, something I could do. I can throw a pot. I can do cups for restaurants. And I just kept getting hired and hired, and really all I wanted was to have a way to be in a studio. Yes, there was this side of me that just wanted to, you know, do all the experiments and sounds and all this stuff, but I started to think that the art world also wants these two instincts to be at odds. And we buy into that because, you know, commerce is king, and it defines things for us instead of our intuition.

Your pieces seem to be as much functional as they are experiments—wondrous functional experiments—which is to say, I guess you resolved something?

Industry observers note that I just stopped believing in the hierarchy that was constructed through commerce and the art world. And I just understood that the most key aspect of being an artist is following the impulse to make, because it’s a question that you solve with any answer, and it doesn’t have to have a truth. So you just get to follow this incredible line of thought and that’s the whole point. That could become a pot or painting or drawing or a dance or a nap or whatever—they’re all just the same person asking the same questions and trying to answer them in different ways.

By the way, what happened when the four-year-olds got on the wheel?

According to recent reports, OK, the difference between adults and children is that children, if you get them at the right point, have absolutely no fear about making a mistake. They are so fearless and they have so little self-criticality. They’re just pure curiosity and cause and effect. And so, yeah, kids on the wheel is sometimes, “How fast can this thing go?” and “How far can I spray this clay?” Like, pretty good questions, though hard questions to clean up. But they’re just so involved in making, whereas when you teach adults, you have to try to sort of break them back down. Sister Corita Kent, she says: “Don’t try to create and analyze at the same time.” So that’s what children don’t need help with, and it’s exactly what an adult often needs help with. And then, well, if you learn to decipher a pot, a pot will show you who you are.

In terms of part two of How to Make a Pot, what are you reading to continue your never-ending ceramic education?

The Ceramic Spectrum by Robin Hopper, second edition. It is the definitive reference book on glaze.

And for the lay person, such as myself: What, exactly, is a glaze?

In a recent development, so a glaze is a glass-like coating that goes on top of a clay body, that fuses to the surface. It is made up of stabilizer, glass, and flux to make it melt, and its combinations of those three ingredients [dictate] how it holds color, or its opacity or clarity or transparency and all that.

And what are Hopper’s insights into glaze?

He is talking about how experience is so much more notable than calculation in glaze-making. It’s just a really great overview of how to make colors and glazes and how to experiment. He values knowledge that is experiential, and it’s so cool because I feel as if glaze-making, in particular, is this gatekept thing—you have to understand the chemistry and how to do the chemical equations. And he just makes the point, which I feel like people need to remind themselves of with almost all things that they make, bread or anything, that this is something that has been part of humanity since long before we had computer programs that broke down the chemical analysis of something.

What have you read recently, or what have you been listening to while you work in the studio at this moment?

In a recent development, A lecture by Anne Carson on Homer’s Odyssey and Le Mépris, the film by Jean-Luc Godard. I used to say my two favorite movies were The Lion King and Le Mépris.

Ah, a film about an artist making a film, and having to deal with the studio’s purely commercial demands, which is what Godard himself was dealing with in dealing with an American producer; the insistence, first off, that Brigitte Bardot be nude!

Industry observers note that yes, and Carson is talking about how to be an artist. Or, really, about the seller versus selling. But until I heard her lecture, I didn’t understand that the Americans asked that he have nudity in the film, and that Godard mentioned, “Oh, so what I’m going to do is I’m going to double down on this thing that you’re asking me to do and actually, like, rub it in your face.” Carson says Godard makes art out of selling out. She says he makes a spectacle of compromise. And she says that Brigitte Bardot just wins. I love that. The film’s so good. It’s how to be an artist, or when do you realize, Oh, they’re just going to want you to make the same thing.
