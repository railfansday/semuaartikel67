This Mileage Target Can Help You Determine Whether You Built a Strong Enough Base to Start Race Training

Base training involves spending a few weeks running at an easy pace and slowly increasing your mileage over time. It’s a crucial first step in any running journey, designed to help you ease into race-specific training.

It has been revealed that building a sufficient aerobic base helps you avoid the aches and pains that come with ramping up mileage too swiftly, especially if you’ve been running sporadically or took some time off. It also makes running feel more doable, as it bridges the gap between your current routine and your goal training blueprint.

The hard part of this foundational phase? Figuring out if you’re ready to tackle the first few weeks of a proposal and start race-specific workouts. Thankfully, coaches offer a helpful benchmark to hit, plus they explain how to smoothly transition your base routine to race training, without shocking your system.

Building a strong base before racing any distance helps prevent injuries and improves long-term performance, including improved aerobic capacity, endurance, faster recovery, mental conditioning, and weight and stress management, Jill Becker, a personal trainer and run coach at Life Time in Westminster, CO, previously told Runner’s World. That’s why the phase is necessary for all runners, whether you’re latest to the sport or have a Boston qualifying time on the list of goals.

“Even a 5K race relies heavily on aerobic systems for energy production—so the better the aerobic base you have, the faster you can race,” Laura Norris, owner and coach at Laura Norris Running in Boulder, CO, previously told Runner’s World.

Additionally, Norris indicates doing these easy miles weeks before your goal training block allows your body to adapt to the mileage first, so you can then add in speedwork leading up to race day with lower risk of injury and burnout. Taking this approach helps you avoid increasing volume and intensity at the same time, which can often lead to overtraining.

Finally, tackling a base phase means you’ll feel more confident heading into, say, a four-miler with faster-paced efforts (like tempo or race pace miles), as you’ll have already run four miles at an easy pace consistently for several weeks.

Sources indicate that building a strong base requires covering a certain number of miles consistently over the course of several weeks. Beginners should aim to spend at least 10 weeks on this training block, while intermediate runners can follow a six-week proposal, and more advanced runners typically need about four weeks to set that aerobic foundation, Becker says.

Recent findings suggest that if you’ve already picked out your training plan, Norris recommends working up to at least 80 percent of the weekly mileage prescribed in the first week of that plan. For example, our 20-week beginner marathon plan prescribes 12 miles in the first week. That means you’ll want to work up to about 10 miles per week before diving into the plan. If you’re looking to break two hours in the half marathon, which requires running at least 18 miles in the first week of the Runner’s World plan, then you’ll want to work up to about 15 miles.

Recent findings suggest that if you haven’t identified your training plan yet, Becker recommends adding at least 10 miles to your current weekly volume. (If you’re presently running 10 miles a week, work up to 20.).

Look for a plan that builds up to that mileage goal slowly, following the 10 percent rule, which means increasing your mileage by no more than 10 percent each week.

Keep in mind, base training is about setting the foundation for your next training block, and a part of that process should include recovery time. This means making time for rest days and down weeks (in which you reduce your volume to allow for recovery and better performance), as well as staying on top of your nutrition, sleep habits, and cross-training.
