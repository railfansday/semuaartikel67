Keto diet slashes liver fat by 67% in just 4 months

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A ketogenic diet may offer significant health benefits beyond weight loss, according to a upcoming clinical trial that compared three popular approaches to eating. Researchers found that people following a very low-carbohydrate, high-fat diet experienced substantially greater reductions in liver fat than those eating Mediterranean or low-fat diets, even though all three groups lost similar amounts of weight.

The results were especially striking for people with prediabetes. After just four to five months on a ketogenic (or "Keto") diet, approximately half of the participants no longer met the diagnostic criteria for the condition. The findings, published in the journal Cell Metabolism, suggest that the types of foods people eat can influence metabolic health in ways that extend beyond the number of pounds they lose.

"We know weight loss can improve metabolic health in people who are obese," notes Samuel Klein, the study's corresponding author at Washington University School of Medicine in St. Louis, Missouri. "Our data suggest that if you have high liver fat content and prediabetes, reducing carbohydrate intake can have vital therapeutic effects on metabolism beyond just weight loss alone.".

Comparing Keto, Mediterranean, and Low-Fat Diets.

According to recent reports, an estimated 40% of American adults have obesity, a condition associated with increased risks of type 2 diabetes, fatty liver disease, and other health problems. Doctors frequently recommend dietary changes to help patients lose weight and improve their metabolic health, which refers to how effectively the body manages energy, blood sugar, and fats.

Three commonly recommended approaches are low-carbohydrate diets, low-fat diets, and Mediterranean diets. Although each can support weight loss, researchers have been uncertain whether one offers additional metabolic advantages when the amount of weight lost is roughly the same.

These eating patterns differ substantially in their nutritional makeup. Low-carbohydrate approaches, including Atkins and ketogenic diets, restrict carbohydrate consumption, while low-fat diets may obtain as much as 70% of their calories from carbohydrates. The Mediterranean diet emphasizes plant foods such as vegetables, nuts, and olive oil, along with moderate amounts of fish and dairy. Ketogenic diets, meanwhile, can include considerable amounts of fat from animal products, including saturated fat.

It has been revealed that to compare their effects, Klein and his colleagues recruited 42 adults with obesity, prediabetes, and fatty liver disease. Prediabetes occurs when blood sugar levels rise above the normal range but have not yet reached the threshold for type 2 diabetes. Fatty liver disease involves excess fat accumulating inside liver cells, which can interfere with the body's ability to regulate metabolism.

A Controlled Clinical Trial With Similar Weight Loss.

In a recent development, participants were randomly assigned to follow one of three diets: ketogenic, Mediterranean, or a low-fat diet emphasizing plant-based foods. To ensure that participants followed their assigned eating plans, the research team supplied all their meals and arranged weekly consultations with a dietitian.

According to recent reports, the diets had sharply different proportions of carbohydrates and fat. In the ketogenic group, carbohydrates accounted for just 4% of daily calories, while fat supplied 73%. The Mediterranean group received 50% of its calories from carbohydrates and 35% from fat. The plant-forward group consumed 70% of its calories from carbohydrates and 15% from fat.

Sources indicate that researchers adjusted calorie intake individually so that participants across all three groups lost approximately 10% of their starting body weight. Reaching this target took about four to five months.

Industry observers note that by the end of the intervention, every group had experienced meaningful reductions in body fat and improvements in insulin sensitivity. Insulin is the hormone that helps move sugar from the bloodstream into cells, where it can be used for energy. Greater insulin sensitivity makes it easier for the body to maintain healthy blood sugar levels.

However, the similarities between the groups largely ended there.

Keto Diet Reduces Liver Fat by 67%.

The ketogenic diet produced the largest improvements in several vital indicators of liver function and metabolic health.

Recent findings suggest that participants following keto experienced an average 67% reduction in liver fat, compared with a 45% reduction in both the Mediterranean and low-fat groups. Because the groups lost comparable amounts of weight, the difference suggests that carbohydrate restriction may influence liver fat through mechanisms beyond weight loss alone.

To investigate these effects, the researchers collected blood samples repeatedly over a 24-hour period, allowing them to observe changes in hormones involved in energy metabolism.

The ketogenic group showed the greatest increase in glucagon, a hormone that helps regulate the body's energy supply and promotes the reduction of fat stored in the liver. Participants on keto also experienced the largest decrease in insulin levels, another change that can help reduce liver fat accumulation.

It has been revealed that one result was particularly unexpected. Despite getting nearly three-quarters of their calories from fat, the keto participants did not show an increase in blood fat or cholesterol levels during the study.

"The most surprising finding was that participants in the ketogenic diet group didn't experience an increase in blood fat or cholesterol levels despite consuming the most fat," claims Max Petersen, the study's first author at Washington University School of Medicine.

Half of Keto Participants No Longer Had Prediabetes.

In a recent development, the benefits extended beyond liver fat. After the weight-loss intervention, approximately 50% of participants assigned to the ketogenic diet no longer met the criteria for prediabetes. By comparison, that milestone was reached by 29% of those on the Mediterranean diet and just 7% of those following the low-fat, plant-forward diet.

Industry observers note that these differences are notable because prediabetes signals an elevated risk of developing type 2 diabetes. Bringing blood sugar levels below the prediabetes threshold is an important improvement, although it does not necessarily mean the condition has been permanently reversed.

Industry observers note that the findings also highlight a potentially vital distinction in weight management: losing weight and improving metabolic health are closely connected, but they are not necessarily the same thing. The composition of a diet may affect how the body handles blood sugar, stores fat, and responds to hormones, even when weight loss is similar.

Sources indicate that researchers emphasize the growing importance of understanding these effects as powerful upcoming obesity treatments become more widely available. GLP-1 medications, which act on hormone pathways involved in appetite and blood sugar regulation, have transformed the treatment of obesity by helping many patients achieve substantial weight loss.

It has been revealed that "GLP-1-based medications have revolutionized the therapy of obesity. We now have a very effective pharmacotherapy for achieving significant weight loss," Petersen indicates. "But in addition, our study shows that the specific composition of the diet can give you added benefits.".

Although the trial was relatively small and lasted only four to five months, its carefully controlled design offers evidence that dietary composition can play an important role in metabolic health. Longer studies involving more participants will be needed to determine whether the benefits persist and how different diets compare over time.
