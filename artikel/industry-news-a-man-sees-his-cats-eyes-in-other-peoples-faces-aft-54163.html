Industry News: A man sees his cat’s eyes in other people’s faces after brain surgery

A rare case report describes a man who sees his cat's eyes in human faces after brain surgery, showing how memory can shape face distortions.

Recent findings suggest that on some days, a 28-year-old man identified as Karl sees his cat’s eyes in every human face he looks at. The researchers who documented his case think the features may come from visual memory.

The case, described in a short report in the journal Cortex, points to how face perception is built.

Sources indicate that the features Karl sees are not generic cat eyes. He recognizes them as his own cat’s.

The authors read that as evidence that memory can feed individual facial features back into what a person sees.

The report covers a single case, written up from Karl’s own descriptions, and its images are reconstructions he helped refine rather than scans of anything in his brain.

Brad Duchaine of Dartmouth College led the report with Antônio Mello, a doctoral student at Dartmouth, and Jennifer Nosker, a neuropsychologist at Brigham and Women’s Hospital in Boston.

“This case of Karl seeing his cat’s features in human faces and vice versa demonstrates that individual features can be pulled from memory and influence our ongoing perception,” Duchaine said.

Karl’s distortions began two years ago, after surgeons removed a tangle of blood vessels from the back of his right temporal lobe, a region central to recognizing faces.

It has been revealed that the condition is called prosopometamorphopsia, or PMO, in which faces look distorted. His version includes palinopsia, in which something seen earlier persists in later vision.

His gray tabby, Luna, is often the first face he sees in the morning. On those days, her enlarged pupils, cat-like eye sockets, and the shading around them can appear on human faces for hours afterward, in person or on a screen.

Recent findings suggest that the transfer runs the other way too. If a familiar person is the first face he sees, that person’s eye shape and pupil size can show up in his cat’s face. Episodes often last several hours, and occasionally they last the whole day.

What makes the case useful to researchers is the precision of the swap.

Only parts of a face move, usually the eye region, sometimes with its coloring and texture, while the rest of the viewed face looks normal.

The transferred features also look coherent. They blend into the face rather than appearing superimposed on it, and they shift naturally as Karl changes his angle or distance.

Sources indicate that and Karl can say whose they are. He identifies the borrowed eyes as belonging to particular people he knows, or to his cat, which led the authors to suggest the features are retrieved from long-term visual memory.

He has frequently experienced human-to-human transfers as well.

Sources indicate that the report opens with a case the same journal published in 1986. Nine months after a stroke, a woman riding a bus looked at a poodle on a passenger’s lap, then looked away, and saw the poodle’s face on every passenger.

The poodle faces persisted for about 30 minutes after she got off the bus.

The 1986 case suggested faces are processed differently from other things people see, because the dog’s face appeared only on other faces.

Karl’s experience sharpens the point: the brain seems to store and reactivate identity-specific pieces of faces, not only whole faces.

Whole-face substitutions like the poodle case are rare, the authors wrote.

Over five years, about 275 people reporting face distortions have contacted the lab through its PMO website, and most describe changes to the shape, position, color, or texture of features.

The 1986 case came with a line drawing. Karl’s came with photorealistic images, generated with an AI model from his verbal descriptions and from a sketch he drew.

The images were then refined over several months until he judged they matched his experience. Karl made some of the changes himself.

“AI provides researchers like us with the ability to generate more accurate visualizations of what people are experiencing when they’re having distorted visual perceptions,” Duchaine mentioned.

“If you know what the person is experiencing, you can much better research what they’re dealing with and try to figure out ways to control it.”.

The images also help patients explain the condition to family and friends, he added.

Duchaine said his lab has heard from people who were told they had a psychiatric problem, and in some cases were put on antipsychotic drugs, when their problem was visual.

The report rests on one person’s account of his own perception, which no one else can see directly. It is a clinical description, not an experiment, and it does not test what triggers an episode.

What the report can’t say is how common this feature-level borrowing is.

The authors wrote only that several other people with PMO have told them of face palinopsia, against a far larger number who report seeing faces warp in shape or color.

The team intends to keep working with Karl, and the authors expect photorealistic reconstructions to become an increasingly valuable tool for recording what patients with perceptual disorders actually see.

The full study was published in the journal Cortex.
