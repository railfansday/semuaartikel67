Cannabis users develop psychosis years earlier, and the gap grows with age

Industry observers note that A major international analysis has found that people who use cannabis experience their first episode of psychosis about two and a half years earlier, on average, than those who do not use the drug. Even more concerning, the difference becomes substantially larger with age, reaching more than six years among people who develop psychosis in their 30s or later.

The findings, published in the journal Biological Psychiatry, come from researchers at UNSW Sydney. They suggest that the potential psychiatric consequences of cannabis use may extend well beyond adolescence and could become more pronounced with prolonged exposure.

According to recent reports, psychosis is a condition in which a person experiences a disruption in their perception of reality. Symptoms can include hallucinations, false beliefs known as delusions, and disturbances in thinking or behavior. It can occur in schizophrenia and several other mental health disorders. Although psychosis most commonly begins during the late teenage years or early adulthood, it can develop at other stages of life. According to Psychosis Australia, up to 3% of adults will experience a diagnosed disorder involving psychosis during their lifetime.

Cannabis Linked to Earlier Psychosis Across Adulthood.

In a recent development, to investigate the relationship between cannabis use and the timing of psychosis, researchers at UNSW Medicine analyzed 149 studies involving more than 71,000 participants. Most of the research had been conducted since 2011 in Europe, North America, and Australia, with the majority of studies focusing on people diagnosed with schizophrenia.

The researchers discovered that the difference in the age at which psychosis first appeared varied considerably across age groups.

Recent findings suggest that in studies where the average age of psychosis onset was between 20 and 24, cannabis customers experienced their first episode approximately 1.6 years earlier than non-users. That difference widened to 4.43 years in studies involving people whose psychosis emerged in their mid-to-late 20s (25-29).

In a recent development, the largest difference appeared in studies where psychosis developed at age 30 or later. In this group, cannabis people experienced the condition an average of 6.3 years earlier than non-users.

These results suggest that the association between cannabis and earlier psychosis may become stronger as people grow older, potentially reflecting the effects of longer periods of cannabis exposure.

Why Cannabis Risks May Increase With Age.

Chief investigator Matthew Large, a psychiatrist and Conjoint Professor at UNSW's School of Clinical Medicine, stated the findings raise important questions about the long-term psychiatric effects of cannabis.

Industry observers note that "We've tended to focus on the dangers of using cannabis in adolescence, when the brain is still developing, but the risk doesn't disappear once you're in your 20s," Prof. Large stated. "What we're seeing suggests a cumulative effect, where harm builds up over time, a bit like alcohol or nicotine.".

Interestingly, the analysis did not identify a statistically significant difference in the timing of psychosis between teenage cannabis customers and teenagers who did not use cannabis.

According to recent reports, however, the researchers cautioned that this finding does not necessarily mean younger cannabis customers face no risk. Psychosis rarely develops before the middle teenage years, leaving relatively little opportunity for its onset to shift to an even younger age.

"There may be a biological floor, in that there is only so much earlier that onset can be shifted. In younger groups, strong development, genetic or other psychosis risk factors may dominate, making any cannabis-related age shift harder to detect," Prof. Large remarked.

According to recent reports, the researchers also stressed an crucial limitation. Because the analysis examined existing observational studies, it cannot establish that cannabis directly causes psychosis or makes the condition begin sooner. Demonstrating such a relationship experimentally would require research that deliberately exposed people to potential psychiatric harm, which would be unethical.

Sources indicate that nevertheless, Prof. Large believes the overall pattern provides meaningful evidence supporting a possible causal connection.

"But the consistency of the evidence, particularly the strong effect in older adults who likely have been using cannabis for longer, does support the theory that cannabis plays a causal role in the timing of psychosis onset, and possibly the onset of some cases among people who would not have otherwise developed psychosis.".

Upcoming Findings Challenge Assumptions About Cannabis After Age 25.

The latest research builds on two earlier investigations into cannabis use and the development of psychosis.

In a smaller 2011 meta-analysis led by Prof. Large, researchers found that psychosis began approximately 2.7 years earlier among cannabis customers. A separate 2015 analysis reported that psychosis typically developed 6.3 years after cannabis use began.

However, neither earlier investigation had enough information to determine how the relationship differed across age groups.

By incorporating a much larger body of research, the new analysis allowed scientists to examine how the association changes across different stages of adulthood.

Lead author Carly Stevens, from UNSW's School of Biomedical Sciences, mentioned the findings undermine the idea that avoiding cannabis during adolescence is sufficient to eliminate its potential psychiatric dangers.

According to recent reports, "What we've found challenges the assumption that you'll be fine if you wait to smoke weed until your mid-20s; that's just not the case," Ms. Stevens said.

"There's been so much emphasis on adolescents not smoking cannabis, which is important, but we need to broaden the warnings for everyone.".

Why Even a Few Years of Delayed Psychosis Matter.

Although a difference of two or three years might initially seem relatively small, the researchers emphasized that the timing of psychosis can have profound consequences for a person's future.

The late teenage years and early adulthood are often periods when people complete their education, begin careers, establish relationships, and develop greater independence. Experiencing a serious psychiatric disorder earlier in life can interrupt these important milestones.

"People may miss out on study, job opportunities, travel, and relationships in those years, and treatment can come with physical side effects that can make life harder as well," Ms. Stevens said.

According to recent reports, prof. Large emphasized the potential benefits of reducing or discontinuing cannabis use and the importance of delaying the onset of psychosis wherever possible.

In a recent development, "It's a major illness, which robs you of your shared reality with family, friends and all those around you," he said. "If we can delay psychosis by even a few years, people will be more likely to reach crucial milestones in education and training, and develop strong support networks, which can protect against psychosocial decline.".

It has been revealed that the potential benefits could also extend to healthcare systems. According to Prof. Large, psychotic disorders account for as much as 3% of health-related spending in developed countries. Preventing or delaying the onset of these conditions could therefore have substantial social and economic benefits.

Scientists Call for Stronger Cannabis Health Warnings.

Despite the inability to establish a direct cause-and-effect relationship, the researchers believe their findings provide sufficient justification for stronger warnings about the possible psychiatric consequences of cannabis use.

Industry observers note that "We can't run direct experiments, so we have to go with the best evidence available," Prof. Large remarked. "In my view, the threshold for health warnings about cannabis and psychosis has definitely been met.".

One concern is the growing popularity of medical cannabis. Prof. Large said its increasing use as a therapeutic product may encourage some people to assume that it carries little risk.

He has also observed an increasing number of patients receiving treatment for psychosis who had previously been prescribed cannabis for medical conditions where evidence supporting its effectiveness remains limited.

"It's got this therapeutic halo around it, which blinds people to the risk.".

It has been revealed that another emerging concern involves cannabis edibles, which can contain elevated concentrations of Tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), the main compound responsible for the drug's mind altering effects. The availability of products containing higher THC concentrations raises additional questions about possible psychiatric risks.

Industry observers note that at the same time, Prof. Large acknowledged that cannabis legalization could create opportunities to reduce harm, even though it would also make cannabis easier to obtain.

Legal regulation could allow governments to establish limits on product potency, improve consumer education, introduce taxation, and require buyers to receive clear information about potential health consequences.

Sources indicate that "You could tax it, regulate it, and require people to show they understand the risks before buying," Prof. Large said.

More Research Needed on Long-Term Cannabis Exposure.

The researchers say further studies are necessary to understand the biological processes that might explain why the association between cannabis use and earlier psychosis becomes more pronounced with age.

In particular, they want to investigate whether repeated or prolonged cannabis exposure produces cumulative changes that influence when psychotic symptoms emerge.

They also recommend additional research focusing on older cannabis customers, an increasingly important population as medical cannabis becomes more common and products containing high concentrations of THC become more widely available.

It has been revealed that another priority is improving how cannabis exposure is measured. The researchers argue that future studies need clearer and more consistent methods for recording cannabis consumption so that findings can be compared more reliably.

Ultimately, the upcoming analysis highlights an crucial gap in public understanding of cannabis and mental health. While warnings have traditionally concentrated on teenage use, the findings suggest that potential psychiatric risks deserve attention throughout adulthood, particularly among people who continue using cannabis over many years.
