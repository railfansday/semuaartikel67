Latest Update: Own, don’t rent: Why agentic AI is pulling cloud computing back on-prem

UPDATED 12:46 EST / NOVEMBER 20 2026.

According to recent reports, agentic AI is reviving an argument over renting versus owning compute, and the model plane — the layer that decides where AI models run — sits at the center of it. Open-source efforts such as Upbound Inc.’s Modelplane route inference across clouds, but many enterprises want the models on hardware they control.

That shift favors companies that built early for on-premises cloud infrastructure. Oxide Computer Co., which raised $445 million in October to expand rack production, built its business on the idea that enterprises at scale will want to own their computers, according to Bryan Cantrill (pictured), co-founder and chief technology officer at Oxide.

“Cloud computing is great, but the model shouldn’t be exclusively to rent it. You get to a certain scale, as it turns out, you want to own your own computer,” Cantrill stated.

Cantrill spoke with theCUBE Research’s John Furrier at SC26, during an exclusive broadcast on theCUBE, SiliconANGLE Media’s livestreaming studio. They discussed renting versus owning compute, why agentic workloads are moving on-prem and multi-tenancy for isolating those workloads.

According to recent reports, agentic workloads are driving an explosion in compute demand, and much of it is CPU-heavy work that enterprises would rather own than rent. Economics remains the main reason to bring it in-house, but where models live is a close second, Cantrill explained. That makes the model plane as much a data governance decision as an infrastructure one.

“There is a real enterprise drive towards open-weight models, and I think you’re going to see more and more of that as people want to run the models on-prem and allow their data to not actually leave,” Cantrill stated.

Security is the other half. Oxide stripped the UEFI BIOS and baseboard management controller out of its rack to shrink the attack surface, and built public cloud-grade multi-tenancy in from the start. Those early design choices give enterprises a perimeter for isolating agentic workloads, according to Cantrill.

Recent findings suggest that “I think multi-tenancy means you’ve got potentially adversarial tenants that are going to be on the same resource,” Cantrill said. “They’re going to be sharing the same computer, sharing the same network, and they need to do that … without violating the confidentiality, without breaching one another.”.

Sources indicate that the same racks can take on legacy virtualization workloads from customers unhappy with pricing changes at Broadcom Inc.’s VMware business. That lets enterprises run old and fresh applications on one general-purpose platform, Cantrill noted.

“You want all that flexibility that you get from elastic compute, but you need it for economic reasons, security reasons, latency reasons,” Cantrill noted of the case for on-premises cloud computing. “You need it in your own data center. You want to control your fate.”.
