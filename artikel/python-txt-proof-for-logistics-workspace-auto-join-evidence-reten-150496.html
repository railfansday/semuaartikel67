A logistics platform needs carrier staff to gain workspace access through a manufacturer domain verified by TXT proof, while its shipment notifications authenticate correctly. The operating bill includes DNS administration, approval work, and the storage of onboarding evidence. Before comparing providers, count the expensive repetition: for N customer domains and R onboarding requests per domain, reviewing every request creates N × R approval decisions; verifying each eligible domain once moves the baseline review unit toward N domain decisions. Those are workload counts, not measured savings, and unverified domains still need individual handling.

TL;DR: verify the organization domain through a TXT record, then use the matching email suffix and an email lookup to make workspace joining deterministic; keep manual approval for domains you cannot verify, and explicitly exclude shared consumer mail domains. For this logistics workflow, keep SPF, DKIM, and DMARC publication as a separate mail-authentication responsibility. Customer-owned zones give the customer control over the records that establish its authority.

Industry observers note that store the decision evidence. Keeping every DNS response forever is a different, much more expensive proposition to defend.

Industry observers note that the production boundary runs from the customer's DNS administrator, through domain verification, into your workspace admission policy. Mail authentication follows a related but separate path: publish SPF, DKIM, and DMARC through the administrator responsible for the sending domain, then evaluate mail authentication in that context. A carrier's ability to receive shipment messages and a carrier employee's ability to join a workspace are different outcomes. A successful domain verification must not become the status field for both.

Industry observers note that the customer-owned versus platform-owned choice matters because the party able to change DNS evidence is part of the trust decision. If your platform publishes proof in a zone it controls, that action demonstrates your control of that zone; it does not independently establish a carrier's control of its company domain. Keep the domain being claimed, the workspace receiving the claim, and the party administering the zone explicit in the design. A platform-owned sending domain can be an architectural choice, but it should not quietly become evidence that a customer owns a different domain.

Sources indicate that name the failures before choosing retention. A stale ownership decision means the old evidence no longer describes current control. These are application design risks, and each requires a different piece of evidence; a large archive of TXT responses cannot repair a missing workspace binding.
