Jonathan Nolan's Longest-Running Sci-Fi Shows Both Share The Same Weakness

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Like Christopher Nolan, Jonathan Nolan is a creative force to be reckoned with. Apart from writing some incredible movies, like Interstellar, The Dark Knight, The Prestige, and Memento, he has also created some iconic sci-fi highlights like Person of Interest and Westworld. His shows are no less brilliant than his movies, but they seem to suffer from one common problem.

Westworld, perhaps, needs no introduction. The dystopian sci-fi Western premiered on HBO in 2016 to record-breaking viewership. Apart from earning commercial success, the HBO sci-fi series also landed in critics' good books. Unfortunately, it gradually lost all steam before being canceled by HBO in 2022.

Although Westworld struggled to maintain the momentum it started with, the show is still talked about.

Person of Interest, on the other hand, landed on CBS in 2011. Unlike Westworld, Person of Interest never became a massive mainstream hit. However, over a decade after its release, the incredible sci-fi show still has a cult following. In hindsight, it seems evident that one change could have given Person of Interest and Westworld far better legacies.

Person of Interest is still considered to have one of the greatest finales in TV history. However, the series' ending could have been even better if its final season had not been cut short. As reports reveal, Person of Interest was initially supposed to have the standard 23 episodes in its last installment. CBS, however, decided to shorten it to 13 episodes.

As a result, Jonathan Nolan and Greg Plageman were forced to compress their planned storyline and rush through one of the most compelling arcs in the series.

While Person of Interest's finale still ends on an incredibly hard-hitting note, it struggles to resolve every underlying story beat. One more 13-episode season could have turned Person of Interest into a radically different and objectively better show.

Unlike Person of Interest, Westworld did not even get the chance to conclude its run. From the beginning itself, Jonathan Nolan and Lisa Joy had planned a five-season arc for the series. Owing to this, season 4 ended with a major development where Dolores (as Christina) had to run one more simulation after being uploaded to the Sublime.

According to recent reports, the HBO sci-fi show's season 5 was designed to resolve season 2's post-credits scene and reframe season 4's nihilistic ending. Unfortunately, HBO suddenly pulled the plug on the series because of its consistently declining viewership, preventing it from realizing its full vision. In hindsight, Westworld's legacy would have been far better if it had lasted for one more season.

In terms of tone, storytelling, and structure, Person of Interest and Westworld might seem like radically different shows. However, a closer look at both reveals some intriguing overlaps. For instance, both obsess over artificial intelligence and how algorithms can influence human behavior. They are also driven by the constant battle between free will and determinism.

According to recent reports, although comparing them seems a little unfair, Person of Interest has arguably aged better. In 2026, Person of Interest almost seems prophetic because it kept its sci-fi ideas grounded in realism and tethered solely in human emotion.

Industry observers note that while Westworld's puzzle-box cynicism is still appealing, the lack of a formal conclusion in the series significantly weighs it down. Jonathan Nolan's Person of Interest, in contrast, is still a hard-hitting and memorable drama because it counterweights its bleak procedural drama with warm character beats. Even its portrayal of AI feels more prescient and relatable in this day and age.

Industry observers note that speaking of AI, Person of Interest also presents a more nuanced portrayal where human characters attempt to give it an ethical framework before it is too late. Unlike Jonathan Nolan's Westworld, the sci-fi show almost feels like a documentary in 2026 because of how brilliantly it projects the near future.
