It has been revealed that when Anna felt low, she went shopping. Anna is a social worker I interviewed while researching financial well-being, and she told me about a particular kind of shop she was drawn to: soft fabrics, quiet music, a faint scent of something expensive. Her home and her workplace were busy and chaotic. In the shop, nothing bad could happen. Her biggest decision was which color coat to choose for spring.

The calm never lasted. It faded almost as soon as she left, and by the time the credit card bill arrived, shame and regret had taken its place.

According to recent reports, today is Black Friday, a day built on the promise that the right purchase will make us feel better. For many of us, as for Anna, the feeling we are trying to shop away is shame.

It is also a feeling we rarely admit to. In a 2025 Bankrate survey, 61% of U.S. adults noted they were uncomfortable discussing their bank balance. Fewer felt that way about their love life (51%), their political views (24%), or their religion (18%).

According to recent reports, psychologist Lisa Feldman Barrett (2017) argues that emotions are constructed by the brain rather than simply triggered. I find it helpful to think of the brain as a weather forecaster. Drawing on past experience, it constantly predicts what our bodily sensations mean, and it revises the forecast as fresh information comes in.

A knot in your stomach before a job interview could be hunger, dread, or excitement. Which one you feel depends partly on how your brain has learned to read situations like this one.

Recent findings suggest that many of us have years of money forecasts shaped by tension and secrecy at home. Opening a bill or splitting a restaurant check can call up an old forecast before we have even looked at the numbers. Often, that forecast is for shame.

Internal shame is the voice that indicates, “I’m bad with money.” It treats missing financial skills as a personal flaw, and a rare one. Everyone else seems to manage. If I struggle, it must say something about who I am.

Recent findings suggest that this belief often starts early. Many of the middle-aged adults I meet, particularly women, tell me nobody talked openly about money with them when they were growing up. They learned through costly mistakes, and each mistake seemed to confirm that something was wrong with them.

In a recent development, external shame is about how others see us. In 1776, Adam Smith pointed out that the ancient Greeks and Romans lived comfortably without linen, yet in his own day, a respectable laborer would be ashamed to appear in public without a linen shirt. What counts as a necessity depends partly on what we need to avoid being looked down on.

It has been revealed that swedish researcher Bengt Starrin and colleagues (2009) tested this idea in a survey of more than 5,600 adults. Both financial stress and experiences of being shamed predicted psychosocial ill-health, and people who reported both were the most affected. Having too little money is hard, and feeling diminished because of it adds to the harm.

Sources indicate that money shame is not limited to poverty. It can be strongest when, by all appearances, there should be enough.

Sources indicate that ann-Marie is a journalist with three books to her name and steady work for well-known magazines. She is a single mother of two, widowed when her daughters were small. She still pays a large mortgage on a country house she has been unable to sell, while renting in the city near her daughters’ music schools. Her income is reasonable, but she has no buffer. One unexpected bill sets her back, and she knows what it means to stretch her last ten euros across a week of groceries.

Industry observers note that she skips most social events because they cost money. She never mentions her worries to her daughters, which makes it hard to explain why they rarely travel or why her laptop comes along on weekends. To others, her life looks successful. She keeps the rest to herself.

Recent findings suggest that ann-Marie is far from alone. In the American Psychological Association’s Stress in America 2023 survey, 82% of adults aged 18 to 34 named money as a significant source of stress.

Sources indicate that shame is a social emotion. It probably evolved to keep us in good standing with our group, but when it takes hold, it does undeniable damage. A meta-analysis of 108 studies found that shame is more strongly linked to depressive symptoms than guilt is (Kim et al., 2011).

According to recent reports, with money, shame can also make the original problem worse. Joe Gladstone and colleagues (2021) studied this using bank records, longitudinal surveys, and comparisons of identical twins. Across six studies, people who felt financial shame were more likely to disengage from their finances. That disengagement led to poorer financial decisions, which deepened their hardship. The researchers call this a financial shame spiral, something many of us are familiar with in our daily lives.

Fear of judgment adds to the feeling of shame. In a study of middle-class consumers in debt, those who expected to be stigmatized hid their debt from friends and family, put off seeking help, and even spent more in social situations to keep up appearances (Moorhouse et al., 2023).

It’s a bit like a leak you are too embarrassed to report. The longer you hide it, the more damage it does to the house.

Sources indicate that the shame researcher Brené Brown (2007) describes empathy as the antidote to shame. Here are some ways to put that into practice with money.

In a recent development, start with understanding rather than resolutions. Vowing to take control and cut out every bad habit rarely lasts. It helps more to ask how you got here. What did you learn about money as a child? What might your spending or avoidance have been protecting you from?

In a recent development, name the feeling. When a money task makes you tense, try saying to yourself, “This is shame.” Brain-imaging research shows that putting feelings into words reduces activity in the amygdala, a region involved in emotional alarm (Lieberman et al., 2007). Naming the feeling may give your forecast a chance to enhancement.

It has been revealed that remember that financial skills are learned. If you never learned them, that reflects what you were taught, and says nothing about your character. In Gladstone’s research, an intervention built around affirming acts of kindness weakened the link between shame and disengagement.

Recent findings suggest that talk to someone. In a field experiment, people who took a financial course alongside others in the same situation paid off more debt than people in the control group. Among those most afraid of judgment, learning in a group also improved well-being more than learning privately did (Moorhouse et al., 2023). A friend, a partner, or a financial counselor can all be a starting point.

Industry observers note that for Anna, the work was learning to find calm without a upcoming purchase. Part of that was looking a little further ahead and asking what feeling each shopping trip would leave behind. That question is worth asking today, before checkout.
