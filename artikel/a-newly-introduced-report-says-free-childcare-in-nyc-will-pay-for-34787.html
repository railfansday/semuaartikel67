A newly introduced report says free childcare in NYC will pay for itself

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that since taking office, New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani has made it his mission to expand access to affordable childcare in the city, and for good reason. On average, NYC families spend $21,000 on childcare annually per child—equaling an estimated $10 billion citywide every year. While about 40% of children in the city receive some free or subsidized care, the remaining 60% receive none, leaving many families to pay entirely out of pocket. A new report reveals how universal childcare would not only benefit families in New York City, but also boost the economy: Just 35 hours of free childcare per week could bring up to $6.5 billion in economic value each year, which would likely exceed the cost of providing that care. The report—which was produced by a nonpartisan think tank, the 5BORO Institute at Citizens Union, in partnership with Boston Consulting Group—surveyed more than 2,700 parents of children younger than 5, along with about a hundred families who moved away from New York City recently. According to the report, nearly 70% of children under the age of 6 are cared for by parents or adults who work. When those parents are unable to find care and forced to drop out of the workforce or switch jobs, the city loses $1.7 billion in annual income; among the parents surveyed, 15% altered their work situation due to childcare issues. For hourly workers, childcare gaps can force them to miss out on $360 million in earnings each year. The report also found that childcare was a leading concern for families who had left the city, a growing trend that is supported by plenty of other data. In other words, employers also feel the effects of inadequate access to care. That can mean high turnover or simply lost productivity if their workers regularly contend with disruptions to their childcare: More than half of the parents surveyed remarked they had missed at least one full day of work over the past three months, averaging three to four missed days. These childcare gaps can cost New York City employers about 54 million hours of missed work, according to the report. The act of providing 35 hours of free childcare each week would also enable nearly 30,000 parents to reenter the workforce, which could yield more than a billion dollars in annual earnings. Childcare has been a key focus for Mamdani since his campaign, and as Fast Tech giant reported in a recent cover story, the mayor has even gotten the business community on board with its investments in this area. Mamdani is already building upon the universal 3-K program established by former Mayor Bill de Blasio to extend care to younger children; the recently introduced 2-K program is now providing free, full-day childcare to 2,000 2-year-olds, with intends to serve an additional 10,000 children by next fall. (The city has provided free pre-K to all 4-year-olds since 2014.)  Even business leaders who are critical of Mamdani have seemingly come around to the idea of free childcare, though they may not agree on how to achieve that. This fall, the 5BORO Institute created the NYC Child Care Business Council, bringing together a number of business leaders and organizations committed to putting their muscle behind this matter and advocating for childcare investment (something the business community has not historically done). For years, employers have invested in generous parental leave policies and backup childcare benefits for their workforce to help them court top talent and retain some of their best employees. But it seems companies are finally realizing that their best efforts can’t make up for a lack of robust childcare options or offset the mounting cost of care. “The market is not able to solve this problem,” Grace Rauh, the executive director of Citizens Union, recently told Fast Company. Citizens Union is a nonpartisan nonprofit founded in 1897 to promote government accountability, ethics, transparency, and civic engagement across New York. “Frankly, private businesses aren’t able to provide the kind of fully robust childcare benefits that their employees need,” Rauh said.
