📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that last week, Professor Mark W. Smith released his upcoming 85-minute film discussing the operations and mechanics of modern firearms, including semiautomatic rifles such as the popular AR-15 rifle. The film, titled "Firearms and the AR-15 Rifle: A Primer for the Bench and Bar," can be found on YouTube here and on Rumble. It has already been viewed over 23,000 times since its release only days ago. The film is broken down into 25 segments so a viewer can jump from topic to topic.

In a recent development, that federal judges are now reviewing Internet videos to learn about firearms is hardly surprising. A few weeks ago Judge Doris Pryor of the Seventh Circuit made reference to this during oral argument in a constitutional fight over the legality of Illinois's suppressor ban. In the consolidated cases of Anderson v. Raoul and Morse v. Raoul, argued October 28, 2026, she acknowledged that she has watched videos in preparing for oral argument. The oral argument audio recording is here with Judge Pryor's comment at the 25:04-25:23 mark.

It has been revealed that as I previously wrote, I had the pleasure of introducing the "AR-15 Primer" film. From there, Rick Vasquez (a former ATF firearm technician and U.S. Marine ordnance officer) takes the spotlight explaining the mechanical operation of today's AR-15 rifle and its typical features.

But there is another important aspect of the film that is worth noting. And that is the impressive animations found within the film that help educate legal professionals and the general public about the internal and related operations of the AR-15 (and most modern semi-automatic rifles).

The animations found throughout the film are informative for those learning about firearm mechanics. Here are a few of the noteworthy ones:.

There is an excellent 2-minute animation illustrating how gas-operated semiautomatic rifles internally deploy the rapidly-expanding gases from the firing of a shot to do two things: first, to send a projectile (the bullet) down the barrel and, second, to prepare the rifle to fire the next round of ammunition. This is called autoloading. This dual use of the gases from firing a single shot is generally not understood or appreciated by those latest to firearms technology, but the film's animation makes it clear. This animation also clarifies that autoloading does not mean automatic fire (like one sees with a M16 machine gun). The animation can be seen at the 14:58 to 17:58 mark of the film.

The film also does a nice job demonstrating the physical reality of recoil by illustrating how the features of an AR-15 like the straight line stock, the buffer spring/buffer tube, and the muzzle brake help to minimize recoil allowing for a more comfortable and effective shooting experience. The 28:09 to 28:22 mark has a nice illustration of the straight-line stock while the 28:28 to 28:59 mark illustrates the role of the buffer and buffer spring in minimizing uncomfortable recoil. The 27:59 to 28:07 mark shows an animation illustrating what constitutes recoil from the firing of a rifle.

According to recent reports, the film also effectively uses animation to illustrate the difference between scopes, red dot optics, and iron sights. A scope magnifies the target making it appear closer than it really is; a red dot optic helps the user aim the rifle using an electronic dot without magnification; and iron sights entail aligning a front post with a rear sight. The 1:00:53 to 1:01:18 mark illustrates these differences.

In a recent development, animation also helps viewers understand that the barrel of a semiautomatic rifle gets hot when the rifle is fired due to the internal gas explosion that results from firing a round. The film's animation shows the rifle barrel heating up and demonstrates the value of handguards (often called "barrel shrouds" in legislation) to protect the user of the rifle. The 46:24 to 46:33 mark illustrates a barrel heating up as the rifle is shot.

It has been revealed that the film's use of animations to explain the difference between a bullet and a cartridge is also worth mentioning. Lawyers (and even some judges) use those terms interchangeably, but they mean very different things. The 1:10:12 to 1:11:08 mark illustrates the four components (primer, gun power, case and bullet) of a modern ammunition cartridge.

It has been remarked that the late President Harry Truman embraced the thought, "I am from Missouri so you have to show me." Well, Professor Smith's fresh film on firearm mechanics works hard to "show" (through animations, illustrations and images) the internal mechanics and features of AR-15 semiautomatic rifles. And I suspect the bench and the bar will be the better for it.
