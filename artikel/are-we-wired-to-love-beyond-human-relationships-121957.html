Recent findings suggest that yesterday I came home from time away with my family—raucous, fun days that felt like summer camp, with lots of time spent in nature. As I was enjoying breakfast on my own—the crossword puzzle, toast, and my DD Refresher—my daughter burst onto the scene, full of energy and light, and interrupted my quiet morning with a loud question: “What happened to this poor plant?”.

I looked up. A small plant sat wilting in its pot, right in front of my eyes, right beside a pitcher of water. I had completely ignored the plant, and I immediately felt guilty. My daughter picked up the pitcher and gave the poor greenie a drink. Within 30 minutes, it had perked back up.

Recent findings suggest that what unsettled me was not just that I had forgotten to water a plant. It was that I had spent days enjoying nature and still failed to notice a living thing's need at my own breakfast table. My daughter saw what I had missed. Her attention made me question where I draw the edges of relationship.

I consider myself relational. I write and speak about the biological imperative to reach out to one another and connect—an impulse rooted in our DNA and physiology. Yet I see a tension between that need and the “power over” dynamics of many Western institutions: Those at the top define “normal behavior,” promote competition over cooperation, and urge people to “stand on their own two feet.” By power over, I mean a way of organizing relationships in which status gives some people authority to define the worth of others. Within such hierarchies, differences can become rankings, and subordinate groups can be treated as having less innate value than the dominant group. Fear can be used to sustain these arrangements, keeping people under chronic stress. In these systems, the fear generates prolonged sympathetic nervous system activation, which disintegrates the mind, body, and relationships.

Sources indicate that I see the same power over logic in the treatment of the natural world. When people are valued mainly for their usefulness to those with power, nature, too, can become an asset to exploit for individual needs. In both cases, relationship gives way to a question of what can be taken.

The plant's responsiveness left me with a question: What would happen if I brought the same passion I have for human connection to my relationships with other living beings? Could “wired for love” become an invitation to notice connection beyond the human world, even when love is not quite the right word?

What would happen if I began to think of plants and trees as having their own “love language”—a metaphor for their ways of connecting, rather than a claim that they love as humans do? My daughter assures me they do, and she has always been more aligned and in tune with nature than I have. Reading about these relationships has been eye-opening. Peter Wohlleben describes underground mycelial networks associated with tree roots as pathways of exchange and communication. In that account, the relationship works in both directions: Trees also provide nutrients to the fungi through their root systems.

EcoTree describes a mutual relationship between the trees and fungi as they exchange resources. Its account gives two ranges: 30 to 40 percent of the minerals captured by the mycelial network are returned to roots, while roots provide fungi with 20 to 40 percent of the carbohydrates produced through photosynthesis. The same account describes pioneer plants—plants that colonize virgin areas—as compensating for the absence of mycorrhizae by developing root structures that mimic mycelial filaments. For me, these details bring reciprocity into view: The relationship described is not simply one organism taking from another but rather a mutual exchange.

Sources indicate that the account also describes relationships that extend beyond nutrient exchange to defense. One example is a tree under insect attack releasing chemicals through its root system; other trees respond by releasing bitter tannins that predators dislike. A separate example concerns airborne compounds: in the 1920s, Russian biologist Tokin coined the term “phytoncides” to describe substances that travel through the air and kill microbes.

Industry observers note that what would it look like if, as a society, we embraced relationality with “all that is,” to use Connie Gunderson's phrase? For me, that would mean understanding ourselves not as separate from nature, but as part of it. It would also mean questioning the lines we draw between ourselves and others, including people of different racial and ethnic backgrounds. Connection need not erase difference; instead, it asks us not to mistake difference for separateness or lesser value.

Recent findings suggest that I strongly believe that widening our attention to include other living beings is a place to begin. My hope is that this practice can help us move away from the sympathetic nervous system dominance—and the fear and anger—that I associate with power over systems. It is not, by itself, a remedy for those systems. But my daughter offered a small example of another way to relate: Notice the need in front of you, and respond. That morning, connection began with a pitcher of water.
