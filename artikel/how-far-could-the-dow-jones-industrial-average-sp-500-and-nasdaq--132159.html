It has been revealed that although history conclusively shows that the stock market rises over the long term, getting from Point A to B on Wall Street is never a straight line. Over the past few weeks, the mature-stock-driven Dow Jones Industrial Average (^DJI +0.33%), benchmark S&P 500 (^GSPC -0.25%), and tech-inspired Nasdaq Composite (^IXIC -0.62%) have reminded investors that stocks can indeed move lower.

Recent findings suggest that as of the closing bell on Nov. 20, the Dow and S&P 500 had fallen into correction territory, with respective declines of 11.4% and 11.6% from their all-time closing highs. Meanwhile, the Nasdaq is knocking on the door of a bear market, with an 18.1% drop since its Oct. 5 record close.

It has been revealed that while there isn't a tool or metric on the planet that can ever predict, with guaranteed accuracy, when stock market downturns will begin, how long they'll last, and where the bottom will be, one time-tested valuation tool has a knack for forecasting substantial declines in Wall Street's major indexes. This tool, with nearly 156 years of backtested history in its sails, offers a clear picture of how far the Dow Jones Industrial Average, S&P 500, and Nasdaq Composite could plunge.

The S&P 500's Shiller Price-to-Earnings (P/E) Ratio, also known as the Cyclically Adjusted P/E Ratio (CAPE Ratio), is the valuation tool in question with an uncanny track record of accurately predicting the future.

Recent findings suggest that most investors rely on the traditional P/E ratio when swiftly evaluating stocks or the broader market. Though the P/E ratio works great for mature businesses, recessions that shift corporate profits into losses can make this traditional valuation tool useless.

By comparison, the Shiller P/E Ratio is based on average inflation-adjusted earnings over the trailing decade, meaning recessions won't adversely affect its readings.

In a recent development, when backtested to January 1871, the S&P 500's CAPE Ratio has averaged 17.45. On Nov. 20, it closed at more than double its long-term average (36.44). Over 156 years, the CAPE Ratio has exceeded 30 during a continuous bull market on six occasions, including the present, and the prior five were all eventually (keyword!) followed by declines in the Dow, S&P 500, and/or Nasdaq Composite ranging from 20% to 89%.

In other words, history shows that premium stock valuations aren't sustainable over the long run.

So, how far could the Dow, S&P 500, and Nasdaq fall? While the Shiller P/E Ratio can't pinpoint a figure, the previous five downturns all retraced to a minimum multiple of 27, with several bottoming out at a CAPE Ratio of 20 to 22.

Industry observers note that the tricky thing with the Shiller P/E Ratio is that earnings growth makes this valuation tool dynamic. For instance, simple math would say that for the Shiller P/E to return to 27 from its Nov. 20 close of 36.44, it needs to fall by 26%. But given the ongoing growth in underlying earnings for S&P 500 companies, we're likely looking at closer to a 20%-25% additional decline in the S&P 500.

Given that the Dow Jones Industrial Average is often less volatile than the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq Composite is typically far more volatile, history suggests the Dow would be expected to decline an additional 20%, with the Nasdaq likely staring down an additional drop of 30%, at minimum.
