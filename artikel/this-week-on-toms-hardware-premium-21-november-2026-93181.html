First up, our resident CPU boffin, Jake Roach, has been on the show floor all week, offering us a direct line to all the goings-on at the conference, including an interesting back-and-forth from Nvidia and AMD on their CPUs. AMD claims that its latest EPYC Venice lineup is the best at agentic workloads, while Nvidia's marketing material characterized Nvidia's Vera CPU as being purpose-built for Agentic AI. At SC26, Nvidia CPU chief Ian Finder rebuked Nvidia's own marketing material, stating that the idea that Nvidia could have predicted the Data Center moment was "absurd," instead saying that the firm's design know-how helped them develop the processor.

In this interview, we sit down with Finder to find out how Vera came to be, what agentic workloads mean for the CPU industry, and where Nvidia's headed next.

It has been revealed that nvidia's Vera CPU was a hot topic at SC26, as the organization explained that existing software cannot always take full advantage of the processor without architecture-specific optimization. The company indicated that under some workloads, Vera's performance can be optimized by up to 39% when taking its Olympus-based Arm cores into account. However, it remains to be seen how much performance can be gained from Olympus-specific hardware optimization workloads other than matrix multiplication.

It has been revealed that nvidia also announced a Rubin GPU variant, named Rubin HPC, a chip that the manufacturer claims is purpose-built for agentic workflows and scientific simulations that demand processing large datasets. While technical details remain scant, Nvidia notes that Rubin HPC sports over 50,000 CUDA cores, or slightly below double the number of cores present in the standard-issue Rubin GPU. We explored all the details and possibilities that the newly announced chip might offer.

AMD is now featured in over 40% of the fresh TOP500 list of supercomputers, with a 14% boost to the company's position. Leading the company is Lux, a supercomputer developed in conjunction with the U.S. Department of Energy. The second supercomputer, Discovery, is expected to debut in 2028, sporting a suite of EPYC CPUs and Instinct accelerators.

In a recent development, within the TOP500, not much has changed... at the top, with the top 10 systems remaining unchanged, as China's LineShine system in Shenzhen remains at the top of the list, with El Capitan remaining second. We take a look at the entire list and how the focus has seemingly shifted toward AI compute capacity. While the demand for HPC hardware is at an all-time high, it appears as though the vast majority of that hardware isn't being tested against the TOP500 benchmark.

Despite AI clusters seemingly eating up compute capacity, AMD recognizes that the burgeoning segment will never wholly replace HPC computing, and that high-precision workloads will continue to be necessary. AMD's Scott Tease further suggested that building a heterogeneous compute stack would be the best path forward for tackling complex research problems. This point dovetails nicely into how AMD additionally touted that its MI430X's native FP64 operations will beat out emulated support for the full-precision data types seen in Nvidia's hardware. This is notable, as the manufacturer claims that Ozaki FP64 emulation could have cascading effects on memory usage, which is in notorious levels of undersupply.

Not wanting to be left out of the conversation, Intel's next-generation Diamond Rapids CPUs surfaced at SC26, but not from Intel itself, curiously. On the ground, we saw the chips turn up at the CXL booth and at Bull, where the CPU was shown installed in a server. While there were no demos available, the firm did show off demos for Xeon 6+ and its Crescent Island GPU.

In a recent development, elsewhere at the show, PCI 6 devices and controllers have started to surface. First, Silicon Motion's SM8466 controller was demoed at the show, armed with PerformaShape 2, which increases the number of quality-of-service sets from 256 to 512, alongside additional boons.

Industry observers note that elsewhere, Phison detailed two upcoming enterprise SSDs: one that comes with a built-in NPU and 64GB of memory, and another that is set to merge pSLC cache, with high QLC capacity on a single controller. We explore what makes these devices notable and why, though the tech giant itself notes that the devices are very much still in development.

Industry observers note that VAST Data explained how petabyte-scale SSDs will outgrow the tried and true RAID parallel filesystems, according to a simulation the company ran on its own 900 PB system, which puts the odds of data loss at a staggering 1 in 11 over a decade. We explain all in detail about how it works.

In a recent development, as enterprise hardware and the titanic growth of AI superclusters push the limits of our existing material infrastructure, from interconnects to grid energy and raw materials, researchers from The Ohio State University discussed how high-performance networking could soon scale across entire regions. We explore how faster networking connections could spell a future where distributed computing systems communicate across vast distances.

While we have a few more articles waiting to be published from SC26, we've also been hard at work publishing stories from other corners of the industry. To read these, you'll need to first arm yourself with a Tom's Hardware Premium subscription.

Industry observers note that this week, Anthropic debuted on the NYSE, but the frontier AI lab's debut is also colored by the backdrop of what seems like an endless barrage of upcoming vulnerabilities, hacks, and AI-powered action by would-be malfeasants. The conversation around AI safety, sparked by OpenAI's HuggingFace incident, is also being used as a Trojan horse for safety legislation, and against this backdrop, we explore how the companies shaping AI safety might also be the ones warning against using competitors, like capable Chinese open-weight AI models.

In a recent development, anthropic has also been busy bringing up more compute resources, notably publishing how it managed to get its Claude Mythos model to optimize its software stack to work with AMD Instinct hardware in around three days, with little human oversight. What once was a relentless task that took significant man-hours, an AI can now help complete in mere days. Such a feat could endanger Nvidia's highly lucrative CUDA moat, as AI continues to transform the hardware landscape.

Intel's acquisition of Quantum computing tech giant Classiq is expected to be completed in the first half of 2027, and following it, Intel is poised to have a chance at becoming the first full-stack quantum computing vendor. The company also prepares to manufacture quantum processors on its 18A process, offer foundry services, and eventually sell quantum computers.

According to recent reports, an unmanned chip fab might be an unthinkable future today, but it's a future that chipmakers are already planning for. SK hynix, Samsung, and Micron, known rivals in the memory industry, have formed a body to standardize robotic charging, with the aim of having robots from different manufacturers share equipment and therefore cut the costs and complexity of deploying fleets of robotic workers.
