Tech Report: Tesla Is Not The Only Automaker To Join The Space Race

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, the Intertubes are practically bursting at the seam with news of another step towards the merger of Tesla and SpaceX, both of which are helmed by on-again, off-again Trump supporter Elon Musk. Buried beneath the avalanche of press is another automotive-aerospace mashup, that being Ford Motor Company’s newly unveiled investment in the US startup Revolution Space. Evidently the iconic automaker is particularly interested in Revolution’s “unique in-space propulsion technologies.” Hmmm…interesting!

Industry observers note that before you get too excited, Ford is not exactly taking over the space business, nor is it necessarily competing against SpaceX. Revolution specializes in spacecraft, not rocket rolls out. In addition, Ford is not merging itself with Revolution. On November 23, Ford revealed that it is taking up a minority stake in the startup for a yet-to-be-disclosed sum, through its Industrial Platform LLC subsidiary.

Alongside its newly minted minority ownership status, the automaker will apply its manufacturing experience to help kickstart Revolution’s spacecraft platform into volume production.

So, what does Ford have that Revolution wants? Apparently, lots. “With Ford’s manufacturing capabilities, the companies are partnering to industrialize Revolution Space’s next-generation spacecraft at scale, unlocking upcoming dynamic missions and proliferated constellations,” Ford explained in a press statement.

Ford’s Chief Strategy Officer, Marin Gjaja, also chipped in his two cents, emphasizing that “Ford’s strength is building complex products in large volumes, something today’s growing satellite market requires.”.

Industry observers note that gjaja also underscored Ford’s immediate interest in the matter, which is to diversify and harden itself against the shifting winds of the global auto market — presumably including the abrupt shift in federal energy policy here in the US, too. As cited by Ford, the “space economy” currently registers about $600 billion globally, which is peanuts compared to the $1.8 trillion anticipated by 2035.

“Increasingly scaled satellite manufacturing is one of the drivers of that growth,” Ford further explains, noting that the firm Future Market Insights estimates the global satellite manufacturing market in the range of $87–$114 billion by 2035, heads and shoulders above the current estimate of $22–$26 billion.

If all goes according to plan, Revolution Space will give the automaker a hand in government and commercial satellite applications. Ford lists communications, Earth observation, navigation, space domain awareness, satellite servicing, and scientific and deep-space missions among the areas of interest. One can  safely assume that military applications are in there somewhere, too.

Industry observers note that as for what Ford may expect in return in terms of technology transfer, that remains to be seen. Ford notes that its experience in the aerospace field includes building communications and weather satellites for NASA and the military here in the US, as well as NATO.

However, that work goes back 50-60 years ago. The satellites of today are a different creature. “Revolution Space delivers and operates advanced space systems with superior performance and propulsion technologies designed to achieve the precision control and sustained maneuverability to enhance existing and create latest, high-value, dynamic missions,” Ford notes.

In the November 24 announcement, Ford also notes that it has deployed its manufacturing experience to pivot into upcoming fields as called for, citing the bombers it built during World War II and, more recently, the ventilators and protective equipment it supplied during the outset of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Drones could be next on the menu. Coincidentally or not, on November 24, the Wall Street Journal reported that Ford is in the running to help supply drones to the Department of Defense, alongside General Motors among other stakeholders. If realized, the program would call for supplying actuators, engines, sensors, lidar and other elements to drone manufacturers.

“Defense Department leaders have spent months enlisting automakers and their well-established supply chains to help scale up production of everything from missiles to tactical trucks. Now, they want those companies to help ease shortages of unmanned systems, including surveillance and strike drones, that have become crucial in global conflicts,” WSJ elaborated.

Good luck with that. Don’t expect anything of consequence to materialize until Defense Secretary Pete Hesgeth leaves office, either voluntarily or by virtue of impeachment over the hash he has made of the Defense Department, including its equipment as well as its personnel.

Still, the WSJ report did yield an interesting nugget about Ford’s minority stake in Revolution Space. For those without a subscription, the Detroit Free Press kindly offers a summary citing Ford spokesperson David Tovar, who noted that Ford has “had discussions with the Pentagon” focusing on its recent investment in Revolution Space, as well as the manufacture of vehicles for the military.

Recent findings suggest that that’s a good question. The Revolution website is rather thin on the technology itself, though it does note that the board is chaired by former NASA administrator Daniel Goldin, with former NASA astronaut Paul W. Richards serving as Chief Engineer and Chief Strategy Officer.

According to recent reports, revolution does drop a few hints about its expectations for the manufacturing hookup with Ford. The company notes that in 2025 it was selected for the DARPA “LASSO” program, with DARPA being short for the Defense Advanced Research Agency.

DARPA describes LASSO as a program aimed at “significantly increasing the United States’ ability to conduct operations in support of space situational awareness by developing both autonomous satellite navigation in stressing orbital conditions and increasing satellite maneuverability through continuous dynamic repositioning based on changing conditions, with the added benefit of creating a proven reserve model for resources relevant to ISRU on Earth’s Moon,” with ISRU being short for in-situ resource utilization.

It has been revealed that if you guessed water on the moon is the aim, run right out and buy yourself a cigar. “The program seeks performers to design, test, build, and deliver a spacecraft capable of determining all areas on the Moon that contain greater than 5% water concentration,” DARPA explains.

The LASSO selection comes on the heels of a flurry of activity in 2024, when Revolution reported that it was awarded a slot in the “Advanced Capabilities & Demonstration Missions” program of the Defense Innovation Unit. DIU also deployed its fast-track authority under the “Other Transactions” program to tap Revolution for an award in support of the company’s responsive modular electrospray thrust technology.

In 2024 Revolution Space also reported that it earned an award from the Defense Department’s “Accelerate the Procurement and Fielding of Innovative Technologies” program, and it nailed down an agreement with NASA to collaborate on space technology.
