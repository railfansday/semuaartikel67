November 20: The Moon passes Saturn and Neptune.

If you’ve got a viewing site with a clear eastern horizon, this morning offers a great opportunity to spot the solar system’s innermost planet. Look for Mercury about an hour before sunrise, when it reaches 7° high and shines at magnitude –0.5. The planet reached its greatest western elongation from our Sun yesterday evening, and is now 20° west of our star in the sky.

It has been revealed that located in Libra, the tiny world sits to the far lower left of bright Venus, higher in the sky. Venus is in the constellation Virgo, standing just north (to the upper left) of the bright star Spica. Another bright star, Arcturus in Boötes, hangs to that pair’s far upper left.

Industry observers note that pull out a telescope to check out the size and phase of each planet. Start with Venus, whose disk spans a hefty 46” — at the moment larger than Jupiter’s apparent size in our sky. Despite its impressive width, however, the disk of Venus appears as just a sliver, only 19% lit. Slide down to Mercury for comparison, and see that its phase is much larger — 62% — while the planet’s overall apparent width is much smaller, just 7”.

If you’re able, keep watching Mercury day by day in the morning sky. Its gibbous phase will continue growing, reaching 84% lit by the end of the month.
