Following vulnerable recruits through six weeks on a Myanmar People's Defense Force bootcamp, this year's IDFA opener is an accomplished debut for Canadian filmmakers Robert Mentov, Dwight Jantzi and Bryan Dickie.

A nasal R&B jam by Justin Bieber isn’t everyone’s idea of comfort listening, least of all played on a crackly phone speaker. But when the singer’s 2020 hit “Intentions” plays midway through “White Tiger Column,” briefly bonding two teen female soldiers in a moment of bubbly release and actual acting-their-age joy, it’s like a shot of oxygen to Robert Mentov, Dwight Jantzi and Bryan Dickie‘s subtly upsetting documentary. Later on, Taylor Swift’s “You Belong With Me” likewise sticks out like a sweet, sore thumb in the film’s otherwise severe milieu: a remote Burmese jungle camp where GenZ volunteer recruits in the Myanmar People’s Defense Force are put through their paces, in the hope of standing up to the country’s military junta. It’s an unduly weighty calling at such a tender age; youth must occasionally, giddily assert itself. Recent findings suggest that the opening film of this year’s IDFA documentary festival, “White Tiger Column” takes on a large, raw subject: the fostering of a resistance movement and mentality, from the ground up, in the wake of the 2021 coup that saw Myanmar’s latest, democratically elected government overthrown by the country’s army. But it does so with delicate humanity, its focus less on collective rhetoric and conflict than on the many individual fragilities and growing pains within an improvised, makeshift corps. Spanning six weeks of basic training, the film doesn’t limn a neat coming-of-age arc: Nobody enters the camp a boy and leaves a man, but this underdog army works with the inexperienced rebels they have.

Industry observers note that A directorial collaboration between Dickie — a Canadian photographer specializing in sites of sociopolitical upheaval — and compatriots Jantzi and Mentov, founders of the hitherto more fiction-oriented Nocte Films studio, “White Tiger Column” takes an approach between the journalistic and the more ambiently observational. There are no talking-head interviews or information beyond some scene-setting title cards, but a patient sense of human interest prevails, as the filmmakers embed themselves in the camp for a full course of training, observing its process without interference. The human subjects are not named, but personalities and personal crises emerge from a rigid, would-be military routine.

In some senses, “White Tiger Column” follows a structure familiar from many a basic-training drama, as unformed young people reckon with the newly introduced strictures and challenges of army life. But the stakes here are very different, not least because everyone present is there by dint of their own urgent belief — having fled to the jungle to escape the reach of the country’s actual military overlords. “To fight the government, we must become soldiers ourselves,” they are reminded by their commanders, who have little more authority or experience than the recruits they’re leading. And the goal here is not mere national service or self-improvement: “I don’t want to just train, I want to fight,” notes one newcomer, speaking for many of his peers who can’t wait to get their hands on a weapon.

According to recent reports, in other respects, however, they’re just kids, and their vulnerability and naïveté are all the more exposed by these extreme circumstances. “I miss my bed, my fridge, my TV, my mom and dad,” indicates one, sounding less like a soldier than a homesick child at a summer camp — which the boot camp quite frequently resembles, with its tranquil setting, team-building, fireside bonding and weekly calls home. Less so the lessons in homemade explosives and rite-of-passage tattooing: adult reminders of the grim responsibilities and dangers that await them after a too-brief education in the ways of war. Upon completing their training, an instructor tells them: “From now on, we are comrades, though you may call me teacher if you wish.” In the resistance, ultimately, everyone’s just figuring it out.

It has been revealed that it’s tough-minded but not without stray moments of tenderness and joy — particularly when focused on the friendship between two intrepid female recruits, a conspicuous minority in the collective — or of lush cinematic beauty, as Mentov, also the film’s accomplished DP, can hardly fail to capture the verdant, shadow-streaked riches of the environment around them. But the filmmakers aren’t inclined to romanticize matters, either in terms of character-based narratives or ideological emphasis: In this simultaneously stirring and frightening portrait, we know exactly what everyone’s fighting for, but as one near-child soldier admits, “we only have theories about what might happen.”.
