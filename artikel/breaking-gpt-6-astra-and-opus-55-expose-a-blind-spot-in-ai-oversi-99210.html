📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a recent development, in METR’s test of AI oversight, Opus 5.5 gave GPT-6 Astra’s proposed AI tests higher scores than the human evaluator did. METR said the gap could change whether its scoring rules recommended pursuing a test. In a separate exercise, leading AI agents placed ahead of most human entrants across 15 past programming contests.

In a recent development, across Al Zimmermann’s programming contests, METR reported median placements from the 96th to the 98th percentile for Fable 5.1, Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra. About four people still beat the strongest models in the median contest.

The agents had 48 hours per contest on a 28-core machine. Each submission gave them a numerical score they could use to see whether their changes improved the result. Human contestants typically had about three calendar months, used their own hardware and could see the public standings. METR compared the agents’ scores with the original contest results.

Sources indicate that in a separate exercise, METR asked agents to design tests that would help researchers assess what AI models could do. AI judges and a human scorer had access to project files, scores from trial runs and the agents’ case for why their tests were relevant and feasible. The human scorer assessed whether each proposed test was worth including in METR’s suite. METR mentioned none of the designs was useful enough to include in its report.

The AI judges tended to agree more closely with the human scorer when a weaker model had designed the test. When the model that designed the test was as capable as the judge or stronger, the judges tended to rate the work more highly than the human did. Opus 5.5 agreed most closely with the human scorer, yet rated GPT-6 Astra’s proposals about 0.2 points higher on average on a scale from zero to one.

Sources indicate that the comparison came from one exercise, and what counted as useful depended in part on METR’s research priorities. METR said models underused some resources in the judgment tests. It also reported strong model performance on some longer machine-learning tasks with automatic scoring. A separate AI peer review audit tested whether reviewers’ objections changed the findings by running code on the underlying data.

METR replayed past coding contests and separately asked models to grade proposed AI evaluations.

In the judging exercise, a rating gap this large could change whether METR’s rubric called for more work on a proposed test.

It has been revealed that contest time and hardware differed between agents and humans. The judging gap reflects one exercise and METR’s own usefulness criteria.
