Sources indicate that architectural drawings rely on measurements and spatial relationships, yet our memories of places rarely follow the logic of a floor strategy. We remember routes, sounds, thresholds, and landmarks, often without knowing their exact positions or dimensions. Reconstructing a place from these fragments requires interpreting what people remember, what they have forgotten, and where their accounts disagree. The resulting representation carries both spatial information and the uncertainties of its sources.

Reconstructing Architecture Through Sound.

Industry observers note that for years, independent investigators could study Saydnaya Prison from the outside, including through satellite imagery, but had no access to its interior. Inside the military prison north of Damascus, much of what could be reconstructed depended on the memories of people who had been held there.

Industry observers note that for years, independent investigators could study Saydnaya Prison from the outside, including through satellite imagery, but had no access to its interior. Inside the military prison north of Damascus, much of what could be reconstructed depended on the memories of people who had been held there.

In 2016, Forensic Architecture worked with Amnesty International to reconstruct the prison from the memories of five former detainees interviewed in Istanbul. Starting with a rough model based on satellite images and earlier written testimony, researchers developed the interior as survivors described cells, stairwells, and doors. The witnesses explored the rooms at eye level, corrected their proportions, and placed objects where they remembered them. Moving through the model sometimes brought back details they had never mentioned.

Sources indicate that sound provided another way to reconstruct the interior. Detainees were frequently blindfolded or kept in darkness, and speaking was prohibited. They became attentive to footsteps, doors, and noises traveling through water pipes and ventilation ducts. Audio investigator Lawrence Abu Hamdan gathered what the team called "ear-witness testimony," using simulated echoes and reverberations to help survivors recall the spaces they had occupied. Mapping the Divided Memories of a City.

At Saydnaya, different recollections helped reconstruct the same building. In Famagusta, Cyprus, entire communities had developed different understandings of the same city.

At Saydnaya, different recollections helped reconstruct the same building. In Famagusta, Cyprus, entire communities had developed different understandings of the same city.

It has been revealed that the 1974 conflict displaced much of Famagusta's Greek Cypriot population and left Varosha, a coastal district, under Turkish military control. Former residents retained memories of neighborhoods they could no longer enter, while those who remained or arrived later experienced a city shaped by territorial divisions. Both groups knew the city, but their memories described different urban landscapes.

Industry observers note that led by architect and urbanist Socrates Stratis, Hands-on Famagusta explored these divided perceptions through what the team described as split mental maps. The project developed a series of counter-mapping tools, including large isometric drawings, an interactive digital platform, and a transportable physical model. These representations were used in workshops, public presentations, and discussions involving participants from across the Cypriot divide.

Tracing Disappeared Architecture at Full Scale.

Barcelona presents a different condition. On the site of the city's former municipal courts, historical records preserve the outlines of buildings that have disappeared, even as their dimensions are no longer visible on the ground.

Barcelona presents a different condition. On the site of the city's former municipal courts, historical records preserve the outlines of buildings that have disappeared, even as their dimensions are no longer visible on the ground.

It has been revealed that for the 2022 Model Barcelona Architectures Festival, FLEXOARQUITECTURA developed Memoria Cronotópica, an installation that transferred historical architectural drawings directly onto the site at a scale of 1:1. Using different colors to distinguish periods, the architects traced the footprints of former structures across the ground, allowing visitors to encounter their original dimensions through movement.

Recent findings suggest that for the 2022 Model Barcelona Architectures Festival, FLEXOARQUITECTURA developed Memoria Cronotópica, an installation that transferred historical architectural drawings directly onto the site at a scale of 1:1. Using different colors to distinguish periods, the architects traced the footprints of former structures across the ground, allowing visitors to encounter their original dimensions through movement.

The drawings brought together several centuries of occupation. Beneath the former courthouse lay a medieval necropolis with sixteen burials dating from the 7th to 9th centuries. The site had also accommodated the Fusina Gardens and the Palau de Belles Arts, an exhibition building constructed for the 1888 Universal Exposition. By superimposing the outlines of these different occupations onto the same surface, the installation made their dimensions and spatial relationships visible simultaneously. Visitors could move between the footprints of structures that had occupied the site at different moments, comparing their scales and positions against the surrounding urban fabric.

The historical drawings, however, recorded only certain moments of the site's development. After the courthouse was demolished, the vacant plot accumulated demolition aggregate, while plants and animals began to occupy the space. By placing historical outlines over these emerging conditions, FLEXOARQUITECTURA also raised questions about the site's future: whether former buildings could have been adapted, their materials reused, or temporary uses introduced alongside the ecosystem that had begun to develop.
