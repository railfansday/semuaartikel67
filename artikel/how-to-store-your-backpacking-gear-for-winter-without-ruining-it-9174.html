How To Store Your Backpacking Gear for Winter Without Ruining It

Winter doesn’t have to mean the end of your backpacking season, but many hikers do feel the need for a little break once temperatures dip below freezing, snow starts falling, and conditions become a bit more dangerous. If that’s you, make sure you store your gear properly for the winter, or you might get an unpleasant surprise next spring when it’s time to pack for your first hike of the year.

Sometimes, we don’t know that our last trip of the season will be the last trip when we get home from it; I’ve found myself putting off unpacking from a trip only to realize in the springtime that my gear has sat abandoned and gross all winter long in my basement. Let this article serve as your reminder: taking care of your gear is one of the easiest ways to extend its lifespan and one of the most underrated ways to keep your budget in check during the hiking season.

If your gear, like mine, is right now caked in mud and stashed in your gear room, spend some time this week getting things in order to wait out a winter.

If nothing else, take five seconds to pull your sleeping bag or quilt out from a compression sack.

Down insulation is an absolute marvel. It has an incredible warmth-to-weight ratio but, if you leave it squished, the clusters of down feathers will lose their loft and their ability to trap warm air. Instead, shake out your bag in a dry room, which will also help any lingering moisture or body oil to escape.

In a recent development, your sleeping bag or quilt only works well if the down inside stays lofted. Photo via Katie Jackson.

According to recent reports, if it smells like traditional hiker stink, you can choose to give it a gentle wash in a front-loading washing machine using a technical soap like Nikwax Down Wash Direct. Follow this up with a long stint in a dryer on low heat with a few clean tennis balls to help break up and redistribute the clumps of down.

Once thoroughly dry, store it in a loose, large bag or hang it in a closet where the down can expand and breathe freely.

According to recent reports, your inflatable sleeping pad requires some attention before going into hibernation, too. Storing an inflatable pad with the valve tightly closed traps moisture and breath condensation inside the chambers, which creates a breeding ground for mold and mildew.

Each time you breathe into your pad to inflate it, you’re also trapping a bit of moisture in there. Photo via Katie Jackson.

Industry observers note that inflate the pad fully, wipe down any dust or salt from your sweat with a damp cloth, and inspect it for slow leaks so you are not surprised when you pull it out next spring. Once it is completely clean and dry, store it loosely rolled or laid flat with the valve left wide open so air can circulate freely and prevent the internal adhesive from breaking down prematurely.

Sources indicate that never pack a tent away even slightly damp, or you will pull it out in March to find ugly black mildew spots eating through the fly and a stubborn, musty odor that is nearly impossible to remove. Trust me, I speak from experience.

Industry observers note that tents are far too expensive to buy a new one each year. Photo via Katie Jackson.

According to recent reports, pitch the tent in your living room or backyard, sweep out all the accumulated dirt and dead bugs, and wipe down the floor and mesh with a damp rag. Store the tent body and fly loosely folded or stuffed inside a large breathable pillowcase rather than its tight stuff sack, and leave the tent poles collapsed without tension so the internal shock cords do not lose their elasticity.

You’re almost done, I promise. From your pack, shake out all the crumbs and dirt from the main compartment, empty every pocket, and scrub away mud spots with a toothbrush and soap.

Industry observers note that of everything in this article, your pack is among the easiest pieces of equipment to winterize. Photo via Katie Jackson.

In a recent development, let the entire harness dry completely in a well-ventilated room so mildew does not take root in the padded hip belts or shoulder straps. Once it is completely dry, store your pack hanging up or sitting loosely in a closet, making sure not to cram it into a suffocating space that will permanently warp the frame sheet or load lifters.

Sources indicate that electronics need a little preventative maintenance to survive the winter without leaking battery acid or losing their charge capacity. Pop open the battery compartments on your headlamp, satellite messenger, and portable power banks to remove alkaline or rechargeable lithium-ion cells if you proposal to leave them sitting for months without use.

Even rechargeable batteries can degrade over time, especially without proper care. Photo via Katie Jackson.

Recent findings suggest that store your power banks at roughly a fifty percent charge rather than completely empty or maxed out at 100 percent, which helps preserve long-term battery health over the cold months. Keep these electronics in a cool, dry place inside your house rather than a freezing garage where extreme temperature swings can ruin the battery.

In a recent development, finally, we look at your kitchenware and footwear. For your hiking boots and trail runners, clean off the caked-on dirt with a stiff brush. Your shoes probably still have a decent chance of getting worn during the winter months, so there’s no need to completely pack these away unless you want to.

Recent findings suggest that be so honest with yourself: how many true, soapy washes did your dirty water bag get this season? It may be overdue … Photo via Katie Jackson.

According to recent reports, for your kitchen and water setup, empty your water bladders and bottles completely, wash them with warm soapy water, and prop the reservoirs wide open so air circulates inside. Most importantly, remember that hollow-fiber filters like the Sawyer Squeeze must never freeze. If residual water inside the fibers turns to ice, it will instantly expand and crack the internal membrane, making the filter completely useless. Be sure your filters are thoroughly dry, sanitize them according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and store them indoors where they will never face sub-freezing temperatures.

Hollow fiber filters that have been stored for winter sometimes lock up, so make sure you test yours before taking it on another trip in the spring; you may need to soak the filter in distilled water overnight, or even for multiple days, to rewet the fibers and get the filter flowing again.

This all may feel like a tedious chore, but being kind to your gear now is how you make sure your gear is kind to you in 2027. Of course, if you truly don’t want to pack up your gear for the season, check out our list of 11 snow-free trips you could take this winter instead!
