Recent findings suggest that after nearly 10 years in business, fashion designer Danielle Frankel Hirsch has opened a 12,000-square-foot location in Tribeca with interiors set to change the bridal game. To create the new space, which opened in early November, Frankel Hirsch worked with AD PRO Directory designer Augusta Hoffman and Studio Partners Architecture.

The nearly two-year design process is complete, and the Danielle Frankel bridal salon is now ready for appointments. “We’re finally at the point where my heart and my head have been,” says Frankel Hirsch, who worked with Hoffman on her previous Los Angeles boutique and Fresh York City atelier. “Because this is our third space together, we can almost speak in shorthand now.”.

Industry observers note that frankel Hirsch on the staircase wearing a shirt she designed from Fortuny fabric. Hoffman used the same Fortuny print throughout the space for wrapping side tables, keepsake boxes on the shelves, as well as for the woven seats on barstools.

The unassuming entrance to Danielle Frankel on Hudson Street.

In the reception room just inside the street entrance, the desk is custom, the chairs are antique, and the custom pendant is by Jamb.

Hoffman worked with the artist Miriam Ellner to create eglomise paneling surrounding the staircase. “The eglomise is one of my favorite things because when we were working with the artist we worked so intimately with her,” Frankel Hirsch notes. “We worked on the placement the way that we do our laces. We kept going back and forth with the drawings because I wanted it to feel like our lace placement.”.

Recent findings suggest that on a recent morning, a bride and her wedding party were busy in the VIP suite making a final selection. The beaming group entered the space through an unassuming brown storefront entrance on Harrison Street, its two large glass windows encased in a sheer linen. From there they ascended a staircase up to a large gallery with over 30-foot floor-to-ceiling windows overlooking the streets below on two sides, and a hand-painted mural pattern above on the ceiling.

The interiors of the original 19th-century Queen Anne–style red brick building were transformed by Hoffman (full disclosure: I am writing Hoffman’s design monograph publishing with Rizzoli in Fall 2027) into a modern, elegant bridal boutique that honors the history of the original New York Mercantile Exchange, first established in the 1880s to keep the standards of the grocer business. “We wanted the space to stay pretty bright and focus on the architecture, but we needed a lot of seating,” Hoffman says. “There was a lot of pressure because we wanted it to feel like Danielle and we wanted to leave our mark on the space, but it’s also so beautiful as is and we didn’t want to have too many interventions that take away from that.”.

The rug, vintage Dutch leather armchairs, and custom clothing racks create one of the four seated zones in the gallery area. The wall features antique mottled glass windows. “We wanted this wall to feel historically accurate,” Hoffman notes.

The custom oak screen features shelves for displaying miniature gowns by Danielle Frankel. The shelves are lined in silver leaf. The antique stool is reupholstered in a Fortuny fabric.

In a recent development, the view of the gallery area from a second floor bridal suite. The vintage teak seating set is reupholstered in a fabric by Manuel Canovas and the glass cocktail tables and Fortuny wrapped side tables are custom. The custom window panels are by Augusta Hoffman Studio for Wovn Home and the custom curtain hardware are bronze leaves designed by Hoffman. Hoffman pulled the leaf design from the details on the columns.

What previously functioned as a large, open space with a small mezzanine, had little existing infrastructure–no electrical, HVAC, or plumbing—and is now an area where brides can plot as they make the most important decision for their wedding day. In the gallery, Hoffman created distinct zones where wedding parties wait before their appointment in a private fitting room.

Much of the furniture was sourced by Hoffman and Frankel Hirsch together on trips to Paris. Antique glass vitrines separate the space, which they included from the previous New York gallery and filled with Frankel Hirsch’s own family wedding photos as well as printed details brides can use for their fitting. One vitrine has a board layered with references of crinoline and interlining. Another has custom Danielle Frankel cufflinks. An oak folding screen lines one half of the room, with miniature gowns set on shelves lined in silver leaf paper. Custom parchment and cerused oak racks Hoffman designed to withstand the weight of a wedding dress line the floor displaying roughly 65 dresses from Danielle Frankel collections.

The reflection of the wall is seen in the mirrored structure designed to house the elevator bank and stairwell to the street entrance. A framed antique floral plaster relief is flanked with 1930s Italian wall sconces in patinated glass and metal.

Industry observers note that the staircase and elevator bank within the mirrored structure. “We had to add the elevator for function and for code, and we were like ‘How do we take this existing architecture and put an elevator in the middle of the room?’” Hoffman claims. “It feels super unexpected, versus trying to hide it, and you get to see more of the surrounding architecture and light in the reflection.”.

The custom rug is topped with an antique stool upholstered in a Fortuny fabric, a 1950s chair, and a 19th-century English pine console table.

The view from the sitting area adjacent to a bar. The vintage teak seating set was among the first items Hoffman and Frankel Hirsch sourced for the space. The custom bar stool is woven with a Fortuny fabric.

It has been revealed that hoffman and Studio Partners Architects took what was formerly the mezzanine, originally separated from the below with only a single-bar metal railing, and made it into a full wall with antiqued mottled glass windows. In the center they constructed a cascading grand staircase, cleverly concealing the stockroom underneath, which leads upstairs to three private bridal suites. The hallways, painted in Wenge by Benjamin Moore, lead to the bridal suites, each designed differently and filled with antique and vintage furniture, custom carpets, lighting, and racks. The walls are painted in Presidente Roman Clay by Portola Paints, an earthy contrast to the white and ivory dresses, which are naturally the center of attention for visitors in each room.

When guests are finished in their suites, there’s a bar in the gallery space where wedding parties can sit and make the final selection. The wedding party that morning had selected the dress and were toasting champagne to celebrate. “It’s just as much about the hospitality and the warmth as it is about the gowns,” Frankel Hirsch says.

In the VIP bridal suite, which can be accessed through a discreet entrance on Harrison Street, the custom sofa is upholstered in Holland & Sherry’s Corbel in Henna. The artwork features fragments of 19th century Japanese brocade.
