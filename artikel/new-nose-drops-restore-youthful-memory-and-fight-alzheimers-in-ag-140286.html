New nose drops restore youthful memory and fight Alzheimer’s in aging mice

🌐 Source: Sciencedaily • 📅 24 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 8 min read (1695 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Researchers at the University of Sydney have created experimental nose drops that significantly improved learning and memory in older mice, bringing their performance back to levels seen in younger animals. The treatment also prevented severe memory decline in mice genetically engineered to develop Alzheimer's-like disease. Remarkably, the approach relies on mRNA technology similar to that used in COVID-19 vaccines.

The discovery, led by Professor Greg Neely at the University of Sydney's Charles Perkins Centre, could open a upcoming path toward treating conditions that affect the brain. Potential applications extend beyond Alzheimer's disease to other neurodegenerative disorders, stroke, traumatic brain injury, and rare genetic and developmental conditions.

"If we can proceed swiftly to clinical trials in humans, this approach could represent a world-first non-invasive delivery system to treat Alzheimer's that bypasses the blood-brain barrier," Professor Neely said.

Industry observers note that the idea behind the research has an unusual origin. The team drew inspiration from Daniel Keyes' science fiction story Flowers for Algernon, which explores the limits of human intelligence through experiments designed to increase intelligence in laboratory mice and people.

How mRNA Nose Drops Restore Youthful Memory.

Industry observers note that the treatment works by delivering messenger RNA (mRNA), a type of genetic instruction that tells cells how to produce specific proteins. These instructions are enclosed in tiny fat-based particles called lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), which protect the mRNA and help it enter cells.

Once delivered through the nose, the mRNA instructs cells to manufacture therapeutic proteins capable of reaching the brain. In effect, the nasal tissue becomes a small drug-producing factory, creating substances that can support brain function.

"In aged mice, we saw learning and memory significantly increased, returning animal cognition to its youthful prime," Professor Neely mentioned.

"What's exciting is that this was achieved with a simple non-invasive nasal delivery system that builds on the same underlying mRNA-LNP technology platform many people became familiar with during the COVID-19 pandemic.".

Industry observers note that the study, published in Advanced Science, used mRNA to stimulate production of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF). This naturally occurring protein helps neurons survive, form connections, and adapt as the brain learns and stores memories. Levels of BDNF tend to decline with aging and are also associated with Alzheimer's disease.

According to recent reports, the results were striking. Older mice that received the nose drops performed about as well as young mice in standardized tests of working memory, spatial memory, and object recognition. These tests measure abilities such as remembering recently encountered information, navigating surroundings, and recognizing familiar objects. Untreated older animals highlighted the expected age-related deterioration in cognitive performance.

Nose Drops Prevent Alzheimer's-Like Memory Decline in Mice.

Recent findings suggest that to investigate whether the treatment might also protect against Alzheimer's disease, researchers turned to a widely used laboratory model known as 5XFAD mice. These animals carry genetic mutations that cause them to develop Alzheimer's-like brain changes and memory problems unusually early in life.

In this model, signs of Alzheimer's-related brain pathology can typically be detected by two months of age, with measurable cognitive decline appearing around four months.

Industry observers note that the researchers began administering the BDNF-producing mRNA nose drops when the mice were two months old, around the time the disease-related changes first appeared. Treatment continued twice a week.

In a recent development, by four months of age, the treated mice demonstrated no further cognitive decline, unlike the severe deterioration normally seen in this model. The findings suggest that supporting brain cells early in the disease process may help preserve cognitive abilities.

Industry observers note that one of the most intriguing results was what the treatment did not do. Although memory and learning were protected, the researchers found no reduction in amyloid plaques, the abnormal protein deposits that accumulate in the brains of people with Alzheimer's disease.

That distinction could be notable because many existing Alzheimer's treatments aim to remove amyloid. The fresh approach instead appears to help neurons remain healthy and functional despite the presence of these deposits. If the same effect can eventually be demonstrated in people, the two treatment strategies might be used together.

Recent findings suggest that "Current Alzheimer's treatments largely focus on clearing these amyloid plaques," Professor Neely said. "However, just under a third of Alzheimer's patients have serious plaques but do not suffer from dementia, and these patients show naturally higher BDNF levels, suggesting if we raise BDNF, it's possible to live with Alzheimer's disease pathology.".

"Our approach seems to work not by clearing plaques but by supporting the health, survival and connectivity of neurons themselves. It raises the possibility these therapies could eventually work alongside each other.".

Bypassing the Blood-Brain Barrier Through the Nose.

Industry observers note that getting medicines into the brain is one of the biggest obstacles in developing treatments for neurological diseases. The blood-brain barrier acts as a protective filter, preventing many potentially harmful substances in the bloodstream from entering delicate brain tissue. Unfortunately, it also blocks numerous drugs that scientists would like to use to treat brain disorders.

The nose offers a possible alternative route. Connections between the nasal cavity and the central nervous system may allow certain therapeutic substances to reach the brain without requiring surgery or injections directly into the brain or spinal cord.

"This is a very exciting proof of concept for a method to deliver mRNA-encoded cargo to the nervous system directly through the nose," Professor Neely mentioned.

According to recent reports, the team adapted mRNA technology built on the same clinically validated BioNTech platform used for COVID-19 vaccination. Instead of delivering instructions that prompt an immune response, the modified system provides instructions for producing proteins intended to benefit the brain.

First author Dr. Maria Bergamasco, from the University of Sydney's School of Life and Environmental Sciences, explained that the findings illustrate how mRNA technology could be used for purposes far beyond preventing infectious diseases.

Recent findings suggest that "The success of mRNA vaccines revealed these systems can safely deliver genetic instructions to cells," Dr. Bergamasco mentioned.

"We wanted to see whether we could adapt that technology to deliver beneficial proteins to the brain in a completely non-invasive way.".

Treatment Stimulates New Brain Cell Growth.

Sources indicate that the researchers also detected increased neurogenesis, the process through which latest neurons are formed, in the hippocampus. This part of the brain plays an essential role in learning and memory and is particularly vulnerable to damage associated with Alzheimer's disease.

Sources indicate that the increase in latest neurons provides another possible explanation for the improvements in cognitive performance. By promoting the health and adaptability of brain cells, BDNF may help maintain the neural networks needed for learning and remembering information.

It has been revealed that the nose drops also appeared to be well tolerated. Researchers reported no major side effects in the mice during the study, although further testing will be needed to establish long-term safety.

Could mRNA Nose Drops Work in Humans?

Despite the encouraging results, Professor Neely emphasized that the treatment remains experimental. So far, its effectiveness has been demonstrated only in mice, and additional animal safety studies are required before clinical testing can begin.

"Before we can trial this therapy in patients, we need to perform a series of safety studies in animals," he remarked. "It is a very promising pathway, but human noses are different from mouse noses, so we will need to test our drugs in humans before we know whether it will work.".

It has been revealed that that anatomical difference is a significant consideration. A delivery method that successfully transports therapeutic substances from the nasal cavity to the brain in mice may not work in exactly the same way in humans.

Nevertheless, the researchers believe the potential applications could be substantial. Beyond protecting against Alzheimer's-related cognitive decline, the technology might eventually help support memory and other mental abilities during normal aging.

"We believe our latest drug, if it works in humans, can promote general cognitive health as people age, should help treat a variety of neurodegenerative diseases, and may even increase cognitive abilities in healthy young people," Professor Neely stated.

In a recent development, the next steps involve refining the nasal delivery technology and conducting further studies to determine its safety and effectiveness over longer periods. The team hopes to advance toward human clinical trials as swiftly as the required testing allows.

It has been revealed that "Based on previous clinical trials, we know the components used in this study to be safe in humans," Professor Neely remarked. "We hope we can move quickly through the requisite safety studies and trial this treatment in humans in the next two years.".

Although the individual components have previously been used in humans, the complete nasal treatment still requires its own safety evaluation. Whether it can deliver similar memory benefits in people remains an open question.
