Democracy Depends on Being Able to Forgive

Posted November 19, 2026 | Reviewed by Jessica Schrader.

In a 60 Minutes interview broadcast this month, President Trump was asked a profound question that rarely comes up in political interviews: Could he forgive his enemies? If entering heaven required him to feel love and forgiveness for James Comey or Nancy Pelosi, could he do it?

Recent findings suggest that “The answer is no,” the president said. “I want to be honest, no.”.

We should resist the tempting thought that only Donald Trump feels this way. We all instead need to look in the mirror: How willing are we to forgive the people we believe have done us wrong?

Sources indicate that for most of us, grievance has a powerful pull—whether it’s rehearsing an argument or replaying a hurtful remark over and over in our heads. In psychology, we can understand forgiveness as a voluntary change in how we feel and act toward someone who’s hurt us in the past. It can loosen the hold of resentment on our attention and, in turn, help us choose how to respond in the future.

In a recent development, these days, the word forgiveness can sound like a concept out of a Sunday school class or a therapy session. But it’s a prerequisite for a functioning democratic society.

A country needs ways to respond to wrongdoing and still leave open the possibility of a shared future. When every injury becomes a reason for retaliation, the work of governing turns into settling scores.

According to recent reports, here’s the thing about democracy: It’s a continuing relationship among fallible people. It depends on our ability to correct errors, acknowledge wrongdoing, and work together again. Forgiveness is a vital civic skill because democratic societies have to do more than settle who was right in the last conflict. Forgiveness makes healthy debate and cooperation possible.

Industry observers note that I once asked the legendary Archbishop Desmond Tutu what forgiveness meant. He simply said, “It means I no longer demand payback from you.”.

Industry observers note that when he was chair of South Africa’s Truth and Reconciliation Commission, Tutu helped bring apartheid-era abuses to public attention. He underscored the principle that forgiveness isn’t blanket absolution. Truth, accountability, and repair can coexist with refusing permanent vengeance. In our own lives, we can forgive someone while maintaining boundaries, including choosing not to resume a relationship.

When I interviewed former Colombian president and Nobel Peace laureate Juan Manuel Santos for my book On Belonging, he recalled advice to recognise an adversary’s humanity in war and peace. His 2016 agreement with the FARC opened a path for former guerrillas to enter into democratic politics.

This month, delivering the Nelson Mandela Annual Lecture in South Africa, Santos told the story of Pastora Mira. She cared for a wounded man who then confessed to torturing and killing her son. She embraced him, saying his acceptance of responsibility had freed her from the prison of lifelong hatred. It’s a breathtaking example. Such forgiveness remains a personal choice.

It’s notable that we avoid thinking that the psychological grip of contempt is unique to any one country. Others—including my home country of Canada—shouldn’t think they’re immune to a politics of contempt. A 2025 Angus Reid Institute–Cardus survey found 87% of Conservative supporters regarded the Liberals as a threat to Canada’s well-being. And 84% of Liberal supporters said the same of Conservatives.

The philosopher Hannah Arendt understood forgiveness as a way to release people from being bound indefinitely to what they’ve done, allowing something new to begin. For her, forgiveness was inextricably linked to respect and to what I call belonging—that we honour people’s humanity, even if we disagree with their preferences.

According to recent reports, earlier this month, Texans elected James Talarico, a Presbyterian seminarian, to the U.S. Senate. He’s spoken eloquently about loving his enemies and seeing opponents as children of God. It’s a message that draws on a Christian tradition that asks us to extend the mercy we ourselves need—a tradition Martin Luther King Jr. brought into public life more than half a century ago.

Ultimately, forgiveness is a skill we can learn. In an international study of more than 4,500 adults, people given a short program to reflect on hurt and consider another person’s perspective reported greater forgiveness and fewer symptoms of depression and anxiety after two weeks than those waiting to begin. The study concerned personal relationships, but it offers reason to take forgiveness seriously as something we can actively cultivate.

We can all put this into practice. Name what happened and how it honestly affected your feelings. Try to consider the other person’s perspective, and think of a time you needed forgiveness for yourself. Give yourself time and space for your feelings to change. Choosing to forgive can precede feeling the emotion of forgiveness.

Sources indicate that let’s face it: In failing to forgive his enemies, Donald Trump isn’t so much of an outlier. But that needs to change. To build a healthy democratic society, we can’t have permanent enemies. We have to build a future together. That work can begin in our own relationships.
