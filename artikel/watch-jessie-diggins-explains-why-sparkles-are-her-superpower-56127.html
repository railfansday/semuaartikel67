According to recent reports, trail Runner managing editor Abby Levene caught up with cross-country skiing superstar Jessie Diggins in Kona, Hawaii, a few days before the Ironman World Championship. In this exclusive interview, Diggins shares how she battled through a deep low to finish (and win) her first 100-miler, the lessons from cross-country skiing that she’s applying to trail running, and why she always puts on face glitter before a race.

After retiring from cross-country skiing as the most decorated American of all time, glide off into the sunset Jessie Diggins did not. The Olympic gold medalist earned one last piece of hardware in Milan-Cortina this spring—on bruised ribs no less—hung up the skis, and dove head-first into trail running.

Diggins, 35, raced the Broken Arrow Triple Crown in June, as has become her annual ritual. She then lined up against the world’s best trail runners at Sierre-Zinal in August, won the Superior 100-miler in September, and just last weekend won the (bear with me) Trail Animals Running Club Stone Cat Trail Festival 50-miler in Massachusetts.

In a recent development, on paper, it’s been an effortless transition into a sport Diggins swears she is just doing for fun. But of course there is so much more to the story once you dig in. I was lucky enough to catch up with Diggins at the epicenter of yet another endurance sport—the Ironman World Championship—in Kona, Hawaii, last month. She’d just come from a snorkeling expedition trying to find a sea turtle. (So far no luck.) Gazing out onto the turquoise waves crashing into shore, we chatted about what she’s learned and loved about trail running so far. That list includes (and is not limited to) how trail running really is a team sport, achieving personal goals is bigger than the results, fueling on a “washing machine” stomach is not easy, and the power of glitter translates from snowy trails to dry ones.

It has been revealed that this interview has been edited for clarity. You can also watch some of it above.

Jessie: It was a really cool experience. It was so humbling, but so exciting to tackle a totally different challenge. I was filled with curiosity: What will this feel like? What’s going to happen to my body? What will happen inside my brain? How does this work?

Having a crew and pacers like that is very different from skiing. [In skiing] you are alone on the trail, and people can cheer for you, but you don’t actually have anyone with you on the trail. And so it was just so exciting I got to do it with a team. That was really big for me. I’m a very team-oriented person, and I would get so excited to come into the aid station and see the crew and to get to have a pacer with me. Even when I was falling apart and couldn’t even  talk—I was at a really low point—it was just like, ‘I’m here with my team and I’m pushing my limits and I’m seeing what’s possible for me.’ And that’s so exciting.

I would just tell myself ‘every step you take is a step you don’t have to take again.’ So I was just like, ‘I’m doing it. I’m slowly working towards that finish line, step by step.’ It was so funny because even when I was deeply uncomfortable, I never actually thought about quitting, which I thought I would. It was more just like I’m so uncomfortable, but I know I can if I just keep moving. Even if I’m slowing down, even if I’m not even able to run, I have to just power hike this section. I’m still moving towards my goal. I’m in the process of getting there, and that’s exciting in and of itself.

It has been revealed that I kind of wanted to set up a charcuterie board of the trail world and try different things because they were all so different, right? Broken Arrow is a huge festival and it was a multi-day thing, so oh my gosh, what is day three gonna feel like? Can I even run downhill on day three?

It has been revealed that and then Sierre-Zinal is so historic, and it was such a cool course. It was beautiful, and it was like experiencing a very classic “short” trail race, right? I was like, ‘this is not short.’ It took me 3 hours, but it was so cool to experience.

And then going into the 100 miler, it was different and more of a backyard format. Or not a backyard in terms of the laps, but like there was no award ceremony. It wasn’t about winning. It was just about doing it, and I love that. That is so aligned with how I feel about racing. It’s like, ‘No, we don’t really care who wins. We just want everyone to challenge themselves and hopefully make their goal of crossing the finish line.’ And that was so cool for me.

Sources indicate that and so getting to be a part of each of these events, I got to experience so many different angles of the trail world and meet so many people. And for me, that was a significant thing, too: feeling the sense of community that was around every single race and making latest friends and trying something new. That was very cool.

It has been revealed that no, I definitely am excited to do more. I don’t know what that’s gonna look like yet, but I feel like just getting to be involved in this world is so cool and so exciting. So, I’m sort of now in the phase of like, ‘OK, I feel like I’m bouncing back with 100. Like, my body’s coming back, and now I can start dreaming and scheming and brainstorming, like what else do I want to do? What else is out there? What excites me? What feels like the appropriate challenge for this phase of my life?’ Yeah, I’m trying to figure it all out.

It has been revealed that I think the biggest thing was in training, like listening to my body. So I stopped wearing a heart rate monitor like five or six years ago, and it was really about being in tune and listening to my body and figuring out where I can push. What does it feel like when I’m riding that line of level three, and what are the signals in my body when I’ve gone over that line and I need to pull it back?

And so really trying to deeply understand how I feel, and I feel like that’s helped me steer away from injuries too, because I’m not relying on metrics. I want to feel how my body feels and respond to that and act on those feelings.I think that’s something I’m taking with me because I’m well aware now that running can really beat you up in a different way. Even listening to my body and learning how much can I eat while I’m running because your stomach is a washing machine and that does not happen in cross-country skiing.

I think it was really exciting in my final years of cross-country skiing, learning more about high-carb fueling and having all these great products come on board. Because in a ski race, let’s say a 50-kilometer race, you come through a feed zone, but you have gloves and your hands are in poles, so you are not like ripping open a gel. You’ll end up stabbing yourself in the face. And so what you do is you tuck your poles under your armpits and you grab the feed bottle, which is usually an actual baby bottle with the lid taken off, from a coach who cannot run with you, they have to just stand there and hold it by the rim. So you grab it while you are gliding quite promptly. You down it so you get like a few good sips, and then you have to chuck the bottle on the side and keep moving. And so having really high carb drink mixes where you can, if you only can get a few mouthfuls, you need them to count, and so having these was huge. Because before it’d be like, ‘I guess we’ll boil down Coke to de-fizz it, and that’s what you can have.’ But it  just wasn’t high carb enough, and the electrolyte balance might not have been great.

Now there’s so many options, and that really changed the way I could race. And so now, in trail racing, there’s all these different gels. You can find the one that works for you. There’s all these different mixes. You can find the amount of salt that works for you. That’s been really cool. Being able to tailor it. I feel like 15 years ago, there weren’t that many options. Now having more options is power.

I do get scared. It’s funny. I just saw on the elevator this morning a woman was wearing glitter, and I was like, ‘Yes!’ That was my whole thing through my ski career. And it came out of being scared and being nervous. And I feel like people don’t always know that that’s the origin. But I would get so nervous for races, and a little bit of nerves is great. It means you care. If you’re not nervous for a race, it’s sort of like, ‘Why am I doing this?’ You know, then it’s time to maybe examine, do I really care about what I’m doing?

At the end of the day, yes, there’s a lot of pressure. There can be expectations. There can be a lot of lights. A lot of people are watching you, but you get to do this. You get to challenge yourself and see what’s possible. And that, in and of itself, is such an amazing opportunity.
