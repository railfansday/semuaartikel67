12 things to know about daily water, from thirst to overdrinking

Recent findings suggest that daily water needs vary by age, health and activity. See 12 facts on intake, dehydration, overdrinking and easy habits.

Recent findings suggest that most people carry a daily water target in their heads, and many suspect they fall short of it. Many of them are right: roughly half the world's people drink less water than experts recommend, according to NIH. Missing that mark rarely leads to harm, though. StatPearls reports that dehydration is uncommon in adults who have no medical problems and can drink water freely. The official amount is therefore a rough guide, and a person who falls short of it on a given day hasn't failed a test. Who a person is and what their body is doing that day matter more than any fixed amount, whether they are working outside in summer heat or recovering from an illness.

Recent findings suggest that for most healthy people, thirst is the best guide to how much to drink. It is also least reliable in the people who most need it. In older adults the body's thirst signal is less sensitive, StatPearls reports, and some drugs for blood pressure can leave a person unable to tell that they are thirsty, according to Cleveland Clinic. For an older adult who takes blood pressure pills, waiting to feel thirsty can mean drinking too little, too late. The opposite problem, drinking far more than the body needs, is rarer but just as real. In both cases, serious trouble with hydration is concentrated among particular people whose sense of need doesn't work reliably, and it doesn't threaten everyone equally.

Mayo Clinic set out to explain how much water people need and how to stay well hydrated. Its guide covered alkaline water, older adults whose thirst fades, endurance runners who drink too much, and a splash of juice in sparkling water. The useful answer is less a cup count than a clear sense of who should drink on a routine and who should hold back. No upper intake limit has been set for water, so people prone to drinking too much are protected mainly by knowing their own risk. For everyone else, the task is to make plain water the easy choice over the sugary drinks it would replace.

The body needs water every day, and the need is large. By weight, an adult's body is about 60% water, according to Mayo Clinic. Even the official guidance gives no single glass count for everyone. The National Academy of Medicine sets a daily amount of about 13 cups for healthy men and about 9 cups for healthy women, so no one eight-glass rule applies to all adults. If people read the amount for men as glasses of plain water, they would drink well past what at least one nutrition researcher thinks they require. CBC News reports that Speros Tsindos, an author in the department of dietetics and human nutrition at La Trobe University, considers even eight daily glasses of pure water more than people seem to need. The useful question, then, is not how anyone could drink 13 glasses, but what counts toward the official amount.

Sources indicate that much of the water a body takes in never passes through a glass. "There's nothing magical about water from a glass of water as opposed to water from a food or any other beverage," Susan Barr, a nutrition professor at the University of British Columbia, told CBC News. A baked potato is 75% water, so people take in water at dinner without lifting a glass. Caffeinated coffee and tea don't dehydrate the people who drink them, either. A person who starts the day with a mug of coffee has already taken in water that a tally of water glasses would miss.

In a recent development, the plain water a person drinks on purpose is only one part of what the body takes in each day. Someone worried about falling short should look at everything they eat and drink before adding glasses to reach a number.

The best-known daily water amounts count every fluid a person takes in, not only glasses of plain water. The National Academy of Medicine sets an adequate intake of about 13 cups for healthy men and about 9 cups for healthy women, according to Mayo Clinic. NIH reports that those totals can include juices and water-rich produce. A person who eats fruit and vegetables and drinks other beverages through the day is already covering part of the amount at ordinary meals. The share that has to come from the tap is smaller than the headline number suggests.

Recent findings suggest that the advised amount depends on what a body is doing, not only on whether the person is a man or a woman. A breastfeeding woman is advised to take in 13 cups, the same as a healthy man and well above the general figure for women. Producing milk lifts her need to the level set for men. The figures for men and women serve as a baseline that changes with a person's stage of life, not as a fixed personal assignment.

It has been revealed that there is a real health reason behind the cup counts, though it is not proof. NIH reports that adults whose blood sodium measured 144.5 to 146 mEq/L faced a 21% higher risk of premature death than adults whose levels measured 137 to 142 mEq/L. A level many people would call fine still carried a cost. That makes hydration something a doctor can check with a blood test, not only something a person tallies in cups. "People whose serum sodium is 142 mEq/L or higher would benefit from evaluation of their fluid intake," said Natalia Dmitrieva, Ph.D., an NHLBI researcher and study author. A higher risk among people with higher sodium shows a link, not evidence that drinking more water would lower that risk. The cup counts are a sensible bet rather than a proven line between health and harm.

For most healthy people, that makes the number a reason to keep water within reach, not a quota to finish by bedtime.

Counting glasses misses water that people take in without drinking it. About 20% of the water a person takes in each day comes from food, according to The Nutrition Source. The CDC reports that foods with high water content contribute to fluid intake. A lunch salad of lettuce, cucumbers, bell peppers and celery adds water to the day, and so does a bowl of melon and berries after dinner. Someone who eats plenty of produce takes in more water than a glass count highlights.

In a recent development, the other drinks in a person's day also count toward the total, and coffee and tea are among them. The one limit to keep in mind is caffeine: someone who takes in more than 180 mg of it a day may urinate more. Below that level, the coffee or tea a person already drinks belongs in the daily tally. Above it, the amount of caffeine deserves closer attention than the type of drink it comes in.

The baseline amount also changes from one day to the next, even for someone who eats well. Fever, exercise, and extreme heat or cold all raise the amount of fluid a person needs. The CDC reports that people in hot climates need more water as well. Cold also appears on the list, so a person working outdoors in extreme cold needs more fluid too. Illness that drains fluid belongs on the same list. Vomiting and diarrhea pull water out of the body, so someone with a stomach bug should drink beyond their usual amount.

Sources indicate that the daily figure is a starting point that each day revises. That leaves the question of what should go in the glass, and whether some waters are worth paying more for.

It has been revealed that alkaline water is sold as an upgrade on the ordinary kind, a drink that does more for the body than keep it hydrated. It lacks proof of any extra benefit, and plain water hydrates about as well, according to The Nutrition Source. ABC News reports that Michele Promaulayko, editor in chief of Yahoo Health, finds no studies showing that the water does anything to improve a person's health. Someone who pays extra for a bottle pays for a claim, not for a result they can count on.

The sales pitch rests on changing the body's chemistry, not on hydration. People who promote alkaline water want to bring the body to a pH of 7.4, according to ABC News. The body already regulates blood to about that level on its own. Researchers have yet to prove in a controlled trial that food or drink can move a healthy person's blood away from that level. "Our bodies need water - it's absolutely essential. But … the idea that drinking alkaline water will affect the PH of your blood is absolute nonsense," said Dr. Richard Besser, ABC News' chief health and medical editor. The strongest case against alkaline water comes from how the body works, not from whether the people drinking it stay hydrated.

Ordinary tap water needs no correcting, and its pH of about 7 is close to neutral. The EPA recommends that drinking water have a pH between 6.5 and 8.5, according to ABC News. Tap water already falls inside that range, so alkaline water fills no gap. That leaves one honest argument for it, which is whether it gets a person to reach for water more often. Someone who drinks more because the alkaline kind appeals to them gains from the extra water, not from its pH.

Anyone who already drinks enough gains nothing from paying more that they couldn't get from the tap.

Waiting to feel thirsty works poorly for people whose sense of thirst has faded. Thirst grows weaker with age and illness, and some people need set drinking routines as a result, according to The Nutrition Source. For them, drinking only when thirsty can mean drinking too little and too late. Older adults also have less water in their bodies to lose before trouble starts. "What happens as you get older, the amount of total water in your body actually goes down. That means you don't have as much water to lose," Ronan Factora, a geriatric medicine specialist at Cleveland Clinic, noted. A drinking routine for an older parent is not fussiness. It makes up for a smaller supply of water and a sense of thirst that no longer gives a reliable warning.

Some medical conditions, among them stroke and dementia, can impair the sense of thirst. People living with these conditions may not feel thirsty when their bodies need water, and a routine can supply the reminder their thirst fails to give. Other people feel thirsty and still drink less on purpose, because they live with incontinence or find it hard to reach the bathroom. For them, drinking less is a choice, and a reminder to drink will do little if the trip to the bathroom is the real obstacle. Making that trip easier is part of preventing dehydration.

It has been revealed that keeping water cool can make a drinking routine easier to follow. People most often drink their water at room temperature, yet they rate cooler water as more pleasing. Keeping cool water within reach costs nothing, and it may help people whose thirst has weakened stick to a schedule.

According to recent reports, dehydration can dull the mind and mood well before anyone would call themselves parched. People who are short by only about 2% of their body's water can already feel tired, confused or moody, according to The Nutrition Source. Tiredness, fogginess and a short temper feel like an ordinary bad day, and few people connect them with what they've had to drink. These signs help only someone who knows to treat them as a reason to pour a glass.

Recent findings suggest that the early signs are also the hardest to confirm. StatPearls reports that there is no gold-standard test for dehydration. A person who feels foggy in the afternoon has no firm way to tell whether water is the cause, and judging hydration by feel stays guesswork. Uncertainty like that leaves room for alarm. No research supports the widely shared figure that 75% of Americans go through life short of water. For most people, a vague symptom is a reason to drink some water. It is not proof of a hidden, long-running deficit.

In long-term care, the early signs are a common sight rather than a rare warning. Of the residents of those facilities, 34% are dehydrated, according to StatPearls. Caregivers and visiting family should expect to watch for tiredness and confusion in the people they look after. Older adults who live at home are dehydrated less often, at 19%, but the risk remains real outside care, even though it runs highest inside it. For an older relative, the useful habit is to notice a tired or muddled day and offer water rather than wait for them to ask for it.

Sources indicate that dehydration can do its harm slowly, through ailments that build up over time rather than in a single hot afternoon. Most people think of it as a headache after too much sun, something they can fix with a glass of water within the hour. Going short of water can raise the risk of constipation, gallstones and kidney stones, according to The Nutrition Source. These are common, nagging problems that develop out of sight. They matter most for people who can't easily drink as much as their bodies need.

Industry observers note that older adults are among those people, and for some of them the obstacle is the physical act of drinking. People with Parkinson's disease can have trouble swallowing, The Conversation reports, and they may want a drink and still struggle to get it down. For someone in that position, wanting water isn't enough to keep fluids coming. A caregiver who offers drinks on a schedule can help close that gap.

Urinary tract infections bring a separate danger for older adults. In an older person, such an infection can bring on delirium, a sudden state of confusion, according to The Conversation. An abrupt change in an older relative's thinking can therefore have a physical cause that a doctor can find and treat.

For anyone caring for an older relative, newly introduced confusion is a reason to check how much they have been drinking and to ask a doctor about infection, rather than to assume a lasting decline.

It has been revealed that A person who drinks a large amount of water quickly can harm themselves, even though most people treat water as the one thing they can't overdo. Rapid, heavy drinking can overwhelm the kidneys and push sodium in the blood to a dangerous low, though this is rare, according to The Nutrition Source. The result is a condition called hyponatremia, or low blood sodium. The harm comes from what a large, fast intake does to the body's salt balance. Nothing is wrong with water itself, so the useful caution is about how fast and how much a person drinks, not about a full glass at dinner.

Recent findings suggest that hyponatremia is uncommon, but its effects can be severe. Some of its signs are easy to dismiss, such as nausea, which few people would connect to the water they drank. Others, such as seizures, mean the brain itself is affected. With effects that range from an upset stomach to seizures, drinking too much belongs among the real risks of hydration.

For most healthy people the danger is remote, because it takes more water, drunk faster, than their kidneys can clear. The risk is greater for people who are ill and whose kidneys work poorly. When the organ that removes extra water is already struggling, a patient has less protection against overdrinking than a healthy person does. For these patients, the common advice to drink more isn't automatically sound.

Like the advice to drink when thirsty, the advice to drink more fails in particular people rather than across the board.

It has been revealed that drinking too much water is a danger for a few groups of people, not for ordinary drinkers. The risk rises with kidney illness, extreme heat, endurance exercise and a smaller body, according to The Nutrition Source. Not everyone in those groups is an athlete. People who are ill and whose kidneys work poorly are also at risk, so overdrinking is a medical concern as well as a sporting one, and these patients should hold back rather than drink by routine. Marathon $MPC runners are another group at risk, and at one race a sizable share of them had low blood sodium. The Cleveland Clinic Journal of Medicine reports that 13% of the runners tested at the 2002 Boston Marathon had it, while only 0.6% reached a critical level. Most of those cases were mild, and a serious case was uncommon but real, which calls for care rather than fear.

Long miles alone do not put athletes in danger, but the extra water they drink does. Athletes lose sodium through sweat, and they get into trouble when they drink far more than that loss calls for. Organizers of one triathlon taught athletes about the risk and offered fewer drink stations, and the share of athletes treated for hyponatremia in the medical tent fell from 22% to 3%, according to the Cleveland Clinic Journal of Medicine. Triathletes face this danger mainly because of how they drink on the course, so they can keep racing and still lower their risk sharply by drinking less.

According to recent reports, women also run into trouble more often than men in long races. At the 2000 Houston Marathon, women developed hyponatremia at a higher rate than men, according to the Cleveland Clinic Journal of Medicine. That pattern matches the higher risk for women and children. A likely reason, though not a proven one, is that slower and smaller runners spend more hours near drink stations while needing less fluid, which also fits the added risk of a smaller body. Sex and size are warning signs that may partly reflect time on the course and chances to drink, not fixed fates.

For every group at risk, the protection is to drink to need rather than at every chance.

For most healthy people, the trouble is the sweet drink they reach for, not a shortage of liquid. The Nutrition Source recommends flavorful water-based drinks as a way to help people used to sweet beverages drink more water. Many Americans fit that description, and about 30% of those 2 and older take in too much added sugar, according to the CDC. That makes this advice a sugar fix more than a hydration initiative. A reader should judge it by how many sweet drinks it replaces, not by how many glasses of water it adds.

It has been revealed that A person who trades soda for water cuts a large amount of sugar from the day. There are 10 teaspoons of sugar in a 12-ounce can of regular soda, according to the CDC, and a person who drinks two fewer cans each day removes 2,100 calories from the week while eating the same meals. The gain holds only when the replacement drink's flavor doesn't come from sugar, because sweetened waters count as sugary drinks. A bottle with "water" on the label can still put a person right back where they started, so the thing to check is what sweetens it.

Flavor alone won't keep someone away from soda if the can sits closer than the water. The CDC suggests keeping a cold jug of water in the fridge and carrying a reusable bottle through the day. Both habits put water within reach first, so a person doesn't have to rely on willpower to choose it. For the healthy majority, how they stock the kitchen matters more than whether they reach a daily cup count.

Recent findings suggest that people can make flavored water at home for less than they pay for the bottled kind. Grocery stores sell flavored waters as separate products and price them as something special. Homemade water flavored with fruit, herbs or cucumber costs less than the store-bought versions, according to The Nutrition Source. That saving counts most for a drink meant for every day. A small cost paid once a day adds up over weeks and months, while a few slices of produce in a pitcher add almost nothing to a grocery bill. A person who makes flavored water in the kitchen turns it from a purchase into a routine.

Recent findings suggest that the habit helps most the people who find plain water too bland next to the sweet drinks they usually choose. Infused water is meant to help people who are used to sweet drinks drink more water, and it uses the taste of fruit, herbs or vegetables instead of sugar to do that. A hint of flavor can be enough for someone to pick up a glass of water without going back to the sweetness they are trying to give up. The flavorings can be as simple as a few rounds of cucumber or some ginger with the skin cut away. Many people already keep these at home, so they can start the habit without a special trip to the store and keep it going with whatever is in the crisper drawer that week.

All the habit requires is a pitcher of water and a few slices of whatever produce is on hand.

People who buy bottled sparkling juices want fizz and fruit flavor, and they can mix both into a glass at home while deciding for themselves how much juice goes in. The Nutrition Source recommends a homemade version that uses only 1 to 2 ounces of juice in sparkling water, in place of sparkling juices. At that amount, the juice is a flavoring, much like a squeeze of lemon, and not a serving to drink. A person holding this glass is still mostly drinking bubbly water with a hint of fruit.

In a recent development, keeping the pour small matters for more than calories, because juice is the part of the glass to be careful with. The CDC classifies fruit juice as a simple carb, so for anyone watching their blood sugar, a smaller pour means less of the kind of carbohydrate they are trying to limit. Juice packs a lot of fruit into a small volume, with about 3 oranges squeezed into one cup, and an ounce or two holds only a small part of that. Even a generous pour is no substitute for eating fruit, since juice is missing the fiber that whole fruit has.

Sources indicate that the splash is a way to drink less juice, not a reason to count juice as a healthy drink. Stirred into 12 ounces of sparkling water, an ounce or two of juice leaves a glass that is almost entirely bubbles and water. For anyone unsure what to drink, the CDC advises plain water. That makes the homemade glass a move toward plain water, not a goal of its own. The pour is the one thing to watch, because an ounce can easily become a few, and then the glass is a sparkling juice again.

The best version of this glass is the one where the splash keeps getting smaller.
