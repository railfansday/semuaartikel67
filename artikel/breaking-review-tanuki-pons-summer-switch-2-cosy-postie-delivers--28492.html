Breaking: Review: TANUKI: Pon's Summer (Switch 2) - Cosy Postie Delivers Sick BMX Tricks & Minigames

Industry observers note that the gaming calendar is busier than it’s been in recent memory. We’re wedged between the Ocarina of Time remake and GTA 6, having only just recovered from the onslaught of games that debuted in September. The prospect of picking up another right now might not be an inviting one, but if any game could do it, it’s Tanuki: Pon’s Summer.

In a recent development, this quiet, cosy, understated gem has really introduced at the perfect time. Playing as the titular Tanuki, you’re tasked with getting a shrine ready for a significant festival towards the end of the summer. You have to raise funds for repair work, and find folks in town who can actually carry it out. To do so, you pick up a delivery job, get to know all of your eccentric neighbours, and pull off some sick BMX tricks along the way.

That sounds simple, but this is just the ramp used to propel you into the ridiculous amount of tasks you can get up to in Tanuki: Pon’s Summer. While primarily all about riding a BMX and delivering parcels, you promptly realise that this is a simulation game with a vibrant, animated world that invites exploration.

A quirk of the delivery outfit you work for is that you don’t just deliver the mail. No, you are the backbone of society. The job isn’t over until the customer has a smile on their face. So, if your round takes you to a local fisherman, and he’s feeling down about how he isn’t getting enough business, it’s your job to cheer him up and try out some fishing yourself. If you’re dropping a parcel off at a bar, and the place is packed, you better get ready to pull pints. If a photographer is lacking inspiration, then you have to pick up a camera and help her find it.

Sources indicate that you get the point; Tanuki: Pon’s Summer manages to pull off so many wildly different minigames with ease, and (mostly) without losing its identity. When you park up the BMX to play some baseball, it feels like you’re diving further into this gorgeous world, rather than stepping into a different game entirely.

According to recent reports, it feels like the idea behind these minigames is that they’re not supposed to overstay their welcome, but I found myself sorely wishing I could return to a few of them. The golf minigame towards the end in particular is one of the best golf sims going. The same goes for bar work; trying to replicate the drinks being ordered was satisfying and moreish, but gone too soon and never playable again. If anything, developer Denkiworks misses a trick in not giving you some kind of game mode that lets you play through these on a loop - I would have happily dived back in for that.

This is not to say that every minigame is pulled off with ease. There are some that just don’t work, and were more of a frustrating slog than anything else. The good thing is that this is a cosy game with low stakes, so if you’re struggling with one of them, there’s almost always a way to make things more generous, or skip over it if you fail enough times. But at times, this can feel like a cop out; the few frustrating minigames could have been improved rather than skipped over.

Unfortunately, the worst game of the bunch doesn’t even let you skip it, at least in my playthrough. This involves you transporting boulders in a cave, rolling them through an obstacle course.

To understand the matter with this, you have to understand that Tanuki: Pon's Summer doesn’t do precise controls. The opening makes this clear when you’re taught how to use the BMX - everything is simplified, and you’re not expected to do anything too intricate. Pon moves around too sluggishly and ragdoll-y for that to work. And for the most part, the game is built around this.

In a recent development, the cave minigame forgets this entirely. Pon has to pilot the boulders through tight walkways, often fighting against the game’s physics to avoid falling off the ledge. If you do fall, you go right back to the beginning, losing all progress. I got through it, but I worried for the state of my Joy-Con afterwards, with how much stress they were put through.

It has been revealed that tanuki: Pon’s Summer is at its best when it’s as difficult as you want it to be. You can try and pull off some tricks in golf to beat your opponent, or play it safe and have her win. You can make a lousy bartender and call it a day, or you can throw your all into it. You can rack up as many points on the BMX as you like, or ride slow and steady. But when a minigame just can’t find its footing, like riding the boulders in the cave, the game loses track of what makes it work.

At times, it can also feel that the game spreads itself a little too thin. I felt this most in the basic base building. You can unlock a whole lot of items to revamp the Tanuki shrine, but you can’t do too much with them. There’s just not enough freedom, so the plots of land became more of a dumping ground for my trophies.

It has been revealed that tanuki: Pon’s Summer is a cosy game that understands what it means to be a cosy game: create a gorgeous world you want to get lost in, with satisfying but lower-stakes gameplay. By the time I had rebuilt the Tanuki shrine and completed Pon’s summer job, I wanted to dive back in and see more of the townsfolk I’d met along the way. I wanted to keep delivering their mail and nosing my way into their business.

Industry observers note that but there were a few sequences in the game I was happy to see the back of, either because they were on the duller side, or just didn’t work with the exaggerated, slapstick control scheme. Equally, I wish Pon’s Summer spent longer on what it does right, letting us go on an infinite number of deliveries around the island, or play as much golf as we please.
