Source: Society for Neuroscience.

Widespread sexualization of women across advertising, entertainment, and social media has long been linked to societal objectification and broad mental health burdens, including body dissatisfaction, anxiety, and disordered eating.

Over time, repeated exposure to objectifying imagery can lead individuals to internalize an observer’s gaze, a psychological phenomenon known as self-objectification, in which a person habitually monitors and evaluates their own body from an outsider’s perspective.

While behavioral studies have established that self-objectification heightens sensitivity to media portrayals, precisely how a woman’s self-perception alters the underlying neural circuitry that evaluates sexualized visuals has remained unclear.

In a study published today in eNeuro, a research team led by Zhennan Liu at Shanghai Normal University and Haoyue Qian at the Chinese Academy of Sciences investigated how individual differences in self-objectification shape both subjective beauty judgments and brain responses to sexualized imagery.

According to recent reports, the investigators recruited 60 healthy cisgender heterosexual female participants with an average age of approximately 20 years. First, the team assessed each participant’s baseline level of self-objectification using a validated psychological scale measuring how strongly individuals prioritize external appearance over internal bodily competence.

While participants across the board rated the sexualized photographs as more attractive than the non-sexualized images, women with higher self-objectification scores assigned significantly higher attractiveness ratings to the sexualized visuals.

When the team examined the accompanying brain activity, they uncovered a striking neural signature: women who scored higher on self-objectification exhibited dampened neural activity in brain regions involved in aesthetic judgment while assessing sexualized images.

Rather than heightened analytical engagement, this reduced activation suggests an altered regulatory or automatic evaluative response when processing culturally idealized, objectifying imagery.

“Differences between individuals [in this study] may come from how their brains regulate and evaluate the attractiveness of these images,” explained Qian.

By connecting self-objectification to measurable shifts in aesthetic evaluation circuitry, the study illustrates how internalized social standards leave a functional footprint on visual and emotional processing.

Looking ahead, the researchers aim to move beyond observational associations to test whether psychological training can buffer the brain against objectifying media.

In a recent development, “We want to test whether experimentally training or manipulating regulation strategies (e.g., cognitive reappraisal) can change how women respond to and rate sexualized images,” Qian noted. “This will help move the work from association toward causal and intervention-relevant questions.”.

What is “self-objectification,” and how did researchers measure it?

Self-objectification is the tendency to view and judge one’s own body primarily from an outside observer’s perspective, prioritizing physical appearance over internal physical capabilities. Researchers measured this trait using a validated psychological questionnaire before participants viewed media images.

How did self-objectification affect brain responses to sexualized images?

Women with higher self-objectification scores rated sexualized images of women as significantly more attractive than peers with lower scores, and showed dampened brain activity in regions responsible for aesthetic judgment, indicating differences in how their brains regulate and evaluate beauty standards.

Could psychological strategies change how the brain reacts to objectifying media?

Researchers hope to test whether training cognitive regulation techniques, such as cognitive reappraisal, which helps people consciously reframe how they interpret visual stimuli, can modify brain activity and reduce vulnerability to sexualized media representations.

Trait Self-Objectification Modulates Attractiveness-Regulating Neural Activity in Sexual Objectification Processing: An ERP Study.

Contemporary mass media frequently emphasize women’s sexual attractiveness, thereby perpetuating sexual objectification and exacerbating mental distress. Trait self-objectification is presumed to be a risk factor for these adverse outcomes; however, the neurocognitive mechanisms underlying individuals’ responses to sexual objectification remain poorly understood.

According to recent reports, the present study examined how trait self-objectification modulates behavioral and electrophysiological responses to sexualized female images. Behaviorally, sexualized portrayals of women increased attractiveness ratings, and this enhancement was further modulated by individual levels of trait self-objectification. Neurally, processing sexualized female images evoked a set of ERPs indexing early neurocognitive processes, including perception-related N170 and EPN, and attention-related P200.

Distinct later neural responses were also identified, including the emotion-related centro-parietal LPP and the regulation-related frontal LPP (f-LPP). Among these sexual objectification-related ERP components, only the f-LPP was modulated by trait self-objectification.

Furthermore, follow-up analyses revealed that the degree to which trait self-objectification increased individuals’ attractiveness ratings of sexualized images was associated with their reduced f-LPP activity.

Collectively, these findings suggest that trait self-objectification modulates the processing of sexually objectifying stimuli by altering neurocognitive processes underlying attractiveness regulation, highlighting the importance of this regulatory mechanism in shaping individual responses to sexual objectification.

The cultural emphasis on women’s sexual attractiveness perpetuates sexual objectification, which is associated with adverse mental health outcomes. Trait self-objectification may modulate these outcomes, yet its relationship with the processing of sexual objectification remains unclear. This study found that trait self-objectification shapes attractiveness ratings during sexual objectification processing.

According to recent reports, although sexualized images elicited multiple neurocognitive components—N170 and EPN (perception-related), P200 (attention-related), cp-LPP (emotion-related), and f-LPP (regulation-related)—individual differences in trait self-objectification were selectively associated with f-LPP activity. These findings highlight the potential role of attractiveness-related regulatory processing in shaping individual responses to sexual objectification.
