It has been revealed that A federal judge has ordered Apple to produce a random sample of 3,200 documents for Epic Games to review, following Epic’s allegations that Apple improperly withheld more than 122,000 documents related to its proposed App Store commissions. Here are the details.

With Apple and Epic trading arguments in the Supreme Court over whether the civil contempt ruling against Apple should stand, the two companies have also been battling in the district court over what commission, if any, Apple should be allowed to charge on purchases made through external payment systems.

In a recent development, in August, Apple submitted its proposal to the district court, calling for commissions ranging from 5% to 15%. Epic, however, is disputing not just the basis for those proposed rates, but also Apple’s handling of document production in the proceedings.

Earlier this month, Epic accused Apple of improperly withholding more than 122,000 documents matching a set of search terms agreed upon by both companies to help identify documents that could shed light on how Apple arrived at those rates.

It has been revealed that […] Apple is withholding well over 99% of the documents returned by running the agreed-to search terms on the files of the four custodians Apple notes decided on the proposed fee (Tim Cook, Eddy Cue, Carson Oliver, and Kevan Parekh (Apple’s fresh CFO)) and three other key individuals (Nate Barton, Alex Roman, and Phil Schiller). Apple claims these withheld documents are not relevant to this proceeding-even though they hit on case-specific terms such as “proffer,” “contempt,” “injunction,” and “link-out.” To put this into context, while Apple produced 632 custodial documents returned by these search terms (including families), Apple is withholding over 122,000 based on a claim of non-responsiveness.

Apple, in turn, pushed back against Epic’s accusations, saying the search terms were overly broad and had captured large numbers of documents unrelated to the proceedings.

[…] at least 95% of the Initial Custodian Document Population hit on generic terms […]. These sweeping terms do not just increase the total volume of the Initial Custodian Document Population, they also captured highly sensitive and irrelevant documents, such as Apple employees’ personal tax or financial records, medical records, documents discussing unannounced products, documents containing passwords, investor-relations materials, and privileged or protected legal communications.

The tech giant also asked the court to reject Epic’s motion to order it to produce all non-privileged documents matching the agreed-upon search terms, and alternatively suggested submitting a randomized, statistically significant sample of the documents it deemed nonresponsive to a Special Master for independent review.

As it turns out, neither company got exactly what it originally wanted, but the judge ruled largely in Epic’s favor on how to determine whether Apple had improperly withheld documents.

In a recent development, following a hearing in which both companies presented their respective proposals for how a randomized sampling of the documents should be conducted, Magistrate Judge Thomas S. Hixson decided to adopt Epic’s proposal, which called for Apple to produce a random sample of 3,200 documents it had previously deemed nonresponsive, along with their associated document families, for Epic itself to review.

All documents in the sample will be drawn from the files of the seven Apple custodians referenced in Epic’s original motion.

Apple, by contrast, had suggested a considerably smaller sample of 383 documents, arguing that this would be statistically significant at a 95% confidence level.

In his decision, Judge Hixson agreed that Epic had legitimate reasons to question Apple’s reasons for withholding the documents:.

In a recent development, apple’s responsiveness rate is so dramatically low that it raises red flags. With Apple screening out more than 99% of the documents that hit on the agreed search terms, it is unreasonable to ask Epic to believe that Apple is making these responsiveness calls in good faith. That is particularly true in this case, in which Judge Gonzalez Rogers has sanctioned Apple for its large-scale abuse of the attorney-client privilege for the purpose of obscuring the evidence.

Judge Hixson also rejected Apple’s suggestion that a Special Master conduct the review, arguing that given the level of familiarity with the case required to assess the relevance of these documents, Epic was the only practical option for determining whether Apple had systematically misclassified them.

Realistically, the only person who has the practical ability to determine if Apple has been systematically mis-coding documents as nonresponsive is its opponent, Epic.

In his decision, the judge agreed with Apple that the organization should be allowed to review the documents for privileged and sensitive information before producing them to Epic, and authorized Apple to designate all documents produced as part of the exercise as Attorneys’ Eyes Only.

Importantly, Judge Hixson has not yet determined whether Apple systematically misclassified responsive documents as nonresponsive, nor has he imposed the daily monetary sanctions originally requested by Epic.

Apple now has until December 8 to complete the document production, with Epic having until December 15 to file any objections, and Apple until December 22 to respond.
