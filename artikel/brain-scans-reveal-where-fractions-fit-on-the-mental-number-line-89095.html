Brain scans reveal where fractions fit on the mental number line

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Brain scans show that adults use a shared mental number line to compare fractions and whole numbers, even as the brain distinguishes between them.

In a recent development, A quarter looks more like four on the page, but its value is much closer to zero. In a new brain scan study, that difference mattered.

As adults compared fractions and whole numbers, brain activity followed a shared number line.

The patterns still distinguished fractions from whole numbers, but numbers close in value tended to produce more similar patterns.

One idea about how the brain handles abstract concepts is that it files related concepts close together, so that similar ideas produce similar patterns of activity.

Numbers are a good way to test that, because everyone agrees on their order.

Daniela Valério and Stanislas Dehaene of the Inserm-CEA Cognitive Neuroimaging Unit, known as Unicog, led the study at the NeuroSpin center outside Paris.

People have long been known to carry a number line in their heads for whole numbers. The team wanted to know whether fractions, which children learn after years of schooling, get added to the same line.

Twenty-two adults took part, all right-handed native French speakers between 19 and 39 who had finished high school with a science track.

Two were excluded, one for a cyst found on the scan and one who had a panic attack in the scanner.

That left 20 participants in the brain scan analysis. A faulty response button reduced the number to 19 for the analysis of speed and accuracy.

Each person lay in a high-field MRI scanner run by the French research agency CEA. The numbers 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 and the fractions 1/4, 1/3, 1/2, 2/3, 3/2, and 5/2 flashed up one at a time, each for one second.

For every number, the person pressed a button to say whether it was bigger or smaller than the one before.

Each run lasted just under ten minutes, and every number followed every other number exactly once. The fractions were built from the same digits as the whole numbers on purpose.

That let the team tell whether the brain was reading 1/4 as a quarter or as a 1 next to a 4.

Fractions slowed people down. A comparison between two fractions took about 1.2 seconds on average, against 0.8 seconds between two whole numbers.

People also got 9.5 percent of fraction pairs wrong, compared with 3.7 percent of whole number pairs.

In an interview with Earth.com, Valério said fractions are harder for a few reasons.

Whole numbers follow a simple counting sequence, so 3 is followed by 4, while there is always another fraction between any two different numbers.

Industry observers note that the size of a fraction is also not always easy to judge from the numbers in it. “For example, although 5 is greater than 3, 1/5 is smaller than 1/3,” Valério said.

It has been revealed that numbers that were close together were also harder than numbers far apart, whichever kind they were. The closer two numbers were, the slower and less accurate people got, and the effect was strongest when both were fractions.

Psychologists have known this distance effect for whole numbers for decades, and here it held for fractions too.

According to recent reports, the team then laid out the speed and error data as a map. The 12 numbers fell along a single curved line in order of size, with the fractions among the whole numbers where their values belonged.

Industry observers note that the scans showed the same thing. Across the brain regions that handle math, the pattern of activity for each number varied along two main directions.

Industry observers note that one separated fractions from whole numbers. The other ordered all 12 numbers by size, from small to large, and ignored whether they were fractions.

The size ordering was strongest in the intraparietal sulcus, a fold near the top and back of the brain that has long been tied to math.

Recent findings suggest that researchers have also linked this region to the ability to estimate quantities. In the newly introduced study, patterns of activity in the hippocampus also reflected how close numbers were in value.

Then the team looked at single points on the cortex.

Within the front part of that parietal fold, many spots responded most to one particular number and less to its neighbors. The spots formed a map, with small numbers at the front and larger numbers farther back.

The map for fractions matched the map for whole numbers. Spots that preferred 0 also responded to 1/4, 1/3, and 1/2, and 1/4 ended up far from 4.

Valério mentioned that when people compare numbers, they have to focus on magnitude, meaning how large or small each one is.

“Our study highlights that the brain organizes this information into a shared map, much like a mental number line, “Valerio told Earth.com.

“This map brings whole numbers, such as 1 and 2, and fractions, such as 1/2 and 2/3, into the same system, according to their values.”.

In a recent development, the size map showed up in only 14 or 15 of the 20 participants, depending on the number type. The fractions also stopped at 5/2, short of the range the whole numbers covered.

Fractions and whole numbers were mixed in one stream, so people may have had to build a single line to keep up.

The authors wrote that if the two kinds had been shown in separate sessions, each might still have run from small to large. The two lines might not have lined up, though.

According to recent reports, valério remarked the study was not designed to explain why fractions are harder to learn or to find the best way to teach them. Other studies in her lab are working on that.

Her next step is to see how the shared map differs across people with different mathematical backgrounds and levels of education, and whether those differences go with mathematical ability.

How the brain represents fractions in children who are still learning them remains an open question.

In a recent development, the team has posted its data and code on the Open Science Framework. It wrote that longer scanning sessions could extend the map to more kinds of math.

The full study was published in the journal PLOS Biology.
