According to recent reports, an international team of neuroscientists has mapped the functional architecture of the human insular cortex for the first time, revealing that it operates through four distinct yet cooperating subregions rather than as a passive sensory relay. Drawing on 15 years of direct intracranial brain recordings across 87 epilepsy patients, the researchers uncovered a hierarchical network explaining how emotions and expectations actively reshape physical pain.

Source: Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf (HHU).

Sources indicate that tucked deep within the lateral sulcus beneath the folds of the frontal, parietal, and temporal lobes lies the insular cortex, or insula, one of the human brain’s most enigmatic hubs. Serving as the primary meeting point where raw physiological signals from the body converge with emotion and conscious thought, the insula shapes everything from acute physical pain and temperature sensation to visceral anxiety and self-awareness.

Yet because of its deep anatomical location, mapping precisely where these distinct functions reside—and how they communicate—has long remained a major challenge in human neuroscience.

Recent findings suggest that now, in a landmark study titled “Functional architecture of the human insula,” an international team led by Dr. Julian Quabs and Professor Dr. Dr. Svenja Caspers at Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf (HHU) and Forschungszentrum Jülich (FZJ), working alongside collaborators at Stanford University and the National Research Council (CNR) in Parma, has created the first comprehensive functional atlas of the human insula.

To peer directly into the living human insula, the team analyzed intracranial electrophysiological data collected over roughly 15 years at Stanford University Medical Center from 87 patients undergoing diagnostic monitoring for drug-resistant epilepsy.

With patient consent, temporarily implanted depth electrodes allowed investigators to deliver targeted electrical stimulation across the insula while recording real-time sensations, neural activity, and directional connectivity with surrounding cortical networks.

This rare dataset revealed that the human insula is organized into four distinct, cooperating functional zones:.

Crucially, connectivity analyses revealed a powerful directional hierarchy: the cognitive insular pole exerts far stronger downward influence over the sensory rear regions than the sensory zones exert back onto the pole.

“Our findings show that the insula is more than a relay station for bodily signals. It appears to actively shape how we experience pain and our own bodies,” explained lead author Dr. Julian Quabs, who conducted much of the investigation during a research fellowship at Stanford University.

“This points to a possible neural mechanism through which emotions and expectations can alter how we perceive pain and other physical sensations,” added senior author Professor Dr. Dr. Svenja Caspers, Director of the Institute of Anatomy I at HHU and head of the Connectivity group at INM-1, FZJ.

By showing how top-down cognitive signals from the frontal lobe and insular pole directly modulate posterior pain-processing regions, the fresh atlas offers a concrete biological circuit underlying phenomena such as the placebo effect—providing a precise anatomical roadmap for future neuromodulation and chronic pain therapies.

What is the insular cortex (insula), and what does it do?

The insula is a region of cerebral cortex folded deep inside the brain that integrates physical signals from the body—such as pain, temperature, and heartbeat, with emotions and conscious awareness, shaping how we perceive both physical sensations and ourselves.

What are the four functional regions discovered in the new insula atlas?

Researchers divided the human insula into four cooperating zones: an upper rear region with a body-part map for pain and temperature; a lower region for widespread bodily sensations; a middle front region linked to chest/abdominal sensations and anxiety; and the front tip (insular pole), which connects to the frontal lobe to direct attention.

How does this study help explain the placebo effect?

The researchers discovered that the cognitive front tip of the insula (the insular pole) communicates strongly with the frontal lobe and sends powerful top-down signals to the pain-sensing regions at the back of the insula, showing how beliefs, expectations, and emotions can actively dial down physical pain.

Functional architecture of the human insula.

The insular cortex has a central role in pain, interoception and feelings, yet its functional architecture and causal electrophysiological relationships among its subregions remain poorly understood.

Here we combine intracranial electrical stimulation and task-based electroencephalography recordings with connectivity mapping within a cytoarchitectonic atlas of the human insula in 87 participants implanted with intracranial electrodes, and identify a quadripartite functional architecture comprising four distinct regions: (1) a dorsal–posterior region encoding somatotopically organized nociceptive/thermoceptive and somatosensory signals; (2) a ventral–posterior region with access to somatic information across multiple body parts; (3) a mid-anterior region associated with visceral sensations and anxiety states; and (4) an anterior–polar region, largely silent to direct stimulation, yet showing robust activation during salience detection and change of action mode.

Critically, this anterior region, with its strong connections with the prefrontal cortex, including the anterior cingulate cortex, exerts strong and direct influence over other insular regions, while receiving less-strong indirect inputs from them, revealing an electrophysiological pathway for cognitive modulation of pain and bodily perception.

Together, these findings define a functional architecture of the human insula that links cytoarchitecture to directed and asymmetric electrophysiological interactions, with mechanistic implications for cognitive modulation of pain and interoceptive experience.
