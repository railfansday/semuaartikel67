Google turns to Blue Origin for a rocket, but its long-term problem remains

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Blue Origin confirmed Thursday that its New Glenn rocket will launch data center satellites for Google in 2029 as part of that tech giant’s Project Suncatcher program.

Google disclosed it was working on scalable orbiting data centers about a year ago and characterized the project as one of its research “moonshots.” The mission on New Glenn is designed to deploy satellites, each with computing capacity at roughly the scale of a data center rack, into Sun-synchronous orbit where they can receive nearly continuous solar energy.

In a recent development, “Project Suncatcher is a long-term moonshot to scale AI infrastructure in space by tapping into the abundant solar power in orbit,” said Travis Beals, senior director of Project Suncatcher at Google, in the news release. “This dedicated launch with Blue Origin represents a crucial milestone on our roadmap as we expand the boundaries of what’s possible for the future of compute.”.

The announcement comes as US commercial heavy lift rockets, including Upcoming Glenn, are filling up their capacity through the year 2030. The Google mission will fly on the 7×2 variant of the New Glenn rocket, the smaller version of the vehicle that has launched three times to date.

The purpose of the 2029 launch will likely be to test the ability to network solar-powered satellites equipped with AI chips and demonstrate machine-learning compute in space. In that sense, it will be a technology demonstration. Google did not say who it was contracting with to build the satellites, which may weigh something on the order of several tons.

Perhaps the bigger question for Google is not whether it can make this technology work—but whether it is tenable for a company to have ambitions for a large constellation of orbital data centers in the 2030s without its own launch vehicle.

There remain a lot of questions about the economic viability of orbital data centers, but almost everyone agrees on one universal truth: Without ultra low-cost, heavy lift capability, there is no way that data centers in space can be economically competitive with terrestrial data centers.

It has been revealed that upon announcing Project Suncatcher a year ago, Google remarked it was planning for the mid-2030s, when launch costs are projected to drop to as little as $200 per kg. The only rocket likely capable of reaching those costs during the next decade is SpaceX’s Starship vehicle, but in recent months it has become clear that SpaceX will prioritize its own payloads (including Starmind data centers) on Starship. For Google to reach its target price of $200 per kg, it would likely require a fully optimized Starship V4 (the firm is currently flying a V3 Starship) that reaches a lift capacity of approximately 200 metric tons to orbit.

It has been revealed that spaceX has powerful incentives to prioritize its own orbital computing infrastructure, potentially leaving Google dependent on whatever Starship capacity and pricing SpaceX is willing to offer outside customers. So how does Google initiative to reach orbit in the 2030s?

Recent findings suggest that perhaps this explains the new partnership with Blue Origin, which presently covers a single launch but could grow to more. By the 2030s Blue Origin should be flying the larger 9×4 variant of its New Glenn rocket, but this vehicle could face a cost disadvantage against a fully reusable, high-cadence Starship. And Blue Origin is planning its own orbital data center constellation, Project Sunrise. So again, Google could be stuck subsidizing a competitor while paying higher launch costs.

Perhaps another manufacturer, such as Rocket Lab, Relativity Space, or Stoke Space, will have a larger rocket by then.

In a recent development, but developing a super-heavy-lift rocket requires enormous capital. SpaceX has spent more than $15 billion developing Starship, according to its 2026 securities filings, and Blue Origin has likewise invested billions in developing New Glenn. In terms of time, both companies have put more than a decade of work into their large vehicles. For now, all of these other US launch companies are working through the growing pains of developing medium-lift vehicles.

According to recent reports, at this point, Google has some not-great options. It can hope that one of the newer US launch companies steps up for commercial satellite operators and decides not to pursue its own constellation. It could buy one of those newer launch companies. Or Google could hire some rocket scientists to start developing powerful rocket engines and begin chasing SpaceX and Blue Origin in launch—both of which have a 25-year head start.
