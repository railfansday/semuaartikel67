The American Genius of Madden

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Hang on at the start of David O. Russell’s Madden, as its story of the Oakland Raiders football coach John Madden shifts between semidocumentary and biopic. Russell’s uneven, sketchy narrative steadily turns into a probing human comedy. It’s also a slyly monumental tale of American genius found in an Everyman’s commonplace ambition.

The key to the film’s geniality and surprise is Nicolas Cage’s astounding portrayal of Madden, a former college athlete who blew out his knee but kept his feeling for football. Cage enters the mindset of a sports fanatic who is also an artist, devising plays and strategies that, considering his size and vision, earn him the nickname “Buddha.” But this is not one of those freak-outs that Cage is known for; he seems to transform himself into John Candy — broad-beamed with a tuft of blond hair and a convincing smile. Cage commits to this genial eccentric who, despite his unreachable idiosyncrasy, is harmless, lovable yet tough — an authentic, original American.

Industry observers note that madden became coach for the Raiders, a team known to play with sheer guts and dedication — qualities the burly Buddha shared with team owner Al Davis (Christian Bale), his wiry, sharklike best friend. This thick-and-thin Laurel-and-Hardy pair sustain an unlikely partnership. It’s one of the most remarkable depictions of brotherhood in movies — a friendship that persists over temperamental tensions, even after Madden leaves the team and pursues media entrepreneurship to sustain his love of the sport. Madden the broadcaster converts his playbook ingenuity and follows his father’s advice: “Get away with doing what you love for as long as you possibly can.”.

Russell redeems himself with the oddest-yet-normal collection of American eccentrics. Acting on the edge of parody, Cage and Bale, with their uncanny sincerity, reward Russell’s interest in quirkiness. (Madden hears Davis’s voice in his head.) The fascinating scoundrels of Russell’s terrific American Hustle were preoccupied with manipulating one another. Here, friendship, comradeship, teamwork, and family complications are genuinely recognizable. Scenes that emulate a documentary approach — Madden’s wife Virginia (gung ho comedienne Kathryn Hahn) rivals Jennifer Lawrence’s volatility when speaking directly to the camera — satirize realism to fulfill the non-cynical view of national character that Russell fumbled in the bohemian sojourn of Amsterdam. No wonder De Niro is left out of this particular silver-lined playbook.

The patchwork of styles in Madden suggests that Russell investigates American eccentricity as he goes along, finding his way back to the coast-to-coast realness that made his Flirting with Disaster so exhilarating (as in Madden’s surreal, unapologetic pitch for the unique holiday dish the Turducken). Russell adopts Madden’s football-analysis style, such as on-screen telestrator vectors, to humorously examine Madden’s own career shifts and twists of personality (Madden instructing his players, or meeting colleagues Pat Summerall, Howard Cosell, and Bob Costas).

A dull-witted filmmaker, say James Cameron, would choose an “immersive” style for a movie concerned with the gestalt of football. But Russell’s film is also about the phenomenon of a sport that becomes a vanguard of technology and culture. Madden is pursued by Trip Hawkins (John Mulaney), founder of video game manufacturer EA (Electronic Arts), who wants to translate Madden’s football theories for a latest generation of at-home fans and gamers. Hawkins’s proposal is scored to Beethoven’s Ninth — another example of Russell’s wit, matching the film’s eclectic score from Barry White and Talking Heads to N.W.A. (“You don’t want to know what that means,” Davis says to Madden.).

Through Madden and Hawkins’s acumen, Russell treats the discovery of a new world as an aftereffect of folk intelligence and ingenuity. Genius is kidded when Hawkins’s team of four-eyed computer nerds demonstrate jock potential after applying Madden’s plays to their algorithms. It’s a sweet way of conveying the phenomenon that would turn EA’s Madden NFL video game franchise into a $4 billion enterprise (some estimates raise that into double digits), with annual compensation going to the Madden family estate.

More important, Russell (who co-wrote the script with Cambron Clark) sees Madden as a visionary who proudly summarizes TV sportscasting as “They state the obvious, which is f***ing boring.” My only complaint is that Robert Richardson’s photography tends toward frowzy (perhaps deliberately aping TV docs), not as splendid as Oliver Stone’s Any Given Sunday — the greatest football movie ever made.

Madden itself shares biopic sensibility with John Ford’s The Wings of Eagles, respectfully eschewing its subject’s mortality for an optimistic appreciation of a man’s potential. It is unique to American character and the experience of competition and dreams of the NFL Hall of Fame that Madden shared with Al Davis. Russell’s tribute comes complete with a motto: “You make people feel good and alive and good to be American, and I’m not standing in the way of that.” Thus Russell proves his own resilience and the achievement of a latest folkloric genre struggling to emerge.

Armond White is National Review’s culture critic and the author of New Position: The Prince Chronicles and Make Spielberg Great Again: The Steven Spielberg Chronicles.
