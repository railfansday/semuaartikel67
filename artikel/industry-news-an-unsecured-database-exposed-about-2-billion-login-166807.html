📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

A database containing about 2 billion login records for sites across the internet was accessible online without a password, security researcher Jeremiah Fowler mentioned in a report published Tuesday by ExpressVPN. The collection included usernames and passwords tied to email, social media, shopping and financial services. Among the files was an ad offering access to email accounts sorted by geography.

The 2 billion figure is an estimate and not representative of the number of people affected, since some accounts may appear more than once and one person can have dozens of logins saved on a single device. Fowler reported the database to its hosting provider, which restricted public access several days later. It’s unclear how long the files had been exposed or who else may have accessed them.

It has been revealed that fowler’s estimate of about 2 billion login records applies to the database he found. Wired put the total at more than 3.5 billion by including a separate database that security firm UpGuard disclosed last week. That database held about 1.64 billion records, including Chinese national ID numbers, phone numbers and school enrollment records.

Some of the files Fowler reviewed appeared to be from infostealers — malicious software that collects saved passwords and other data from infected devices. Once that information is collected, brokers can buy it in bulk, sort it by country or account type and resell it in smaller batches. A buyer interested in email accounts in a particular country can pay for that slice of the data. The people selling it can make money from the same records more than once.

Fowler saw signs that the files came from multiple sources, but couldn’t determine who assembled the collection or whether it was used for sales, research or another purpose.

I saw a version of that business in my reporting on the dark web for CNET. On Genesis Market, buyers could shop for stolen accounts by location and category, including banking, email and social media. By the time authorities disrupted the marketplace in 2023, it had offered access to data from more than 1.5 million compromised computers, according to the Justice Department.

Recent findings suggest that you can look up your email address in Have I Been Pwned to see whether it appears in a known data exposure. A clean result only tells you what’s in the service’s index.

According to recent reports, if you see an unfamiliar login or suspect your device is infected, use a different, trusted device to change the affected password and sign out of other sessions. Start with your email if several accounts may be affected, since that’s where password reset links often go. Replace reused passwords with unique ones, use a password manager to keep track of them and turn on two-factor authentication where it’s available.
