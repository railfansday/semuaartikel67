10 Reasons You Will Be Playing GTA 6 For More Than a Decade

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

After a week of GTA 6's availability, there are two things I can say with complete certainty: first, Red Dead Redemption 2 is the best thing that could have happened to Rockstar, and second, we'll be following Jason, Lucia, and company for a long time.

The developer's magnum opus offers an immersive and expansive world that will take us many years to fully decipher, so I can already imagine how numerous ecosystems, from video games to content creation, will be completely permeated until, perhaps, the release of the seventh installment.

However, far from wanting to theorize about future adventures of the IP, the priority is to appreciate the one we already have in our hands and savor it slowly, since that's what the game itself aims for by offering a story, mechanics, and context that evolve at a much more leisurely pace.

Therefore, in order to play fortune teller and anticipate how long we will be hanging around Leonida, I invite you to read this list of ten reasons you will be playing GTA 6 for more than a decade.

Analyzing the evolution of video games, especially considering their affinity with technology, is an activity that involves intertemporal comparisons, and GTA 6 has established itself as the generational benchmark we look to when we want to see if the industry has moved forward.

Titles like The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time, BioShock, The Elder Scrolls V: Skyrim, and The Last of Us Part II have stood for years as monoliths of their respective genres and platforms, garnering enduring attention that regularly draws us back to reaffirm their influence.

Thus, considering the technical, graphical, and design marvel that is Rockstar's title, particularly within the context of open worlds, it will be impossible for it to escape associations with future representatives of the genre, who will undoubtedly seek to learn from it.

Although comparisons are generally odious, it's also true that they constitute a timely method to contrast the evolution, or involution, of a medium, so I can already see many scholars of this art revisiting its confines to diagnose its eventual state.

While I acknowledge the omnipresence of the internet and our hyper-connected state has made the task of hunting for secrets virtually impossible, I know I'm not the only one who has deliberately avoided all discussion about the game in order to discover it for myself.

By choosing not to use guides, watch gameplay videos, or even compare opinions, the game's lifespan is instantly extended due to its immeasurable size and content, much of which is hidden behind hundreds of hours of spontaneous and meticulous exploration.

You may finish the campaign and feel satisfied with what you've experienced, but for a game that has taken so long to arrive and generated so much anticipation, I'm sure that a large portion of its player base will continue playing until there's no dialogue, NPC, mission, weapon, or random event left undiscovered.

This largely depends on how much you choose to interact with its online discourse, as you will surely encounter spoilers of all kinds as the days and months go by, yet a whole second life awaits you if you choose to turn a blind eye to other people's experiences and strive to discover everything on your own.

Assuming I'm not the only oddball who's spent countless hours wandering around open-world games listening to their tunes without engaging in any particular activity, GTA 6's deep and outstanding soundtrack will behave like a music player with extra steps.

In fact, a large part of my GTA 5 playtime consisted of countless nights spent hopping into any legitimately acquired vehicle and cruising around the city's interiors and exteriors, and after just an hour of playing this new installment, I already knew the future would be the same.

Whether you enjoy pop, urban music, rap, or listening to the radio as if it were real life (which it is closer to than I'd like to admit), there's a station for every type of listener, helping the game also become an album that will remind you of the era in which it was released.

Never underestimate the power of songs as tools to prolong our connection with a video game because, just like with Secunda in Skyrim or I Really Want To Stay at Your House in Cyberpunk 2077, I'm sure RHYNO will have us going around in circles in Vice City for a very, very long time.

A major surprise I encountered in my first few hours of playing GTA 6 was the influence of immersive sims on its mission design, as there isn't just one way to solve many of the main and side objectives in the campaign.

Obviously, we're not talking about the depth of an Arkane Studios title, though there's enough density and player agency to allow for diverse approaches, ranging from peaceful resolutions if you know who to talk to, to stealthy sieges where no one necessarily has to be killed.

Add to that the game's inherent spontaneity, which throws you into special and unexpected encounters every second, all waiting to grab your attention, and you realize its replayability stems from a design specifically intended to be open and multifaceted.

I hope GTA 6's approach helps the genre emerge from its slumber, but while I invoke every possible deity to make that happen, I'm content to return to Leonida every few years to see what latest interactions Rockstar prepared without me noticing in my first five playthroughs.

In conjunction with the above, the title places a notable emphasis on role-playing elements, influencing both the story and gameplay according to how the player chooses to interact with their world, allowing Jason and Lucia to be canvases with clear forms yet ultimately emphasized by the player.

Between choosing the type of relationship they develop, making choices that influence the narrative at key plot points, and the constant decision-making involved in adhering to (or not) morality and law, you can have completely different experiences if you shift your mindset enough.

In this sense, for my first playthrough, I have a more restrained and rigorous Jason and a more antisocial and rebellious Lucia within a loving relationship, always acting in accordance with these personality archetypes. Regardless, for my second playthrough, I plan to take a completely opposite path.

The result? At the very least, one more run with hundreds of hours and variations that will make me even more amazed by the immeasurable talent of the Rockstar workers, to whom I feel I owe the respect of playing the game at least three times to properly appreciate the countless hours they invested in shaping the product.

The third of my three planned playthroughs, designed to fully exploit the game, will consist of not killing anyone unless the game forces me to, which brings us to the mechanism that has kept hundreds of video games alive online: challenge runs.

Recent findings suggest that beating the entire game with only fists, without breaking any laws, without using money, with only a knife, using only public transport, in the shortest time feasible, as the worst possible partner... I don't know if the possibilities are truly infinite, but they certainly come close.

Games like Dark Souls and Hollow Knight have demonstrated for years that human creativity knows no bounds, especially when it comes to creating content that borders on masochism, so it's only natural that GTA 6 will be like a second home for all these inventive people.

I don't know if I'll be among them, yet my experience being chronically online assures me with complete certainty that GTA 6 will sustain entire communities based on challenges, rising exponentially if mods are added to the equation.

Heraclitus stated it's impossible to step into the same river twice because everything changes, both in the river itself and in the person crossing it, and I believe the mythical Greek philosopher made this aphorism with the belief that we would eventually have the boundless body of water that is GTA 6.

Experiences with open worlds, like the aforementioned Skyrim or, more recently, Elden Ring, tend to be special thanks to the organic nature of their worlds, given you feel like you're in an alternate, digital reality where you're truly part of a larger cosmos.

Leonida, however, takes these sensations to uncharted territory, where it genuinely feels impossible to experience the same thing twice because of how reactive and multifaceted its systems are, like hundreds of thousands of windows that only open if you're in the right place at the right time.

This contextual fertility ensures that there will almost always be a latest experience to go through, and as long as the flame of belief that there are unexplored horizons remains alive (something that GTA 5 already demonstrated), there will be parishioners who remain absorbed watching the fire, waiting to hear an unprecedented crackle.

The fear of missing out on a cultural event is fascinating because, contrary to popular belief, it can have a timeless quality; for example, it's the reason I played the first iterations of Red Dead Redemption and The Last of Us, even though it was a decade after their respective releases.

Given GTA 6 is and will be a cultural touchstone of modern entertainment, many will return to its realm years later to satisfy a curiosity they perhaps didn't solve at launch for one reason or another, ranging from waiting for a price drop to the complete optimization of its features.

Beyond the causes, and considering a legacy that is already titanic after just one week of release and will become even more staggering as time goes on, it seems that playing GTA 6 will be like watching The Godfather or reading the Odyssey: an essential ritual for understanding the path its medium will take in the future.

Even if you're not a fan of the series or the genre, the curiosity to understand the reasons behind its multi-million sales and resounding unanimous praise as a masterpiece on Metacritic will be compelling for those who value video games as an art form, since great works always deserve attention, even if it's to criticize them.

Rockstar has found its closest model to follow in GTA 5, the same recipe that allowed them to maintain the product's relevance for over ten years with virtually no significant drop in popularity or impact, whose success hinges on spacing out re-releases, which will rekindle the unresolved desires of a significant portion of the gaming community.

Motivated by the release of the PC version and potential upcoming consoles from Microsoft and Sony, or even by the anticipation of a product with as few bugs and inconsistencies as possible, many people will refrain from jumping on the bandwagon at its peak, instead patiently waiting for the waters to calm down.

The 235 million copies sold of its predecessor didn't happen overnight; they materialized gradually and steadily year after year, and it's quite easy to predict that market behavior this time around will be virtually the same.

Many of them are repeat buyers, of course, but that doesn't invalidate the title's statement: whether it's your first time or your fiftieth, the hardware will give you very appealing reasons to discover or check out how GTA 6 is, even if it's 2036.

However, by far, the biggest reason to be sure you'll be playing GTA 6 for more than ten years lies in its multiplayer mode, a fundamental pillar that sustained GTA 5 throughout its lifespan and which, without a doubt, will repeat that feat with this newly introduced installment.

If we assume Rockstar will be able to create a product that surpasses its predecessor as the base game did, the only sensible conclusion about the next iteration of GTA Online is that it will be a candidate for the most massive, complex, and profitable multiplayer experience of all time.

Phenomena like Fortnite or League of Legends cast doubt on such a claim, but the fact that this is even the scale used to measure GTA 6's multiplayer is indicative enough of what it will mean for the franchise and the market once it's released.

More than the sheer scale of its world, more than sociocultural pressure, and more than even any traditional DLC, what could allow Rockstar to go two generations without releasing a game and still be the most popular developer in the world is the online variant of GTA 6.

Not only because of its name and legacy, which also mean a great deal, but because of the game's inherent quality, which suggests that its size, freedom of action, and overall quality will be remarkable enough to carry the Grand Theft Auto name for as long as it lasts.
