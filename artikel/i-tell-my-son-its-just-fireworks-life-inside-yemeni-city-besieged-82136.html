'I tell my son it's just fireworks': Life inside Yemeni city besieged by Houthis

Houthi attacks on Taiz have killed more than 140 civilians since August, local government officials say.

In the Yemeni city of Taiz, ringed by jagged mountains and Houthi rebel fighters, Ashwaq has only one way to try to soothe her frightened child - lie.

Sources indicate that "When my 11-year-old son hears a loud sound, I tell him, 'Don't be afraid, it's just fireworks'," she claims. "I don't want him to panic, but deep down I know he is old enough to understand what is happening around him. He has lived through war for most of his life.".

Taiz has long been a prize - and a front line - in Yemen's civil war.

Houthi forces tightened their grip last month, fully encircling the government-controlled city, and the population - estimated to be between 600,000 and 1 million.

Since then, Yemen's third-largest city has been cut off from the world, and its residents have been largely unheard. We reached seven people inside the city who mapped out the anatomy of the siege.

It has been revealed that they gave detailed accounts of the Houthis' death grip on Taiz. Only a minimal amount of food can get in. Prices for staples like rice, sugar and cooking oil have doubled or even trebled. Essential supplies, like medicine, water and fuel, are dwindling.

Burning piles of rubbish are producing toxic smoke that hangs over the city.

On streets that used to hum with traffic and background chatter, a silence has fallen. Schools are closed and restaurants are shuttered. Suffering is being heaped on suffering. Piles of rubbish are trapped inside the city, alongside the people. When residents try to get rid of it by burning it, there is a toxic smoke. Even the air is now a Houthi weapon.

From positions on the hills, rebel fighters - and snipers - can wage war on the city below.

In a recent development, estimates for the death toll vary widely. At the Office for Siege Affairs, a local government department, they say 148 civilians have been killed by Houthi attacks since August.

According to recent reports, some of those we contacted in Taiz do not want to be identified because they fear for their safety. Many in the city are reluctant to even speak on the phone because the Houthis control key mobile carriers.

Industry observers note that "Sometimes it feels like the city has become a huge prison," indicates Ashwaq, a teacher and mother of three, who only wanted her first name to be used. She yearns for the old Taiz.

"It used to be a lively city," she states. "People loved life, worked hard, visited one another, and had hopes for the future, despite all the difficulties. Now, we hardly go out anymore. The [Houthi] shelling can happen any time, and sniper fire near the front lines makes even a short trip dangerous.".

She keeps her son and two elder daughters indoors.

"I worry about them every single day, every hour, and honestly every minute," she indicates.

Taiz has been a major battleground in Yemen's civil war for more than a decade.

War is not fresh to Taiz. The strategic city is the gateway to Aden - the temporary seat of the internationally recognised government - and to southern Yemen.

Recent findings suggest that houthi rebels - backed by Iran - reached the outskirts of Taiz in 2015, cutting off roads, and laying siege from three sides. A year earlier, they had seized Yemen's capital, Sanaa.

Recent findings suggest that more than a decade on, the government, which is weak and fractured, has been unable to reclaim the capital and much of north-western Yemen. That's despite the backing of Saudi Arabia, and its air strikes.

In recent months, the conflict has flared up and expanded after a four-year lull. The Houthis made a lightning advance, taking territory from the government, and striking Saudi Arabia with missiles and drones.

A long-running civil war has become a threat to regional stability.

In Taiz, it is a threat to survival.

An emergency room doctor, whom we are calling Yaser, bears witness to that.

Among the war wounded recently brought to his hospital were four children, shot by Houthi snipers. They usually aim for the head and chest, he tells us. Two of the four children died.

The hardest thing he faces, as a father himself, is treating wounded children. "I have to rise to the responsibility, compose myself and support the families," he indicates. "It's a difficult situation that I cannot even put into words, when I see a father looking at his badly wounded child right in front of him.".

It has been revealed that most of the injured are victims of shelling. Reaching the hospital can be an ordeal. Some families have to walk for hours from outlying areas.

According to recent reports, they arrive exhausted, like the doctors who will treat them, and who are struggling with "everything", claims Yaser. Even sterilising fluid for surgical instruments is in short supply.

He tells us the hospital is running on empty, and can carry on for two more weeks, "maybe".

Taiz's hospitals have lost 70% of their capacity and 120 health centres have closed, its governor says.

If the emergency rooms are busy, so are the graveyards.

"Funerals heading to the cemeteries have become the defining scene in Taiz, around the clock," notes Taiz's Governor, Nabil Shamsan.

It's not just bombs and bullets that kill, he tells us.

Sources indicate that "The people of Taiz are losing their lives due to supply shortages, lack of food, and loss of income," he states. "Every single day, we bury dozens of Taiz residents.".

We have not been able to independently verify these figures.

Sources indicate that he remains inside the city, and inside the siege. "I live among my people," he says.

On a video call, he looks weary but delivers a lengthy chronicle of a city being starved of oxygen - literally.

He tells us that hospitals have lost 70% of their capacity, and 120 health centres have closed. Diseases are spreading, neonatal deaths have increased, and malnutrition is surging. Hundreds of kidney patients are not getting dialysis because they can't get to hospitals or because fuel is short when they do.

Industry observers note that the Houthi leader, Abdul Malik al-Houthi, has denied Taiz is besieged. Late last month, he claimed that routes remained open for food, medicine and humanitarian aid.

That is denied by the government, residents of Taiz, aid agencies and the United Nations.

What's at stake in the battle for the city is more than territory, says Shamsan.

He states the Houthis - who mostly follow a branch of Shia Islam - want to change the character of this predominantly Sunni city, long known as a seat of learning and a bastion of resistance.

Industry observers note that "Everyone knows that Taiz is a hub of culture, a city characterised by freedom, political pluralism and all forms of civic life," he says. In sharp contrast, he says, the Houthis aim to impose their strict religious doctrine and drag the city "back to the dark ages".

Whatever the pressure on the city and its people, the governor insists the Houthis will never win.

How long can the city hold out, I ask.

"Until the population perishes," he replies.

Essential supplies, like medicine, water and fuel, are dwindling.

They are defending their city, alongside relatives, friends, neighbours, and five brigades of pro-government troops, who can't do the job on their own.

They don't have heavy weapons or enough numbers for the expanding front line.

Training camps have sprung up for volunteers, but the lessons can be as brief as a day or two before they are sent to join the fight.

Akram (not his real name) is already a veteran. He's 31, with a child on the way.

When Taiz was fully encircled before, in 2015, he broke away from his studies and took up arms. In that round of fighting, the Houthis gained control of his district on the outskirts, and he was uprooted. He hasn't seen his old home since then.

Last month, when Taiz was completely cut off once again, he bought a rifle, paying about $1,200 (Â£910) for a Chinese version of a Kalashnikov. It used to cost less than half that sum.

He tells us this fight is different to 2015.

"This time the Houthis have new technology and new missiles," he notes. "They can wage a war in the air with drones. We fear the death of our families and our civilians, and ourselves.".

He has already lost four friends in the past month, who left behind wives and children.

It has been revealed that but Akram, and many others, see defending Taiz as their duty. He indicates that "all the sons of Taiz" will fight the Houthis "and their extremism".

The armed group is presently designated as a foreign terrorist organisation by the US (which has gone back and forth on the glitch) but not by the UK or the European Union.

It has been revealed that "They say God gave them the right to rule everyone in Yemen. We don't accept that," states Akram. "We live in freedom. We cannot surrender; we cannot leave. We cannot breathe without our city.".

Almost 350,000 people have been displaced by the fighting in recent months.

But it can be suffocating inside Taiz, where nightfall brings no rest.

According to recent reports, "Every night we have bombing and shelling," indicates Ahmad Al-Basha, a photographer. "I think the Houthis prefer to fight at night. Maybe they don't want us and our children to sleep inside our homes.".

In a recent development, the people of Taiz feel let down, he tells us, by their government and by the international community. "We feel frustrated and sad," he claims, "because we don't see any move to help us.".

Many believe the Houthis will keep a chokehold on Taiz from the outside, and exert significant control over the city without trying to enter it.

But could they fight their way in?

Ahmad believes it won't come to that but fears the worst if it does.

"Maybe they will clean Taiz of any resistance, kill people and settle scores with anyone who is against them," he indicates.

His greatest concern is for his children, who cannot escape the sounds of war.

Like Ashwaq, the teacher, he tries to explain it away.

"I tell them it's a party," he notes, "and if it's really loud, I say it's a major party.".

As Taiz endures bombing, shelling, and a sense of abandonment laced with growing hunger, Ashwaq has a parent's plea for the international community.

"Please don't forget Taiz," she notes. "We are ordinary families. We want our children to study, play and dream like other children around the world. We are not just numbers in news reports.".
