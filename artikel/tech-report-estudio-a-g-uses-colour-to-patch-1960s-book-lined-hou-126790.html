Local studio Estudio A-G has foregrounded a sculptural timber structural element while implementing bold colours for the renovation of a 1960s house in Mexico City.

Originally built in 1962 by Jaime Ortiz Monasterio, Casa Magnolia is located in the residential neighbourhood of San Ángel.

In a recent development, monasterio designed the home as a domestic library for his brother, who was an avid reader and book collector. Over the decades, the house was renovated multiple times, obscuring the home’s original character.

In 2025, Andrés Gutiérrez, head of Estudio A-G, was commissioned to reinterpret the interior of the 275-square-metre residence for new inhabitants, preserving the architectural qualities that made the original design unique.

“The restoration was extremely respectful of the house’s original architecture, but we never wanted the result to feel nostalgic,” Gutiérrez told Dezeen.

“Quite the opposite – once restored, the house needed to feel completely fresh, fresh and contemporary, which was exactly what the clients were looking for.”.

Recent findings suggest that completed in February 2026, the design uses colour to achieve the transformation, bringing new energy to the original architecture without competing with it. The studio aimed to have the house be recognisably Mexican without falling into tropes.

“I wanted the house to feel unmistakably Mexican, but without relying on folklore or the usual clichés associated with ‘Mexican interiors’.” he remarked.

“I was much more interested in expressing that identity through materials, craftsmanship, colour and contemporary Mexican design.”.

At the centre of the house, a dark-toned timber structure rises through a series of split levels like a sculptural tree connecting the entrance hall, living room, dining room, study and reading areas before unfolding into a double-height ceiling.

“More than a structural element, it serves as a spatial anchor, establishing continuity throughout the interior and reinforcing the vertical dimension of the house,” Gutiérrez stated, explaining that wood acts as a warm, unifying element throughout the house.

The studio restored and reintegrated the original wood flooring, ceiling finishes and structural elements. It also reinstalled most of the original wooden bookshelves that had been dismantled and stored.

“Overall, the intervention focused on restoring the elements that were worth preserving while introducing the necessary contemporary updates to make the house fully functional for the way its new owners live today,” he said.

The studio replaced some window frames and all the glass, updated the lighting and electrical systems and rebuilt the bathrooms and kitchen.

However, one of the most significant interventions were the colour blocks and new materials included in the space.

“Colour plays a central role in the project,” Gutiérrez remarked, calling out soft blue walls that are a backdrop for deep plum sofas, moss green dining chairs, lilac rugs, green serpentine stone and terracotta-toned marble.

“Rather than acting as decoration, colour is used as an architectural tool to organise space, establish hierarchy, and shape atmosphere.”.

It has been revealed that he also designed custom furniture that pairs with historic pieces – like Antonio Attolini’s 1969 armchairs – and contemporary Mexican art. Works by Cecilia Barreto, Francisco Muñoz, Jorge Rosano, Daniel Berman and Adriana Silva embed culture into everyday life.

Previously, Estudio A-G designed a rooftop bar as a “matriarchal universe,” complete with crocodile-shaped furniture and shrine-like water, for a boutique hotel in a former Spanish colonial manor house in San Miguel de Allende.
