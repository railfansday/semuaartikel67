📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The Federal Communications Commission ordered California to stop enforcing its Carrier of Last Resort (COLR) rules against AT&T, setting up another battle between the Trump administration and California over the state’s right to enforce its own telecom laws and regulations.

According to recent reports, AT&T’s status as a Carrier of Last Resort in California requires it to offer telephone service to any potential customer in its territory. AT&T wants to stop offering basic phone service to new customers and discontinue service for 184,000 residential customers and 15,000 business customers on June 1, 2027.

Recent findings suggest that the order released today granted AT&T’s request to preempt state rules, following up on the FCC’s previous approval of AT&T’s petition to discontinue service. AT&T had tried to achieve the same result in court, but a federal judge rejected its request to override California’s rules in July.

It has been revealed that the FCC order probably isn’t the last word, as California could sue the commission. California has stood alone against AT&T, which has received relief from COLR obligations in 20 of the 21 states in its wireline service territory.

“California’s Carrier of Last Resort framework may have made sense when it was established 30 years ago,” FCC Chairman Brendan Carr noted in a statement released with today’s order. “But those rules were designed for a bygone era and no longer make sense in today’s competitive, advanced marketplace.”.

If AT&T ends service on its old copper lines, many homes and businesses would not be offered modern fiber networks. AT&T claims a wireless home phone service that connects to the cellular network will be good enough, and the FCC agreed.

In a recent development, “The FCC has granted AT&T’s applications to replace legacy voice service with a modern, IP-based alternative in certain areas of the state. California’s state requirements are blocking AT&T from proceeding with a federally authorized transition,” Carr noted.

California indicates its rules allow wireless replacements if they meet the state’s standards for basic phone service, but that AT&T must demonstrate that its wireless phone service would meet the COLR obligations. California’s requirements include flat-rate options for unlimited voice calls, discounted Lifeline rates for eligible low-income customers, and telephone relay service for people who are deaf and hard of hearing.

Industry observers note that FCC Democrat Anna Gomez voted against the order, saying that state telecom commissions “are often the last line of defense for consumers. They know their communities, they take their complaints, and they answer to the people who rely on these services in a way that a federal agency in Washington cannot always match.”.

Gomez stated the California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC) has an open proceeding on how to modernize its COLR rules, and that the FCC should let that process play out. Large technology transitions work best “when the state regulator is a partner with a real role,” but the FCC is treating the CPUC “as an obstacle to be cleared,” she said.

The CPUC “proceeding is the right place for California to weigh what a modern basic service requirement should look like, with input from the people who will actually be making that switch,” Gomez mentioned. “AT&T has said it does not initiative to discontinue service before June 1, 2027, which leaves time for the CPUC to finish its work. Rather than wait, this item tells the CPUC to write its new consumer protections around a federal ruling it opposed.”.

In March, the FCC issued an order that made it easier for carriers to discontinue copper networks. The order asserted that state rules are subject to preemption if they conflict with the FCC’s discontinuance authorizations and authority. AT&T cited the order in its lawsuit against California.

Recent findings suggest that trump officials and telecom companies have been trying to block California’s rules for years. The first Trump administration lost an attempt to preempt state net neutrality laws throughout the country in 2019, and California later defeated ISPs in a lawsuit that challenged its net neutrality law in court.

In a recent development, california’s net neutrality law and other telecom regulations are again being threatened by rules the Trump administration applied to federal grants, but California rejected US demands to stop enforcing its laws this month. In that case, California may have to choose between enforcing its telecom laws and receiving $1.42 billion to deploy broadband to 270,571 locations.
