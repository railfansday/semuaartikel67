4 Ways Parents Can Support Teen Online Safety and Well-Being

Sources indicate that I recently asked 30 young people what they wished their parents understood about their online lives. One young person’s story highlights how easily parents can overlook online risks.

Blake (not their real name) was in fifth grade when an adult approached them on Amino, a now-defunct social media platform. Their conversation rapidly evolved into what Blake thought of as a romantic relationship.

Looking back, Blake told me, “It was obviously incredibly unsafe,” and added, “I wish someone had found out about it earlier on.”.

It has been revealed that blake never told their parents because they believed that they were “doing something wrong.” While Blake’s parents monitored their YouTube watch history, Blake now wishes their parents had engaged in more open conversations about their online life and set “more (tech) restrictions early on.” Contrary to what many parents might think, Blake didn’t want to be left alone. They wanted their parents to ask them about their online life and set clearer boundaries, making it easier to ask for help.

In my conversations, young people shared four practical tips for talking with teens about their online lives and supporting their online safety and well-being.

Due to fear of punishment or disapproval, young people often hide their online lives from parents. This secrecy is driven by the same reason teens don’t share that they were offered a drink at a party. They’re afraid it will get them in trouble, or they'll be cut off from their friends.

In a recent development, one young LGBTQ+ woman, for example, said that her very religious dad monitored her social media feeds and told her to delete any post he disagreed with. He did the same to her adult siblings. This didn’t stop her from posting. Instead, she learned how to moderate what he could see.

According to recent reports, other young people described what happened when parents responded with curiosity rather than judgment. When one young woman’s mom found her secret Twitter account, she expected to be grounded “for a million years.” Instead, her mom calmly asked who she was talking to. The young woman said that her mom’s response made room for conversation and connection.

When teens feel like they have to hide their online lives, they’re less likely to come to their parents when something goes wrong. Young people told us that when parents act as digital coaches, staying calm and walking them through difficult situations, they build online safety skills, and their relationships with their parents grow stronger. Being a coach doesn’t mean ignoring risks or abandoning rules. It means listening, understanding what happened, and helping your teen work through difficult situations.

Recent findings suggest that existing research and our conversations with young people both point to the importance of talking with teens about their online lives. Similar to talking to teens about other sensitive topics like drinking and sex, it’s more effective to have the conversation before it's relevant. Think of it less like a “Sit down; we have to talk” lecture and more like a series of low-pressure chats.

There are many good conversation guides for parents, including from Internet Matters, Common Sense Media, and the Spark and Stitch Institute. By using nonjudgmental, open-ended questions such as “What’s something you’ve enjoyed online lately?” or “How do you feel about your school’s AI policy?” parents can show curiosity without making teens defensive. Current events, like the recent Meta settlement, can also serve as a springboard for conversation.

Recent findings suggest that it can help to keep expectations for these conversations low. Even the best-timed and well-intentioned conversations are likely to be met with eye rolls by teens. That’s OK. As teen psychologist and author Lisa Damour explains, “Your (kid) may roll her eyes, but do it anyway. (Kids) can listen and roll their eyes at the same time.” Her podcast episode, “Untangling All Things Digital for Parents,” is worth a listen.

As a parent, I was surprised and heartened to learn that not all tech rules feel restrictive to teens. Several were even grateful for them. One teen boy I spoke with said, “[My parents] make me leave my phone downstairs … I think that's a good idea because I don't wanna be using my phone. I'm trying to sleep because I have to wake up really early.”.

Recent findings suggest that this aligns with recent research showing that teens and young adults value solo downtime, face-to-face time with friends, and sleep even more than they value time spent online. Parents can respect their teens’ desire for independence by inviting them to help draft family “tech agreements” based on shared values such as sleep, time with friends, and privacy. Internet Matters offers a family tech agreement template that works well for tweens and younger teens, and Screenagers provides broader, more flexible guidance. Use these templates as starting points and revisit the agreement as your teen’s needs and independence change.

It has been revealed that young people told us that their parents’ tech use shaped their own, for better and worse. One young adult stated a lack of healthy boundaries at home left “no real role model to follow.” Another said her mother now tells her to ask an AI chatbot before coming to her for advice.

Industry observers note that modeling healthy habits is one of the most powerful tools parents have. When parents spend a lot of time on digital media, their teens tend to do so as well. Moreover, teens who feel like their parents are more focused on their devices than on them report higher depression. Parents can strengthen their relationship and teach healthy tech habits at the same time by holding themselves to the same standards that they set for their teen. This might look like modeling the importance of sleep by agreeing that everyone in the house, including parents, keeps phones out of the bedroom after 10 p.m.

It has been revealed that you don’t need to model perfection. You can acknowledge when you get distracted or struggle to maintain a boundary. Saying, “I’ve been checking my phone too much during dinner, so I’m going to leave it in the other room,” makes your own effort visible. Shared expectations show that healthy tech habits are a family responsibility, not just something parents impose on teens.

It has been revealed that parents are being asked to make high-stakes decisions about fast-changing technology, often without shared norms or practical support. They shouldn’t have to carry this burden alone, but tech companies and federal regulatory agencies aren’t adequately stepping up.

Fortunately, you don’t need to be a tech expert to listen to teens, set thoughtful boundaries, and make it easier for your teen to ask for help. What helps teens most is a strong, trusting relationship with their parents. Research backs this up: A warm parent-child relationship, alongside clear boundaries, predicts safer internet use among teens. Over time, a compassionate and collaborative approach can help teens navigate technology skillfully, with less secrecy and power struggles and more mutual respect.
