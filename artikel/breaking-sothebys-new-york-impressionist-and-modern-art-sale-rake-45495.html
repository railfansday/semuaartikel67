Sources indicate that SOTHEBY’S SUPERLATIVES. It was an evening of superlatives at Sotheby’s, whose two auctions of Impressionist and modern art in Latest York last night raked in a total of $777.8 million, reports ARTnews. The lump sum is the highest for a single night at the auction house, surpassing the $706 million it achieved a year ago at the same time. The highlights from the evening included the unexpected sale of a tiny Claude Monet with a wild backstory, There was also Vincent van Gogh’s Châtaigniers en fleurs (1890), which sold to dealer William Acquavella for $190.4 million with fees, after a week dominated by a Nazi-era restitution dispute. The sum was more than Sotheby’s bargained for and beat the artist’s previous record by just over 62 percent, marking a clear victory for the auction house. But there were hitches, including a Remedios Varo drawing that failed to sell, and three lots were withdrawn while the sale was in progress. According to recent reports, ROUTING OUT PINO RAUTI. Italian lawmakers have ordered the removal of imagery containing fascist references from an exhibition in Rome marking the centenary of far-right politician Pino Rauti, who died in 2012, reports Reuters. The exhibit at the Palazzo Valdina complex, sponsored by the Italian culture ministry, has caused an uproar in Italy, where Rauti is remembered for joining Benito Mussolini’s Nazi-collaborating government during World War II and later becoming a leader of the neo-fascist Italian Social Movement (MSI). The controversial content, which was reportedly removed by Wednesday, included a photograph of Rauti beneath a flag bearing a Celtic cross linked to neo-fascism, while the figure of Mussolini was also given pride of place. Rauti never denounced his past, but that didn’t stop him from being voted into Italy’s lower house of parliament for five terms, until 1992. The order to take down parts of the exhibition was sparked by demands from left-leaning members of parliament, who called the whole endeavor “a disgrace.”.

Sources indicate that BACKWARDS BEAUTY. One might be tempted to think that current beauty standards were whipped into the collective consciousness via modern mass media, but a upcoming study confirms the source goes much further back and can be found glaringly apparent in historic paintings. Research published in Proceedings of the Royal Society B analyzes hundreds of paintings throughout history to demonstrate how Western beauty standards for women have evolved away from the curvy female ideal to straighter and androgynous silhouettes over the past 300 years, reports the Times of London. Authors of the study examined paintings of upper-class women in Western paintings from 1650 to 1950, and asked over 1,500 people to judge them for things like waist-to-hip ratio. Little surprise: Women’s bodies tended to move from the hourglass shape once deemed the ideal beauty associated with fertility to a straighter, “more tubular” silhouette. To Jeanne Bovet, co-author of the study, the artworks are seen as “cognitive fossils,” or relics of vanished societies and their values. “People have always paid attention to appearance,” stated Bovet. “And usually it’s because they have wanted to send a signal.” It seems to have worked.
