Experts Warn That a Deadly Disease That Causes ‘Immune Amnesia’ Is Surging in the U.S.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

This week, the United States officially lost its measles elimination status, after 26 years. The Pan American Health Organization (PAHO), which oversees measles elimination status, said in a statement on Tuesday that the U.S., along with Bolivia and Mexico, “lost their status after the same measles virus lineage circulated uninterrupted for 12 months or more.”.

It has been revealed that but measles cases have surged in the U.S. throughout 2026. The most recent CDC surveillance stats on measles report 5,412 confirmed cases in the country, with 47 states affected. Given that measles hasn’t been a large problem in America for decades, it’s fair to be unsure what to think right now.

Sources indicate that doctors fall squarely into the “very concerned” camp for a few reasons. “Measles is not a trivial infection,” says William Schaffner, MD, an infectious disease specialist and professor at the Vanderbilt University School of Medicine.

Not only can measles be miserable to experience, but it can also cause lingering effects that can lead to everything from deafness to brain infections, Dr. Schaffner points out. Measles can even cause something known as “immune amnesia,” where your body’s immunity to a range of pathogens is basically wiped out. (More on that in a moment.).

Here’s what infectious disease doctors say:.

Sources indicate that meet the experts: Thomas Russo, MD, professor and chief of infectious disease at the University at Buffalo in Latest York; William Schaffner, MD, an infectious disease specialist and professor at the Vanderbilt University School of Medicine; and Amesh A. Adalja, MD, a senior scholar at the Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security.

Measles, which is also known as rubeola, is a very contagious viral infection, per the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). Measles is so contagious, in fact, that up to 90 percent of people who come into contact with someone with the virus will get it if they’re not protected (like through the vaccine).

Measles usually causes symptoms like a cough, runny nose, tiny spots inside the mouth, a rash, and a high fever. It can also lead to severe complications like pneumonia, immune amnesia, and encephalitis (aka swelling of the brain). “Even with uncomplicated measles, people are usually miserable for a week,” Dr. Schaffner claims.

It has been revealed that measles can also cause “immune amnesia,” meaning the virus can wipe out memory B cells and memory T cells that your immune system has built up from previous infections. “There’s a window after having measles where your immune system has taken a hit, and you have increased susceptibility to viruses, pneumonia, and diarrheal illness—it’s a real thing,” says Thomas Russo, MD, professor and chief of infectious disease at the University at Buffalo in Newly introduced York. This can last for several months, or possibly even longer, he says.

Again, measles was considered eliminated from the U.S. back in 2000, meaning the virus wasn’t considered to be spreading in the U.S. While the country did see some measles cases, they were usually in people who had visited other countries. However, according to the latest CDC stats, only 18 of the 5,412 confirmed cases were from international travelers this year.

Cases began slowly creeping up last year, with CDC data showing 2,289 cases were reported. This trend continued when, this July, a little over the halfway point of the year, there were already 2,318 cases reported. At the time, Amesh A. Adalja, MD, a senior scholar at the Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security, noted that this was the highest number of cases seen since the disease was considered eliminated from the country. “Cases were higher only in the early 1990s.”.

Doctors agree that lack of vaccination is the significant element behind this increase in cases. “It’s all about vaccination,” Dr. Russo says. “The anti-vaxx movement is more potent now, and they’re winning.”.

According to recent reports, with fewer parents giving their children the measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine, measles cases are creeping back up, Dr. Russo points out.
