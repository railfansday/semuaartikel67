The Drawdown Breaker Blind Spot: Realized PnL Hides Unrealized Bleeding

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Most drawdown guards are built on a simple, reasonable-sounding premise: track losses, and when losses pile up, stop trading. The implementation almost always looks at the same number — realized PnL. A trade closes, the result is booked, the counter increments. Three losses in a row? Cool off. Daily loss limit hit? Flatten and stop.

It has been revealed that the problem is that this guard is blind to the exact moment it matters most: while positions are open and bleeding. An unrealized loss of 4% sitting in three open positions is invisible to a realized-PnL guard. The counter still reads zero. Every safety number is green. Then the positions close — and the drawdown "suddenly" appears, fully formed, in one tick of the counter.

This post dissects the blind spot, walks through a scenario where every guard reads fine while the account is drowning, and presents a dual-viewport design: realized drawdown and pool-wide mark-to-market unrealized PnL tracked separately, with the unrealized view gating new entries.

Consider a typical consecutive-loss breaker:.

This code is correct for what it measures. The issue is what it doesn't measure. on_trade_closed only fires when a trade closes. Between the open and the close, consecutive_losses and realized_pnl_today are frozen. If you have five positions open and all five are down 1.5% each, the breaker sees zero losses, zero realized drawdown, and happily approves a sixth entry.

The guard is not wrong. It is answering a different question than the one you think you're asking. "How much have I lost?" and "How much am I presently down?" are different quantities, and the gap between them is where accounts die.

It's not laziness. Realized PnL is easy: it's a single scalar that updates on a discrete event. Unrealized PnL is a moving target that requires a mark price per position, updates continuously, and has to handle stale quotes, funding, and partial fills. The realized-only guard is the 80% solution that ships in an afternoon. It's just missing the 20% that contains the worst days.

Here's the concrete version. Suppose the account is 100,000 units of quote currency, the consecutive-loss breaker is set to 3, and the daily loss limit is 2%. Position sizing is 20,000 notional per entry, so a 1.5% adverse move on a position is a 300-unit unrealized loss (0.3% of equity).

09:30 — Two positions open. Both drift against you. Unrealized: −600. Realized today: 0. consecutive_losses: 0. Breaker: open for business.

Industry observers note that 10:15 — The market trends against the book. A third and fourth position open (the breaker approved them, because nothing has closed). Unrealized across four positions: −2,400 (2.4% of equity). Realized today: still 0. consecutive_losses: still 0. can_open_new returns True.

According to recent reports, 11:00 — A fifth entry. Unrealized: −3,600. The daily loss limit is 2%, but it's computed on realized PnL, so it reads 0%. The breaker is structurally incapable of firing.

13:00 — Stops trigger across the book in a correlated move. All five close within twenty minutes. Realized today: −3,900. consecutive_losses: 5. The breaker finally fires — after the entire drawdown has been realized, and after it approved two entries during the bleed.

At 11:00, the account was down 3.6% on a mark-to-market basis. Every guard reported it was down 0%. The pool was drowning while the dashboard revealed a calm surface. This is the blind spot: the guard's measurement point (trade close) is downstream of the risk event (adverse mark-to-market drift on open exposure).

The design change is to stop treating "drawdown" as one number. Track two:.

The realized view answers "how bad has it been?" The unrealized view answers "how bad is it right now?" Entries should require both to be acceptable.

Sources indicate that the mark-to-market gate is checked on entry, not on a timer. You don't need a background thread recomputing the pool every millisecond. You need the check to run at the one moment it can still do something: before a upcoming order goes out. If the pool is down 1.6% and the cap is 1.5%, the next entry is refused — regardless of what the realized counter claims.

The two limits are separate knobs with separate meanings. max_unrealized_dd is typically tighter than the realized limit, because unrealized losses are still recoverable and you want to stop adding risk early. max_realized_dd can be looser, because realized losses are sunk and the response there is cooldown, not prevention. Conflating them into one number forces a bad compromise.

The guard change fixes the behavior; you also want the diagnostic so you can see the blind spot forming. Log both viewports on every entry decision, and log the gap:.

The gap column is the interesting one. When realized_dd − unrealized_dd grows large and negative — realized flat, unrealized deeply negative — you are inside the blind spot of a realized-only guard. If you ever run a realized-only breaker in parallel (legacy system, comparison study), this log tells you exactly how many entries it approved that the dual-viewport guard would have refused, and what those entries cost on a mark-to-market basis.

Industry observers note that be clear about the scope. The unrealized gate does not prevent the first position from going against you, and it does not replace per-position stops. It addresses one specific failure mode: the guard that approves latest risk while existing risk is already underwater. Correlation is the amplifier here — five positions that all bleed together look like five independent decisions to the entry logic and like one concentrated bet to the mark-to-market pool. The pool view catches that; the per-trade view never will.

Sources indicate that the general principle generalizes beyond drawdown breakers: any risk control whose measurement event is downstream of the risk event has a blind window. Realized PnL is downstream of unrealized PnL. Trade close is downstream of trade open. The fix is always the same — measure at the point where the risk actually lives, and gate at the point where you can still act.
