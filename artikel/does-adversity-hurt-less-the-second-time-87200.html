My grandfather owned a black World War II winter coat, long and heavy, the kind exhibitionists in movies wear, and in high school and college, I wore it everywhere because it reminded me of this sweet man who would watch Woody Woodpecker cartoons on television with me such that his laugh shook the bed. One night, outside a Upcoming York City bar, I realized it was too warm for wool and threw the coat back in my car. When my friends and I came back a few hours later, the window was broken in, and the thief took one thing: my grandfather’s coat.

Industry observers note that nietzsche wrote that what doesn’t kill you makes you stronger, so by his logic, I should have shrugged off my next problem easily. Three psychiatric researchers in Australia borrowed this overplayed line in the title of their scientific article, "What Doesn't Kill You Doesn't Always Make You Stronger: The Impact of Repeated Exposure to Adverse Life Events on Mental Health." Their work addresses questions taken for granted in a sloppy literature on resilience.

Developmental psychologist Ann Masten coined the phrase that resilience is “ordinary magic” in 2001. It has since been dropped into commencement speeches and self-help books, usually shrunk down to the comforting idea that everyone has some. Masten’s own data say something far more precise. She spent years studying homeless families and described their reading and math grades:.

Our studies of homeless families have revealed striking variability within homeless populations, both in the degree of risk and also the level of competence or problems. For example, if one examines individual growth curves for children identified as homeless/highly mobile in the district, the variability becomes evident … Some children show consistent academic resilience, if one defines resilience as average or better reading or math achievement scores, whereas others show consistent problems, covering a range of 5 SD [five standard deviations] or more.

Variability means that some people experience amazing outcomes while others fall apart. Five standard deviations is a spread wide enough to stretch from the bottom 1 percent of the bell curve to the top 1 percent within a group of kids who all lack a stable bedroom. On average, homeless children scored below poor children who had housing, who in turn scored below children who were neither. Masten never mentioned resilience is a universal human endowment.

In a recent development, the myth of universal human resilience has a price. If most people bounce back on their own, the logical policy is to leave them alone. They will be fine... eventually.

Recent findings suggest that asking whether people are resilient is about as useful as asking whether people are tall, because the answer depends on who, measured against what, and when. The better question is what happens when you experience the same difficulty twice and whether the second hurts more or less than the first.

The Australian psychiatrists studied 23,109 adults who, between 2002 and 2023, experienced adversity. They looked at people who:.

Every year, the same people answered the same question: How much of the time during the past four weeks:.

Scores ran from 0 to 100, which let the researchers compare how far a person sank after a first challenge with how far the same person sank after a second.

Industry observers note that some might say the first time your finances blow up, you can blame bad luck, such as a crooked landlord. The second time, the explanation arrows start pointing at you.

Recent findings suggest that in the study, a first major financial loss lowered scores by 6 to 7 points, the steepest drop of the seven events. A second loss lowered them by 7 to 8 points, and a third hurt about as much as the second—so the harm grew, and then it leveled off.

According to recent reports, the people hit repeatedly were poorer to begin with, earning less before their first event. That is, the study showed people with tough lives experience terrible events more often. It’s not exactly the most awe-inspiring finding, but it should make us more compassionate. My hypothesis is that a second financial loss comes after the savings account is empty, and you have already asked friends for a loan. By then, you feel incompetent and risk fraying your social ties.

A first theft or break-in lowered scores by about 1 point out of 100—the smallest effect in the study. By those stats, my coat should have been a shrug, and it wasn’t. A question about property crime can’t distinguish a stolen car stereo from my grandfather’s WWII coat. The authors close their paper with the same mentality, arguing that how much an event hurts depends on what the lost thing means to the person and how much is in their psychological arsenal to absorb the pain.

Divorce and serious personal illness or injury both did less harm the second time, so some might offer advice that your second divorce will sting less. As a divorced man, I’m in no hurry to find out, and you shouldn’t either.

Recent findings suggest that A smaller mental health reaction can also mean a person stopped feeling much. For instance, people assaulted three or more times reacted less to each fresh attack, and 64 percent of them were women, which unfortunately we know is often a sign of domestic violence.

I want to warn you and everyone you know that emotional numbing should not be misunderstood as resilience or growth.

Sources indicate that so did losing my grandpa’s coat make me stronger? Not in the slightest. It was an isolated experience that pissed me off. You, too, have similar one-offs that do not fit tidy little psychological models. Do not assume resilience or breakdowns. Pay attention, ask questions, and listen to better understand the person beside you.
