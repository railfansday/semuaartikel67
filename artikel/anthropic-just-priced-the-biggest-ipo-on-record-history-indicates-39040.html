Anthropic (ANT +0.00%) priced its initial public offering (IPO) on Wednesday at $83 a share, selling 980 million shares, mostly upcoming stock from the company itself. The price topped the $70-to-$78 range the company had expected, and it takes the amount raised to around $81.3 billion. Its shares are due to start trading on Nasdaq on Thursday, Nov. 19.

Recent findings suggest that it's the biggest IPO on record, if deals are compared before their extra shares. SpaceX (SPCX +1.88%) raised $75 billion in its base deal in June, then around $86.3 billion overall after its underwriters bought more shares.

Industry observers note that so I'll measure every deal here by its base deal, the shares sold before any of those extra purchases. (If Anthropic's underwriters buy all 147 million extra shares they have an option on, its total would reach around $93.5 billion, above SpaceX's on that basis, too.).

Sources indicate that the S&P 500 (^GSPC +0.81%) heads into the listing around 6% under its Oct. 6 record close of 7,818.93, after ending Wednesday at 7,323.63. How has the index done over the year after past record-setting IPOs?

Five U.S. IPOs set the size record on this basis between 1999 and 2014, each one topping the last. United Parcel Service raised around $5.5 billion in November 1999. AT&T Wireless, which traded as a tracking stock, almost doubled that with around $10.6 billion in April 2000. Visa raised around $17.9 billion in March 2008, and Alibaba raised about $21.8 billion in September 2014.

According to recent reports, general Motors sold around $15.8 billion of common stock in its November 2010 IPO, short of Visa's deal. But it sold around $4.35 billion of preferred stock at the same time. Including both, as the deal was widely reported, it was around $20.1 billion and set the record.

It has been revealed that to measure what followed, I compared the S&P 500's close on each stock's first trading day to its close a year later. If the anniversary landed on a weekend, I used the previous trading day. These are price changes only, without dividends.

The numbers aren't encouraging.

Recent findings suggest that A year after UPS's debut, the S&P 500 was down under 1%. After AT&T Wireless, it was down around 14%.

Sources indicate that the year after Visa's listing was much worse, with the index down about 40% as the financial crisis deepened. The only gain came after GM's debut, when the index climbed around 2%. And it dropped about 3% in the year after Alibaba's.

An ordinary year has been much better. By my count from daily closes, the S&P 500 has been up 12 months later around three-quarters of the time since 1950, and the typical 12-month change has been a rise of about 11%.

Industry observers note that notably, the index wasn't standing still along the way. In four of the five years, it rose around 6% or more above its starting level at one point (as much as 14% after GM's debut) before losing most or all of that ground.

I don't think the IPOs themselves were to blame. The two major drops came in the dot-com bust and the 2008 financial crisis, and Visa listed with the S&P 500 already around 17% under its October 2007 record.

It's tough to see how one share sale, however large, could trigger a bear market.

Sources indicate that but the timing arguably isn't random, either. Companies and their early owners tend to sell the most stock when buyers are willing to pay the most for it. UPS listed a few months before the index's March 2000 peak, AT&T Wireless a month after it, and Alibaba a day after a record close.

Put another way, I think a record-setting deal can be a sign that stock prices are already high, which could leave less room for a strong year afterward.

Recent findings suggest that spaceX's record hasn't settled anything yet. From its June 12 debut, the S&P 500 rose to its Oct. 6 record, around 5% higher. It's now a bit more than 1% under where it began.

Of course, five finished cases are way too few to trade on. I wouldn't sell an S&P 500 index fund like the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF over Anthropic's listing -- I'd keep adding to one in regular amounts over time. But I'd keep my expectations for the coming 12 months modest.
