Latest Update: A First Drive With the Fiat Topolino in Upcoming York City

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The Fiat Topolino isn't exactly built for New York City. Fiat has been selling this tiny electric vehicle in Europe for three years, but it's now arriving in the US for $14,985 as a street-legal micromobility vehicle. Except it's more like a golf cart than a small EV.

It has a max speed of 25 miles per hour, a 46-mile range, and no airbags. Classified as a “Low Speed Vehicle (LSV),” it can only operate on roads with a posted speed limit of 35 mph or less. (You still need a driver's license, and the Topolino must be registered and have auto insurance.) It also doesn't have a traditional EV charging plug; instead, it uses a 10-foot AC plug that works in any wall outlet—which can be tough if you're in a Brooklyn apartment.

In a recent development, “It's a little bit of an experiment,” says Andrew Waterhouse, head of brand at Fiat USA. “This segment of micromobility—and that's really golf carts—based on the data we have, it's going to grow 28 percent in the next 5 years. People are driving golf carts not to play golf—they're driving them to go to the beach, to go to the clubhouse, to go get coffee, dinner, but they're all just generic. Topolino adds a little bit of that Italian flair and charm, and it's just so unique.”.

That’s hard to refute. There's nothing like the Topolino on the market, EV or otherwise. Its adorable looks have drawn the attention of passersby, who've flicked me a thumbs up as I drove past, or pulled out their phones to sneak a quick pic. The Topolino (which is Italian for “little mouse,” and also what Italians call Mickey Mouse) comes with a lot of compromises, but I've had a blast piloting this thing around the city.

Industry observers note that from my conversation with Waterhouse, it was clear that Fiat expects to sell more of these Topolino cars in places like suburban Florida. Gated communities, large residences with private land, homes by the beach—you get the idea. There are also hopes to turn the Topolino into a fleet vehicle for campuses and the like. But I wanted to see what it would be like in the bustling streets of Latest York City. After all, I already drive the Fiat 500 as my personal vehicle.

The Fiat Topolino, left, next to the already small Fiat 500, right.

Recent findings suggest that the first thing that surprised me—other than the minuscule size, which you won't stop thinking about—is how plasticky the thing is. I leaned on it for a second and felt the plastic exterior sink in a little, which immediately made me realize the Topolino won't handle a crash very well; no crumple zones, no airbags—just seatbelts. So yes, don't take this thing on the highway.

It's built for two people and not much else. The passenger can stow a suitcase in front of them, and there's a rack on the back of the car to store another. But besides mesh pouches in the door frames and an elongated glove compartment on top of the dashboard that runs the width of the car, that's about it for storage.

According to recent reports, I should also mention the Topolino comes in a few variants: The standard model I tried has full doors and a fixed glass roof, but there's an Open Air model with a roll-back soft-top roof, and the Dolce Vita model has rope doors (aka no doors) and the soft-top roof. You can buy the car in mint, orange, or brownish-gray.

Thankfully, despite my 6'4" frame, I fit quite comfortably in the Topolino. I do have to swing my leg under the steering wheel as I get in, and the seats are a little firm, but the inside feels quite roomy. Only the driver's seat can move forward or back—the passenger seat stays put, and you cannot adjust the steering wheel height.

The asymmetrical, reverse-hinged doors are very unique. The driver's side opens outward from the front, and the passenger door opens the opposite way. There's also a button on each side to open the door, and weirdly, to get out, you pull a fabric strap. Now, the driver-side strap is to the left of the steering wheel, which makes sense, but the passenger-side strap is in an awkward spot behind the seat, near the seatbelt strap. A few of my passengers found that annoying. Also annoying is that you need to use the key to unlock both doors individually—no auto-unlock button plus no key fob. (The car starts with a second key.).

Industry observers note that the horizontally split, manually hinged windows flip up, but on rougher roads, they tend to flip back down. You can adjust the small, circular side mirrors and the rear-view mirror, which also doubles as a backup camera display. I have found the backup camera mostly useless, especially at night, when it's just really hard to see what’s going on.

Good thing visibility in the Topolino is excellent, so it's really easy to swing your head around. Unsurprisingly, you can park this thing anywhere. I've parallel parked the Topolino in several tight spots that no other car can fit in, and the lack of parking sensors or the lackluster backup camera didn't bother me. In a city as crowded as New York, that's incredibly valuable.

You should know there's no air-conditioning or heating. There's a windshield fan to defog the windows, which, along with the single wiper and wiper fluid, was useful on cold, rainy days. But in the winter months, you'll have to bundle up, or in the summer, affix a battery-powered (or USB-C) fan to the dashboard. On the dash, you have a simple instrument cluster that indicates speed and remaining range, and a built-in phone mount to the right.

You can store luggage on the rack—there's no trunk.

In a recent development, turn the key to start the car, disengage the parking brake, and tap D or R to start driving. There's no power steering, so turning the wheel requires a little more force than you're used to, and the turn signals don't automatically disengage after you turn. Still, the car feels very responsive. The 8-horsepower electric motor feels fairly quick when you put the pedal to the metal, though you quickly hit the Topolino’s top speed. Good thing the default speed limit in New York City is 25 mph.

But as you start driving, you'll experience arguably the worst thing about the Fiat Topolino: the sound. Because of federal regulations around electric LSVs, the Topolino has a pedestrian alert sound—the kind of angelic hum you might have heard when an electric car is reversing or rolling up to a stop sign. Except the sound is constant, and it gets louder the faster you go. I'm not sure why it's so loud in the car. It feels like it's alerting me more than pedestrians around me.

Sources indicate that the sound only disappears if you go past 22 mph, which isn't often, since streets in most cities have a stop sign or traffic light after every block. The first time my wife and I rode in the car, we could barely hear each other talk over the hum. You get used to it over time, but it's very annoying. You can try playing music over it, but the car has no speaker system. Instead, Fiat sells a pair of Monster Bluetooth speakers that you can magnetically stick to the top frame of the car. That works OK, but I didn't like having to keep them charged, so when they died, I played music from my phone. It was sad.

You can fit the Topolino anywhere!

In a recent development, the ride is a little stiff. I drove over one of the worst roads in Brooklyn (Broadway), and you could feel every single bump. When I drove with my brother, they often just exclaimed “Jesus!” every few minutes. Cobblestone was equally pretty bad. But it's doable, and on well-maintained roads, the Topolino was a smooth ride.

According to recent reports, new Yorkers rejoice! You legally cannot drive this car into New Jersey. (As if you needed a reason.) All of the bridges and tunnels to the Garden State have speed limits higher than 35 mph. However, I could take the East River bridges to go from Brooklyn to Manhattan, and I did exactly that. Driving over the Manhattan Bridge was a little terrifying when I was holding up a few cars in my lane and everyone decided to race around me. But I got a few thumbs-ups along the way, which helped. Everyone finds this car cute, which goes a long way toward how people treat you on the road.

As for range, it's not great. I wish it were closer to 80 miles instead of 46 miles, especially at this price. It's perfect for quick hour-long round trips around the city; anything longer will give you range anxiety. That trip over the bridge? I went from Bed-Stuy, Brooklyn, to Lower Manhattan and back (around 12 miles). The indicated remaining range went from 46 miles to 30 by the time I got home.

Recharging takes around five hours from zero to full, and the charging cord is built into the car near the passenger-side door. Annoyingly, the 10-foot cable isn't retractable, so you awkwardly have to stuff it back into the door frame when you're done. But the bigger concern is the length. And the standard AC plug. EV charging stations don't typically have AC outlets like the ones in your home. And 10 feet is far too short to reach any apartment or residence in Upcoming York City.

Charging the Fiat Topolino requires a standard wall outlet—and, in Brooklyn, probably a long extension cord.

It has been revealed that I bought a 50-foot extension cord and ran the cable from my outdoor parking spot to my backyard outlet. It worked perfectly well, but I'm also an outlier, with access to a parking spot and a backyard. Most people don't have those luxuries, so you'll need your own solution.

Sources indicate that initially, after my first ride with the Topolino, I was apprehensive. It felt like I was sitting in a plastic box on wheels, with a blaring sound that wouldn't stop, and every big bump sending a shock into my body. After a week, I was driving the thing everywhere, hand dangling out the half-window, fist-bumping other drivers in bumper-to-bumper traffic, and parking in the tiniest available spots around Brooklyn. Now, a week without the little mouse, I miss it dearly.

According to recent reports, it's far from the perfect city car. There are small things I would change to improve the overall rideability. And like the Fiat 500e, it's way too expensive. But as someone who likes their car to stand out, I find so much of what's available in the US market uninspiring. The Topolino breaks the mold. We need more small cars like it.
