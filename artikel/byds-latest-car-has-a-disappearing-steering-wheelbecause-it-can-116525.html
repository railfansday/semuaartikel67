BYD’s latest car has a disappearing steering wheel—because it can

🌐 Source: Fastcompany • 📅 24 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 8 min read (1688 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways
  • BYD just killed the steering wheel as we know it.
  • Sources indicate that BYD just killed the steering wheel as we know it.
  • It only needs 10 seconds, then puff, the wheel is gone and the dashboard is clean.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

BYD just killed the steering wheel as we know it. Permanently bolted in front of the driver, it’s a fundamental part of the car. Unless you’re BYD. The Chinese automaker’s new six-seat plug-in hybrid SUV features a disappearing steering wheel that folds itself into a dashboard compartment at the push of a button. It only needs 10 seconds, then puff, the wheel is gone and the dashboard is clean. The driver suddenly has about 10 more inches of legroom. BYD bills it as the world’s first in a production car. Aside from race cars with their pop-in and pop-out steering wheels, nobody has ever seen anything like this. The magic wheel comes only with the $49,400 Folding Smart Cabin trim of the Tai 9, a model from BYD’s sub-brand Fang Cheng Bao. During the November 19 launch, Fang Cheng Bao’s general manager, Xiong Tianbo, noted that another carmaker floated a folding wheel back in 2016. It wasn’t possible at the time. “It has always been difficult to bring into mass production,” Xiong said. The reason, he explained, is that in a traditional car, “a mechanical steering column connects the steering wheel and the wheels,” adding “that one column limits the imagination for the entire driver’s space.” [Image: BYD] The Tai 9 doesn’t need a hard steering column because it uses a steer-by-wire system. Rather than mechanically pushing the road wheels to a new angle through a shaft, a rack, and a pair of tie rods, the system uses a new folding steering wheel that works like a game controller. Turn it, and electronic sensors “read” the movement, sending an electronic signal to the motors that actually turn the road wheels. The same trim also gets brake-by-wire, which swaps the brakes’ mechanical link for electronic signals, too. This is similar to the fly-by-wire technology that modern fighter airplanes use. Rather than directly driving heavy hydraulic systems, the pilot uses a joystick similar to a computer controller. It sends impulses that get interpreted by a computer, which in turn operates the mechanics of the airplane to make it change direction in the air. BYD indicates it has five layers of redundancy covering sensing, control, actuation, communications, and power supply. The Chinese car manufacturer also claims that its folding system has an ASIL D rating (for automotive safety integrity level)—that’s the highest safety level in the risk classification standard for road vehicles as defined by the International Organization for Standardization. BYD sets strict usage conditions and safety interlocks, which means the wheel stays out while the car is being driven and only stows once it’s parked, or while it parks itself. How it works Without the metal shaft connecting to the car’s direction, Fang Cheng Bao’s designers had nothing to stop them from playing with the steering wheel in any way they wanted. To pack it inside the dashboard, the steering wheel uses a motorized folding mechanism with a precision hinge that swings through up to 180 degrees. Once folded, it recedes into the dashboard like Homer Simpson disappearing into a bush. When the wheel emerges, the hinge hides itself. Drivers get a D-shaped rim they can grip all the way around, proper switches, and an adjustable position. Despite the futuristic tech, the wheel should feel familiar. The muscle behind all this, according to the organization, is an automotive-grade brushless motor, the same kind BYD is using in the dexterous hands of its new AI-powered robots. Xiong said the folding cabin took 36 months to develop, and Fang Cheng Bao says its durability tests folded the wheel 96,000 times in various environments, including at room temperature, in the cold, and in the heat. This thing also folds on its own when you use its self-parking mode—BYD’s autonomous tech that parks your car in any parking garage completely on its own. The wheel retracts on the way into the spot, since nobody is using it, and only returns on the way out of the parking spot to meet its owner. Good boy. [Image: BYD] Nap time With the wheel tucked away, one more button push raises the driver’s seat cushion by about 3.5 inches and tilts the backrest to 165 degrees, all within 20 seconds. The car is now a nap pod. BYD says it put five volunteers in the seat and tracked their sleep for 100 minutes, scoring it by American Academy of Sleep Medicine guidelines. All five drifted into light sleep. Three made it all the way to deep sleep. According to Xiong, the folding cabin in this trim of the Tai 9—which bundles steer-by-wire, brake-by-wire, the lie-flat driver’s seat, and the folding wheel—is a bet on what comes next: “It’s forward-looking preparation for the era of L3 and L4 intelligent driving.” Level 4 cars may not need a steering wheel, but L3 cars will still need one in case the driver must take control of the car from the car’s AI. The Tai 9 isn’t there yet. But it’s siesta-ready. That’s good enough for now.
