Why Sleeping 9 Hours After 50 May Be a Warning Sign for Your Career

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

More sleep does not necessarily mean more years at work, as a large Finnish study links both restless nights and unusually long sleep to shorter working lives.

Industry observers note that as retirement ages rise and people are expected to work later into life, researchers are looking more closely at what helps people remain in the workforce as they grow older. A large Finnish study suggests that sleep may be part of the picture. Adults who frequently struggled with sleep after age 50 tended to spend fewer years working, while those who regularly slept nine hours or more a night also had shorter expected working lives.

Sources indicate that people with severe sleep disturbances could expect to spend around eight fewer months in the workforce than those who rarely experienced sleep problems. Among people who slept at least nine hours a night, the gap ranged from roughly nine months to more than two years, depending on the study group.

The findings, published online in Occupational & Environmental Medicine, come as more than half of the countries in the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) have raised statutory retirement ages in response to aging populations. Previous research has explored why people leave their jobs early, including disability, illness, and retirement decisions. However, researchers had not examined whether sleep duration and quality were associated with how long people remain employed.

To investigate, the team focused on working life expectancy, which measures the average number of years someone can expect to remain employed at a particular age. Rather than measuring how long people live, the researchers examined their expected years in the workforce between ages 50 and 68.

The analysis drew on two Finnish studies involving more than 76,000 adults. The Finnish Public Sector (FPS) study included 70,339 public sector employees between 2000 and 2016, approximately 80% of whom were women. The Health and Social Support (HeSSup) study provided a random sample of 6,071 working-age Finnish adults between 1998 and 2013, with women accounting for 54% of participants. Researchers assessed sleep using the first survey each participant completed after turning 50, then examined employment information from the Earnings and Accrual Register, maintained by the Finnish Centre for Pensions.

The surveys asked participants how long they usually slept and how often they experienced difficulties falling asleep, staying asleep, waking too early, or feeling unrested after sleeping. These disturbances were assessed over the preceding four weeks. People who experienced them no more than once a week were considered to have no sleep disturbances, while those reporting problems two to four nights a week were placed in the moderate category. Severe disturbances meant experiencing these problems five or six nights a week to nearly every night. Sleep duration was classified separately as short, meaning less than seven hours, mid-range at seven to eight and a half hours, or long at nine hours or more.

The results revealed a consistent relationship between disrupted sleep and fewer expected years of employment. Participants without sleep disturbances had an average working life expectancy of 13 years and five months after age 50. That fell to approximately 13 years among those with moderate disturbances and 12 years and 10 months among those with severe problems. Compared with people reporting no disturbances, these groups could expect to work nearly five and eight fewer months, respectively. The association appeared among both men and women and across three broad occupational categories: professional, intermediate, and routine jobs.

Sources indicate that employment differences became more apparent when researchers examined sleep alongside occupation and sex. Professional women who reported no sleep disturbances had the longest expected working lives in this comparison, at nearly 14 years after age 50. Men in routine jobs who experienced severe sleep disturbances had the shortest, at approximately 12 and a half years.

Sleep duration revealed a somewhat different pattern. In the Finnish Public Sector study, participants sleeping fewer than seven hours a night and those sleeping between seven and eight and a half hours had the same average working life expectancy of 13 years and two months. Those who regularly slept nine hours or more, however, had an expected working life of just 12 years and five months. Compared with the mid-range sleep group, that represented approximately nine fewer months of employment.

In a recent development, the association was even stronger in the HeSSup study, where sleeping nine hours or more was linked to just over 30 fewer months of expected employment compared with sleeping seven to eight and a half hours. Researchers found no clear evidence that people sleeping fewer than seven hours had shorter working lives than those in the mid-range category, regardless of sex or job type.

Sources indicate that education and occupation also appeared to shape these patterns. When researchers compared the most advantaged participants, those with professional jobs or higher education and no sleep disturbances or short to mid-range sleep, with the least advantaged, those in routine jobs or with lower education and severe sleep problems or long sleep, the differences were considerable. The gap in expected working life was approximately one year and five months in the FPS group and five years in the HeSSup group.

According to recent reports, in a more detailed analysis combining sleep duration with socioeconomic circumstances, 50-year-old men and women in professional jobs who reported short nightly sleep had the longest expected working lives in the FPS study, at around 13 years and seven months. In HeSSup, highly educated women had the longest working life expectancy, at approximately 11 and a half years. At the other extreme, men in routine jobs or with lower levels of education who regularly slept nine hours or more had an expected working life of only about six years and five months in both study groups.

According to recent reports, although the findings suggest a connection between sleep and working life, they do not establish that poor sleep or longer sleep directly causes people to leave employment earlier. The study was observational, and the researchers could not account for several potentially influential factors, including physical and mental health, underlying medical conditions, risky health behaviors, and working conditions such as shift work. These factors could affect both sleep patterns and the ability to remain employed.

Still, the findings suggest that helping people manage sleep problems in midlife may be worth considering as countries look for ways to support longer working careers, particularly among people facing socioeconomic disadvantages.

“Promoting sleep health and providing support for managing sleep disturbances at midlife may be especially notable for those with a lower socioeconomic status when aiming to increase workforce participation with advancing age,” the researchers concluded.

“This could include cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia delivered by occupational health services or workplace interventions, such as educational or health promotion interventions for sleep.”.

Reference: “Sleep disturbances and sleep duration as predictors of working life expectancy among adults aged 50 years and older in Finland” by Saana Myllyntausta, Katriina Heikkilä, Jaana Pentti, Holendro Singh Chungkham, Sakari Suominen, Mika Kivimaki, Jussi Vahtera, Paola Zaninotto, Jenni Ervasti and Sari Stenholm, 18 August 2026, Occupational and Environmental Medicine. DOI: 10.1136/oemed-2025-110766.
