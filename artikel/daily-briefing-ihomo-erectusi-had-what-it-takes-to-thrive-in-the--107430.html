Fossil teeth suggest that Homo erectus thrived in dense rainforests in southern China as recently as 180,000 years ago. Researchers had thought that these ancient hominins had been limited to savannahs and open woodlands, says Kira Westaway, a geochronologist and a co-author of two newly introduced studies. “This is a really big deal, because we thought Homo sapiens were the only species that had the skill set to survive in really challenging rainforest environments.”.

Reference: Nature paper 1 & paper 2.

Artificial-intelligence giant Anthropic has made history with the largest initial public offering (IPO) on record on 19 November. The firm — now valued at a staggering US$1.5 trillion — has major ambitions in science. It made waves earlier this year when it reported that its model Claude had made several major mathematics breakthroughs. The company also rolled out their first ‘wet’ laboratory for life sciences research and has unveiled plans to “substantially” scale up its work in AI-enabled drug discovery. Pitched as a company that prioritizes AI safety, some researchers question whether an IPO — and the accompanying investor demands — might disrupt some of Anthropic’s safety commitments.

Recent findings suggest that roughly 180 million people could be carrying a previously unknown type of giant virus in their guts. Researchers found DNA from these ‘egoviruses’ in gut samples from people, buffalo, goats, chickens, deer and pigs. Six clades were unique to people, one of which is estimated to be present in roughly 1% of the population. Microbiologists say that egoviruses could be the missing link between giant viruses that infect single-celled organisms and distantly related poxviruses, such as those that cause smallpox and mpox, which can infect people and animals directly.

Here in Washington DC, tens of thousands of researchers have gathered for the Society for Neuroscience’s annual meeting — a showcase for everything from neural implants to memory research.

But when I asked a panel of leaders at the US National Institutes of Health what in neuroscience remains underappreciated, several pointed to the brain’s relationship with the rest of the body. Nora Volkow, director of the National Institute on Drug Abuse, lamented the lack of research into brain metabolism — a blind spot exposed by the surprising neurological effects of GLP-1 drugs.

Amy Bany Adams, acting director of the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke, offered a reminder of how much remains to be discovered. Of the glymphatic system, the brain’s waste-clearance network, she remarked: “We didn’t even know it existed when I was in graduate school.” I’m leaving with a longer list of questions about what else we’ve been overlooking.

Neuroscientists are uncovering how the brain orchestrates a tailored mixture of symptoms in response to either a bacterial or viral infection. Presenting at the Society for Neuroscience’s annual meeting on 15 November, researchers suggested that the brain decodes the type of pathogen that has invaded, accesses memories of past bouts of illness and can even mobilize immune defences at the sight of a sick person. The unpublished findings challenge the long-held view that neurological responses to infection are a one-size-fits-all response to inflammation.

Sources indicate that artificial-intelligence governance requires more than companies’ oversight of their models — it requires “a system of accountability” that works like a national constitution, argues legal scholar Cindy Skach. Firms should be held legally liable when individuals are affected by AI actions that infringe on their fundamental rights, Skach suggests. She proposes ‘human dignity’ as the most important value for the system to uphold. When a person’s dignity is violated — by an AI-generated deepfake or by a chatbot that treats a distressed user as just another interaction — they could petition a court directly to hold the perpetrator accountable.

It has been revealed that do you find networking a nightmare? Three experts share — in the form of short videos — how to grasp the nettle. They suggest thinking about networking as a professional skill that can be learnt and developed, considering arriving early for events and not worrying about volume. One high-quality connection and follow-up conversation is worth more than 100 business cards, notes public-speaking coach Susie Ashfield.

In the second episode of Nature Podcast’s climate miniseries, host Nick Petrić Howe hears from scientists about what humanity can actually do to stop the runaway train of rising temperatures. The rise of renewable-energy technologies is already making a dent in greenhouse-gas emissions, experts say. But creating meat alternatives and a move away from an economic system that incentivizes ‘growth at any cost’ could help to really shift the dial.

Today I’m considering buying a prickly pear cactus (Opuntia) in case of a power cut. Researchers have found that the plants’ unusual method of photosynthesis can be harnessed to generate a small amount of electricity, with around eight prickly pears together generating nearly three times the voltage of a standard AA battery.
