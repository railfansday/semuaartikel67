Industry observers note that here are five newly translated works of crime fiction, featuring selfish tourists, corrupt cops, desperate horse racers, antiquities smugglers, and amateur sleuths. Enjoy!

Yves Ravey, Taormina Translated by Alyson Waters (Newly introduced York Review of Books).

Taormina is a ruthless condemnation of our selfish modern era, a road trip novel in which a married couple on vacation in Italy run over a migrant child, then proceed to do….nothing. They do not check on the body. They do not go to the police. They do not accept any form of responsibility. They don’t even bother to cover up their crimes (or at least, they cover for themselves only lazily and ineptly). The only action available to either spouse, by dint of their warped world view, is to blame others for their problems. A vicious gem that wouldn’t feel out of place in mid-century noir, although the story’s catalysts are firmly grounded in the 21st century.

Gustavo Eduardo Abrevaya, The Bullet Inside Me Translated by Andrea G Labinger ( Schaffner Press).

Recent findings suggest that the Bullet Inside Me takes places at the height of Argentina’s Secret War; in this hard-boiled thriller of compromise and conspiracy, the young daughter of a fascist functionary is kidnapped, in a case that threatens the entire dictatorial order. By the same author as the impressive Sanctuary, this one’s just as compelling, and just as vicious.

Genki Kawamura, The Horse and I Translated by Chikako Kobayashi (Flatiron).

A beautiful tale of the lengths we go to for love. In The Horse and Me, a shy clerk forges a deep bond with a racing horse, one coveted by a wealthy rider who seems poised to destroy the stallion’s spirit. Without means of her own, the clerk begins stealing from her own union’s coffers in order to purchase, then maintain, her newfound soulmate. When her colleagues begin to catch on to the thefts, Kawamura’s heroine finds herself backed into a corner and desperate to turn her horse into a champion.

Sacha Naspini, The Witch’s Mouth Translated by Clarissa Botsford  (Europa).

Grave robbing in Tuscany from ancient Etruscan graves has been substantial business since before the war, but with fresh regulations cracking down on antiquities smuggling elsewhere in Italy, the little band of thieves at the center of the novel face new gangs trying to muscle into their territory, renewed inquiries from weary local authorities, and internal divisions that threaten to dissolve their operation entirely.

Indrek Hargla, The Ghost of Rataskaevu Street Translated by Adam Cullen (Pushkin Press).

It has been revealed that hargla’s second novel to feature apothecary Melchior, released for the first time in the US this month, takes his hero on a dark journey stretching far into the city’s past. When three different townspeople die just after seeing a ghost, the pharmacist and amateur sleuth decides to dig into the matter. In between brewing tinctures, infusing remedies, and proscribing his usual treatments, Melchior keeps circling back to a house in the center of town, once the setting of a terrible crime, and key to understanding the more recent killings.
