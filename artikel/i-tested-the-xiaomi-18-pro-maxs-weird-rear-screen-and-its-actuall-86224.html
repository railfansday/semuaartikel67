I tested the Xiaomi 18 Pro Max's weird rear screen — and it's actually useful

I have many gripes with modern smartphones, but one of the things that really grinds my gears is the trend of Godzilla-sized camera bumps on the backs of phones, Apple's doing it, Samsung' seems to be planning on it, and a lot of Chinese brands look like they've been hit by a rogue hockey puck. I've been testing the Xiaomi 18 Pro Max, which also has a large, iPhone-looking camera bump, but this time things are different.

Xiaomi has taken on the task of making the most out of that normally-wasted space on the camera bump by turning it into a screen. This is not the first phone to feature a rear-mounted screen, or even the first Xiaomi flagship to do so, but in a world of phones that all seem to feel and look the same, it's quite exciting to see a phone company offer something that helps its phones stand out.

But the question is not whether the Xiaomi 18 Pro Max's Back Display is interesting, it's whether it's able to justify its own existence.

Looking at the 18 Pro Max, my mind immediately jumped to the cover displays on compact foldables. The way the display wraps around the two main cameras is reminiscent of the Galaxy Z Flip 8 and the Motorola Razr. It's a little smaller than those screens, measuring 2.9-inches compared to those 4+-inch screens, but all three share a common purpose.

This is a way to use your phone without actually using your phone, using the smaller screen to look at basic information like the time or notifications on a smaller and more power-efficient display. At the same time, and as Xiaomi is keen to emphasize, it's a way to customize the look and feel of your phone in a way that cases and other accessories just can't manage.

My initial thoughts upon looking at the back of the Xiaomi 18 Pro Max and playing around with the screen weren't totally positive. I couldn't stop comparing it to the cover screens on foldable phones, and the fact that those screens are front-and-center when you take out your device. Xiaomi's screen is on the back, which is not part of my phone I tend to pay very much attention to.

But the more I used it, and the more I figured out that it wasn't just a glorified clock and notification alert, the more I realized that my initial reaction was the wrong way of looking at things.

The idea that this is potentially a very valuable feature clicked when I opened up the camera on the Back Display. This allows you to take selfies using the main cameras, in a similar fashion to foldable phones, using the rear-mounted screen as a viewfinder. This means you're taking selfies with the 200MP main camera, rather than the lower resolution hole-punch camera on the front.

Industry observers note that I'm not going to lie, though. This isn't a fully-fledged phone screen capable of running all the apps and services that you've got installed. It is significantly more limited than that, and the difference is like describing the difference between a smart home hub and a tablet.

The home hub may be useful for its own specific use case, but it doesn't have the same versatility or usefulness as a tablet running Android or iPadOS.

The Back Display is great for glancing at notifications and the time, especially if you tend to leave your phone lying face down. It even supports Live Activities on a handful of apps, including letting you control music playback on Spotify. Plus, it's possible to customize your screen with different backgrounds and clockfaces, depending on what your own personal tastes and style might be.

So far so good, though I can't say that I've ever felt using my main screen for those tasks has ever been a particular burden — especially since I'm just not used to looking at the back of my phone.

Then again, if you already have a smartwatch, that's not going to mean much, since you already have the bulk of this information available on your wrist.

Xiaomi does seem to be hinging on Generative AI to turn this screen from a gimmick into something you'll want to use throughout the day, thanks to a built-in system that lets you vibe code your own apps.

In a recent development, anyone that remembers the flop that was Windows Phone will know that success of a mobile platform hinges on app support. Your apps give the system value, and without developers actively contributing to an app store, there's little reason for people to actually invest in one of your devices.

So how do you get app developers on board to add utility to a niche display on the back of a handful of phones? You don't. You hand that job to the customers, and let generative AI do all the hard work. So Xiaomi added a prompt-based system for creating Back Display apps from scratch, as well as an App Store of sorts that lets you see a curated selection of what other people have created.

Industry observers note that options I can see include workout schedules, mini games, educational tools, timers, and all kinds of different options, though I have no idea how effective or accurate they actually are compared to full-coded apps. Still, it's nice to see that there's a way to improve the usefulness of the Back Display — even if it would be nice to have proper Android apps on offer.

I found that basic prompts did work, but the more detail you could offer the better the outcome. Xiaomi can "polish" your prompt to better your chances of generating a usable app.

However, this does not guarantee that the app would successfully generate — or that it would work. It took me multiple attempts to create a Doom clone, and even when it did, I kept getting killed off by a monster hiding out of my line of sight.

It has been revealed that so it's not quite as painless as having a developer-made app, and playing games on a small screen isn't ideal. But in the absence of additional features, this system is fairly good at filling that niche.

I've spent my entire life looking at the front of my phone, and never really concerning myself with what's on the back. Even in the pre-smartphone era the screen was everything, whereas the back was just... not vital. If rear screens start to take off, that could change the way people interact with their phones in a big way. And that's obviously going to take a lot of getting used to.

According to recent reports, that's probably the biggest hurdle these rear screens will face. There's certainly no shortage of utility, especially with the generative AI apps. It's just reprogramming user habits to glance at the back of the phone rather than looking at their main display. It's going to be an uphill battle to convince people that these screens are worth it, especially when they have to compete with wearables.

Sources indicate that if it weren't for the AI apps I'd say these screens don't have much of a chance. I'm not convinced that they will take off, aside from a few niche gimmicks, but at least they have some sort of unique selling point to work with.
