From lawns to sealed concrete, you might be surprised at how much damage a snow shovel can do.

According to recent reports, clearing snow from walkways is vital for safety, but winter shoveling can easily damage wooden decks, asphalt driveways and other surfaces. So, as the days get shorter and colder, it’s important to solidify your shoveling plan before the first snow flies.

“Snow can camouflage uneven areas and patches of ice, posing slip-and-fall risks, particularly when you’re rushing to shovel,” indicates landscaping expert Jose Lopez. “Being mindful and taking a relaxed, careful approach will help you save money and keep everyone safe all winter long.”.

Ahead, experts walk through how to shovel eleven tricky surfaces around your home, to keep everyone safe, plus prevent costly springtime repairs.

Industry observers note that shoveling gravel is frustrating because those pebbles tend to come up with the snow, then get scattered onto the landscaping. “You will end up cleaning about $150 worth of it off your lawn in the spring,” claims grading and paving contractor Scott Flores.

To make life easier, use a plastic shovel, keep the blade about an inch above the gravel and clear the snow in short passes. “You don’t have to expose all of the gravel,” says Lopez. “It’s fine to leave a thin layer of snow behind.”.

Sources indicate that A metal snow shovel blade can actually scratch and gouge concrete. “It can cause $3,000 worth of damage to your pavement in 20 minutes and you won’t know it ’til April,” says Flores. Initially, the damage “may not look like much, but once water gets under it and freezes, it will cause spalling and make your pavement look 10 years older than it is.”.

Luckily, the fix is easy on this one: always use a poly-blade (plastic) or rubber-edged shovel on concrete.

Aliaksandr Bahdanovich/Getty Images.

Aggressive shoveling can damage paint and sealers, even if they’re touted as “scratch-resistant.” “That layer is about three millimeters thick, and your steel shovel will chip it away like you’re peeling your fingernails,” notes Flores.

Recent findings suggest that the fix: use a poly-blade shovel, shovel with a slight upward motion and leave a half-inch of snow for the sun to melt, says Flores. “Many believe that steel shovels will last longer. Yes they will, but that’s what makes them bad,” he says.

Pro-tip: If you use a deicer, make sure it’s safe for your particular coating, states Lopez.

Just like with concrete, asphalt seems indestructible, but metal shovels can damage it, especially if it already has imperfections or cracks. So, the best approach is to use a poly-blade (plastic) or rubber-edged shovel, and shovel the snow before it partially melts and refreezes.

It has been revealed that “Avoid pushing your shovel into cracks or potholes, and don’t bang it against the surface in an attempt to dislodge stubborn ice,” notes Lopez. “Instead, choose a deicing product that’s safe for asphalt.”.

Industry observers note that A shovel can easily catch the edge of a paver and scratch, break or knock it out of place. To prevent that, use a poly-blade shovel, take shorter shovel strokes, push snow at an angle and slightly raise up the leading edge.

In a recent development, “Shoveling and deicing aggressively won’t make your job faster or easier, it takes some finesse,” indicates Lopez. Also, use de-icers that are safe for both the paving and jointing material, and inspect the walkway for loose bricks once the snow melts.

After a snow, it can be tricky to tell where the concrete ends and the lawn begins, and one wrong move means damaged grass come springtime. To keep from marring your grass, mark walkway edges with small flags before it snows. While shoveling, keep the blade even with your walkway, versus angling it toward the grass.

Sources indicate that pro-tip: “Slow down and work in short passes near the edges, rather than trying to force the shovel along,” says Lopez. “.

In a recent development, try not to pile snow on shrubs and other perennials, as that can snap branches. It’s also best to keep deicers and their runoff away from landscaping.

Recent findings suggest that “Before winter arrives, strategy where you’ll pile or toss snow to keep it away from sensitive plants,” says Lopez. “You may find it helpful to use a smaller shovel, which will give you better control over where the snow lands.”.

Also, try not to move soil, which can expose plants to the cold, and never strike frozen branches to remove snow. “Wood breaks more easily than people expect,” notes moving professional Leon Schwab.

In a recent development, aggressive shoveling is sure to damage a deck, regardless of its composition. To preserve yours, use a poly-blade shovel and push the snow in the direction of the boards. If it’s safe to do so, leave a thin layer of snow, instead of shoveling all the way down to bare decking, then apply a deicer that’s recommended for the surface type.

Safety tip: “If wet, heavy snow is accumulating and you notice the deck sagging or hear creaking noises, seek professional advice,” notes Lopez. “Don’t continue using the deck if you suspect a structural concern.”.

Stairs are particularly hazardous when its slippery out, and clearing them can be precarious, with snow obscuring their boundaries and confined spaces limiting your range of motion.

Sources indicate that to stay safe, “Wear boots with good traction and make sure your staircase is well lit,” says Lopez. “Don’t rush the job. It’s better to take your time and shovel safely.”.

Pro-tip: “Use a small shovel to clear one step at a time and maintain a stable position,” states Schwab.

According to recent reports, sometimes it’s prudent to clear large accumulations of snow from the roof, but it’s also a dangerous job. To stay safe, roofing contractor Andre Kazimierski recommends using a roof rake, which allows you to pull off snow while remaining on the ground. Focus on clearing off as much as you can, but don’t worry about leaving it completely bare, and try not to touch your roofing material.

In a recent development, “Do this sooner rather than later, because if it starts to warm up a bit the snow will become denser and heavier, making it harder to remove,” says Kazimierski. “And never walk on your roof when it’s even the slightest bit snowy or icy up there.”.

Obviously, you don’t want to take a shovel to your car, as that would do a lot of damage. Instead, use a snow brush or broom that’s designed to be gentle on vehicle finishes. Flores recommends this one from Snow Joe.

Recent findings suggest that start by clearing the roof in sections, then move on to the hood, trunk, windows, headlights, taillights and mirrors. You’ll especially want to get the roof and hood fully clear; otherwise, once you start driving, snow can blow up or slide down onto your windshield, obstructing your vision and endangering other drivers. “That ice can fly off your car at 40 miles per hour,” claims Flores.
