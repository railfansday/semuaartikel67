It has been revealed that through the lens of time, the early part of the Aughts can seem like a simpler age when phones were usually dumb, politicians were sometimes smart, and families gathered quaintly around one TV set to watch the same program at the same time. High-resolution internet video and the streaming revolution were distant spots on the horizon. But the changing landscape unwittingly created the perfect conditions for an accidental phenomenon: a TV show about cars with an estimated global audience of 350 million, a broadcasting triumph that would be impossible to replicate today. Because the Aughts was the decade that seeded the improbable success of a self-confessed “poky little motoring show” called Top Gear.

This story originally appeared in Volume 38 of Road & Track.

Sources indicate that in fact, Top Gear predated this century, debuting on BBC television in 1977 and plodding through the Eighties and Nineties with sensible car-buying advice and cozy trips to steam rallies. The series added Jeremy Clarkson to its hosting lineup in 1988 to bring a bit of wit to what was still a fundamentally earnest format. In 1999, he left the show, and two years later, the BBC confirmed that Top Gear was going to be “rested,” a polite British way of saying “canceled.”.

At the same time, Jeremy and an old school friend, TV producer Andy Wilman, who had been a lesser Top Gear presenter, were working on prepares for a bigger, more boisterous car show called Carmageddon. When the pair pitched it to the head of the BBC Two channel, she liked the idea and agreed to commission the show with one proviso: Carmageddon should become a reboot of Top Gear. The fuse had been lit, but free­wheeling global success wasn’t the master blueprint. In fact, there was no grand scheme, only a vague hope to make a halfway-­decent show for audiences in the U.K. I know this because from 2002 to 2015, I was Top Gear’s script editor.

Industry observers note that this, like so much of the show, was something of an accident. I’d worked briefly with Jeremy on the old version of Top Gear, and he’d liked my satirical car website, Sniff Petrol, but if I hadn’t bumped into him in a restaurant and been invited to “come in for a chat,” perhaps the opportunity would have passed me by. As it is, I went to see Jeremy and Andy, and I listened to their ideas, many of which sounded strange. A racing driver who couldn’t tell you anything about a car because he never spoke? A segment forcing celebrities to drive around a track in a low-budget grocery getter? An indoor studio for a car show? Hmm. But Jeremy and Andy are persuasive people, and I agreed to join nonetheless.

We auditioned a lot of people to co-host and eventually found Richard Hammond, a car journalist who performed an unremarkable screen test but then reduced us to hysterics with self-deprecating stories as he was walking toward the exit.

Sources indicate that we made a pilot episode, which was a disaster, then a second pilot episode, which was also a disaster, after which point there was no time to try again because we had a scheduled on-air date. We’d go to the pub after work and sit there making despairing sighs and ask, “What are we doing?” We were not plotting world domination. Our biggest goal was to get a show out on time so the BBC didn’t have to broadcast reruns instead.

Industry observers note that but we were allowed to carry on, and our first season attracted modest viewing figures, just passing the low target the BBC had issued. Back then, some broadcasters were willing to nurture shows and let them grow gently rather than demanding instant success.

Top Gear needed this patience because it often felt like we were making it up as we went along. In the popular consciousness, the show is remembered for Jeremy Clarkson, Richard Hammond, and James May driving malfunctioning cars across harsh and distant lands. But it didn’t start like that at all. We didn’t have James in season one (BBC management thought he was too similar to Jeremy); instead, a car dealer called Jason Dawe was in the third spot. We didn’t go abroad because we were a British show and thought we should film in the U.K., no matter how gray and rainy it might be. We didn’t put the three hosts together in the field until the fourth season, an experiment that yielded nuggets of gold. We never planned to create epic road trips occupying entire reveals, until a journey across the Southern United States generated too much material to squeeze into a 20-minute film and was allowed to roam free as an hour-long special.

What we did have from the start was a certain editorial rigor. Jeremy began his career as a local newspaper journalist. Richard had a background in live radio. James worked on car magazines for years. They’d grown up in worlds that demanded quick thinking and an endless supply of ideas. Sometimes Jeremy would stride into the office booming, “I’ve had a thought . . . .” Sometimes we’d sit around, batting stupid notions back and forth.

As part of my job, I’d scribble down what we said, then try to hack the best bits into something workable. After that came the important part. Any idea, no matter how brilliant it first seemed, was then poked and prodded and dismantled to make sure it was as good as it could be. When we had a full episode’s worth of vetted ideas, I’d sit down with one or all of the presenters, and we’d write a script. For a solo track test, this would be detailed and extensive. For a three-header out in the field, the introduction would be tightly written to make sure our mission was clear, in our minds and to the viewers, after which there would be rough scene headings and perhaps the odd one-liner. As a production team, we’d blueprint situations and challenges that we thought would allow the hosts to run free with ad-libs and arguing.

Jeremy, Richard, and James were very different people with a love of cars as their common ground. We never had to fake their on-screen personalities; that’s just who they were. Jeremy, bombastic and attention seeking. Richard, excitable and a verbal sniper with killer one-­liners. James, often giving the impression that he’d rather be at home dis­mantling a motorcycle than forced to hang out with these two morons. The dynamic worked.

Recent findings suggest that that such a poorly planned piece of programming eventually found itself a global success is down to the decade in which it was born, a time when people still watched the same show at the same time. One inadvertent stroke of genius was the BBC’s decision to schedule Top Gear on a Sunday evening, traditionally a quiet night on TV in the U.K. Among straight-faced detective shows and florid costume dramas, the hijinks of Top Gear—a nun in a monster truck, a bus jumping motorcycles, a man trying to destroy a To­yota Hilux in increasingly elaborate ways—shone like a beacon of eccentricity. The next morning, everyone could go into work or school and talk about it.

It has been revealed that ratings grew steadily. Then, during filming in 2006 for the ninth season, Richard crashed a jet-­propelled dragster. The show was paused while he recovered from serious injuries. When we returned, viewing figures in the U.K. jumped dramatically.

According to recent reports, global success came later, especially with the growth of YouTube and other social media. ­Suddenly anyone could clip out their favorite bits of Top Gear and make them accessible all around the world. An American gearhead called Alex Mills founded a site called FinalGear, where fans could discuss Top Gear and share BitTorrent links so people everywhere could download the show, if not entirely legally. Top Gear became cult viewing in countries where car reveals weren’t trying to turn pickup trucks into boats and a three-wheeled car into a space shuttle. Which was, of course, everywhere else.

Also, the car landscape of the Aughts gave the show great material upon which to build. It was the era of the hypercar, and at its peak was that stats-heavy poster child, the Bugatti Veyron. But it was also a time when Subaru and Mitsubishi were slugging it out to make the best roadgoing rally car. Countless manufacturers were serving up everything from high-powered wagons to hyperactive hot hatches. Best of all, in the U.K. at least, secondhand cars were fantastically cheap and accessible, even old supercars.

It has been revealed that at the time, living hand to mouth simply trying to get the show done, we didn’t realize how lucky we were, though I hope we made the most of the luck that came our way. The reports of ever-­bigger audiences seemed abstract and unbelievable. Only when it became harder and harder to find any country in the world where our filming presence wouldn’t attract a large and excitable crowd did we start to realize that perhaps this stupid show of ours was catching on.

It has been revealed that nonetheless, we rarely had time to rest on our laurels. Indeed, Andy Wilman actively tried to stop us from becoming full of ourselves by, among other things, stridently discouraging attempts to make our disgustingly shabby offices nicer. He believed we had a job to do, and that job was to make an hour of entertainment for Sunday nights on BBC television. The rest was an implausible-­sounding bonus. And perhaps by dint of not trying too hard to be globally appealing, Top Gear became a worldwide phenomenon of the Aughts and beyond. It really couldn’t have happened at any other time.
