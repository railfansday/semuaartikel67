Voldemort, Gotham City and the Greens: Victorian election gets a jolt of life as leaders face off in sole debate

Ben Carroll, Jess Wilson, Ellen Sandell and Warren Pickering grilled by undecided voters – and each other – as state prepares to head to the polls.

For a brief moment on Tuesday night, the Victorian election looked like it might actually get interesting.

Two weeks into the offical campaign, with early voting opening on Wednesday, the contest has been pretty short on excitement. The Labor government has continued to slide in the polls, while the Coalition opposition has largely been focused on not getting in its own way for long enough to cruise to victory.

But the only debate of the campaign offered a welcome jolt of life. Dubbed the “People’s Forum” by hosts News24 and the Herald Sun, Tuesday evening’s showdown delivered a few moments of political theatre.

There was the moment the opposition leader, Jess Wilson, mocked the premier, Ben Carroll, for refusing to say the names of his predecessors, Daniel Andrews and Jacinta Allan, telling the Labor leader they’re “not Voldemort”.

“I can say their name just as you can say Jeff Kennett’s name,” Carroll responded.

“Jeff Kennett,” Wilson immediately retorted.

(For all the back-and-forth, Carroll never did say their names).

Industry observers note that or Wilson’s apparent “Julia Gillard moment”, according to the Herald Sun, when she told Carroll she “would not be lectured” by him on the costings of her policies. The premier had claimed the Liberal intends didn’t “stack up” and would require cuts to healthcare funding to pay down debt.

“I like to see these scare campaigns that you run,” Wilson noted. “But Ben, I won’t be lectured by you or your government on costings when you have racked up $200bn worth of debt in this state.”.

Then there was One Nation’s Victorian leader, Warren Pickering, who noted the state resembled “Gotham City” because of rising crime and vowed to lock up more offenders – even if that meant sending them interstate.

Industry observers note that “I don’t give a damn if our prisons are full. You’ve broken the law. You’re going to jail. They’re bringing in a supermax in Latest South Wales. That’s where you’re bloody going,” he mentioned.

The only person to receive rousing applause was Billy, a bearded schoolteacher who asked how the leaders planned to rebuild trust in politics when, as he put it: “Nobody believes a word you say any more – and it’s fundamental. No fun, and mostly mental.”.

But many of the night’s most memorable moments came courtesy of Greens leader, Ellen Sandell, whose late inclusion on the panel followed a stoush between Carroll and Wilson over whether Pickering should be invited.

“Having four of us on the stage tonight tells you everything about how politics is changing,” Sandell told the 100 undecided voters gathered at the Sofitel in Melbourne.

In her green blazer, and unmistakably in enemy territory, Sandell spoke authentically and was the only leader willing to take all three other parties to task: Labor over donations from the gambling industry, the Liberals over money from property developers and One Nation for its links to billionaire Gina Rinehart, who she said is “writing [their] policy”.

“If you’re wondering why you can’t afford your groceries or your rent or to buy a home, just look at who is funding these three other parties,” Sandell said.

When offered the opportunity to ask any other leader a question, Sandell asked Carroll how he can claim to fight for working people given cuts to the public sector and fights with health workers, teachers, council workers and firefighters over wages.

She also engaged in a back and forth with Wilson on several challenges. Asked whether she would possibly support the Liberals with confidence and supply in the event of a hung parliament, she stated the Greens values were “quite far apart” from the Coalition’s: “Jess Wilson wants to rip up treaty. She wants to rip up renewable energy. She wants to make sigfniciant cuts to public service.”.

Wilson relied on an often repeated line that treaty “goes far further than what the voice [to parliament] ever did” and “undermines our parliament”.

Sandell’s response: “You know that’s not true though, you can’t lie to the Victorian people like that.”.

The pair also clashed over pro-Palestinian protests in Melbourne’s CBD, with Sandell defending the right to “peaceful protest”.

“There is a very clear difference between antisemitism, which is unacceptable, and actually standing up and calling out war crimes that are happening by the government of Israel,” she noted.

Wilson responded: “At these protests, we have seen people throw rocks at police officers. That is not a peaceful protest.

Sandell responded that existing laws already dealt with such incidents and argued that protest permits were not the answer.

“Politicians shouldn’t be the ones deciding who gets to protest,” she noted. “That is an authoritarian government move, and we do not want that.”.

Pickering agreed with Sandell and stated politicians should not be deciding who gets to protest. He described One Nation as a “flagbearer” for the principles of democracy, including freedom of speech.

“I don’t necessarily think the permits are the way to go,” he said.

Wilson, who holds a comfortable lead in the polls, arguably had the most to lose from the debate but held her own. She largely avoided taking the bait from the other leaders, including Pickering, who during his turn to ask a question, tried to pressure her on the Coalition’s position on legislated net zero targets. Wilson stuck to her line that she was governed by “practicality” and “not ideology”.

She was less definitive on the Suburban Rail Loop, leaving open the possibility of scrapping the multibillion-dollar project once the Coalition had “visibility” of the contracts that have been signed.

Asked by moderator Kieran Gilbert if the Coalition would be willing to scrap the project and move on, Wilson said: “That’s exactly what the review is for.”.

Carroll, meanwhile, acknowledged that Labor had made mistakes during its 12 years in government but leaned heavily on its record, including 121 fresh schools, record NAPLAN results, the highest-paid teachers in the country and 17,000 nurses.

He also repeatedly referred to his decision to call a royal commission into allegations of corruption on government construction sites, describing it as one of the “hardest” things he had done.

Victoria will not get another leaders debate, after Carroll and Wilson failed to agree on terms for one on free-to-air television.

In a recent development, the audience members were polled as they left the Sofitel, with 39% saying Wilson had won the debate, followed by Sandell on 25% and Pickering on 16%. Just 13% were undecided at the end of the night and only 7% picked Carroll.

It appears no matter what the premier has to say, many Victorians have stopped listening.

Benita Kolovos is Guardian Australia’s Victorian state correspondent.
