Changes in the way that key media platforms measure impressions mean that performance and pricing benchmarks may be thrown into disarray come the new year.

Sources indicate that amazon has estimated brands will see 63% fewer impressions for display ads appearing across its retail properties, including its homepage, product pages and search results, according to a notice distributed among advertisers seen by Digiday. It’s due to the platform’s decision to shift from counting impressions the moment they begin downloading to a user’s device, to when they actually render on the user’s screen, from Jan. 1. Google announced a similar transition at the beginning of September, effective Feb. 17; spokespeople from both tech companies declined to comment.

In turn, six buyers who spoke to Digiday remarked they’re working to amend reporting methodologies and soothe client nerves.

Buyers fear that a reduction in reported impressions could mean that supply is constrained, driving up CPMs. “If there’s going to be fewer impressions, your CPMs will be higher,” explained Hannah Schatz, vp of programmatic operations at Coegi.

At the same time, the stricter impression reporting methodology means that lower-impact display placements further down Store pages will become even less desirable for advertisers — while top-of-page slots that stand a greater chance of influencing shoppers will see an increase in demand.

“There’s going to be probably more competition on the platform,” said Nola Ladd, awareness media supervisor at media agency Collective Measures.

Amazon has not issued guidance regarding the expected impact on pricing or costs, or whether it might enhancement its billing methodology around the new impression reporting, according to Stagwell Media Platform’s global product managing director, Dru Sil.

“The implications are up to us to discover,” he noted.

While the drop in impressions might unnerve some clients, it’s unlikely to provoke advertisers to reduce spending. Sil suggested that most advertisers will maintain spending levels, in order to establish new performance benchmarks in January and February.

“I don’t expect clients to like dramatically scale up budgets to try to get ahead of the curve. You’re likely going to continue to run what you’ve been running because you have an expected outcome that you’re trying to obtain from Amazon, regardless of the specific ad format,” noted Sil.

He added: “You need to build that baseline anyway, right? And the only way to build that baseline is to be running ads.”.

Benchmarking may be the real problem buyers face when they shake off the upcoming year’s hangover.

In a recent development, automated reporting processes, or media mix models that use impressions as a key input, might need to be recalibrated to account for the expected drop in reported impressions. “If you have a lot of automated processes, optimizations, reconciliations, built around these data sets already, you’re going to have to do a little bit of work,” said Paul DeJarnatt, vice president and managing director of integrated client solutions at Novus.

For most buyers, Amazon’s and Google’s tinkering with impression reporting merely underlines the importance of focusing on outcomes, rather than media KPIs, to understand campaign performance.

“The bottom line is, we don’t expect this to change campaign results. The shift will make reporting metrics look different, but it shouldn’t move ROAS or CPA, which are the outcomes our clients care about,” stated Jared Smith, senior director of strategy and innovation, January Digital.

It has been revealed that “Impressions, CPMs, they shouldn’t be north star metrics,” stated Ernie DeColte, svp of media at indie agency Level. “They’re tool metrics we use to optimize towards business impact. This might be a wake-up call to a few advertisers. Maybe it’s time to think about buying differently.”.

Still, communicating this change to clients who might be alarmed when they see impression counts fall off a cliff (or CPMs spike) is something media buyers across the industry are attempting to get ahead of.

“The client, even though that metric is misleading, is used to seeing a major number,” remarked Ladd.

“They’re used to seeing really low CPMs. They’re used to seeing really great impression volume, and that change and that difference is going to require agencies and media experts to really think about the way that they present success to the client,” she added.

Just because it’s got a donut graph doesn’t mean a report is saying very much. According to an Experian survey of 400 marketers, the profusion of dashboards and data isn’t helping marketers back up their decisions.

“In the past, a company like Unilever, buying massive media in Brazil from TV Globo, had a significant competitive advantage. Now you are buying from Meta or from Google or from Amazon or from Walmart or from TikTok, and that model doesn’t favor a scale of purchase. It favors the quality of engagement you generate on the platform.”.

—Unilever CEO Fernando Fernandez, speaking during its Capital Markets Day last week, on why better creative could be the secret to making its marketing operation work harder.
