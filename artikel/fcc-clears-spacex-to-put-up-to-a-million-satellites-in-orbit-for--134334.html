The Federal Communications Commission has authorized SpaceX to send up to a million satellites into orbit for its Starmind data-processing network.

In a news release, the FCC touted today’s decision as “the first authorization in U.S. history for a constellation of data centers in space” and echoed the high-flown language that SpaceX used to describe the network in its application 10 months ago.

“It promises to bring the human race closer to a Kardashev II civilization — one that can harness the full power of the sun,” the agency said.

FCC Chairman Brendan Carr remarked the plan “opens a bold fresh frontier for the computing infrastructure needed to power America’s leadership and growth.”.

Carr signaled that he was all in on the Trump administration’s support for rapid data center expansion, despite a growing backlash that figured in this month’s midterm elections. He said that “compute campuses or data centers are essential to sustaining America’s leadership” in artificial intelligence.

“By moving compute capacity beyond Earth, SpaceX and the many competitors working on their own orbital data centers are meeting the moment,” Carr mentioned.

In a post to X, SpaceX CEO Elon Musk told Carr that his praise was “much appreciated.”.

“This will be a great asset to America and the world,” Musk remarked.

Proponents of solar-powered orbital data centers say such constellations will address the top concerns about terrestrial data centers, including land use, power consumption and water requirements for cooling. Critics point to the engineering challenges, including concerns about cooling the satellites, managing space traffic and dealing with orbital debris.

Industry observers note that in approving SpaceX’s application, the FCC rejected opposition from Amazon and Viasat, as well as from the American Astronomical Society, which raised concerns about the constellation’s impact on the night sky. The AAS remarked in a statement that it was “disheartened and dismayed” by the FCC’s decision.

The FCC attached several conditions to its authorization, including requirements for communications backups, regular updates and a detailed strategy for orbital debris mitigation.

SpaceX was cleared to operate its satellites at orbits ranging from 500 to 1,002 kilometers (310 to 623 miles) in altitude. The first satellite is to be put into operation no later than 2033, and the FCC calls for the full constellation to be operating by 2040.

In a recent development, in August, SpaceX announced that it was partnering with Nvidia to design the compute payload for the Starmind AI1 satellite. “Each of the Starmind satellites will include Nvidia Rubin GPUs and Vera CPUs for datacenter-class space compute,” SpaceX said at the time.

SpaceX’s facility in Redmond, Wash., makes the satellites for the Starlink telecommunications network — but in a filing related to this year’s initial public offering, SpaceX noted its operation in Bastrop, Texas, would be put in charge of producing AI compute satellites.

The FCC stated it is continuing to review additional orbital data center proposals and intends to issue decisions on each proposal “according to the agency’s regular procedures.”.
