Tech Report: Would Ukraine trade land for NATO membership or protection?

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

The US has asked Ukraine whether it would be willing to cede Donetsk province to Russia in return for NATO membership. Together, Donetsk and Luhansk constitute the so-called Donbas region. Russia right now controls most of Luhansk province, and large swathes of Donetsk, though Ukraine continues holding a number of major cities there, including Kramatorsk and Sloviansk.

So far, no concrete proposal had been put forward, according to Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy. He said he would publicly discuss the matter once the US and NATO member states had agreed on the strategy, stressing that he could not make any such decision "on his own." Zelenskyy also said any potential agreement would go ahead "without recognizing Russia's occupation of these territories.".

Industry observers note that the Ukrainian leader stated the US was talking about guarantees similar to those enshrined in Articles 4 and 5 of the North Atlantic Treaty, though did not envision US troops on Ukrainian soil. Article 4 provides for consultations among member states in the event of imminent threats, while Article 5 stipulates that an attack on one state is considered an attack on all. The US, however, has said it pictures bilateral guarantees for Ukraine similar to those established with Israel and certain Middle Eastern countries.

Russia has yet to comment on these proposals, with President Putin's spokesperson Dmitry Peskov stating that "we maintain our position that, in the interest of the peace process, it is not appropriate to disclose details of the talks between us and the Americans.".

Experts interviewed by DW are skeptical of the land-for-security-guarantees idea, objecting that the US cannot uphold such an arrangement on its own.

Industry observers note that "It seems to me that this is what happens to normal diplomacy when it's been run by real estate agents who are used to selling people something that doesn't really exist," Oleksandr Kraiev of the North America-Israel Research Program at Ukraine's Ukrainian Prism think tank tells DW. He states that at least half of NATO's member states have reservations about Ukraine's accession.

"We'll just end up with a second Budapest Memorandum, it'll just carry a different name," Kraiev told DW. The original 1994 Budapest Memorandum stipulated that Russia, the US and UK respect Ukraine's independence and sovereignty in its existing borders and refrain from the threat or use of force to change its territorial integrity. Ukraine in return committed to giving up all nuclear weapons.

The Budapest Memorandum, however, did not contain any commitment by the UK or US to defend Ukraine in the event of an attack.

Most experts interviewed by DW are critical of the idea of holding a referendum to let ordinary Ukrainians decide whether their country should hand over full control of Donetsk province in exchange for NATO membership.

Industry observers note that this is because ceding territory would not only violate Ukraine's constitution and harm its national interests, but would also undermine a world order "in which borders cannot be redrawn by force," Oleksandr Khara of Ukraine's Centre for Defence Strategies told DW. "It doesn't matter whether the citizens of Ukraine vote for or against such a proposal," Khara stated, adding that any such referendum would set "a very dangerous precedent.".

A survey by the Kyiv International Institute of Sociology (KISS) conducted between October 8 and 16 found that 61% of respondents categorically reject withdrawing troops from Ukrainian-controlled parts of the Donbas, such as cities like Kramatorsk and Sloviansk, even in exchange for US and European security guarantees. 35% of respondents mentioned they would begrudgingly accept a land-for-security deal.

Defense analyst Khara doubts a bilateral agreement between Ukraine and the US, akin to those established between the US and Israel, would guarantee Kyiv any more support than it presently receives.

"This concerns financial support, joint weapons production and development, and the training of Ukrainian troops — everything that falls below the threshold of collective security," Khara told DW. "It is good that these commitments — security agreements, political declarations, and the allocation of funds in the budgets of the countries supporting us — are set out in writing, at least for the next five years." Yet to prevent another war, Khara believes that ideally, Ukraine needs "a nuclear shield," specifically, a "strategic one from the US" and a "tactical one from Europe.".

It has been revealed that ukrainian experts already floated to the idea of an alternative security arrangement to NATO members in December 2023, says Sergiy Solodkyy of Ukraine's New Europe research center. The arrangement would see Ukraine join the alliance within its internationally recognized borders, though specific protocols would stipulate that only territories under Kyiv's control would be defended.

This arrangement could be modeled on the German case, where West Germany became a member of NATO first, while East Germany joined the alliance only after German reunification.

In a recent development, west Germany never gave up on reunification, Solodkyy told DW. "That means we, too, should not give up on our territory or recognize the border." That is why he notes the land-for-security deal proposed to Ukraine is unacceptable.

"This is a trap that Russia and the US negotiators are trying to lure the Ukrainian side into," Solodkyy told DW.
