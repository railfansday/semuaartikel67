📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that princess, or Ñusta in the Andean indigenous language Quechua, is not your standard coming-of-age film. At the heart of Ecuadorian writer-director Christian Rojas’ lyrical debut feature is Kallpa, 12, who has a big dream: to be chosen as Ñusta, the princess in the teenager’s Andean community’s Winter Solstice Festival. But having been born in the body of a boy, Kallpa is barred from taking part. Rigid tradition and community rejection mean that Kallpa must find a newly introduced home and family among a group of local trans women.

In a recent development, kuri Fuerez stars alongside La Bruja de Texcoco, Bruno Giganti, Quispe Nuna, Romel Vargas, Kayita Quizhpe and Adele Pesantez. Princess, which is a co-production between Ecuador, Mexico, Argentina, Peru and Bolivia, features cinematography by Christian Hidalgo and editing by Jorge Macaya and Cristian Bermeo. The producers are María Ángeles Palacios and Daniela Fuentes Moncada. MMM Film Sales is handling sales. Princess, featuring dialogue in Spanish and Quechua, just celebrated its world premiere in the First Feature Competition of the 30th edition of the Tallinn Black Nights Film Festival (PÖFF) in Estonia.

After the premiere screening, Rojas and members of the production and creative team discussed Princess with the audience.

Sources indicate that “Ñusta was born when I was 10 years old, after I had experienced sexual abuse that [affected] my childhood and my life for a long time,” Rojas shared. “I dedicate this film to those who have survived, to those who are still fighting, and to those who open their eyes every day [with] the strength to keep on [being] who they are – everyone fighting for their freedom, their identity, and their right to a life without fear.”Rojas lauded his collaborators as “the best team I could ever have,” concluding: “I say thank you to everyone for being part of it, part of healing this wound collectively – and not just my wound, but those wounds of everyone who’s still fighting and thinks they’re alone. We have to resist, raise our voice, and hug each other as a family.”.

According to recent reports, safeguarding and ensuring a safe space, especially for the young actors, was a core focus for the Princess team. “The indigenous families … knew the story from beginning to end, but not the children,” Rojas said. “We had a psychologist on set and in pre-production, trying to develop this secure place for everyone so that we could actually enjoy making this movie.”.

Sources indicate that and the writer-director shared: “It was crucial to me for the children to feel like it was a game that they were playing, and that it was not a rigid process. So with this amazing team, what we did was play and go through it by playing.” One of the elements they used in the creative process with the kids was “a map of emotions,” Rojas remarked.

The Princess team also emphasized the importance of working in and with the local community with respect. “We didn’t want to extract anything,” the director emphasized. “What we wanted was real participation and reciprocity.”.

The film team also included members of different indigenous communities, including some leaders within those communities who work on diversity and related problems.

Sources indicate that the movie, set in 1997, includes an infamous real-life event that scarred Ecuador’s queer community that year. Police in the city of Cuenca back then raided a trans beauty pageant and arrested several people, who were then exposed to abuse and violence in custody.

“In terms of the visuals, we wanted to create the universe where Kallpa could travel, because this is a journey, and we wanted to always be with Kallpa on his journey,” Hidalgo explained. “And second, we wanted to be very close, so we are always with Kallpa.”.

The sound also plays into Princess‘ aesthetic of closeness, allowing the audience to hear things we cannot see from a distance.

When it came time to screen Princess for the community, “I was really afraid,” Rojas shared. But he felt validated when “a 70-year-old lady stood up and said, ‘We need to talk about these stories here in our community.'”.
