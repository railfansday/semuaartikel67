Space Exploration Technologies (SPCX -7.69%) just reached another major milestone with its Starship program, and the outcome could have huge implications for where its stock heads by 2030. Specifically, Flight 15 launched Nov. 13, and SpaceX caught the upper stage the day after. However, the Super Heavy booster aborted its catch and was lost in the Gulf.

Overall, the catch could strengthen confidence in SpaceX's ambitions, while the lost booster could raise concerns around development timelines.

Here's what investors should watch and how the mixed result could affect SpaceX stock.

In a recent development, starship is a heavy-lift launch vehicle designed to be fully reusable, enabling SpaceX to launch, recover, and fly its rockets again rather than building a new vehicle for every mission. Now that Starship's upper stage has been caught for the first time, the vehicle could be a future game changer for SpaceX as the organization could change the economics of getting into space and back.

NASDAQ · As of 4:00 PM ET 11/20/26.

Industry observers note that from there, if the company can make that process (reliably) repeatable, it could dramatically lower launch costs and increase the number of missions it can undertake. Plus, with a much larger payload capacity, Starship could potentially carry more cargo and satellites into orbit than existing rockets.

That could lead to cheaper launches and make previously uneconomical businesses more attractive, from satellite deployment and space-based communications to orbital infrastructure and lunar transportation.

Beyond that, Starship could also make it easier and cheaper for SpaceX to expand its Starlink business, allowing it to deploy more satellites, ramp up coverage, and increase network capacity.

Of course, the upper stage catch was only half of the planned double recovery. What does the lost booster mean for SpaceX?

SpaceX shares slipped about 0.6% on launch day, but have since edged higher. Even so, the lost booster could raise questions about development timelines, future launch costs, and the organization's ability to deliver on its longer-term ambitions.

The unsuccessful booster recovery attempt could delay the timeline for achieving full reusability.

According to recent reports, there's also a timing consideration. SpaceX has major ambitions for Starship, including its role in NASA's lunar program and the expansion of Starlink. Any delays would affect expectations surrounding both opportunities.

Recent findings suggest that still, the lost booster doesn't necessarily undermine the investment case for owning SpaceX. After all, Starship is a much more ambitious launch vehicle, so the development process is naturally more complicated. And if/when Starship finally becomes operational, it could provide the company with a latest avenue for growth.

Today, SpaceX stock trades at a price-to-sales (P/S) ratio of about 61, and based on the latest reported sales data, that works out to $2.36 in sales per share. This high multiple may be justified, assuming revenue increases significantly as SpaceX successfully recovers Starship.

Data source: Calculations by the author.

In a conservative case, if SpaceX generates $11.95 per share in sales, an 8-times compressed multiple could put shares near $96 by 2030, about 32% below today's level. At $17.49 per share in sales and a 12-times multiple, shares could trade near $210 by 2030, about 48% above today's level. Finally, in a more optimistic case, sales of $24.77 per share at 15 times sales could put shares near $372 by 2030, about 163% above today's level.

To be clear, these are scenarios, not promises, but they show where Starship's progress after Flight 15 could take the stock by 2030. In the end, the bigger opportunity is Starship's potential to make space transportation dramatically cheaper, opening up entirely newly introduced markets.
