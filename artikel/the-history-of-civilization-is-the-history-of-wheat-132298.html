The history of civilization is the history of wheat

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that according to Sumerian myth, when the world was young, there was no farming, no cattle, and no weaving. The original gods were forced to live in poverty. To solve this problem they created Ashnan, the goddess of grain, and Lahar, the god of sheep, and sent them to humans to cultivate their gifts.

In a recent development, together, Ashnan and Lahar furnished the rest of the pantheon with bread, beer, milk, and hides. But the fresh gods quarreled about which was greater. Enki, the god of wisdom, judged Ashnan the superior, reasoning that without grain there would be no bread, no beer, and, by extension, no cities and no Sumer.

The ancient Sumerians recognized something still true today: The story of wheat is the story of people. Human consumption of wheat is older than cities, older than writing, older than the wheel. In fact, some of the very earliest surviving examples of writing, which, like wheat, originate in the Middle East, aren’t epic poems or religious texts but administrative records of the storage and taxation of grain. Other staple crops dominated in other parts of the world (like rice in East Asia, and maize in the Americas), but it was wheat that fueled the Roman Empire and the Industrial Revolution. Wheat now provides one-fifth of the world’s calories. The fact that it does so is thanks to centuries of human ingenuity and scientific improvement.

Six thousand years ago, when the Sumerian civilization was beginning to emerge in what is now Iraq, human consumption of grain was already ancient history. Remains of domesticated wheat from around 10,000 years ago have been found in Anatolia. These leftovers were wild emmer and einkorn (two progenitors of wheat), which looked a lot like the other wild grasses growing around them: a tall stalk topped by a small seed head. They were variably distributed throughout the landscape, and their seeds ripened at different times, so collecting them would have been laborious. Nonetheless, the people of the ancient Levant found the effort worthwhile, as the seeds were calorie-dense and stored well.

By farming wheat, humans unintentionally made it worse but also improved it. Wild wheat had reproduced without human assistance for millions of years. Its seeds, the grains that we eat, are attached to the head of the plant by a thin stalk called a rachis. When the seeds are mature, the rachis shatters, dispersing the seeds on the ground.

Industry observers note that this process is essential for wild wheat to reproduce. Human intervention removed this selection pressure, allowing plants that did not shatter to reproduce. Because nonshattering is a recessive trait, nonshattering plants cannot produce shattering offspring.1 As a result, nonshattering plants proliferated, supplanting their wild predecessors. This is how archaeologists observed that domestication was underway.2.

Neolithic farmers collected and planted seeds from the heads of plants that remained intact, whereas seeds that had already fallen to the ground were less likely to be harvested or planted. Stores of emmer and einkorn dated as early as 7600 B.C.E. show rough scars, not the smooth tears left by spontaneous shattering, indicating that the natural reproductive process had been interrupted by harvesters. Once the seed-shattering trait had disappeared, wheat was almost completely dependent on humans collecting and planting its seeds to reproduce. In perhaps a thousand years, millions of years of selection for seed shattering had been undone.

On the positive side, yields went up. Grains grew bigger thanks to human cultivation. Naturally, grain size varied, but larger grains were less likely to be blown away or slip out of harvesters’ baskets and were therefore overrepresented in the seeds saved and replanted. On top of that, humans tend to plant seeds deeper than they naturally scatter, and larger seeds are more likely to germinate at greater depths. These processes are thought to have increased grain size by a quarter over the first 500 to 1,000 years of cultivation. By this time, wheat could no longer thrive without help from humans. But the transition to agrarian, sedentary communities enabled by bigger harvests also marked the beginning of our dependence on farming.

The next crucial step came just as wheat was beginning to spread to surrounding regions, when it absorbed a whole latest set of genes from another species.

It has been revealed that one way plants differ from animals is that they can hybridize much more easily, sometimes even happily adding whole fresh genomes to their own. Long before domestication, emmer wheat had already done this: Wild emmer is a naturally occurring amalgam of a cousin of einkorn and another species known as goat grass (Aegilops speltoides). Emmer is therefore tetraploid, meaning it has four sets of chromosomes.

In a recent development, emmer and einkorn wheat diffused throughout Anatolia to Cyprus and beyond as farmers migrated out of the Fertile Crescent. Around this time, emmer hybridized again, incorporating the genome of a second species of goat grass (Aegilops tauschii), bringing its chromosome set count to six. This hybridized wheat, which first appears in the archaeological record in northeastern Turkey around 6000 B.C.E., thrived because it had two valuable traits. First, it was free-threshing. Before this, farmers had to pound grains with a mortar and pestle to free them from their tough outer husks, adding a labor-intensive step to every harvest. Second, this upcoming hybrid had higher gluten content, meaning it could form stronger doughs that hold gas, allowing bread to rise. Initially, this saved labor and enabled tastier food. It is now known as bread wheat, or common wheat, and makes up more than 95% of wheat produced today.

Over the next several millennia, cultivation of bread wheat (and agriculture, more or less) spread throughout Europe and Asia. If a plant failed to grow in its upcoming soil or was killed by autumn frosts before reaching maturity, it would be superseded by its better-acclimated relatives. Over thousands of years, incremental changes resulted in countless subtly different local “landrace” populations. Wheat also evolved through further hybridization with closely related plants. This is how we got spelt, which combined the larger grains of bread wheat with the strong shells of emmer, protecting it against the wet and cold and allowing it to thrive further north.

Sources indicate that much of this adaptation was probably incidental, just as early selection for larger seeds and nonbrittle rachises had been. But even in ancient times, careful attention was paid to wheat husbandry and to which varieties thrived in which environments. In 50 B.C.E., the Roman writer Varro advised farmers that “on the threshing-floor the largest and best ears should be placed apart, to furnish the best seed.”3 Long before genetics, farmers understood that good seed begat good crops. This contributed to thousands of years of gradual improvement and specialization.

Industry observers note that the Romans may have been intentionally selecting the best plants, but modern scientific wheat breeding began in western Europe in the 19th century. This dramatically sped up yield improvements. A fortuitously large number of early English estate records have survived, meaning we have data for wheat yields starting from before 1275, at which time farmers were producing around 0.6 metric tons per hectare. This was actually worse than the (continental) Romans, who achieved yields up to 1.3 metric tons per hectare.4 Yields remained approximately flat before the 16th century, when they began increasing (and have not stopped).

From the early 1600s through to 1805, yields in Britain almost doubled, increasing from 0.74 in 1604 to 1.26 metric tons per hectare in 1805.5 Deliberate plant selection contributed to this improvement, but intensive selection — that is, aggressively multiplying the very best strains — didn’t really take off until the second half of the 18th century.

According to recent reports, farmers had always wanted to find high-performing seeds, but now they could buy them. Seedsmen emerged, trading hyperadapted local variants like Red Lammas, a strain popular in the 18th century that was known for thriving in chalky soils and making good-quality flour.

Cereals were big business: The original London Corn Exchange6 was built in 1747; the Paris exchange in 1767; Amsterdam had its own Korenbeurs more than a century earlier. From 1765, Britons began importing wheat with the express purpose of finding better varieties, and middlemen living in other countries would send samples of promising grains to contacts in Britain. Yields reflect the results of an increasingly commercialized sector. After stasis for hundreds of years, then steady 2.5% per decade growth in the previous two centuries, yields grew 60% between 1805 and 1865.

It has been revealed that in the 19th century, progress was still driven by entrepreneurs, usually curious farmers, rather than scientists. One key innovation, pioneered by Patrick Shirreff in Scotland and John Le Couteur in Jersey, was transitioning from selecting a handful of the best plants to breed from, to selecting the best single individual plant, and multiplying it. They didn’t understand the underlying biology, but the results were clear.

In addition to producing more of the best strains, repeated selection and rebreeding of individuals made the traits of the multiplied strains more consistent from generation to generation. As with shattering seed heads 10,000 years earlier, rebreeding meant undesirable genetic variants were eliminated from the population. Early breeders called this “breeding true,” and though the genetic basis for it was not yet understood, it was important to farmers, who prefer more consistent and predictable crops.

In a recent development, another key development was the intentional crossing of two plants to create a hybrid. A wheat plant can self-pollinate because it contains both male and female reproductive organs, fertilizing its ovules with its own pollen. The overwhelming majority of wheat plants are produced in this way, and therefore have just one parent, which acts genetically as both mother and father. This process doesn’t produce clones initially because each pollen grain or ovule, even from the same individual, is genetically distinct. But it does result in offspring with extremely limited genetic diversity, which diminishes even further with each successive generation of self-breeding.7.

Sources indicate that but sometimes cross-pollination produces plants that come from two parents rather than one. In nature this occurs randomly, but plant breeders deliberately take pollen from one plant and use it to fertilize the ovule of another. Shirreff was a pioneer of this technique, too. The best offspring resulting from these crosses would outperform both parents on some key traits. These high-performing varieties would then be multiplied and the seed sold to farmers.

Recent findings suggest that these techniques were adopted throughout Europe, where the Vilmorin Seed Manufacturer in France and the Swedish Seed Association in Sweden were among the first to institutionalize cereal breeding. In 1884, The Technology of Bread-Making identified as many as 65 wheat varieties, local and imported, available in English grain markets.

In a recent development, before the Industrial Revolution, Britain was a largely agricultural nation. But by the end of the 19th century, the population had increased more than sevenfold from about 4 million people in 1600 to 30 million in 1900, and only a tenth of workers were employed in agriculture. This would have been impossible without improvements in agricultural yields. The “dark satanic mills” of the Industrial Revolution needed wheat just as much as they needed coal and child labor.

The pace of improvement increased as researchers began to understand more about genetics and evolution. The discoveries of Darwin (natural selection) and Mendel (the inheritance of simple genetic traits, like seed shattering) were essential to understanding why and how techniques applied over the previous centuries had worked. Also critical were contributions of Wilhelm Johannsen, who untangled how much traits were shaped by genes as opposed to the environment, and Ronald Fisher, the “father of modern statistics,” who figured out the underlying maths. Fisher also highlighted how continuous traits like height could be determined by the cumulative effect of many individual genes. These insights, around 100 years ago, led to the development and widespread adoption of statistical tools to identify the best seed varieties and to predict how their offspring would perform.

Breeders generated a vast array of upcoming varieties for testing through a combination of exploration and crossing. Exploration, which was often sponsored by the state, meant collecting seeds from the many unique wheat varieties that had proliferated across the earth since the dawn of time. Britain’s Watkins Landrace Wheat Collection, for example, created in the 1930s, acquired more than 800 wheat cultivars from 32 countries, which could be crossed to create more than 300,000 new hybrid varieties. The USDA had the largest program. Using genetic material from these collections, nurseries would generate crosses for initial testing, multiplying the seeds until there were enough to carry out trials. Trials across several locations allowed researchers to distinguish the effects of genes and environment.

Recent findings suggest that but there were still constraints holding back wheat yields. One was wheat rust, a fungal disease that leaves a characteristic orange-brown powdery residue on plants before shriveling and killing them. Ancient writers knew about this disease: The Romans even had a yearly festival, the Robigalia, in which they performed rituals meant to keep it at bay. In the worst-affected years of the mid-20th century, wheat rust was killing off nearly 20% of American wheat. Breeding programs developed resistant varieties, but any respite was short-lived as the fungus would quickly evolve a newly introduced line of attack.

Another was wheat plants “lodging,” or falling over under their own weight. Fertilizer had become much cheaper thanks to the Haber-Bosch process for making ammonia from nitrogen gas, developed in 1909. But its usefulness for improving wheat production was limited because although fertilizer could in theory make the plants grow larger than before, applying it would often make the stems collapse, making the wheat unharvestable.

According to recent reports, at the time, these problems seemed to show that there were fundamental trade-offs limiting yields. It was assumed that further improvements would be incremental. This fueled worry that there would soon be insufficient food for the growing global population; biologist Paul Ehrlich predicted in 1968 that “in the 1970s hundreds of millions of people will starve to death.”.

Industry observers note that these gloomy forecasts turned out to be dead wrong. Between 1950 and 1990, wheat yields in many countries — rich and poor — doubled, as did the yields of other crops: the fastest improvement to farming efficiency ever seen. This explosive growth, enabled by advances in breeding together with mechanization and the wider adoption of fertilizer, is referred to as the “Green Revolution.” Norman Borlaug, an American agronomist often called the father of the Green Revolution, was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize for his work. In 2009 the director of the World Food Programme stated that Borlaug “saved more lives than any man in human history.”.

Borlaug didn’t discover anything new — he just implemented existing techniques better than anyone had before. In 1944, he joined the Mexican Agriculture Program (an initiative founded and funded by the Rockefeller Foundation) under which he produced about 6,000 new crosses over a decade, a huge quantity at a time when most breeders managed fewer than 200 per year. This quantity was essential to generating the high-performing outliers that could become popular new varieties. It is even more important when two traits you want (like yield and gluten content) are negatively correlated: The more varieties you test, the likelier you are to find one that bucks the trend, performing well on both.

Rather than approaching yield directly, the program focused on two main limiting factors: wheat rust and lodging. The intensive program of crossing and selecting made it possible to develop varieties that carried several forms of resistance to rust at once, and crossbreeding with Japanese “dwarf” wheat varieties, which were shorter and more robust, produced plants that could be juiced up with fertilizer without collapsing. By the 1960s, Borlaug had produced wheat varieties that had it all: resistance to lodging, multiple resistance to wheat rust, and high yields.

In a recent development, one way this breeding program differed from previous ones was that it was split between two separate regions. The original purpose of this was to squeeze two growing seasons into one year, halving the time it took to produce a new generation. After producing the first generation of offspring in the Toluca highlands, west of Mexico City, Borlaug moved the plants north to the lower-altitude Yaqui Valley, then produced the second generation in time to move back south for the following year. In these stages, selection was crude: Only varieties that failed to germinate or were affected by rust were culled. But later stages of the breeding program included yield trials and statistical selection methods.

Alternating between two regions had another, perhaps serendipitous, benefit.8 By advancing candidates that survived in both locations, Borlaug had selected those that could perform well across different environments. Selection for photoperiod-insensitive seeds (those able to mature in both despite differing daylight exposure) meant that varieties developed in Mexico might be successfully grown in other countries, and therefore could be shared across the world. Not all plants, and certainly not all wheat, can be photoperiod insensitive, but varieties tested by the Mexican Agriculture Program were, which may explain why wheat was the first crop to flourish in the Green Revolution. Maize, for example, is so sensitive that breeders operate semi-independent breeding programs for different latitudes.

If Ehrlich was aware of this work when he predicted mass starvation in 1968, he cannot have known its significance. In the mid-1960s, India and Pakistan began importing Borlaug’s new wheat varieties. One descendant of these imported wheats (called Kalyan Sona or “golden savior”) performed extremely well and became one of the most commonly planted varieties in the region. From 1965 to 1975, wheat yields in India and Pakistan nearly doubled;9 production increased so much and so quickly that there were shortages of labor for harvest. Over time, other less developed countries followed a similar path.

The next stage of plant breeding was identifying specific genes for the precise properties that farmers wanted. The problem was that genotyping and genome sequencing were expensive and slow. The first genome was sequenced in 1977, but it belonged to a bacteriophage (a virus that infects bacteria) with a genome nearly a million times smaller than a human’s. Sequencing the first human genome cost somewhere between $300 million and $3 billion, and was achieved much later. And this was just a diploid human genome. Wheat has a genome five times larger than that, partly because of its three hybridized subgenomes.

Breeders came up with a comparatively quick and dirty solution: marker-assisted selection. Instead of tracking genes directly, they used much cruder tests to find genetic features (“markers”) that correlated with given traits. One technique was restriction fragment length polymorphism analysis, in which scientists use enzymes to chop DNA into pieces and then literally measure how long the resulting fragments are by how promptly they are pulled through gel by an electric current. While breeders would not know which genes were responsible for traits, they would know that a trait was correlated with a particular pattern of fragment lengths when cut with a particular enzyme.

Recent findings suggest that despite the crudeness of the method, it still worked for some traits, especially simple ones like rust resistance, which is often controlled by one or two genes. This allowed farmers and breeders to work out whether a variety was going to be good without going through a whole season of growing, as Norman Borlaug had to. This marker-assisted selection for many crops became routine in the 1990s. Field trials remained important, but most strains were filtered out at the marker identification stage, and few ever made it to the field. At the beginning of the decade, only a few hundred markers were known, but by the end, this had expanded to thousands.

Early marker-assisted selection was of limited use for traits like yield, which are influenced by thousands of genes. But we are no longer restricted to looking at a small number of genetic markers. DNA sequencing costs have decreased by a factor of 100,000 since 2000. Now breeders will often use “genomic selection”: essentially, using the entire genome as a marker and letting statistical software predict which genomes are associated with different traits. Genomic selection can tell us if 500 different genes each contribute 0.2% to a trait where traditional marker-assisted selection would have been useless.

This has revolutionized the process of plant breeding — if not the outcome.10 Global crop yields continue to increase at a steady rate, reflecting the fact that progress is getting harder to come by. We can now use the same genetic prediction tools for complex traits, like yield, grain quality, and drought tolerance, that we once did for simple ones. At the time Norman Borlaug was working, it took months or years and thousands of wheat plants to evaluate a new variety’s performance. Today, it can take just days to germinate a seedling and make predictions based on a small leaf sample.

Sources indicate that pessimists would say that wheat domesticated us, that we have spent thousands of years in backbreaking labor serving its reproductive ends. Optimists would argue our coevolution has benefited humanity far more, liberating us from a state of subsistence and creating a caloric surplus to sustain the lives of billions.

The human species used to live hand to mouth: Virtually all our labor was devoted to growing and harvesting food to fend off starvation. Now, in most developed countries, only a tiny fraction of the population works in agriculture. Wheat’s astonishing success is a crucial part of this change. It is the most widely grown crop on earth, covering 1.5% of the planet’s total land area, and is responsible for one-fifth of the world’s calorie intake. But the amount of labor required to produce those calories has decreased enormously. Since 1900, despite the world’s population increasing more than fourfold, the price of wheat is roughly a quarter of what it was.

That transformation is the result of sustained, incremental improvement to yield, to pathogen resistance, to ease of harvest, to taste, to gluten content, and much more. The advancements came from early farmers unknowingly favoring larger seeds; from finicky varieties that couldn’t handle frost, drought, and other hardships being killed off by the elements; and from breeders making active choices about what traits they wanted to cultivate, increasingly backed by the might of modern science. Today, we can sequence wheat’s entire genome and select for exactly the traits we want. Advances in breeding mean that similar stories can be told for every other major crop and farm animal. Each of us now enjoys the fruits of these advances at every mealtime.

Industry observers note that A lot has changed since the first wheat was farmed in the Fertile Crescent. But Enki is just as correct now as he was thousands of years ago: We would have no Sumer without grain.
