I let an LLM fill my AI tools directory. 11 months later, 41% of its picks were dead.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In December 2025 my AI tools directory, AI Platforms List, had two jobs that asked DeepSeek for newly introduced tools and saved whatever came back into platforms.json. On December 9 I pushed a commit called "CRITICAL FIX: Stop DeepSeek from hallucinating fake platforms". The main change was this block in the prompt:.

Recent findings suggest that each call went to deepseek-chat over the plain chat completions API. It couldn't search or open a page, so it had no way to verify anything.

Recent findings suggest that in November 2026 I rebuilt the directory, and we opened every website the old list pointed to. Of the sites that only my two DeepSeek jobs had added, 41% were gone. For the tools the list started with, it was 5.7%.

It has been revealed that the daily job was a GitHub Action. It ran at 06:00 UTC from December 5, 2025 to January 11, 2026, in three rounds of up to 8 tools. Its prompt asked for tools that "Have a working website/product (no vaporware)" and ranked affiliate programs above everything else: "PRIORITIZE platforms with active affiliate/referral programs (highest priority!)". Every tool it added was saved with added_by: "ai-auto-discovery" and verified: false.

According to recent reports, the mass job went in at 04:00 UTC on December 9, in a commit titled "Add ULTRA HIGH VOLUME discovery modes - 90,000 platforms/month". It ran inside a generator script that looped with 30 seconds between cycles. Every second cycle it called DeepSeek, and after each cycle it pushed its changes to GitHub. Between December 9 and December 18 it added 317 entries.

Industry observers note that both compared each fresh name and domain against the list to skip duplicates. Neither one opened the site before saving it.

About two hours later I added a URL validator, and the generator ran it every tenth cycle. It sent a HEAD request (a GET if the site refused HEAD) with a 10-second timeout. To catch parked domains, it looked for eight strings in the final URL after redirects, such as sedo.com/search and hugedomains.com. It never read the page.

Sources indicate that its first run, at 06:12 UTC on December 9, checked 729 entries and flagged 96 for removal: 81 dead and 15 with DNS errors. Three minutes later I put 34 of them back, because they were real sites that refuse HEAD requests. That run flagged 0 parked domains.

The prompt fix came about an hour and a half later, and its commit message highlights what the validator was up against: "63% of discovered platforms were FAKE (90 out of 143 in Cycle 71)" and "DeepSeek was making up platform names and guessing URLs".

Industry observers note that A template turned the list into pages. On January 8, 2026, a commit titled "Generate 1,140 vibe-coding content pages" added 301 comparison files, and every one of them opens with the same line: "Both are powerful vibe coding platforms, each with unique strengths and use cases." Zapier, an automation tool, got that line too. 26 of the files have undefined in the name, where a slug was missing. zapier-vs-undefined.json is titled "Zapier vs Claude for VS Code: Which is Better for Vibe Coding in 2026?".

Three and a half hours later came a second commit, "Add 3,023 comparison pages for all platform combinations". The next day another commit put the whole site at 4,836 pages and 2.75 million words.

The best day was January 19, with 1,265 impressions. January 24 had 502, and January 25 had 20. Google's Search Status Dashboard reveals no ranking upgrade in that stretch. The December 2025 core update ended on December 29, and the February 2026 Discover update started on February 5. The mass job's last entry was on December 18, and I turned the daily job off on January 11. I don't know which pages Google reacted to.

On November 8, 2026, we ran a crawler over all 1,031 websites in the old list (1,095 entries, since some tools shared a site). It made one request per site plus redirects, waited at most 15 seconds, never sent two requests at once to the same host, and identified itself as AIPlatformsListBot with a link back. Each site that failed got a second try a few minutes later and a third about half an hour after the first. We only counted a shutdown when the site itself stated so. For each gone site, we asked the Internet Archive for a capture from 2023 to 2025 and opened it to tell a real page from a parked domain.

Sources indicate that 44 sites blocked automated checks. Of the other 987, 763 still work (68 of them under a upcoming name or address) and 176 are gone, which is 17.8%. A parked or for-sale page was the most common way a gone site failed, 51 of the 176.

Another 20 checked sites had entries from more than one source, and 1 of those is gone. Counting the two DeepSeek jobs together, including one site both of them added, 134 of 327 checked sites are gone, or 41%.

According to recent reports, after the Dec 9 commit, the mass job's gone share fell from 65.1% to 37.6%. That is still more than six times the rate of the list I started with. For 81 of the mass job's 121 gone sites, the Internet Archive has nothing real from 2023 to 2025: 42 have no capture at all, 37 only a parked page and 2 an empty one. Many of those 81 may never have been products.

The groups aren't the same age. Most of the original list was in place by June 2025, while the DeepSeek entries came in December, so they had different amounts of time to fail.

Some parking pages bounce you to /lander with a line of JavaScript, which the last test catches. A site that now opens a different domain fails too. And since anyone can submit a URL, including one that points into your own network, the check follows redirects one hop at a time and refuses private addresses at every hop.

In a recent development, when I relaunched the list in November 2026, I dropped the mass job's entries and the research batches. 21 sites that only the daily job had added are still listed, all real products (Krisp and Vectara among them), each opened again on November 10. A tool goes live only after the check above passes, and a person approves every free listing.

The study I published on November 8 told bot entries apart by a discovered_by field and an id prefix. The daily job set neither, so its 55 entries were counted with the original list. With them moved, the original list's gone share drops from 7.3% to 5.7% and the bot side's from 36.4% to 35.0%, so the gap grows from five times to six. The study page now shows the corrected numbers.
