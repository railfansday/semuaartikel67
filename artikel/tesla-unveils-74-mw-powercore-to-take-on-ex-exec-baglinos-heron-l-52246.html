Tesla unveils 7.4 MW Powercore to take on ex-exec Baglino’s Heron Link

🌐 Source: Electrek • 📅 17 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 7 min read (1375 words, 📚 Deep Dive)

  • Update: Tesla has since released a full spec sheet for Powercore, including efficiency, warranty, and its data center aims.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Tesla has unveiled Powercore, a solid-state medium-voltage transformer that also handles the inverter and breaker functions in a single unit. Tesla rates it at 7.4 MW (9 MVA) continuously at 34.5 kV, with grid-forming support up to 10 MVA of peak power.

It’s made in America and scheduled to start operating in 2027. That’s the same year Heron Power, the startup founded by Tesla’s former top engineer Drew Baglino, intends to start volume production of its own solid-state transformer, the Heron Link.

Update: Tesla has since released a full spec sheet for Powercore, including efficiency, warranty, and its data center aims. We’ve updated the article with the fresh details.

Sources indicate that tesla first remarked it would build its own transformers in September 2025, when it unveiled Megapack 3 and Megablock. Megablock connects four Megapack 3 units to a transformer and switchgear, but Tesla shared next to nothing about the transformer itself.

Powercore is that missing piece. Tesla confirmed it today through its Tesla Megapack account on X:.

About an hour later, Tesla followed up with a much longer post on X laying out the full spec sheet:.

It also packs a built-in solid-state medium-voltage breaker that Tesla notes clears faults up to 1,000x faster than a conventional breaker. There’s no flammable oil, and it’s modular, so components can be serviced in the field without swapping the whole unit.

Industry observers note that tesla is pitching one product for three markets: battery storage, utility-scale solar, and 800 Vdc data centers. For Megablock, Powercore replaces the current two-step setup, where Megapack’s inverters output 480 V AC before a separate transformer steps it up to medium voltage. Tesla says that old architecture had twice the losses. For solar, Powercore works as a central inverter for today’s 1,500 Vdc arrays and upcoming 2,000 Vdc systems.

Tesla still hasn’t shared pricing, customers, or a factory location. It only states Powercore will be made in the US “of U.S. and imported parts” with first units operating in 2027.

The idea behind a solid-state transformer is simple enough. A typical solar or battery project uses low-voltage inverters to convert DC into AC, then a big oil-filled, iron-core transformer to step that up to grid voltage. A solid-state unit does both jobs with silicon carbide power electronics, which means less gear, a smaller footprint, and active control over voltage and frequency. That control is what “grid-forming” means: the unit can help hold the grid steady instead of just following it.

And with lead times on conventional transformers stretching into years as data centers and renewable projects pile into interconnection queues, there’s real demand for an alternative.

You can’t look at Powercore without thinking of Heron Power.

According to recent reports, baglino spent 18 years at Tesla and finished as SVP of powertrain and energy, in charge of batteries, motors, power electronics, and energy products. He left in April 2024 and rolled out Heron Power in 2025 to build solid-state transformers.

In a recent development, he brought a lot of Tesla’s power electronics talent with him. As we reported when Tesla first declared its transformer aims, Heron hired engineers who led power electronics design for Powerwall 3, the next-gen Megapack, and Superchargers, along with the person who ran Tesla’s power electronics controls team for charging and energy products.

Since then, Heron has raised a $38 million Series A, a $140 million Series B led by Andreessen Horowitz and Breakthrough Energy Ventures, and a $60 million credit facility from J.P. Morgan and TriplePoint Capital. Former Tesla CFO Zach Kirkhorn sits on its board, and Tesla co-founder JB Straubel is an investor.

It has been revealed that it’s also building Heron Factory One, a 286,000-square-foot plant in Morgan Hill, California, designed to make 10,000 Heron Links a year. That’s over 40 GW of annual capacity, with commercial production planned for late 2027.

Sources indicate that and the customers are lining up. Vantage reserved more than 1 GW for its data centers, Intersect reserved 1.5 GW, LG Energy Solution Vertech is integrating Heron Link with its US-made battery systems, and RWE signed a pilot to install Heron Links at a Texas battery storage site in April 2027. Baglino indicates Heron has 50 GW of orders in the pipeline.

According to recent reports, on paper, Powercore is the bigger box, and now it has the better numbers too. At 7.4 MW continuous, it carries about 50% more power per unit than the 5 MW Heron Link, and its 98.86% peak efficiency edges out Heron’s 98.6%. But Tesla’s figure is a peak number, and Heron doesn’t label its own as one, so it’s not quite apples to apples. Neither firm has shared pricing.

It has been revealed that tesla is also going straight at Heron’s data center pitch. Powercore converts medium voltage directly to 800 Vdc, the rack architecture Nvidia is pushing, and Tesla says it’s working with Nvidia on 800 Vdc designs. Heron’s data center version does the same 34.5 kV AC to 800 VDC conversion with built-in storage, and data centers make up about a third of its business. Tesla’s answer to the storage piece is Megapack on the DC bus, and it says it already has 10 GWh of Megapack running at data centers.

This one is a little awkward for Tesla.

A significant chunk of the people who designed Tesla’s power electronics over the last decade now work at Heron, and the guy who ran the whole org is its CEO. Tesla’s energy business also lost its longtime leader, Mike Snyder, last month, according to the WSJ.

According to recent reports, this is all wrong. Tesla started developing this 3 years ago. Before Heron. The team selling Powercore is same as Megapack that has a way larger customer base than Heron. Also Heron is not a bankable company per project finance terms. So they will struggle to get projects financed through equity and senior debt . Not as Tesla.

To be fair, Tesla still has plenty of power electronics expertise. Its silicon carbide inverters are in millions of cars and every Megapack. And it has something Heron doesn’t: a massive installed base and a sales channel. Tesla notes it’s almost sold out of Megapacks for 2027, and bundling Powercore into Megablock is an easy upsell. Plenty of developers will take the one-stop shop.

Tesla’s specs now match or beat Heron’s on paper. But Heron has named customers, a pilot site with an install date, and a factory under construction. Tesla has none of those yet. I wouldn’t be surprised if Heron is actually ahead here.

But either way, the transformer shortage is significant enough for both companies to sell everything they can build.
