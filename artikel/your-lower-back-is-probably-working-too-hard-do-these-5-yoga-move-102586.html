If you’re seeking ways to strengthen your back, chances are you won’t find a lot of solutions for how to strengthen your mid-back. Yoga teacher and certified personal trainer Amber Sayer, who works with desk workers as well as competitive athletes, explains why the missing mid-back component is so essential and how to effectively strengthen it.

When it comes to strength training and your back, almost everyone talks about avoiding lower back strain or correcting tech neck. But there isn’t much information about how to strengthen and improve endurance in the muscles of the mid-back.

It has been revealed that and that’s a shame. This overlooked part of the body not only contributes to your ease of posture and movement during everyday life, but it also ensures that all of the muscles in your back can work together effectively. Basically, when any part of the back is underutilized, it creates more work for the other parts. Practicing exercises that challenge and build endurance in your mid-back supports functional back strength throughout the spine and helps minimize the risk of forcing the muscles in the lower back to overcompensate. Think of your mid-back muscles as one of those things you don’t tend to think about until there’s a problem.

Recent findings suggest that these muscles attach to the thoracic spine and include the middle and lower trapezius, rhomboids, latissimus dorsi, erector spinae, and serratus posterior. You’ve probably already been practicing yoga poses or other exercises that strengthen the muscles in the mid-back in various ways without realizing it.

But when you become more intentional about including the following poses, you ensure that you target the various muscles that, collaboratively, strengthen your entire back.

Rather than practicing the entire list of yoga poses all at once, incorporate a couple into your workout routine, occasionally changing up which ones you explore.

Or try a single pose as a one-off whenever you could use a respite from sitting and staring at your screen.

This beginner-friendly pose is your starting point as you’re learning to strengthen the mid-back muscles. Because Sphinx Pose (Salamba Bhujangasana) props you on your forearms to create the backbend, it can be almost entirely passive for the back muscles. But there are a few subtle actions you can take that engage the mid-back in significant yet non-intense ways.

Lie on your belly with your forehead on the mat and your legs straight behind you, hip-distance apart, with the tops of your feet on the mat. Bring your palms and forearms to the mat with your elbows beneath or slightly in front of your shoulders. Actively press down through your forearms to lift your chest off the mat. Draw your shoulder blades down and away from your ears and toward the middle of your back. And the crucial part for your mid-back is to keep your forearms still but feel as if you’re dragging your elbows toward your hips. Keep that action as you stay here for several breaths.

If the backbend feels too intense, inch your forearms slightly in front of you to lessen how much your chest is lifted from the mat.

But when you hover your hands in Cobra Pose (Bhujangasana), you rely exclusively on your mid-back and core muscles to create the shape. The point isn’t how far you lift yourself off the mat. It’s that you create any lift at all.

Then press the tops of your feet into the mat and engage your mid-back muscles to lift your chest any amount off the mat. To intensify the workload on your mid-back muscles, lift your palms an inch or two above the mat and continue to hover them. You can also bring your arms straight out from your shoulders like the letter T, and then turn your thumbs toward the ceiling to increase the strengthening potential for the trapezius.

But leaning forward in Chair Pose (Utkatasana) without completely collapsing forward and reaching your arms overhead also challenges—and strengthens—the muscles of the mid-back. Specifically, the lower trapezius and erector spinae.
