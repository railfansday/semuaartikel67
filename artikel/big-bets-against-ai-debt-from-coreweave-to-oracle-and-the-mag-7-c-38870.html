Big bets against AI debt, from CoreWeave to Oracle and the Mag 7, could soon hit the ETF market

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Concerns about balance sheets and valuations in the artificial intelligence sector have hit the market hard since October, and investors who have shorted publicly traded tech companies in the AI trade have profited. Most investors don't think as much about "shorting" debt, but there will be a way through exchange-traded funds to profit from widening credit default swap spreads of top AI companies.

Both Tuttle Capital and Defiance ETFs have filed for funds that target the credit default swaps of companies closely linked to the AI trade, from CoreWeave and Oracle to the Magnificent Seven: Alphabet, Amazon, Meta Platforms, Microsoft Corporation, Nvidia, Tesla, and Apple.

Recent findings suggest that credit default swaps are contracts in which investors pay for protection in case the borrower can't repay its debt. Wider spreads indicate investors view the bonds as riskier. CDS is a closely watched indicator of issuer health in the debt markets, especially in the AI market this year. The ETFs are designed to gain when the CDS spreads of these companies widen, either as a group in the case of a Magnificent Seven CDS fund, or individually in the case of proposed ETFs targeting Oracle, CoreWeave and Meta. The funds would lose value when the market assesses the creditworthiness of these debt issuers as improving.

Both ETF managers filed for CoreWeave and Oracle CDS funds, specifically, among broader AI and semiconductor organization CDS portfolios.

If the exchange-traded fund managers get approval from the Securities and Exchange Commission, these portfolios would be the first of their kind in an ETF market that has become more aggressive in recent years about launching higher-risk strategies revolving around more complex financial instruments than just public stocks and bonds. The ETF managers blueprint to employ leverage in the portfolios, which both increases risk and the potential for outsized returns on any given day, similar to the way existing single-stock leverage and inverse ETFs function.

The ETF managers say these CDS funds can serve an important role in a market on edge about AI debt.

"As AI infrastructure investment accelerates and companies take on significant capital commitments to fund that growth, we see an opportunity to give investors more targeted tools to manage credit risk and hedge potential financial stress across some of the biggest names driving the AI buildout," Sylvia Jablonski, CIO of Defiance ETFs, said in a statement to CNBC.

Neocloud companies like CoreWeave are a major focus for AI skeptics, an AI bubble watch compounded by the exit of Jensen Huang from Nvidia, who is seen as a critical player in financing the AI buildout, with Nvidia called by some the "central bank of AI.".

Recent findings suggest that apollo Global Management CEO Marc Rowan on Tuesday said that neocloud tech giant AI projects could face an existential crisis if Nvidia funding dries up. "There was a strong incentive for Nvidia as a business matter to get these projects off the ground," Rowan said in an interview with CNBC's "Squawk on the Street." "If that banking function, that guarantor function, gets taken away, access to capital for a large number of neoclouds is going to disappear.".

In its earnings filings and conference call on Tuesday, Nvidia shifted the tone in discussing the way it finances the AI ecosystem.

Matthew Tuttle, founder and CEO of Tuttle Capital Management, noted it is less likely that these ETFs will find an audience among retail investors than smaller institutional players.

According to recent reports, "It may be a stretch for the average retail guy," he mentioned, but he added that having managed leveraged and inverse ETFs, it is clear that many investors "get interested" when a particular asset is seeing outsized moves, as has been the case with AI manufacturer-linked CDS in 2026. "The stuff that moves a lot is the stuff that tends to generate the most interest," he said.

In a recent development, both prospectuses stress that these funds are not designed to move inversely to the reference companies' stocks and can lose money while a tech giant's shares decline. They also note these funds don't short bonds or bet directly against a company's stock, and to buy CDS protection, they pay premiums, which are a unique cost to the fund manager whether or not credit conditions worsen.

The CDS of these companies have been on the move in a major way as part of the AI trade over the past year.

CoreWeave's 9.75% bond due in 2031 is yielding close to 16%, while spreads on its five-year credit default swaps were recently between 1,100-1,200. Spreads over 1,000 are generally considered a sign of distress.

Oracle CDS contracts have moved up over the past year from below 80 to a current range above 300.

Sources indicate that the CDS spreads on the biggest tech companies, including Amazon and Meta as examples, rose over 100% within the past year, as their free cash flow turned negative from spending heavily on AI. Even though the market is much less concerned about their balance sheets than that of the neoclouds, major tech company CDS spreads did hit five-year highs earlier this month.

Tuttle mentioned that these ETFs, if approved, would be the "easiest" to use for smaller institutions and family offices that want CDS exposure and "are either too small to trade with the Goldmans of the world or lack the in-house expertise to trade CDS on their own.".

"It is a different way to hedge the AI trade," Tuttle stated.

ETF investing experts expressed skepticism about this approach and market reception. "I don't see much demand materializing for these types of products," remarked Nate Geraci, president of NovaDius Wealth Management. But he added it does make sense to target these ETFs "at retail investors who are seeing scary headlines around AI capex spending. Yet another way to speculate (gamble)," he said.

According to recent reports, todd Sohn, chief ETF strategist at Baird Strategas, said he foresees confusion with how these funds will work. "How many non-professionals know what CDS are?" he said.

According to recent reports, tuttle notes it won't take billions in assets to make an ETF focused on AI CDS a minor financial success. "My criteria is I want to see a runway to $20 million, which is about our breakeven. I see it here. It doesn't mean it will happen, but I think we have a decent chance.".
