Arta Moves Beyond Shipping Art, to Helping Collectors Pay for It

Industry observers note that for years, Arta has helped collectors get art from a gallery or an auction house to their homes. Now, it wants to help them pay for it, too.

The Fresh York–based technology tech giant has rolled out Arta Pay, a new financing service that allows collectors to buy art, watches, jewelry, and other luxury goods on credit, paying for them over 10 months while sellers receive the full purchase price within days.

It has been revealed that the service offers credit lines ranging from $1,000 to more than $100,000, targeting a part of the market that has often fallen between conventional consumer financing and the large art loans offered by private banks. Here’s how it works: A collector buying a $10,000 painting would put down at least 10 percent, or $1,000, and pay the remaining balance in nine monthly installments. There’s a flat financing charge of 12.5 percent on the amount borrowed, disclosed upfront. In this example, that would add $1,125 to the cost of the painting if the buyer takes the full nine months to repay it.

In a recent development, the gallery, meanwhile, gets its $10,000 within five business days, without having to worry about collecting the remaining payments. Arta takes on the repayment and fraud risk, and charges sellers no processing fees.

Recent findings suggest that “We’ve spent years building the post-purchase technology that powers commerce across art, luxury, and collectibles,” Adam Fields, Arta’s founder and CEO, remarked. The latest service, he added, is designed to make the transactions easier.

Arta has become a familiar name behind the scenes of the art market, providing shipping, insurance, and other logistical services to auction houses, galleries, and online marketplaces. Its clients include Sotheby’s, Bonhams, and Artsy, and the firm says it has facilitated the movement of more than $1 billion worth of objects across more than 65 countries.

The move into financing brings Arta into an earlier stage of the transaction, when a collector is deciding whether to buy something in the first place.

Sources indicate that for galleries, the attraction is fairly straightforward. Many already allow trusted clients to pay in installments, but doing so can mean waiting months to receive the full price of a work. Arta Pay gives them a way to offer similar flexibility without extending credit themselves.

The service can be used at online checkouts, in person, or through an ordinary invoice, a useful feature in a business where plenty of sales are still negotiated over email, WhatsApp, or a conversation at an art fair. Collectors can also apply for a credit line before deciding what to buy, using it across purchases from different sellers.

Among the first businesses to adopt the service are Rago Wright, the auction house group, and Swiss Watch Expo.

“Our collectors want flexibility in how they buy, and Arta Pay gives them that,” Richard Wright, CEO of Rago Wright, noted in a statement. For the auction house, he added, the advantage is offering payment plans without having to manage the financing or assume the risk.

Sources indicate that the idea isn’t entirely upcoming. Art Money, another organization that allowed collectors to buy art in installments, stopped taking new business in June 2024 after running out of operating capital. Its founder, Paul Becker, is now managing director of Arta Pay, and several former Art Money employees have joined the operation. Fields told ARTnews that the two businesses are separate, but argued that Arta has an advantage in its established technology platform and relationships with major art-market businesses.

That existing network is central to Arta’s intends. Rather than building a financing business from scratch, the company is adding another service to a platform already used by galleries, auction houses, and collectors.

For now, Arta Pay is available to US citizens and residents, although they can use it to buy from participating sellers overseas. Applications take a few minutes, and most applicants receive a decision in real time following a soft credit check. The credit lines are issued by Lead Bank, with Arta handling the service.

Industry observers note that the question, of course, is whether giving collectors another way to spread out the cost of buying art will encourage them to buy more of it. For dealers who would rather spend their time selling paintings than chasing payments, the appeal is obvious.
