'We slept here': Flight delays linger as airlines recover from ground stop, but schedule improves Monday

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

U.S. flights delays eased on Monday as airlines scrambled to get back on track after the Federal Aviation Administration grounded departures nationwide for more than four hours on Friday.

Nearly 300 U.S. flights were canceled and close to 1,000 were delayed Monday, a fraction of Sunday's disruptions, according to FlightAware data.

In a recent development, transportation Secretary Sean Duffy over the weekend tied the issue that prompted officials to call for the ground stop to a router in southern Arkansas and work to update copper wires to a fiber-optic system overseen by L3Harris Technologies. FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford said the upgrades were part of "routine" maintenance to switch over to fiber optic.

Sources indicate that officials ordered the ground stop before 4 p.m. ET on Friday and it lasted until after 8 p.m. ET, marking the longest halt to U.S. flight departures since 9/11.

Industry observers note that hundreds of thousands of travelers were stranded and about 18,000 U.S. flights were canceled from Friday through Monday morning, according to FlightAware.

In a recent development, over that period, American Airlines had the most cancellations with 5,367 flights, close to 22% of its schedule, followed by Delta Air Lines with 3,434 cancellations, or 17% of its schedule, according to aviation data firm Cirium. Southwest Airlines and United Airlines each canceled more than 16% of their schedules. Bad weather on Thursday and Friday had also contributed to disruptions before the ground stop.

On Sunday, disruptions lingered but were starting to improve.

"We slept here," stated Beth Martin, who was trying to travel home to Birmingham, Alabama, from Latest York's LaGuardia Airport on Sunday at the end of her daughter's birthday trip. The two were booked for Saturday night, and when delay announcements started rolling in from Delta, they decided to make good use of the extra time: After strolling through Central Park, they went window shopping on Fifth Avenue.

Industry observers note that but when the flight was canceled, the standby list for a backup flight looked grim and their original midtown hotel was charging about $700 for an additional night, the two slept at LaGuardia on Saturday. She remarked they found wheelchairs to sleep in outside security.

Airlines waived change fees and fare differences and offered pilots and flight attendants extra pay to ease the delays.

Customers told CNBC they were booked on several flights before getting on board.

Justine Fritz, who works in sales for a consumer goods company, was in Upcoming York for a business event on Friday. She tried five different flights before getting on one to Houston, then flew four hours out of her way to get back to Minneapolis after her Saturday flight was canceled. Fritz said she had so many boarding passes that the Transportation Security Administration wouldn't let her through security at first.

According to recent reports, "It's been bananas," she told CNBC at the gate. Fritz mentioned she made the best of it by shopping and getting a haircut, but she's still disappointed.

"I'm missing the first hour of the Vikings game," she remarked.

Martin noted her trip home with her daughter will take even longer. Delta booked them to Memphis, and her husband will drive the four hours from Birmingham to pick them up.

Officials are investigating the root cause that led to what the FAA described as "instability" in data feeds. Duffy noted Sunday that two routers, the original one detected as a problem in southern Arkansas, and another in Amarillo, Texas, are in an L3Harris lab in Florida.

"We are supporting the FAA in its investigation to understand the conditions that led to the Nov. 13 ground stop of our nation's air travel," L3Harris mentioned in a statement Saturday afternoon.

President Donald Trump remarked Monday he didn't "take any responsibility" for the ground stop.

It has been revealed that shortly after the ground stop, Duffy stated he was asking Congress for as much as $50 billion for upgrades to the aging U.S. air traffic control system. A day later, Democratic lawmakers pushed back, citing unspent money from existing funding, which includes Congress' approval last year of $12.5 billion for airport and air traffic control improvements over five years.

In a recent development, "Our committee, on behalf of the flying public, will hold the administration accountable for what happened to make sure this chaos does not happen again," said Reps. Rick Larsen, D.-Wash., ranking member of the House Committee on Transportation and Infrastructure, and Andre Carson, D.-Ind., ranking member of the aviation subcommittee, in a joint statement.
