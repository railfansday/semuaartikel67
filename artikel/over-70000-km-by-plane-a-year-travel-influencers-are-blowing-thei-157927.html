Over 70,000 km by Plane a Year: Travel Influencers Are Blowing Their Carbon Budgets and encouraging Europeans to Do the Same

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that travel influencers have a disproportionate climate footprint, generating an average of 10.0t of CO₂e emissions each just from flights in 2025, according to a upcoming report by T3 Transportation Think Tank, commissioned by T&E. This is 16.7 times higher than the average European’s four leisure round trips and around 0.6 t CO₂e per tourist each year across all modes of transport for the 95% of their trips taking place in the EU. This does not yet include the travel emissions of the people they influenced.

The study tracks 25 primarily European and US travel influencers representing millions of followers, and examines their role in the advertising strategy of airlines. Influencers took an average of 16 flights per year, visiting four times as many destinations as a standard European.

Sources indicate that the study indicates that influencers predominantly take long-haul flights (49%) over medium (24%) and short-haul (27%) journeys. Long-haul flying is the most polluting form of transport: an economy long-haul flight generates roughly three to four times more emissions than a short-haul flight.

Recent findings suggest that in total, these influencers flew a total distance of 1.8 million kilometers in 2025, equivalent to approximately 45 trips around the globe. Individually, each influencer flew an average of 72,244 km, equivalent to 34 return holiday flights between Paris and Athens.

Travel influencers are frequent flyers, with a disproportionate climate footprint. They regularly post about luxury, comfort and aspirational destinations. Influencers play a significant role in shaping where people choose to travel, thereby driving demand for high polluting flying. Through travel influencers, frequent air travel is normalised and encouraged.

The study indicates that European and Middle Eastern airlines are ranked among the top 30 carriers that advertise on social media and websites. These include easyJet, Emirates and KLM. Even though airlines are not presently obliged to disclose their advertising budgets, the analysis reveals that social media, including influencer marketing, likely accounts for the largest marketing expenditure, around one third, with the UK seeing the highest spend (€50.2 million).

In a recent development, the list is dominated by airlines headquartered in the respective country and those that hold the most slots at the major international airports in each country. For example Air France spends more on social media advertisements in France (€2 million) compared to Spain (€29,000). Other carriers spending a lot on social media are easyJet and Aer Lingus, spending almost €26 million and around €7 million respectively. EasyJet spends more than twice on social media and online advertising than the following airlines.

“Airlines have found a ridiculously cheap way of promoting climate intensive behaviour. They pay influencers to make very frequent and polluting long distance flights look normal and desirable. The average of 72,244 km shouldn’t be something to brag about… that would take an average European nearly 23 years to match” said Aiza Rodrigues Akhtar, T&E Aviation Campaigner. “The EU must enforce disclosure of airline-funded influencer promotions of harmful high-emission flights and enforce accountability of both parties.”.

According to recent reports, for airlines, partnering with travel influencers is remarkably cost-effective: it can cost just a few thousand euros¹ or just a free seat upgrade, yet it leads to significant results. Compared to traditional channels such as TV broadcasts or billboard displays, social media ads have a higher return on investment: posts that are liked, shared or reposted remain in the digital space for longer, significantly boosting visibility.

In a recent development, yet, both influencers and airlines are not fully transparent when it comes to advertising. Influencers do not always clearly label their promotional content, and airlines do not publicly disclose how much they spend on digital advertising. Consumer organisations underline that any commercial promotion on social media should be clearly labelled so that consumers know what they are being marketed to and when. The EU Digital Fairness Act, at the moment under development, is key to tackling misleading influencer marketing and closing transparency loopholes. As travel influencers promote high-emission flights, any paid promotions must be clearly labelled as such.

Recent findings suggest that “Transparency is a first step, but it is not enough to prevent the damage caused by influencer ads for flying”, says Rémi ter Haar, legal campaigner at Fossil Free Advertising. “Calling for more truthful advertising in the aviation sector is like playing a game of ‘whack-a-mole’. We must get ahead in the game and ban their advertising, for the sake of the climate and public health. We cannot expect people to behave more sustainably, only to then manipulate them through advertising into doing exactly the opposite.”.

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) recognises that regulating advertising is key to changing the behaviours needed to limit global warming to 1.5°C. Marketing portrays luxury goods as necessities, and the IPCC cites long-distance holiday ads as an example of how the industry creates and normalises carbon-intensive desires. While the EU Audiovisual Media Services Directive prohibits promotions that encourage behavior harmful to the environment, this rule is often interpreted too narrowly. Airline advertising, which promotes carbon-intensive behaviour, should be included in its definition.
