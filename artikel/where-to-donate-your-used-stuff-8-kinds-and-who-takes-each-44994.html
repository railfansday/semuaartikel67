Sources indicate that sort your used stuff by kind before you donate it, then give each kind to a group that puts it to use, like a furniture bank for a dresser or a computer refurbisher for an old laptop. Call each place first to confirm what it takes, and get a receipt for every drop-off in case you itemize.

Industry observers note that donated goods lower your taxes only if you itemize your deductions. The deduction for people who don’t itemize, which starts with the 2026 tax year, covers only cash gifts, and the IRS says clothing and other property don’t qualify. Under IRS Publication 526, an itemizer deducts most donated items at their fair market value, what a buyer would pay for them used, while a car’s deduction depends on what the charity does with it. Clothing and household items also need to be in good used condition or better, unless you deduct more than $500 for one item and attach an appraisal from a qualified appraiser. Starting in 2026, itemizers can deduct only the part of the year’s charitable giving above 0.50% of adjusted gross income, the income a return highlights before the standard or itemized deduction, according to IRS Publication 505. For an itemizer with an adjusted gross income of $80,000, the first $400 of the year’s gifts earns no deduction.

Sources indicate that to claim the deduction, get a receipt from the charity for each drop-off, or keep your own written record of what you left at an unattended bin. Once the year’s noncash deductions total more than $500, attach Form 8283 to your return. Before you give away anything worth real money, compare what it would sell for with what the deduction would save you. Each move below names who takes one kind of used stuff and what to check before it leaves the house.

Whatever has no taker of its own on this list belongs with this move, from kitchenware and bedding to small appliances that still work.

Call the charity or check its website before you drop off used stuff, since it has to pay to throw out anything it can’t sell or use. Bring only clean, working, complete items, and get a dated receipt that lists each one, which backs up a tax deduction if you itemize.

Emptying a closet, your own or a late relative’s, turns up everyday wear and dressier pieces alike, and they don’t all belong in the same bag.

Industry observers note that give used clothes to the group that needs what you have: work clothes to Dress for Success, coats to a One Warm Coat drive, shoes to Soles4Souls, and everyday wear to Goodwill, the Salvation Army, or a local shelter. Check what each one takes before you go, and get a receipt if you’ll deduct the gift.

A sofa or a dresser takes two people to move, so line up its taker well before moving day or the day a dumpster arrives.

Match each piece of used furniture to a place that will take it, since rules on size, condition, and mattresses vary. Many Salvation Army and Habitat for Humanity ReStore locations pick up sofas and dressers for free, and veterans’ groups collect smaller pieces left outside your door. Get a receipt if you’ll deduct the gift.

Books in good shape can be read for years after they leave your shelves, so they’re worth giving to readers rather than recycling.

Before you donate used books, sort them by who will read them: picture books for a classroom, novels for your library’s used-book sale, and paperbacks for a prison book program. Then check what each place accepts, because many turn away encyclopedia sets, old textbooks, and water-damaged copies.

For families whose children have outgrown a playroom’s worth of toys, this move keeps the good ones in use instead of in a closet or a landfill.

Sources indicate that take your used toys where they’ll be resold, handed to a child, or played with, such as thrift stores, foster care programs, child care centers, and neighborhood giveaway groups. Check that each is clean, complete, and not recalled, and skip children’s hospitals and holiday toy drives, which usually take only fresh ones.

Old laptops, phones, and tablets can still hold your photos, messages, and saved passwords, so they take more care to give away than a box of dishes does.

Donate a working computer, phone, or tablet to a group that refurbishes it for someone who needs one, but clear it first by backing it up, signing out of your accounts, and resetting it to factory settings. Choose a group that takes that kind of device, and get a receipt if you’ll deduct the gift.

Clearing out after an illness or a death often turns up a wheelchair, a walker, or a hospital bed, things a general thrift store may turn away.

Give a used wheelchair, walker, or shower chair to a program that cleans it and passes it to someone who needs it, such as your state’s assistive technology program or a local loan closet. Return anything Medicare or another insurer still rents to its supplier, oxygen equipment included, since it isn’t yours to give.

This move fits a car that still runs but that you’d rather not sell yourself, whether it’s a spare nobody drives or one a relative left behind.

According to recent reports, if you itemize and a charity sells your donated car for more than $500, you can deduct no more than the sale price. Give the car to a charity that will use it or hand it to a family in need, and you can deduct up to what a private buyer would pay.
