The agreement will also improve Danish and Greenlandic security, increase American military contributions to Arctic security, strengthen Greenland’s role in the transatlantic security architecture, and ensure that there is no risk of adversarial malign influence even if Greenland’s political status changes. The agreement acknowledges and respects the sovereignty and territorial integrity of the Kingdom of Denmark and the Greenlandic people’s right to self-determination.[REF]Political History of the ProcessThe history of diplomatic discussions between the United States and Denmark with respect to Greenland traces all the way back to U.S. Secretary of State William Seward, who purchased Alaska from the Russian Empire in 1867 and explored a similar purchase of Greenland.[REF] Later, the U.S. purchased what became the U.S. Virgin Islands from Denmark in the 1916 Convention between the United States and Denmark for the Cession of the Danish West Indies and proposed at roughly the same time to acquire Greenland as well. Denmark declined to include Greenland in the sale, and in a declaration signed with the convention, the U.S. stated that it would “not object to the Danish Government extending their political and economic interests to the whole of Greenland.”[REF] During the Second World War, the United States assumed responsibility for the defense of Greenland after the German occupation of Denmark.[REF]Following the war, the U.S. again expressed interest in Greenland, and negotiations between Washington and Copenhagen over Greenland’s future security architecture culminated with the 1951 Defense Agreement.[REF] This deal enabled the U.S. to build and operate bases in Greenland.[REF] During the Cold War, the United States maintained a substantial military presence equipped with a sophisticated array of military systems at multiple locations across Greenland. After the Cold War ended, as the United States and Europe de-emphasized great-power competition and defense budgets fell across NATO, the United States closed all but one installation in Greenland, leaving only Pituffik Space Base (originally Thule Air Base). Several amending protocols to the initial 1951 Defense Agreement have been signed since then, but the most consequential prior to 2026 was the 2004 supplemental agreement, which officially included the Greenlandic government as a party to the accord.[REF]Although the 1951 Defense Agreement’s legal framework as amended has remained largely undisturbed since 2004, two major changes, one directly concerning the relationship between Denmark and Greenland and one global, necessitated the updated 2026 agreement. The first was the June 12, 2009, Act on Greenland Self-Government, under which the decision for or against independence is acknowledged to be the right of the people of Greenland; an independence agreement with Denmark then requires the consent of Greenland’s parliament, a referendum in Greenland, and the consent of the Danish parliament.[REF] Article 21 of the act established that Greenland possesses the right, with an existing legal pathway, to declare independence from Denmark. The Preamble to the new agreement signed on September 22, 2026, succinctly captures the need to address this shift, “[n]oting that the procedure for Greenlandic independence is set out in Article 21 of Act no. 473 of 12 June 2009 on Greenland Self-Government.”[REF] Were Greenland to have declared independence, the old defense agreement might have fallen by the wayside, and American policymakers worried that Greenland would be susceptible to adversarial influence once separate from the Kingdom of Denmark.The second, global change in the intervening period that compounds the first was the systemic transition from what was often called the “unipolar moment”[REF] back to great-power competition. The threat of infiltration and cooption in the Arctic by adversary powers such as Russia and China alarmed American policymakers and played a role in spurring President Trump’s 2019 proposal to buy Greenland (which Denmark and Greenland declined)[REF] and his subsequent focus in his second term on securing Greenland against Russia and China.[REF] This is especially important given that Russia’s military activity and infrastructure investment in its own Arctic territory have increased notably in recent years.[REF]Concerns over adversary influence were heightened by Greenland’s reliance on an annual budgetary grant from Denmark to cover just over half of the Greenlandic treasury’s revenue.[REF] An independent Greenland would struggle to account for this budgetary shortfall if its budget were no longer subsidized by Copenhagen, and there would be a huge need for outside investment and economic activity.Elsewhere in the world, China has displayed an opportunistic approach to disbursing and leveraging capital in similar instances, providing debt-trap loans and then seizing infrastructure as payment when the country accepting the Chinese loan is unable to pay.[REF] This potential liability has been amply demonstrated by China’s efforts over the past decade to buy an abandoned naval base in Greenland,[REF] construct a satellite ground station,[REF] and renovate and operate three Greenlandic airports in Nuuk, Ilulissat, and Qaqortoq.[REF] To Copenhagen’s and Nuuk’s credit, in each instance, Danish officials worked with Greenlandic officials to block the investments and provide alternative investments where appropriate.President Trump brought the American concern over these liabilities to the public’s attention in March 2025 when he stated in an address to Congress that “[w]e need Greenland for national security and even international security. And we’re working with everybody involved to try and get it. But we need it really for international world security. And I think we’re going to get it. One way or the other, we’re going to get it.”[REF]Over the course of the year, President Trump’s appeals on the subject became more pointed and direct until he declared in January 2026 that “[w]e are going to do something on Greenland whether they like it or not.”[REF] This prompted a January 14, 2026, meeting in Washington at which Vice President JD Vance and Secretary of State Marco Rubio met the Danish and Greenlandic foreign ministers and they agreed to establish a high-level trilateral working group to find a compromise solution.[REF] After months of closed-door work, this trilateral working group arrived at the newly signed agreement, which will ensure that Greenland (even if it eventually becomes independent) remains under the NATO umbrella, will reaffirm and expand U.S. forces’ access, and will review and protect critical sectors from adversaries’ investments and control.Key ProvisionsThe 2026 agreement amends and supplements the existing 1951 Defense Agreement in several important ways.Expanded U.S. Military Presence and Access. The agreement expands the U.S. permanent military presence in Greenland through the establishment of an additional Defense Area at two sites—Narsarsuaq and Mestersvig—bringing the total number of U.S. sites in Greenland to three. Narsarsuaq in southern Greenland is the former site of a Cold War–era air base, and Mestersvig on Greenland’s east coast currently hosts a Danish special forces unit.[REF] The United States will also be allowed to modernize and expand its activities at Pituffik Space Base. The United States committed to awarding construction, maintenance, and servicing contracts to local Greenlandic companies to the maximum extent possible.The agreement also augments existing free movement rights for U.S. forces and explicitly grants U.S. public vessels undersea access within Greenland’s territorial waters “without restriction except as mutually agreed upon by the Parties.”[REF]Establishment of Military Installations by Non-NATO States. The updated agreement adds a prohibition of “manned or unmanned military installations by third states” unless otherwise agreed, intended to exclude Russia and China from ever establishing a presence in Greenland.[REF]Strategic Investment. The recent agreement’s most novel provision, Article X, concerns foreign direct investment in strategic projects (“Particularly Sensitive Sectors or Activities” in the agreement).[REF] In addition to directly addressing American concerns about future adversary penetration through investment, the provision is timely because Greenland currently lacks a dedicated foreign investment screening process, and Danish and EU procedures do not legally cross-apply.[REF] The agreement specifies that:[S]tates or investors from a state that is not a member of NATO, a NATO partner, or an EU member state shall not be allowed to have (i) control, (ii) significant influence, or (iii) access to non-public information that may constitute a threat to national security or public order within Particularly Sensitive Sectors or Activities in the territory of Greenland (including the Territorial Waters), unless agreed between the Parties that the activities of such states or investors would not constitute a threat to national security or public order.[REF]“Particularly Sensitive Sectors or Activities” is defined for purposes of the agreement as “including but not limited to critical infrastructure and the extraction of resources,”[REF] and the implicated provision explicitly includes Greenland’s territorial waters within the area of consideration.[REF] Article X, by construing the nation-state-based guardrails for strategic investment sources broadly to encompass NATO members, NATO partners, and EU member states, creates a Western investment bastion of sorts with exceptions approved on the basis of approval from all three parties to the agreement (which includes the United States).[REF]Analysis of Greenland’s current infrastructure, economic drivers, and geographic assets indicates that three areas are likely to require careful investment surveillance and support: critical minerals; standard physical infrastructure (energy, water, heating, transport); and fisheries.Greenland possesses substantial geological reserves of rare earth elements with 1.5 million tons of proven reserves and an estimated 36 million tons of total resources, putting it among the world’s top 10 countries in terms of geological assets. Greenland also possesses substantial uranium deposits as well as many other non–rare earth minerals with wide commercial applications.[REF] However, two principal difficulties—ice and regulation—will slow the pace of investment and development. The Greenland Ice Sheet extends across 656,000 square miles (roughly 80 percent of the country) and reaches thicknesses of more than 1.9 miles, making any significant extraction operation extremely complicated.[REF] With respect to regulation, Greenland banned uranium mining in 2021 and banned exploration of mineral deposits with even low levels (100 parts per million) of uranium, which caused a halt to a major rare earth mine because of colocation of minerals.[REF] If these difficulties are overcome, Western investment in Greenland could bring major secure upstream production of rare earth elements online.The combination of a small population and a harsh and geographically isolating environment has caused the majority of Greenlandic communities to function independently with nearly no road networks apart from a few that link sheep farms in southern Greenland. Because of the difficulties involved in providing such essentials as power, water, and heating, these needs are serviced by a combination of national and local government entities.[REF] Therefore, any investment by non-allied nations in these utilities should be subject to Article X’s provisions because communities could effectively be held hostage if provision of these services were to be interrupted or maintenance were to be delayed.Finally, fisheries deserve attention with respect to critical investments in Greenland not only because they represent the primary national industry, but also because of the vital economic lifeline they provide for certain settlement economies.[REF] In addition, trends in ice distribution appear likely to herald an increase in fishing grounds off West Greenland that could spur economic growth in this sector.[REF] These economic opportunities could cement their outsized importance to the Greenlandic economy as consumer demand for seafood is expected to rise for decades;[REF] therefore, these benefits should be retained for local producers and fair partners.It should be noted that Denmark has already proven itself to be highly cooperative in this regard, having blocked the aforementioned attempt by a Chinese company to purchase the abandoned Grønnedal naval base as well as another attempt by China to build new airports in Greenland.[REF]Permanence. One of the most important features of the updated 2026 agreement is that it addresses concern arising from the potential impact of Greenlandic independence by establishing that the agreement allowing the U.S. military presence, prohibiting non-NATO military bases, and establishing a mechanism for excluding malign investment by non-NATO governments will endure even if Greenland becomes independent. Specifically, the agreement provides that:In case Greenland exercises its right to self-determination to become independent, the Government of the Kingdom of Denmark and the Government of Greenland shall together ensure that the independent Greenlandic state will (i) agree to remain in NATO, including by applying to become a member if necessary, and (ii) affirmatively assume, as from the date of independence, all of the rights and obligations of the Kingdom of Denmark specified in this Agreement, including any implementation agreements or arrangements between the Parties, as well as the Defense Arrangements.[REF]ConclusionThe American, Danish, and Greenlandic peoples have a shared interest in a secure and sovereign Arctic where the shortest approaches to North America from Russia and China are secured and the rights of all Arctic states are respected. The September 2026 agreement protects the sovereign security and economic interests of Washington, Copenhagen, and Nuuk and helps to ensure transatlantic security cooperation through NATO in the Arctic for decades to come.[REF] This major diplomatic achievement expands U.S. defense areas in Greenland, prohibits non-NATO military presence, protects critical sectors of the Greenlandic economy that otherwise could have been exploited for dual-use purposes by adversarial governments, and permanently cements Greenland’s status and role in transatlantic security.Wilson Beaver is Senior Policy Advisor for Defense Budgeting and NATO Policy in the Douglas and Sarah Allison Center for National Security at The Heritage Foundation. President Donald Trump’s successful conclusion of the Greenland deal with the governments of Denmark and Greenland is a major achievement that will have positive security outcomes for the United States far into the foreseeable future. The agreement will also improve Danish and Greenlandic security, increase American military contributions to Arctic security, strengthen Greenland’s role in the transatlantic security architecture, and ensure that there is no risk of adversarial malign influence even if Greenland’s political status changes. From both the American and European perspectives, the agreement cements and expands the U.S. role in Arctic security, primarily through the North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO), and signals sustained American interest in transatlantic cooperation on Arctic security for many years to come.

The latest agreement’s provisions would continue to apply even if Greenland were to become independent.

The Preamble to the new agreement signed on September 22, 2026, succinctly captures the need to address this shift, “[n]oting that the procedure for Greenlandic independence is set out in Article 21 of Act no. 473 of 12 June 2009 on Greenland Self-Government.”[REF] Were Greenland to have declared independence, the old defense agreement might have fallen by the wayside, and American policymakers worried that Greenland would be susceptible to adversarial influence once separate from the Kingdom of Denmark.

Recent findings suggest that the second, global change in the intervening period that compounds the first was the systemic transition from what was often called the “unipolar moment”[REF] back to great-power competition. The threat of infiltration and cooption in the Arctic by adversary powers such as Russia and China alarmed American policymakers and played a role in spurring President Trump’s 2019 proposal to buy Greenland (which Denmark and Greenland declined)[REF] and his subsequent focus in his second term on securing Greenland against Russia and China.[REF] This is especially crucial given that Russia’s military activity and infrastructure investment in its own Arctic territory have increased notably in recent years.[REF].

Concerns over adversary influence were heightened by Greenland’s reliance on an annual budgetary grant from Denmark to cover just over half of the Greenlandic treasury’s revenue.[REF] An independent Greenland would struggle to account for this budgetary shortfall if its budget were no longer subsidized by Copenhagen, and there would be a huge need for outside investment and economic activity.

Elsewhere in the world, China has displayed an opportunistic approach to disbursing and leveraging capital in similar instances, providing debt-trap loans and then seizing infrastructure as payment when the country accepting the Chinese loan is unable to pay.[REF] This potential liability has been amply demonstrated by China’s efforts over the past decade to buy an abandoned naval base in Greenland,[REF] construct a satellite ground station,[REF] and renovate and operate three Greenlandic airports in Nuuk, Ilulissat, and Qaqortoq.[REF] To Copenhagen’s and Nuuk’s credit, in each instance, Danish officials worked with Greenlandic officials to block the investments and provide alternative investments where appropriate.

President Trump brought the American concern over these liabilities to the public’s attention in March 2025 when he stated in an address to Congress that “[w]e need Greenland for national security and even international security. And we’re working with everybody involved to try and get it. But we need it really for international world security. And I think we’re going to get it. One way or the other, we’re going to get it.”[REF].

Over the course of the year, President Trump’s appeals on the subject became more pointed and direct until he declared in January 2026 that “[w]e are going to do something on Greenland whether they like it or not.”[REF] This prompted a January 14, 2026, meeting in Washington at which Vice President JD Vance and Secretary of State Marco Rubio met the Danish and Greenlandic foreign ministers and they agreed to establish a high-level trilateral working group to find a compromise solution.[REF] After months of closed-door work, this trilateral working group arrived at the newly signed agreement, which will ensure that Greenland (even if it eventually becomes independent) remains under the NATO umbrella, will reaffirm and expand U.S. forces’ access, and will review and protect critical sectors from adversaries’ investments and control.

The 2026 agreement amends and supplements the existing 1951 Defense Agreement in several crucial ways.

It has been revealed that expanded U.S. Military Presence and Access. The agreement expands the U.S. permanent military presence in Greenland through the establishment of an additional Defense Area at two sites—Narsarsuaq and Mestersvig—bringing the total number of U.S. sites in Greenland to three. Narsarsuaq in southern Greenland is the former site of a Cold War–era air base, and Mestersvig on Greenland’s east coast at the moment hosts a Danish special forces unit.[REF] The United States will also be allowed to modernize and expand its activities at Pituffik Space Base. The United States committed to awarding construction, maintenance, and servicing contracts to local Greenlandic companies to the maximum extent possible.

The agreement also augments existing free movement rights for U.S. forces and explicitly grants U.S. public vessels undersea access within Greenland’s territorial waters “without restriction except as mutually agreed upon by the Parties.”[REF].

It has been revealed that establishment of Military Installations by Non-NATO States. The updated agreement adds a prohibition of “manned or unmanned military installations by third states” unless otherwise agreed, intended to exclude Russia and China from ever establishing a presence in Greenland.[REF].

Strategic Investment. The recent agreement’s most novel provision, Article X, concerns foreign direct investment in strategic projects (“Particularly Sensitive Sectors or Activities” in the agreement).[REF] In addition to directly addressing American concerns about future adversary penetration through investment, the provision is timely because Greenland at the moment lacks a dedicated foreign investment screening process, and Danish and EU procedures do not legally cross-apply.[REF] The agreement specifies that:.

[S]tates or investors from a state that is not a member of NATO, a NATO partner, or an EU member state shall not be allowed to have (i) control, (ii) significant influence, or (iii) access to non-public information that may constitute a threat to national security or public order within Particularly Sensitive Sectors or Activities in the territory of Greenland (including the Territorial Waters), unless agreed between the Parties that the activities of such states or investors would not constitute a threat to national security or public order.[REF].

“Particularly Sensitive Sectors or Activities” is defined for purposes of the agreement as “including but not limited to critical infrastructure and the extraction of resources,”[REF] and the implicated provision explicitly includes Greenland’s territorial waters within the area of consideration.[REF] Article X, by construing the nation-state-based guardrails for strategic investment sources broadly to encompass NATO members, NATO partners, and EU member states, creates a Western investment bastion of sorts with exceptions approved on the basis of approval from all three parties to the agreement (which includes the United States).[REF].

Analysis of Greenland’s current infrastructure, economic drivers, and geographic assets indicates that three areas are likely to require careful investment surveillance and support: critical minerals; standard physical infrastructure (energy, water, heating, transport); and fisheries.

Greenland possesses substantial geological reserves of rare earth elements with 1.5 million tons of proven reserves and an estimated 36 million tons of total resources, putting it among the world’s top 10 countries in terms of geological assets. Greenland also possesses substantial uranium deposits as well as many other non–rare earth minerals with wide commercial applications.[REF] However, two principal difficulties—ice and regulation—will slow the pace of investment and development. The Greenland Ice Sheet extends across 656,000 square miles (roughly 80 percent of the country) and reaches thicknesses of more than 1.9 miles, making any significant extraction operation extremely complicated.[REF] With respect to regulation, Greenland banned uranium mining in 2021 and banned exploration of mineral deposits with even low levels (100 parts per million) of uranium, which caused a halt to a major rare earth mine because of colocation of minerals.[REF] If these difficulties are overcome, Western investment in Greenland could bring major secure upstream production of rare earth elements online.

The combination of a small population and a harsh and geographically isolating environment has caused the majority of Greenlandic communities to function independently with nearly no road networks apart from a few that link sheep farms in southern Greenland. Because of the difficulties involved in providing such essentials as power, water, and heating, these needs are serviced by a combination of national and local government entities.[REF] Therefore, any investment by non-allied nations in these utilities should be subject to Article X’s provisions because communities could effectively be held hostage if provision of these services were to be interrupted or maintenance were to be delayed.

Finally, fisheries deserve attention with respect to critical investments in Greenland not only because they represent the primary national industry, but also because of the vital economic lifeline they provide for certain settlement economies.[REF] In addition, trends in ice distribution appear likely to herald an increase in fishing grounds off West Greenland that could spur economic growth in this sector.[REF] These economic opportunities could cement their outsized importance to the Greenlandic economy as consumer demand for seafood is expected to rise for decades;[REF] therefore, these benefits should be retained for local producers and fair partners.

It should be noted that Denmark has already proven itself to be highly cooperative in this regard, having blocked the aforementioned attempt by a Chinese tech giant to purchase the abandoned Grønnedal naval base as well as another attempt by China to build fresh airports in Greenland.[REF].

Sources indicate that permanence. One of the most crucial features of the updated 2026 agreement is that it addresses concern arising from the potential impact of Greenlandic independence by establishing that the agreement allowing the U.S. military presence, prohibiting non-NATO military bases, and establishing a mechanism for excluding malign investment by non-NATO governments will endure even if Greenland becomes independent. Specifically, the agreement provides that:.

In case Greenland exercises its right to self-determination to become independent, the Government of the Kingdom of Denmark and the Government of Greenland shall together ensure that the independent Greenlandic state will (i) agree to remain in NATO, including by applying to become a member if necessary, and (ii) affirmatively assume, as from the date of independence, all of the rights and obligations of the Kingdom of Denmark specified in this Agreement, including any implementation agreements or arrangements between the Parties, as well as the Defense Arrangements.[REF].

In a recent development, the American, Danish, and Greenlandic peoples have a shared interest in a secure and sovereign Arctic where the shortest approaches to North America from Russia and China are secured and the rights of all Arctic states are respected. The September 2026 agreement protects the sovereign security and economic interests of Washington, Copenhagen, and Nuuk and helps to ensure transatlantic security cooperation through NATO in the Arctic for decades to come.[REF] This major diplomatic achievement expands U.S. defense areas in Greenland, prohibits non-NATO military presence, protects critical sectors of the Greenlandic economy that otherwise could have been exploited for dual-use purposes by adversarial governments, and permanently cements Greenland’s status and role in transatlantic security.
