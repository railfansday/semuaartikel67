Sources indicate that AI factories are outgrowing the traditional data center as models get bigger and distributed inference moves from concept to deployment. The infrastructure behind those models now has to span racks, sites and even scattered pockets of power.

Dell Technologies Inc. is betting that the answer lies in building complete systems rather than components, from rack-scale management to fully liquid-cooled designs. The size of modern AI clusters dwarfs anything high-performance computing has handled before, according to Ihab Tarazi (pictured), senior vice president and chief technology officer for AI, compute and networking of Dell.

Sources indicate that “The difference with AI is the scale is massive. Tens of thousands, hundreds of thousands of GPUs that are all on a single fabric. It may be multiple clusters, but they end up being a single fabric at the end of the day,” Tarazi mentioned.

Industry observers note that tarazi spoke with theCUBE Research’s John Furrier and Rebecca Knight at SC26, during an exclusive broadcast on theCUBE, SiliconANGLE Media’s livestreaming studio. They discussed Dell’s 100% liquid-cooled networking system, distributed inference and turning stranded energy into AI capacity. (* Disclosure below.).

Dell’s latest move takes liquid cooling beyond the GPU. The manufacturer is shipping what it calls the first 100% liquid-cooled networking system, with switches, optics and cabling pretested in its factory and delivered as a single rack, Tarazi explained.

“The fact that it’s pretested as a single-rack system means, instead of having to assemble hundreds of pieces on-site and take maybe two weeks or three weeks to find the bugs, it all comes in, you install it [and] we turn it up in hours,” Tarazi noted.

Recent findings suggest that the networking launch follows Dell’s debut of liquid-cooled storage for AI racks. Compute, networking and storage now share the same cooling and power specs. That matters as operators design fresh facilities around water cooling, according to Tarazi.

“Instead of building two data centers — one for GPU, one network — being able to do one is a massive reduction in time and cost for us,” Tarazi said.

Sources indicate that software is the next lever for distributed inference. Customers want a single control plane that can work across different GPUs and CPUs and link as many as 1,000 small sites that each have some spare power, Tarazi noted. That approach turns stranded energy into AI capacity for companies that can’t build a massive data center rapidly.

“You’ll be surprised what power availability means,” Tarazi mentioned. “It doesn’t mean just that you have power in your store. It could also mean methane coming out — every source of power possible. People are going backwards to say, ‘How do I leverage that power for compute and enable inferencing in a distributed manner?'”.
