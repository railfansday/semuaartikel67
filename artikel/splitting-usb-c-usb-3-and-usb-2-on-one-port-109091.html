Splitting USB-C: USB 3 and USB 2 on one port

I started building restorekit.org dongles a few months ago at the Recurse Center. They are small inline USB-C boards that sit between a host computer and a target mac. They set the target mac into DFUDevice Firmware Patch: Apple's lowest-level recovery mode built into the boot ROM, so even a bricked Mac can be reflashed. mode over USB-PDUSB Power Delivery: the communication protocol over the CC wire used to negotiate power, roles, and vendor-defined commands. without having to touch the target at all, and work with an open-source toolkit for restoring macs without the need for another mac.

Recent findings suggest that the original board, the Dongle-Lite, is USB 2 only. A single USB 2 hub (CH334F) connects both the dongle’s MCU and the target mac through a single cable, while a PD PHYPhysical-layer transceiver chip that encodes and decodes USB Power Delivery packets on the CC wire. (FUSB302B) sends Apple’s DFU commands on the CC line.

I recently learned from timo at Twocanoes that newer Macs support USB 3 speeds for recovery. I obviously immediately wanted to support this on the hub for faster recovery, but the problem was cost. At first I designed a “proper” USB 3 board using a GL3510 USB 3 hub and a pair of TI SuperSpeed muxes (HD3SS3220 and HD3SS3212). It basically doubled the parts count, and BOMBill of Materials: the full list of components and quantities required to assemble one board. cost, required me to increase the size of PCB to ~100 mm, and topped out at 5 Gbps transfers between host and target (USB 3.1 Gen 1).

I really didn’t want to make a board that cost more than twice the Dongle-Lite just to get USB 3 speeds.

In a recent development, so with the 2nd version Dongle-Pro, I tried a kinda crazy USB-C experiment: drop the USB 3 hub completely, keep a cheap USB 2 hub on D+/D-, and route the USB 3 SuperSpeedUSB 3.x's dedicated high-speed TX/RX differential pairs, physically separate from USB 2's D+/D- wires. lanes as a straight passthrough from host to target on the same USB-C port. The idea being that in theory the USB 3 and 2 controllers on a USB-C are completely independent, and should work simultaneously.

Since high-speed USB 3 is super prone to interference I didn’t really have a choice other than to purchase a view boards and test it out.

In a recent development, well… I just got the boards in from JLC and… THEY WORK! They establish a full 10 Gbps USB 3.2 Gen 2 connection while connecting the dongle’s USB 2 hub and MCU over the exact same port. After some aggressive cost optimization, the 10 Gbps board also ended up cheaper and smaller than the USB 2 Dongle-Lite.

Like I mentioned earlier, USB-C is not a single data bus. Inside a standard USB-C port, USB 2 and USB 3 sit on completely different sets of pins:.

Every USB-C port has two mirrored 12-pin rows: A1 through A12 on top, and B12 through B1 on the bottom, so a cable works rotated either way.

Sources indicate that this continues to the computer’s xHCIExtensible Host Controller Interface: the USB controller inside your computer that manages both USB 2 and USB 3.x root ports. controller: a single physical USB-C port is actually wired to two independent root ports: a USB 2 port and a USB 3 port.

Industry observers note that A standard USB 3 hub chip like the GL3510 is actually just two hubs in 1 package (a USB 2 hub and a USB 3 hub). On my first iteration of the Dongle-Pro, everything ran through that chip even though the dongle’s own MCU only speaks USB 2 Full Speed (12 Mbps) and has no need for the USB 3 speed.

On the Dongle-Pro v2, the two buses are split apart:.

When the boards arrived, I immediately plugged one into my mac, plugged an iPhone into the target port, and inspected the bus with system_profiler SPUSBHostDataType and ioreg -p IOUSB.

The raw system_profiler SPUSBHostDataType output indicates both sides active at once:.

Recent findings suggest that 0x01100000 is the USB 2 half of the physical port, and 0x01200000 is the SuperSpeed half of the same port. The iPhone enumerated straight through both passive CH9441P muxes at 10 Gb/s (USB 3.2 Gen 2), while the CH334P hub and Dongle-Pro-v2 MCU enumerate at 480 Mb/s and 12 Mb/s on D+/D-.

Splitting the bus required one interesting change in the host SDK (restorekit).

On the Dongle-Lite, whatever device you plugged into the dongle always indicated up as a child of the dongle’s hub. That made it easy to tell which target Mac belonged to which dongle when you plugged in several at once.

On the Dongle-Pro v2, that breaks whenever the target connects at USB 3. Just like iPhone@01200000 above, a SuperSpeed target bypasses the CH334P hub completely and shows up as a sibling directly on the host’s root hub.

Turns out the OS already tracks which USB 2 and USB 3 root ports share the same physical port and it exposes it directly:.

So restorekit can still know with 100% certainty which 10 Gbps device is plugged into which dongle.

I originally planned to have a Dongle-Lite and significantly more expensive USB 3 Pro, but that means having to maintain two hardware lines, so if I could cut the cost of the USB 3 version to be comparably priced to the original Dongle-Lite it felt like a no brainer.

According to recent reports, if you are building an inline USB-C tool where your MCU only needs a slow control channel and the host just needs fast passthrough to the downstream device, you might not need a USB 3 hub. USB-C gives you two independent buses in the same port.
