What Is Internet Cinema?

According to recent reports, during last June’s heat wave, I found myself standing outside an unassuming office building in Crown Heights, Brooklyn, baking in the sun’s glare. The Gallery, a nomadic curatorial platform, had set up an exhibition, titled “WeWork (oralmoral)”, in one of the building’s vacant office spaces. In celebration of the show’s closing the curators were screening films, some of which fall into the category of what is being called “internet cinema,” a burgeoning movement, or method, that has been in the making since late 2020. Once inside, I wandered around the cool, unlit space, laughing at funny paintings and sculptures until it was time to find a spot on the floor for the screening. Someone in front of me was hastily scrawling the lineup of the screenings on paper with a black marker and passing them around as fast as he produced them. One passed back to me. The first film on the list was Zarina Nares’ I am going to die (this is my sign) (2025). The projector was fiddled with and a rectangle of white with a buffer symbol appeared on the blank wall in front of the seated crowd.

A chorus of female voices rose up, all in some general distress, and while I couldn’t see these women, by the tone, I could tell they were speaking into a front-facing camera. The chorus cut out to a single voice, which spoke over dashcam footage of a car crash: “You’re having problems at work, your boyfriend broke up with you, your parents are mad at you, everything is going wrong, your car broke down – that is a good sign. Like, that is the very best sign.”What followed was a montage of TikTok videos featuring motivational advice. One video bled into another, linked by the formulaic hooks used by podcast bros, emotional car seat influencers, clips of celebrities’ acceptance speeches, athletes in interviews, or passionate pastors, all of which was set to music. One after another these TikToks reveal ‘the one thing that you need to learn’ in a high-speed carousel of intense emotion that feels increasingly forced as it becomes clear how many of these impassioned speeches are copies of one another.

“Internet cinema isn’t necessarily about the internet or its aesthetic but about its logic, how it functions and how it makes us feel,” Zarina Nares mentioned in a phone call. On the process of making I am going to die, she added that “Clearly, I watched enough advice videos for too long that I kept getting more. Once you watch so many you see the formula behind them, whether it’s a certain cadence of speaking or certain music.”.

Internet cinema was first defined in a 2007 paper by Chuck Tryon titled “New Media Studies and the New Internet Cinema.” The technologies and formats that concerned Tryon —iPods and camera phones, software like Final Cut Pro, and home movies—are far dated, but his paper was prescient, having tapped into impulses and concerns that reflect contemporary internet cinema: a do-it-yourself ethos that blurs the line between amateur and artist; the popularization of “niche exhibition;” and a “self-reflexive style” that works “to call attention to the narrative codes and technological specs that shape cultural productions.”.

Contemporary internet cinema has its roots in the COVID-era, which saw an explosion of participation in internet culture and production, that often bled out into one particular place: Dimes Square, a patch of Manhattan where Chinatown and the Lower East Side meet.

Industry observers note that one of the most memorable dramas that played out at the time was puppeteered by a then-anonymous internet figure who went by the name Angelicism01. This figure posted creeds about extinction on Substack, and gained infamy for growing a cult-like following among young women in Fresh York (who had never met him). With the help of various film makers, he made a three-hour long movie called Film01 which includes fast paced montages of memes, tweets, videos of natural disasters and terrorist attacks, alongside long scenes featuring the ‘it-girls’ he had recruited. Writer Madeline Cash described Film01 in the art publication Spike as “an unsophisticated collage of localized content that mimics, rather than intervenes or questions, our digital conditions,” which ultimately did nothing more than tap “into the scene’s libidinal narcissism.”Yet, with some distance, Film01 has proven to be a productive experiment. One of Angelicism01’s collaborators, the filmmaker Dana Dawud, found internet footage to splice into the film, created short edits that became a part of the larger film, and remotely directed certain sequences. Dawud would go on to launch Open Secret, an international screening series of internet cinema that has since played a large part in consolidating disconnected artists and practices under the term.At the same time that young women were running around Dimes Square at Angelicism01’s request, people were locked away at home with TikTok. More than ever, people around the world were subjecting themselves to hours and hours of scrolling on this relatively new video content platform.“When you’re doom scrolling, there is, technically speaking, no specific author to this montage that you’re watching. So, it’s as if you’re exposed to radical experimental cinema all the time by a completely cynical industrial apparatus,” noted 0nty, the co-author of “Dialogues on CoreCore & the Contemporary Online Avant-Garde” during a Zoom interview also attended by his co-author Mischa Dols. “The question is, how do you even begin to think about this problem? And I think internet cinema is an answer,” 0nty said.0nty and Dols describe internet cinema as a method, rather than a genre or an aesthetic, which seeks to expose or comment on the way that the internet functions through editing found footage.It’s worth noting that some works under the umbrella of internet cinema do feature the production of new images. Dahlia Bloomstone’s 20,000 Tokens (2025) is a screen recording of the artist chatting with men and exposing herself on the webcam site Chaturbate (the video is spliced with a glitching 3D-rendering of a blonde avatar in a black void). For The Sight is a Wound (2024), Parham Ghalamdar filmed himself burning his own paintings, as a statement on how tthe seemingly endless documentation of the genocide of Palestinians in Gaza has made images useless. Yet the particular process of editing found footage is usually highlighted in definitions of internet cinema because this method represents the folk practice that preceded internet cinema: corecore.

Core, or rather, -core, is a suffix that denotes some kind of bundle of aesthetics and moods. Cottagecore, for example, is a reference to an image universe of milkmaid tops, galoshes, a straw basket full of wildflowers, a shingled cottage, butter made with wooden tools, but also, beyond images, a wish for simple, optimistic, pastoral life. But corecore emerged not as a specific aesthetic but as a way of making video content through editing together music, clips from movies, podcasts, and other TikToks.These videos are often melancholic ruminations of the deep and unrecognized suffering of young men, the terrible sublimity of being alone with your screen, an alienated awareness of hurtling towards extinction while scrolling on your parents’ sofa. Watching compilations of corecore I reminded me intensely of my time on Tumblr as a teenager.

On Tumblr, the practice of posting and reblogging images, fragments of text, and GIFs under an “aesthetic” was a way of cultivating and nurturing a feeling: a world of images that created a visual language for all that we thought life could be. Painful, dramatic love; intensity chased by the doom of its own ephemerality; cold, beautiful suffering. In the pivot to video platforms, corecore takes that tendency to collect images and feelings and turns it into a form much more contained and shareable than a blog: the film.

Removed from the usual logics of personal branding and sponsorship deals, it became an exciting trend for those who take internet-based cultural production seriously. Corecore, as a mass movement of people making films on the same devices they watch them on, represented the realization of an old dream of experimental cinema: film as a truly collective practice.“Authorship gets really muddy, you have content creators and video artists making films on the same platforms. Dana [Dawud] has compared internet cinema to the way that SoundCloud functions, where people can steal your edit, steal your beat, steal a sample, and then it gets lost who put it online first, and that’s okay,” mentioned Edison Niebla Rodriguez, a curator who also creates video art with his wife, Dayana Matasheva, under the name Demon Lovers Inc. “Obviously, it’s kind of at odds with video art which is very hard for people to see because of the way that the art market functions.”.

According to recent reports, that said, video art has been a major inspiration for artists working in internet cinema. Arthur Jafa’s Love Is the Message, the Message Is Death (2016) in particular is often cited as a kind of totem of legitimacy for internet cinema. The film is a montage of civil rights–era footage and grainy videos of Black Americans circulated online: reaction memes, women twerking, women getting slammed to the ground by police, men getting shot by police, arms raised in the air. It’s profound love and profound despair, but also a comment on how images of Black people circulate online. When I first saw it, I was struck by how internet it felt: the video was playing on a laptop, which had been filmed by a phone camera. Of course, it turned out I’d been watching a bootleg, and that extra frame wasn’t part of the original.

Dawud’s film Palcorecore (2024) can be read in Jafa’s lineage. Like Jafa, Dawud cuts together found footage in an act of memory, love, and grief. The footage indicates Palestinians flying kites, breakdancing, rapping, riding horses, and playing by the sea. Children surround a tank; a mute man desperately mimes a murder he witnessed. “The internet exists to document its own martyrdom,” a computerized voice says, playing on the fact that the Arabic word for martyr comes from the root meaning “to witness.” “We are martyrs together,” the voice says: we are witnesses together.“What interests me about internet cinema is that it creates a history of internet artists, the thoughts and languages they develop online, and the possibility of establishing a continuity between what they produce on the internet and how it can be given a temporal form through cinema,” wrote Dawud in an email. “Internet cinema doesn’t merely document an existing culture; it participates in the creation of its history. It is a way for the internet to historicize itself.”Ultimately, internet cinema is less a practice than a name: one that gives form to a whole range of ways of making online that are often lost in the tumult of the internet’s incessant production. If there is one thing that separates the art world from online cultural production, it is that the art world is nothing if not a form of memory. In its obsession with provenance, catalogues, and archives, it is a network of stewardship. Internet cinema is a means of making an object of what is so fleeting online, by turning the internet into a film.
