In a recent development, bitly, the URL shortener website, got in hot water this week as users (or 'bad actors' as the firm calls them) found out they could hijack old broken links and put whatever they like in their place. As you can imagine, chaos ensued.

Sources indicate that many of the links have since been fixed, but it seems like the McDonald's Twitter account once accidentally said the Swedish McDonald's team are responsible for 9/11,, and KFC linked to the Mewgenics wiki page for Steven, an NPC from the game. McDonald's was caught multiple times in this hijacking, with the Canadian X account hijacked to link the We Are Charlie Kirk song.

Though it's hard to verify now, it also seems like the Windows X page accidentally linked to the "r/FuckMicrosoft" subreddit too.

Industry observers note that big brands weren't the only ones caught in the mass hijacking. President Donald Trump seemingly tweeted a lot of Bitly links back in 2011, which were hijacked to instead link to a 'Trollface' meme song, a picture of a soggy cat titled "Soggy cat", and a link to the Epstein files Wikipedia page.

images for archival purposes pic.twitter.com/fRRr8LMGeENovember 15, 2026.

Recent findings suggest that A day after this exploit started to make its way around X, Bitly shared that it had identified the matter and said, "We have suspended the accounts involved and are disabling the affected links. Our Trust and Safety team is working to address the issue and strengthen protections against similar misuse in the future.".

Industry observers note that it seems like the links in question were broken before being hijacked, and people simply managed to redirect those links to whatever site they wanted. However, in Bitly's documentation, it says the "back-halves used on these links cannot be re-used — whether they're automatically-generated or custom back-halves." So it's not entirely clear how these users manage to use links when Bitly says they supposedly cannot be reused.

Industry observers note that earlier today, we identified an glitch that allowed bad actors to repurpose certain old, inactive links to redirect people to content unrelated to the original posts. In the grand scheme, brands linking to various songs and Wikipedia articles are relatively harmless, but the ability to hijack links does pose a problem: what if hackers could do the same with a link to something containing malware? As such, it's probably a good reminder to Bitly and any other URL shortener websites to double-check that customers can't reuse old broken links, as who knows who could have possibly made them first.
