UK unemployment unexpectedly eases to 4.6% as government releases Milburn report

The UK unemployment rate has unexpectedly fallen as the government released an important report on the youth jobs crisis.

The unemployment rate was 4.6% in September, dipping from 4.7% in August and down from 5% earlier in the year, according to the Office for National Statistics.

However, the number of vacancies fell as employers delayed hiring in response to global economic uncertainty and the use of artificial intelligence, which many analysts expect will lead to widespread job losses.

Business groups stated the slump in vacancies posed a problem for the government as responded to Alan Milburn’s report on young people not in employment, education or training (Neets).

Milburn, a former health secretary, unveiled his long-awaited report into youth unemployment on Tuesday morning in London, urging ministers to cut disability and sickness payments for under-25s and invest the money in back-to-work schemes.

The British Chambers of Commerce remarked it was “alarming” that the number of vacancies had tumbled to its lowest level since 2014 and unemployment for 18- to 24-year-olds was nearly triple the average for UK adults, at 13%.

“This will put further pressure on today’s Milburn review to deliver a sea change in the government’s approach to tackling youth joblessness,” it remarked.

The number of vacancies dropped to 690,000 in October after soaring to more than 1.3m after the pandemic.

Wage growth also fell to 3.5%, excluding bonuses, in the latest official figures for the three months to the end of September, down from 3.7% in the three months to August.

City economists had expected unemployment to remain steady and wages, excluding bonuses, to fall slightly to 3.6%.

With inflation also at 3.5% and expected to rise for a fourth consecutive month to 3.7% when figures are released on Wednesday, the average worker faces a fall in real wage growth to zero or even negative figures.

In a recent development, martin Beck, the chief economist at the consultants WPI Strategy, stated: “The UK labour market remained relatively resilient in late autumn, but beneath the surface, there were more signs that the economy is struggling to generate jobs. The jobs market isn’t falling off a cliff, but is going nowhere fast.”.

In a recent development, the jobs market has proved difficult for analysts and politicians to assess in recent years. It has been called weak after a dramatic slide in the number of vacancies, which illustrates the reticence of companies to hire new staff.

Ministers are known to be concerned that some large employers, including several banks, have stated they intend to use AI to reduce their staffing levels.

Economists are divided over whether AI will enhance productivity across all the big sectors of the economy – after signs it has boosted GDP – or lead to improvements in certain industries such as healthcare.

Employer groups have complained that Labour’s increase in employer national insurance contributions, a higher minimum wage and changes to employee rights have increased costs, leading to a fall in the number of advertised vacancies.

The Bank of England increased interest rates earlier this month after a rise in the consumer prices index to 3.5% in September.

It has mentioned that its own surveys indicated employers did not expect to increase pay beyond 3.5% to 4% next year, despite the higher level of inflation, and also reported little change looking ahead in their own prices.

However, a growing number of the Bank’s policymakers believe the prospect of high oil and energy prices next year as the Iran war drags on will push inflation towards 5% and force employers to offer higher pay, which will be passed on in higher retail prices.

A spokesperson for the Department for Work and Pensions stated the Milburn review would “shape our next steps for young people”. “Despite the difficult global backdrop, the unemployment rate is falling, and the number of people in work is rising,” the spokesperson added.

Recent findings suggest that “We are determined to go further. Our £2.5bn youth employment support will give almost 1 million young people opportunities to earn and learn, while wider reforms including improving jobcentres, will create a more personalised work and skills advice service for all.”.
