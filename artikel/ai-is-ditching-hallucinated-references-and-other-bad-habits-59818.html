AI is ditching hallucinated references — and other bad habits

Industry observers note that hallucinated academic citations have become a hallmark of the artificial-intelligence era. But they might soon be gone for good. Researchers have found that the latest large language models (LLMs) rarely, if ever, attribute information to sources that don’t exist.

The finding is part of an analysis that reports that many LLM tendencies, such as overthinking simple maths problems and complying with illogical requests, fade over time as AI companies release fresh or updated models with improved capabilities. Many of these tendencies disappear about 16 months after the phenomenon is first described in a published paper. The analysis, described in a blog post, has not been peer reviewed.

“We, as users of these systems, need to not have such a fixed view of what AI can do and what it can’t do,” says co-author Owen Queen, an AI researcher at Stanford University in California.

Sources indicate that but just because an LLM behaviour fades as models improve doesn’t mean that the effect immediately disappears, says Maxim Topaz, a health-care AI researcher at Columbia University in New York City. Topaz has found1 that the number of hallucinated citations in academic papers has risen drastically since 2023, probably because more researchers than ever before are using LLMs, and they often aren’t using the latest models.

Industry observers note that queen and his colleague identified 50 widely cited LLM behaviours that were described in publications between late 2022, when some of the first LLMs became widely available, and late 2025. They then gave the same prompts that first elicited the behaviour in the original publications to 20 LLMs that were released from mid-2024 to late 2026 to see if the effect has strengthened or weakened over time. For example, they asked each model to count the number of r’s in ‘strawberry’ — something that LLMs have found difficult but can now do more reliably.

Sources indicate that nearly half of the behaviours they checked have largely disappeared from newer models, leading the researchers to deem those effects ‘expired’. Expired behaviours had an average lifespan of about 16 months after publication, although some were fleeting — models stopped overthinking simple maths problems just a month and a half after this tendency was published. Other traits persisted, such as the stereotyping of Black women by disproportionately describing them as resilient, which lasted for longer than three years.

In a recent development, although most of the retested LLM behaviours have faded over time, three have become more pronounced. Today’s models praise what their users like even more often than they used to, LLMs more strongly favour their own writing compared with that of other LLMs and people; and LLMs are now even worse than they used to be at generating truly random numbers.

Industry observers note that in a 2023 study2, OpenAI’s GPT-3.5 hallucinated 55% of citations when asked to write literature reviews. Models have, on average, fabricated fewer citations over time since then, but the newest model in the latest analysis — GPT-6.1 Sol — was the only one to hallucinate zero citations. Still, the researchers deemed this behaviour to be expired as of April because the latest models hallucinate very few citations.

It has been revealed that this decline in hallucinations is not yet reflected in published papers. Earlier this year1, Topaz and his colleagues analysed 2.5 million biomedical papers published between early 2023 and early 2026 and found that the rate of hallucinated citations had increased 12-fold. Topaz says that researchers are increasingly using LLMs and that papers published in 2026 were probably written in 2025 or earlier with models that were more hallucination-prone.

Research suggesting that LLM behaviours have expired might oversimplify reality, claims Maarten Sap, an AI researcher at Carnegie Mellon University in Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania.

Recent findings suggest that LLMs often have different training and optimization processes, Sap notes, and it’s possible that some of the models tested in this research had training that was better suited to avoiding these behaviours than did others. Understanding how each model was trained in relation to the prompts could yield more specific insights into LLM capabilities, says Sap. “Whether they can do a certain thing is less interesting to me than why they are doing it.”.
