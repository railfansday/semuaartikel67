The power grid’s toughest hour of the year may no longer come on a summer afternoon

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Heat pumps could reshape the power grid more than climate change, pushing the worst demand spikes in cold regions into winter by 2060.

Ask when the power grid has its hardest day and most people picture a heat wave, with every air conditioner in town running at once.

Across much of the northern United States, that day may soon be a cold winter night instead.

Latest modeling finds that as homes swap gas furnaces for heat pumps, the worst spikes in electricity demand in cold regions could grow by half to nearly double by 2060.

With today’s heating and cooling equipment, warming alone changes extreme peaks by less than 10% in either direction.

The model projects much larger increases in cold regions as buildings switch to electric heating.

Mai Shi, a postdoctoral researcher at the University of Michigan‘s School for Environment and Sustainability, led the work with colleagues at Michigan, MIT and Cornell University.

In a recent development, their projections cover 183 counties through 2060 and hold the number of buildings fixed. Every change they report comes from weather and equipment, not from new construction or population growth.

“We focused on the extreme peak demands because we want to keep our power systems reliable,” Shi said.

Industry observers note that “If the system works for moderate conditions but fails in extremes, it doesn’t work. That’s not how we build the power grid. We need to blueprint for extremes.”.

Industry observers note that an electric heat pump can both heat and cool a building. When it replaces a gas furnace, heating shifts onto the power grid. The standard heat pump in the study lost heating capacity as outdoor temperatures fell.

The standard unit in the study keeps only half of its rated heating capacity at 5°F (-15°C).

In the team’s model, heat pumps and insulation spread through buildings at the pace home air conditioning spread across the country, about 10% a year.

With that pace of adoption and today’s weather, the most extreme peaks grow by 60% to 104% by 2060 across the six cold regions.

Those are New England, New York, the Midwest, the Mid-Atlantic, the Northwest and the Great Plains. The season changes too.

At the start of the projections, more than 95% of those regions’ yearly peaks fall in summer.

Recent findings suggest that by 2060, more than 95% fall in winter. Insulation lowers demand, but in cold regions it can’t cancel out the furnaces being replaced.

Run the same buildings through a warming climate with today’s equipment and the changes are smaller.

In a recent development, extreme peaks rise 3% to 9% in every region but the Northwest, as extreme heat pushes up cooling demand. In the Northwest, winter heating already sets the peak, and a milder winter trims it by 3%.

Put the two drivers together and they don’t simply add up. Warmer winters offset part of the heat pump demand, cutting the growth in cold-region extremes by up to 18% compared with technology alone.

The net result in those regions is still a 48% to 93% increase through 2060. That is up to roughly 10 times what warming alone would do. Warming also delays the switch to winter peaking rather than stopping it.

Researchers call the spread of a technology through homes tech diffusion.

“Tech diffusion may have a larger impact than climate change on peak demand in a lot of regions,” stated Shi, who is also a member of the university’s Center for Sustainable Systems.

In the projections for California and Texas, the peak stays in summer. Heat pumps and insulation make cooling more efficient there, so the technology lowers extreme peaks instead of raising them.

With warming included, those two regions’ extremes end up 1% to 9% lower than they would be under climate change alone.

The Southwest and Southeast are in between. Technology alone pushes their extremes up 17% and 11%, because about 60% of their yearly peaks move to winter while the rest stay in summer.

Recent findings suggest that A winter peak on a cold night can run higher than a summer one. Warming lowers those winter extremes by 4% to 6%.

In Texas, faster adoption lowers the peak further. Under climate change alone, the largest peak between 2020 and 2060 is 12% above today’s.

With the fastest spread of heat pumps and insulation, that increase shrinks to 5%.

The team trained 209,352 machine learning models on simulated hourly heating and cooling use for 362,227 homes and commercial buildings across 183 counties.

The counties were chosen so that each state’s mix of weather zones matched the real one.

The researchers used 100 simulations from four climate models and three emissions scenarios to project how future weather could affect electricity demand for heating and cooling.

It has been revealed that adoption speed came from history. The researchers looked at how fast 18 past technologies spread, from vacuum cleaners at about 2% a year to cell phones at about 36%. Home air conditioning, at 10%, was their main case.

The researchers used the 95th percentile of projected annual peaks to estimate extreme demand. Only about one in 20 of those peaks exceeds that level.

Sources indicate that in the projections, peaks change little through the early 2030s and the weather is nearly the whole source of uncertainty. In cold regions, that changes in the late 2030s and the 2040s.

Heat pump adoption reaches the steep part of its curve and winter peaks grow quickly. From one climate run to the next, it becomes uncertain whether a year’s peak falls in summer or winter.

Sources indicate that the spread of possible peaks in those regions widens to three or four times its 2020 level by the 2050s. By then, winter peaking is established in most of them.

For grid planners, the source of uncertainty moves with that transition. By the 2050s, how fast people adopt the technology is the largest single factor in Newly introduced England and New York, at 65% to 75% of the total.

Climate still drives 85% to 99% in the Southeast, the Northwest and California.

Michael Craig is the study’s senior author and an associate professor at Michigan.

“Climate change still matters a lot. If I’m thinking about my power system and what I should worry about, climate change is still an notable source of risk and uncertainty,” Craig said.

Industry observers note that the team also tested a heat pump that keeps 90% of its heating capacity at 5°F instead of 50%. With it, extreme peaks come out 5% to 40% lower, with the biggest savings in cold regions.

It has been revealed that the projections hold the equipment’s performance fixed over 40 years, so they don’t include heat pumps improving, or smart thermostats and thermal storage moving demand off the worst hours. They cover heating and cooling only.

Electric vehicle charging isn’t in them, and neither are data centers.

One recent tally of utility forecasts attributes roughly half of the projected growth in U.S. peak load through 2030 to data centers.

Industry observers note that humidity isn’t in the model either, only temperature. And it assumes heat pumps spread at the same pace everywhere, when energy prices, housing and policy will set different speeds in different states.

How fast adoption will actually run in each region is the open question. Shi stated that number, unlike the weather, is one that local rules and incentives can steer.

The authors wrote that the next step is building those regional differences into the projections, so that each region’s planners see the projection that applies to them.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
