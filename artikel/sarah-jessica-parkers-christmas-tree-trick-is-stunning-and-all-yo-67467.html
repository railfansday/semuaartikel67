Thanks to her stint in The Family Stone, Sarah Jessica Parker's name has long been synonymous with the holidays for fans – and even more so now she's shared her Christmas tree trick with us all!

Yes, it is now possible to give your Christmas tree that Carrie Bradshaw stamp of approval! And, somewhat gloriously, SJP's approach to festive decor has the same sort of wonderfully unfussy vibe as Michelle Obama's twig tree, which means you don't need a celebrity-sized budget to recreate the look. In fact, all you really need is baby's breath.

Sources indicate that this underrated flower has spent most of its life being a supporting actor to bigger and better roses, or a wispy filler flower tucked into grocery store bouquets. But when Kristin Davis posted an Instagram video of herself arranging flowers blindfolded (as one does), Sarah Jessica Parker left a surprising comment: “You can use babies breath in your Christmas tree.” Say what?

Now, Kristin Davis (who worked at a florist in college) wasn’t keen on using it for her own tree (very Charlotte-coded), but she did share a rule worth stealing: if you're going to do it, then always arrange flowers in odd numbers, like three or five.

For me, though, SJP’s comment about using baby’s breath in her Christmas tree has set my imagination alight! And with that in mind, I asked some experts how I could use them in my own holiday decoration this year.

Industry observers note that the biggest risk when it comes to stealing Sarah Jessica Parker's idea is overdoing it. Roberta Sabbatini, founder of ItalianTouch Events, claims, “The main mistake is using too much. The beauty of baby’s breath is its lightness.” Morgan Brooks of Plant Style Home puts it even better, saying, “One loose spiral is elegant. Three is a snowstorm, and the tree disappears behind it.”.

Industry observers note that remember that odd-number rule? CEO of Florals Memories Nadi Melam clips her baby’s breath into “clouds” and adds them in threes, noting, “Baby’s breath looks beautiful on trees because of negative space.” Translation: let the tree breathe!

Recent findings suggest that for a spiral, Brooks gathers five to seven stems, wires each bunch at the base, and tucks the ends deep into the branches. Sabbatini also skips pushing loose stems into the tree and instead secures each little bundle with florist wire or ribbon.

Fresh baby’s breath is a diva out of water. Brooks claims fresh stems “wilt within a day or two indoors,” while dried versions keep their shape all season and can be packed away for next year. Melam agrees that dried is “far easier to work with.” If you do love fresh, just be sure to add it on Christmas Eve.

If you don't have time for the drying process, this Bexikou dried baby’s breath bouquet comes ready to use, and its short white stems are well suited to wiring into small, airy clusters for your tree.

Industry observers note that drying your own for a Sarah Jessica Parker-style tree is simple. Bundle five to seven stems with twine, hang them upside down in a dark, warm, well-ventilated room, and check after five days, as outlined in our guide to growing baby’s breath. If you’re short on time, ready-dried bunches work just as well.

On an artificial tree, Brooks adds fine floral wire around the branch wire, since the stems are too light to stay put when someone brushes past.

Industry observers note that warm white lights, clear glass ornaments, and velvet ribbon are baby’s breath’s best friends. Brooks adds pearl baubles and silver or soft gold ribbon to the list, but warns that rustic burlap and heavy red ornaments “fight it.” Treat baby’s breath as one of just two or three ideas on the tree, not the whole script.

Pair your baby’s breath with a strand of Northlight 100-count warm white LED mini lights, which stay cool to the touch and give the soft, candlelit glow that suits a delicate white filler.

Sources indicate that dried stems are brittle and, as Melam notes, highly flammable. Use cool LED lights and keep bunches away from hot bulbs and open flames. Baby’s breath is also mildly toxic to cats, so if pets are around, keep it to the upper two-thirds of the tree and sweep up anything that falls.

Baby’s breath is easy to grow, so sow seeds next spring, cut and dry the blooms in summer, and next December’s tree is sorted. Will your tree look like a Manhattan brownstone from a certain Sarah Jessica Parker-fronted TV series? Possibly, but only if you resist the urge to add a fourth bunch.
