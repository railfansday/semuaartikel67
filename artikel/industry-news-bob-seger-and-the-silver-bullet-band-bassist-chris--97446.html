In a recent development, bob Seger and the Silver Bullet Band bassist Chris Campbell, who joined forces with Seger in 1969 and remained a core member of the band through Seger’s farewell tour in 2019 — playing on timeless hits like “Turn the Page,” “Night Moves,” “Rock and Roll Never Forgets,” “Still the Same,” “Hollywood Nights,” and “Against the Wind” — died on Sunday. He was 78.

Campbell’s wife, Courtney, confirmed his death with Rolling Stone, adding that Campbell died of natural causes.

“I’m grieving the loss of my long-time friend and Silver Bullet bandmate, Chris Campbell…..RIP,” Silver Bullet Band vocalist Shaun Murphy wrote. “Prayers and condolences to his wonderful wife, Courtney Campbell, and his three beautiful daughters!! I am forever grateful for all the cherished memories I have.”.

Campbell grew up in Plymouth, Michigan, and was a regular on the Detroit club scene that gave rise to the MC5, the Stooges, and the Amboy Dukes. “You were playing to smaller crowds and things were more intimate,” he told the Observer & Eccentric in 1987. “There weren’t any barriers in front of the stage with six-foot security guards.”.

Just one year after the Bob Seger System scored a nationwide hit with “Ramblin’ Gamblin’ Man” in 1968, Campbell was offered a spot in the band. “I was hired in what I thought was a temporary job as bass player because Dan Honaker had just broken his wrist about a week before the Mongrel tour [in a motorcycle accident],” he told Rolling Stone in 2011. “I had a week to prepare. I had to learn this album frontwards and backwards.”.

In a recent development, honaker’s bad luck turned out to be the opportunity of a lifetime for Campbell because of what transpired not long after the Bob Seger System dissolved. “Bob called me…I think it was ’71, ’72,” Campbell told Rolling Stone. “He stated, ‘I’m thinking of putting a band together.’ I said to him, ‘That’s kind of bizarre, I have a keyboard player and a drummer.’ He goes, ‘I got a guitarist, Drew Abbott.’ That was the start of the Silver Bullet Band.” Editor’s picks The 250 Greatest Albums of the 21st Century So Far The 100 Best TV Episodes of All Time The 500 Greatest Albums of All Time 100 Best Movies of the 21st Century.

Industry observers note that the early years of the Silver Bullet Band were a brutal grind, memorialized in the lyrics of 1972’s “Turn the Page,” but everything changed once the concert LP Live Bullet took off in 1976. The follow-up LP, Night Moves, turned Seger into one of the biggest names in rock.

According to recent reports, unlike similar groups like Bruce Springsteen’s E Street Band and Neil Young’s Crazy Horse, membership in the Silver Bullet Band was somewhat fluid, and studio musicians often backed Seger on albums. This arrangement didn’t suit Abbott, who quit the group in 1983 and never returned. But Campbell, background singers Shaun Murphy and Laura Creamer, and saxophonist Alto Reed were lifers. Grand Funk Railroad keyboardist Craig Frost also became a key component of the group for decades.

Sources indicate that for Campbell, it wasn’t always an easy ride. “We have all [thought about leaving] at one time or another,” he told the Observer & Eccentric in 1987. “It would be an out-and-out lie to say we haven’t. You get tired, and it’s terrible on relationships.”.

Recent findings suggest that the job got easier after the tour for Seger’s 1995 LP It’s a Mystery wrapped, and the group went off the road for more than a decade. “Bob works at his own pace,” Campbell told Rolling Stone in 2011. “I can go out and do something else. It’s the way it’s always been since 1969. I wouldn’t know any other way. This is my life, and I’m very happy with it. I have wonderful kids and a good life, and we’re all friends. We’re all very fortunate. There’s no animosity and no fighting. Bob is our rock, our patriarch. We all look up to him. He’s so easy to work with. According to recent reports, two years after the tour wrapped, Campbell filed a lawsuit against Seger and longtime manager Punch Andrews over alleged unpaid royalties from albums and tours. “Bob Seger and Punch Andrews have loyally supported our friend for 45-plus years,” they remarked in a statement. Industry observers note that campbell kept a low public profile after the farewell tour. But in 2011, he told Rolling Stone why he enjoyed the latter-day tours so much. “We’re kind of old-school,” he mentioned. “We aren’t glitzy. You don’t see any flash pots going off up there. We just do things the way we do. We aren’t really concerned about press agents and all that. We just play. And the older we get, the more fun we have. When you get older, you don’t take it as seriously as you once did when you were younger. If you can’t have fun at our age, don’t do it.”.
