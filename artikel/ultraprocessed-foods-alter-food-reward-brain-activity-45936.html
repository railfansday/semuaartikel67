Ultraprocessed Foods Alter Food-Reward Brain Activity

Source: Virginia Tech / Obesity.

It has been revealed that ultraprocessed foods, products formulated with industrial processing methods and ingredients rarely found in a home kitchen, account for more than half of the calories consumed by young adults in the United States. While extensive research links these foods to obesity, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular disease, neuroscientists are increasingly uncovering how industrial processing directly interacts with the developing human brain.

In a controlled feeding study published this month in the journal Obesity, researchers at Virginia Tech’s Fralin Biomedical Research Institute at VTC and Department of Human Nutrition, Foods, and Exercise found that emerging adults ages 18 to 21 exhibit distinct neural changes in food-reward circuitry after consuming an ultraprocessed diet for just two weeks.

Led by first author Dr. Emma Leslie alongside neuroscientist Dr. Alex DiFeliceantonio and registered dietitian Dr. Brenda Davy, the findings complement earlier results from the same trial showing that 18- to 21-year-olds consumed excess calories at a buffet meal after eating ultraprocessed foods, even when they were no longer hungry.

Sources indicate that to separate the effects of industrial food processing from basic nutritional content, the researchers recruited 22 healthy adults ages 18 to 25 (including seven participants ages 21 or younger) for a randomized crossover feeding trial. Each participant followed two strictly controlled diets for two weeks apiece, separated by a four-week washout period:.

Critically, both diets were engineered to have identical macronutrient profiles (50% carbohydrates, 35% fat, 15% protein), matching levels of fiber, added sugars, saturated fat, sodium, energy density, and overall nutritional quality, calibrated to keep each participant’s body weight stable.

Before and after each two-week dietary phase, participants underwent functional MRI (fMRI) brain scans while receiving small sips of an ultraprocessed milkshake or a tasteless control solution through a specialized delivery tube.

When the researchers analyzed the neuroimaging data, they discovered a striking developmental divide:.

“Among the younger participants, consuming a diet high in ultraprocessed foods for just two weeks was associated with decreased brain response to a rewarding food — a milkshake — in the orbitofrontal cortex,” explained Leslie, who conducted the work as a doctoral student at Virginia Tech and is now a postdoctoral associate at the University of Michigan.

Located just behind the eyes, the orbitofrontal cortex is one of the final regions of the human brain to finish maturing, continuing to remodel into the mid- to late 20s. It plays a central role in evaluating food rewards, regulating cravings, and exerting executive self-control to stop eating.

Because the two diets were matched across nutritional metrics, the findings point toward non-nutritive properties of ultraprocessed foods, such as chemical additives, emulsifiers, texture alterations, or compounds generated during industrial processing, as potential drivers of altered brain signaling.

“This study is crucial because you have this change in an area of the brain known to govern self-control and potentially put a stop to eating,” said DiFeliceantonio, associate professor at the Fralin Biomedical Research Institute and interim co-director of its Center for Health Behaviors Research. “This is giving us insight into how ultraprocessed foods are affecting the bodies and brains of younger people, who might be especially vulnerable to their effects. And that’s important to know for things like dietary guidelines, doctor recommendations, and policy decisions.”.

While the researchers note that the sample size was small and the age-based findings were exploratory, and did not directly correlate individual fMRI shifts with buffet calorie intake, the results highlight adolescence and early adulthood as critical windows of neurodevelopmental sensitivity.

According to recent reports, next, DiFeliceantonio and Davy plan to investigate whether adolescents under 18, who consume the highest proportion of ultraprocessed foods of any U.S. demographic, show even stronger neural sensitivity, and whether brain responses recover once individuals return to whole-food diets.

How do ultraprocessed foods affect the brains of young adults?

A Virginia Tech study found that after eating a diet high in ultraprocessed foods for just two weeks, young adults ages 18 to 21 indicated decreased brain activity in the orbitofrontal cortex, a region involved in food reward, valuation, and self-control, when tasting a milkshake. Adults ages 22 to 25 did not show these brain changes.

Was the brain effect simply caused by extra sugar, fat, or calories?

No. Both the ultraprocessed diet and the unprocessed comparison diet contained the exact same amounts of calories, carbohydrates, fat, protein, added sugars, saturated fat, fiber, and sodium. This suggests that industrial food processing or additives themselves, rather than basic nutrients, drove the brain changes.

Why are 18- to 21-year-olds more sensitive to ultraprocessed foods than slightly older adults?

Sources indicate that the orbitofrontal cortex is one of the last brain regions to finish developing, continuing to mature into the mid- to late 20s. Because this self-control and reward-evaluation circuit is still actively wiring in 18- to 21-year-olds, it appears more sensitive to dietary shifts.

The average American consumes 55% of daily energy from ultraprocessed foods (UPF), created through industrial processes and additives not used at home. We investigated if a high-UPF diet alters brain response to milkshake compared with a diet free from UPF in emerging adults, who are in a critical brain development period and typically consume high amounts of UPF.

Sources indicate that in a randomized controlled crossover trial participants aged 18–25 years completed two 2-week controlled feeding periods including a UPF (81% UPF) and non-UPF (0% UPF) diet. Before and after each diet intervention, participants consumed milkshake concomitant with functional magnetic resonance imaging.

In the entire cohort, there were no differences between diet conditions in brain response. An exploratory analysis revealed orbitofrontal cortex response to milkshake decreased after the UPF diet and increased following the non-UPF diet in late adolescents (18–21 years) but not young adults (22–25 years). In all participants, habitual UPF intake (g) was positively associated with orbitofrontal cortex response to milkshake, independent of diet condition.

According to recent reports, an acute UPF dietary intervention may only alter brain response in late adolescents. Further work is needed to determine potential vulnerability of late adolescents to changes in dietary UPF on brain response to rewards.

ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05550818.
