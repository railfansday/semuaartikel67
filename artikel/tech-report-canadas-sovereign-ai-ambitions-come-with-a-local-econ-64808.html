Sources indicate that data center backlash is pushing builders to show communities what they get in return, and public-private partnerships are becoming one answer. As the race for sovereign AI heats up, the question is shifting from where intellectual property lives to where compute runs and who profits from it.

Sources indicate that canada offers a sharp test case. A modern data hall employs few people, but its profits can fund the startups and jobs a region needs, according to Sharif Virani (pictured), chief executive officer of SPUR Innovation Center.

It has been revealed that “There’s a big misnomer right now in the world that’s kind of landing from previous tech, where people think where your IP is where the wealth creation is going to be,” he said. “It’s not the case anymore. You have to make sure your inference happens in your country or in your region.”.

According to recent reports, virani spoke with theCUBE Research’s John Furrier and host Rebecca Knight at SC26, during an exclusive broadcast on theCUBE, SiliconANGLE Media’s livestreaming studio. They discussed sovereign AI, reinvesting data center profits in local entrepreneurs and winning over residents through public-private partnerships. (* Disclosure below.).

SPUR treats its Waterloo, Ontario, site as an innovation campus with an attached data hall rather than a pure data center. The company reinvests about 10 percent of the campus’s data center profits in local entrepreneurs through a venture studio and takes first right of refusal on their office space and compute, Virani explained. Canada’s Dominion List counts more than 500 founders who have left for San Francisco, he noted.

“We have entrepreneurship at an all-time low, productivity at an all-time low [and] GDP in freefall,” he said. “That’s really the result. We’ve got to reverse that.”.

One of SPUR’s early investments went to two international students who had full scholarships to the University of Toronto and internships lined up in San Francisco. Instead, they launched a startup called Auditorium AI and hired staff, according to Virani. The model also reaches Main Street, where on-campus hackathons helped a former long-haul trucker build AI software for logistics.

“AI allows these people who know the problem, who have earned the right to solve the problem, to mobilize technology to do it and make the lives of their friends, families and neighbors better,” Virani said.
