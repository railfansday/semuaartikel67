In a recent development, now imagine Maya. She is also intelligent, but her suspected ADHD symptoms present differently. She daydreams in class and quietly falls behind. She is emotionally sensitive, often taking criticism to heart and becoming overwhelmed by setbacks that her peers seem to brush off. Her teachers describe her as capable but inconsistent. Because she isn’t disruptive, her struggles are attributed to a lack of effort, disorganization, anxiety, or immaturity.

Year after year, she works harder while feeling increasingly frustrated. As academic demands increase, so do her feelings of self-doubt and inadequacy. By the time she receives an ADHD diagnosis at age 16, her grades have suffered, her confidence has eroded, and she is considering leaving school altogether.

The paths of these two children, one who received early intervention with medication and one who remained untreated until her mid-teens, illustrate a growing concern in ADHD care: It’s not simply about whether children are diagnosed, but when.

A recent study published in JAMA Psychiatry examined educational outcomes among more than 580,000 individuals with ADHD. The researchers found that children diagnosed earlier in life had significantly better educational outcomes than those diagnosed during adolescence. Earlier diagnosis with associated with higher grade point averages, a greater likelihood of pursuing academic educational tracks, and substantially lower rates of school dropout.

The differences were striking. Individuals diagnosed during the early elementary school years had school dropout rates of approximately 9 percent by age 20, compared with nearly 30 percent among those first diagnosed around age 16. Similar patterns were observed in both males and females. The researchers concluded that adolescents diagnosed later represent a particularly vulnerable group.

It has been revealed that the findings do not suggest that a diagnosis alone changes a child’s future. Rather, earlier recognition creates opportunities for intervention. Children identified sooner can access school accommodations, behavioral supports, parent education, and, when appropriate, medication – before years of academic struggles, self-doubt, and declining confidence take hold and begin to shape their identity.

This raises an vital question: How many Mayas are sitting in classrooms today, struggling silently while waiting years for recognition or a diagnosis?

Recent findings suggest that universal ADHD screening offers a potential solution. Many children with ADHD are identified only after academic, social, or emotional difficulties have become impossible to ignore. Screening creates an opportunity to recognize concerns earlier before the consequences of undiagnosed ADHD begin to accumulate. Importantly, screening is not diagnosis. It is simply an invitation to look more closely.

It has been revealed that many critics of universal screening worry about overdiagnosis or unnecessary labeling. Those concerns deserve consideration. However, screening itself does not determine whether a child has ADHD. It merely identifies children who may benefit from a more comprehensive evaluation, meaning that fewer children with significant challenges get overlooked.

According to recent reports, growing evidence suggests that every year of delayed recognition carries educational and emotional costs that are difficult to reverse. Universal screening will not identify every child. But it may help more children find the right path before they wander too far afield.

For many children, the greatest risk may not be receiving an ADHD diagnosis. It may be never receiving one at all.
