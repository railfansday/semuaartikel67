Tech Report: Why You Should Never Put Frozen Meat In A Slow Cooker, According To Experts

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that with busy schedules and limited time each day, slow cookers are key to getting dinner on the table. They can take simple ingredients and transform them into a complete, ready-to-eat meal. Because the slow cooker does all the work for you, you may think that it can also handle defrosting meat.

Recent findings suggest that but think again. The idea seems convenient in theory, but in practice, it raises some serious food safety concerns. We consulted health experts to learn more about adding frozen meat to a slow cooker. So next time you proposal a hands-off dinner, you can make sure your food is delicious and safe to eat.

Sorry to be the bearer of bad news: You should never put frozen meat directly into your slow cooker. “I am all for cutting out unnecessary steps in cooking, but using frozen meat in your slow cooker is not one of them,” claims Melissa Koch, RD, MEd.

Raw meat can carry harmful bacteria that cause foodborne illness. Freezing stops them from multiplying, but doesn't necessarily kill them. And because slow cookers heat food gradually, frozen meat can spend too long in what food safety experts call the “temperature danger zone.”.

Sources indicate that “The danger zone refers to a temperature range of 40°F (4°C) to 140°F (60°C) over two or more hours when harmful bacteria grow or multiply at a fast rate,” Koch explains. It can take hours for food in a slow cooker to move above the danger zone—and starting with frozen meat delays that process even further.

“The appliance heats food slowly, and starting with frozen meat means the internal temperature may sit in the ‘danger zone’ for too long,” says chef and dietitian Sharniquia White. “This gives bacteria like Salmonella or E. coli more opportunity to multiply before the food reaches a safe cooking temperature.”.

Sources indicate that what happens after eating food that has sat in the danger zone? The consequences vary based on your immune system, the extent of the contamination, and the strains of bacteria present. But none are pleasant, and some may even send you to the hospital.

“Symptoms of food-borne illness can include nausea, vomiting, stomach cramps, and fever,” Koch indicates. “You can experience the effects of food-borne illness within a few minutes to days after consuming a load of harmful pathogens or bacteria.”.

In a recent development, cooking frozen meat on the “low” setting of your slow cooker increases the time it spends in the danger zone, but the risk is present regardless. “The 'high' setting reduces the amount of time food spends at unsafe temperatures, but still doesn’t fully eliminate the risk when starting with frozen meat,” states White.

Industry observers note that the ingredients you're using can also increase your risk. “Larger, denser cuts (like roasts or whole poultry) take longer to thaw and heat evenly, so they pose a greater risk of staying at unsafe temperatures for extended periods,” White explains.

Smaller pieces may thaw faster, but that doesn't necessarily make them safe to cook from frozen. White explains that ground meat is “particularly risky” because of the way it's processed, so it should be cooked thoroughly.

“I feed three young children at my home, so in my opinion, I wouldn't chance cooking frozen meat, no matter the size, in a slow cooker,” Koch says.

The answer is simple: thaw your meat before turning on your slow cooker. But your ideal method depends on how much time you have.

In a recent development, the safest, easiest, and least involved thawing method is to put your frozen meat in the fridge. The cold temperature of your refrigerator lets your food defrost gently without entering the danger zone.

Our editors recommend placing the meat on a plate or baking sheet to collect any juices and avoid contaminating other food in your fridge. This process can take anywhere from a couple of hours to a couple of days, depending on the cut of meat. “This requires some thought and effort, but in the long run it's worth your safety,” Koch notes.

No time for that? No problem. You can thaw meat faster by submerging it in cold water in a leakproof bag, changing the water every 30 minutes, or defrosting it in the microwave, though the latter can compromise the meat's texture. Both methods require cooking the meat immediately afterward. “Once thawed, meat should go directly into the slow cooker,” White explains.

It all takes a little extra effort and planning, but it's worth it to avoid getting sick from your slow cooker dinner.
