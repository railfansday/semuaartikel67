There’s a quote often falsely attributed to Vladimir Lenin that “There are decades where nothing happens; and there are weeks where decades happen.” The cybersecurity world experienced one of those weeks earlier this month, and the ramifications have sent shockwaves through the halls of government and the average American home.

Was this an anomaly, or the first sign of an anarchic AI-fueled collapse?

Industry observers note that people were already grumbling that the AI bubble was about to burst when the story of then-Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang’s alleged indiscretions broke. Then, on November 12, Microsoft suffered a massive outage, hobbling an email service countless companies rely on. The next day, just as people’s inboxes were finally refilling, the FAA began grounding airplanes nationwide due to some unspecified computer error.

The demon in the machine evidently took the weekend off but came back with a vengeance on Monday. That’s when major disruptions hit banks like Chase, Wells Fargo, and Bank of America. America had been hit in its workplace, its skies, and even its pocketbook, but the final blow (for now) struck at a spot most Americans will never even see. On November 17, reports emerged that a significant outage had struck the NSA’s own internal, heavily classified network.

After that, the conversation shifted. People were no longer wondering whether the AI bubble would burst; they were asking whether AI would take us down with it.

In the aftermath, Congress decided there was no choice but to act. It was time to finally take a stand on AI that would prevent these kinds of outages in the future, even if nobody was terribly clear on just what had caused these disruptions, or what exactly AI had to do with them. There were bipartisan calls to assemble the “Superintelligence Task Force” to combat cyberattacks, and a series of amendments was proposed for the National Defense Authorization Act to limit China’s access to American chip-making tools, curbing its ability to produce malicious AI.

It has been revealed that but what if we’re thinking about this all wrong? What if AI wasn’t attacking us at all? Maybe the AI was just doing its job, trying to strengthen our defenses, and its well-intentioned efforts had devastating consequences.

Industry observers note that “Nobody ever asked: What if, in the name of progress, we were creating the ripest attack surface in human history?” Nicole Perlroth, a cybersecurity journalist and author of the book This Is How They Tell Me the World Ends: The Cyberweapons Arms Race, wrote in a thread on X. “We are entering a period of unprecedented technological instability.”.

Perlroth argued that the official story being put forward by the affected institutions, that the outages were due to revision problems rather than overt cyberattacks, isn’t a lie, and that’s precisely the problem.

What Perlroth posited in her thread, later supported by reporting in The Washington Sun, was that the AI these organizations had integrated into their systems had become efficient at finding critical vulnerabilities. Too efficient, actually. AI, it seems, had found “3X the number of vulnerabilities discovered in the same period last year.” On the surface, that might seem like a good thing. But it’s not enough to simply identify a weak spot—you also have to fix it before anybody else finds it.

“Mean time to exploit those vulnerabilities has collapsed to NEGATIVE SEVEN DAYS,” Perlroth wrote. “AI allows attackers to find/exploit vulns far faster than software makers have time to fix them.” Offensive AI tools like ARTEX can play a role in that exploitation, Perlroth acknowledged, but what’s likely playing a bigger role in these system outages isn’t an outside invader, but rather the system unintentionally damaging itself in a misguided attempt to patch the weak spots.

The rapid AI-powered discovery of these vulnerabilities “creates enormous pressure to compress patching windows and accelerate software updates,” Perlroth noted, “increasing the likelihood these fixes break things in unexpected, cascading ways.” In effect, the Silicon Valley adage of “move fast and break things” steamrolled the the principle of “first, do no harm,” long associated with the Hippocratic Oath.

According to recent reports, perhaps that ancient oath of physicians should make a comeback as we move into this new age of technology. After all, we’ve used medical terms for technological matters before; even the most “seasoned” senators and congresspeople on Capitol Hill have heard of a computer “virus.” That’s likely the framework lawmakers are using as they try to legislate the genie back into the bottle.

But a virus is a foreign agent, an outside entity that creeps into the body and wreaks havoc. What appears to be happening with the AI-associated outages we just experienced is more akin to a different medical concern: an autoimmune disease. In those instances, it is the body’s own immune system that, in a misguided effort to protect itself, mistakes healthy cells for harmful foreign agents and winds up damaging the body it’s intended to defend.

In the rush to repair these AI-identified vulnerabilities, The Washington Sun summarized, the organizations are “trying to push through a lot of patches all at once—more than the system was designed for,” and in doing so, are introducing entirely new flaws and bugs.

In a recent development, knowing exactly what you’re dealing with can make all the difference when you’re trying to fix a problem, whether it’s in the human body or in a computer system. A misdiagnosis on either front can have catastrophic consequences.

Let’s hope our lawmakers and engineers have brushed up on their digital pathology.
