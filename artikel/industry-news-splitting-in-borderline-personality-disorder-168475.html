In a recent development, have you ever met a upcoming friend who seemed to think you were too great? You can do no wrong, you’re the first person to ever truly understand them, you’re so much better than their other friends. But then you have a disagreement or miss a birthday party, and they seem to just…turn on you? Now they feel sure that you were only ever kind to them because you wanted something; you’re just like everyone else.

Sources indicate that idealization and devaluation like this are two sides of the same coin—splitting, which is most associated with borderline personality disorder, or BPD. But it, or behaviors that closely resemble it, can also occur in people with narcissistic traits, depression, anxiety, autism, and other conditions. In fact, we all do it to some degree; teenagers are notorious for it. And it happens in all kinds of relationships: friendship, family, professional, marriage or partnership.

It has been revealed that obviously, neither extreme is a complete or fair perception of you. The truth of who you are exists somewhere between the ideal and the devalued, but that liminal space isn’t something your friend has access to right now.

And within that inability to see the space between all-good and all-bad lies the definition of splitting. In all of us, certain cognitive processes go offline when we become dysregulated or lose homeostasis: You don’t communicate well when you’re hungry, you can’t appreciate the beauty of a sunset when you’re angry, and so on. Likewise, when your friend or loved one becomes triggered and splits, their ability to see the nuance of who you are goes offline.

In a way, you each experience a similar betrayal. You’re late coming home from work, and by the time you arrive, your spouse’s overactive threat detection network has them convinced you’re seeing someone else. You don’t really love them. You’ll probably leave. You’re abandoning them.

It has been revealed that of course that’s not what you’re doing. So, you’re likewise blindsided by their response. Your spouse is fuming because you betrayed them, and they have some choice words for you. Every positive aspect of your relationship is read back to you in a negative light. It feels like they hate you.

Industry observers note that there is no law that notes you must stay in a relationship with this dynamic. However, if you’re committed to it, then equanimity is paramount. Your key to coping is not to internalize either idealization or devaluation. Even though it feels so personal, it’s not about you. It’s about their pain and fear.

In most cases, people with borderline personality disorder experienced feeling chronically unsafe, uncared for, or unseen during an important developmental stage. Their brain is hyper-wired to detect this type of threat and respond defensively.

Recent findings suggest that you’re under no obligation to engage in any relationship that doesn’t work for you. You decide what’s okay for you, and it’s a neutral choice. No one has to be right or wrong. And if a relationship ever involves threats or physical harm, that crosses a line no diagnosis excuses. Leave the situation and get help right away.
