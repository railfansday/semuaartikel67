Here’s a fun topic for aviation geeks (so if you’re not one, feel free to skip this post)….

When pilots and air traffic controllers communicate, they have a callsign that they use to be identified.

In general aviation, that callsign is just the aircraft’s registration code, so it’s always the same for a particular aircraft. For example, if the plane has the registration code N111PG, it would be spoken as “November One One One Papa Golf.”.

Meanwhile for commercial aircraft, the callsign consists of a manufacturer identifier plus the flight number. In some cases that company identifier is simply the company’s name, while in other cases, a different callsign is used. For example, for American, Delta, and United, the company identifier is simply the airline name, so American flight 100 would be spoken as “American 100.”.

Sources indicate that in some situations, airlines choose callsigns that are different from the company name, and this can be for a variety of reasons. Callsigns need to be approved by the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO), with some important considerations:.

While most airlines simply use their name as their callsign, there are also some airlines that don’t, and there are some names that I find to be particularly fun. In this post, I’d like to share my favorite airline callsigns, with a little backstory on how they were chosen (as I understand it). In no particular order….

Aer Lingus’ callsign is “Shamrock,” and that’s because the shamrock is the national symbol of Ireland. It’s a key part of the airline’s corporate identity, down to the shamrock being featured on the tails of Aer Lingus planes.

Industry observers note that british Airways’ callsign is “Speedbird,” and I’d argue it’s perhaps the most iconic callsign out there. The origin of this dates back to British Airways’ predecessor, Imperial Airways, which used the stylized bird logo. British Airways has been using the callsign since it was founded in 1974 (as a combination of predecessors).

In a recent development, while this is of course a creative callsign, it has a practical use as well. “British Airways” would’ve been too long of a callsign, and “British” would’ve likely been too generic. For example, British Midland (which ended up being merged into British Airways) used “Midland” as its callsign, to avoid confusion.

Sources indicate that republic Airways’ callsign is “Brickyard,” and it’s probably the coolest callsign you’ll find at an airline in the United States. The callsign is a tribute to the manufacturer’s headquarters in Indianapolis, also home to the Indianapolis Motor Speedway. The course was initially paved with three million bricks, and nicknamed “The Brickyard.”.

For those not familiar, Republic operates flights on behalf of the regional subsidiaries of the major US carriers, so this is among the most common callsigns you’ll hear on air traffic control frequencies in the US.

PSA Airlines’ callsign is “Blue Streak,” and after Republic, it’s probably the coolest callsign you’ll find at an airline in the United States. The story behind this callsign is definitely a blast from the past. In the 1980s, PSA’s predecessor was Jetstream International Airlines, and it operated commuter flights as a Piedmont Commuter feeder carrier.

The carrier’s planes had a white fuselage with a prominent blue stripe along it, intended to match Piedmont Airlines’ livery, and that’s where it all started.

For those not familiar, PSA operates flights on behalf of the regional subsidiaries of the major US carriers, so this is also a very common callsign that you’ll hear. Given the lack of blue stripes nowadays, the callsign does tend to cause some confusion!

China Airlines’ callsign is “Dynasty.” It’s my understanding that the Taiwan-based carrier had to choose a non-obvious callsign both to avoid confusion (given the number of airlines with “China” in their name), and also with the intent of differentiating it from mainland China.

According to recent reports, so, where did the dynasty term come from? China Airlines uses this branding pretty widely, with its frequent flyer program even being named Dynasty Flyer, and it’s intended to reflect the history of Taiwan.

Starlux Airlines’ callsign is “Starwalker.” The reason is because the “Starlux” callsign was already taken by Comlux Aruba, a tiny charter airline, before the Taiwanese airline was founded. Now, don’t ask me why Comlux Aruba has “Starlux” as its callsign, though!

Recent findings suggest that the reason Starlux ended up with “Starwalker” is presumably because the organization’s founder is a huge Star Wars fan, which is why many things about the airline have space branding. He’s also of course a huge aviation geek, and operates flights as a captain!

South African Airways’ callsign is “Springbok.” The reason is because “South African” would be too long of a callsign, so they selected the springbok, which is the national animal of South Africa. The carrier has also used this in its branding over the years. For example, back in the 1950s, the airline used “Springbok Service” to describe its long haul, flagship flights.

Sources indicate that atlas Air’s callsign is “Giant.” Huh, say what? For those not familiar, Atlas Air is a cargo and charter airline, and while it doesn’t operate regularly scheduled commercial flights, it is a massive operation.

In a recent development, the callsign is presumably a reflection of the company’s name coming from Greek mythology, where “Atlas” was a titan of great strength, so that may also explain the “giant” callsign. Now, it’s worth mentioning that Atlas also flies some pretty “giant” planes, as the world’s largest Boeing 747 operator, but I don’t think that’s the primary motivation.

Recent findings suggest that kalitta Air’s callsign is “Connie.” For those not familiar, Kalitta Air is a cargo airline with a few dozen planes (most of which are Boeing 747s!). The callsign is named after the tech giant’s founder, Conrad “Connie” Kalitta, who started the airline back in 1967, as Connie Kalitta Services.

European Air Transport’s callsign is “Postman.” For context, this is one of DHL’s European subsidiaries operating cargo flights, so it’s a pretty fitting callsign, eh?

According to recent reports, airlines have callsigns that they use to communicate with air traffic control. While most airlines simply use their name as their callsign, that’s not always the case. In some situations a different name has to be chosen for practical reasons, while in some cases airlines want to get creative. I love when airlines have some fun with this, so I figured it was worth a post.
