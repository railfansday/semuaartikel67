It has been revealed that advertisers are building AI systems around the way their teams already work. These teams have their own agents, their own data and their own rules for what an agent may and may not do. At the same time, most of the specialized intelligence they depend on still sits inside separate vendor platforms.

As a result, vendor intelligence shapes a recommendation while remaining disconnected from the system acting on the recommendation. When planners carry conclusions between tools, the evidence and conditions behind those recommendations often fall away.

Sources indicate that that’s the problem agentic media has to solve next. Can the intelligence behind the agent go where the work happens?

Models and agent frameworks are widely available. Two vendors can build on the same foundation and ship agents that look identical in a demo. What separates them is what the agent knows, and whether the advertiser can put that knowledge to work. In media buying, that knowledge is specific.

A broad content classification offers only a starting point for a media decision. Suitability depends on the particular advertiser and market, and those judgments can change promptly when breaking news alters the significance of a placement. A general-purpose model can’t reconstruct that brand-specific knowledge or historical context from a prompt alone. Classification depth and performance history can.

For Channel Factory, that means IAB 3.0 classification across more than 15 million YouTube channels, and more than 10 years and $4 billion of campaign performance data. To be clear, this isn’t an argument for keeping that intelligence locked inside a platform; it’s the opposite. Intelligence that only works inside its owner’s interface is as limited as a capable agent no one can reach.

It has been revealed that so the test is portability. Can an advertiser’s own agent call on a provider’s intelligence directly, from inside the planning and buying environment it already uses? That’s how Channel Factory is building, with contextual and suitability intelligence made available through APIs and MCP servers, and delivered through modular agents that run inside an advertiser’s environment or alongside it.

Industry observers note that with this architecture, market-specific suitability analysis becomes part of an advertiser’s own planning process. An agent can evaluate inventory against the provider’s classifications and historical performance data, applying the relevant standards for each market. Those findings can directly inform the strategy, with the advertiser’s existing rules governing what the agent can recommend or change.

Recent findings suggest that portable intelligence doesn’t mean identical agents. Two advertisers can draw on the same underlying intelligence and make different decisions. For instance, one avoids content when signals are unclear while another favors reaching inside defined boundaries. Both are valid because the thresholds, priorities and approval rules are the advertisers’.

It has been revealed that an agent’s knowledge source shouldn’t determine the scope of its authority. The brand decides what acceptable judgment looks like. The provider keeps the classification, history and models current.

Learning needs the same discipline. Feedback and outcomes should refine recommendations, but advertisers should decide what shapes future decisions and which policies stay fixed. On its own, a successful campaign shouldn’t rewrite a brand’s suitability standards.

Intelligence that moves into someone else’s systems has to carry its accountability along. As agents shift from recommending to acting, teams need to see what changed, why and whether it stayed within approved limits.

The principle is simple: Agents do the reasoning, and the engine owns the state, so every action is authorized, logged and traceable to the signals behind it. People define the strategy. Autonomy is earned in stages: alerts first, then recommendations, then bounded actions. An agent might make a small budget adjustment under set conditions while sending a larger change for human approval. How much authority to delegate is the advertiser’s call, and the system should make that call easy to enforce and easy to review.
