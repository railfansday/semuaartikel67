It has been revealed that engineers usually spend a lot of time intentionally avoiding eclipses, because they can be really hard on the spacecraft itself. The solar arrays stop generating electricity, draining the battery, and everything on the craft gets much, much colder. However, the precise timing of how those blackouts travel across the solar panels of a spacecraft holds valuable information.

Astronomers know the exact positions of the Sun, Earth, and the Moon years in advance using advanced planetary maps. Given how much data weâve collected on those three bodies, we also know the precise shape and locations of the shadows those three bodies cast into space. Each shadow comes with two nested cones - the penumbra, where the Sun is partially blocked, and the umbra, where it is completely cut off.

When a spacecraft crosses into one of those cones, the amount of solar radiation hitting its solar panels drops precipitously, but its electronics continue to operate due to the on-board battery engineers placed there precisely to deal with these eclipses. By recording the precise timestamp where the voltage starts to drop, and when it starts to pick back up again after the spacecraft leaves the shadow, a navigation algorithm can determine its three dimensional location in space without needing any cameras, star trackers, or radio signals.

Sources indicate that that sounds simple in theory, but in practice getting that precise measurement correct is difficult. The Moon has no atmosphere, so its shadow is very jagged and tracks the mountains and craters dotting its surface. Each of those features can change the eclipsesâ timing by a few seconds. Earth, on the other hand, is a whole other ball of wax - or ball of gas and rock as the case may be. Itâs squished slightly at the poles, and its atmosphere can blur the Sun, as well as enlarging its shadow, making it difficult to differentiate precisely what part of the planet the spacecraft is passing over at any given time.
