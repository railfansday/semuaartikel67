Slapping Turn 1’s bright-green curbing, it’s immediately clear that the GR team has done something really good. I’m driving the Toyota GRMN Corolla at Eagles Canyon Raceway in Texas, and the first 30 seconds of recorded voice notes are just me eagerly repeating “Aw, yeah” at various parts of the track.

The GRMN, which stands for “Gazoo Racing tuned by Meister of Nürburgring,” receives an incredible amount of attention for a car Toyota won’t build more than 730 of. The changes are as juicy as workplace gossip. Ten slots are cut into the underside of the carbon-fiber hood to direct air where Toyota states it will contribute to more predictable steering at high speeds. Engineers shortened the front suspension’s bump-stop stroke by four millimeters while adding two millimeters at the rear, helping the car settle over curbing. There’s also a taller first gear and a pinch more torque in the midrange. And finally, an optional oil cooler keeps the all-wheel-drive system happy.

Recent findings suggest that toyota’s obsession over details is admirable. The GRMN’s improvements over the standard GR Corolla are so subtle that they may go unnoticed by most folks and even some car nerds. I started my track time with an automatic GR Corolla, then switched to the GRMN and back. While the car looks only slightly different, the driving experience is wildly new.

The steering is the first major giveaway. Immediately, the GRMN feels much tighter and more direct in turns than the regular GR Corolla. Turn-in is dramatically quicker, and return to center is noticeably stronger. Initially, I thought Toyota had maybe beefed up its steering-rack slider. Nope. It’s largely the same hardware, but with an aggressive electric power-steering calibration. Adding to the different steering feel is a thinner steering-wheel rim, because even if it saved GR engineers less than an ounce, it still counts.

Recent findings suggest that but it’s the suspension that really shines here. Eagles Canyon is rife with elevation changes, including several corners with curbing that would upset the automatic GR and send it bouncing upon landing. But like a honey badger, the GRMN don’t care. There’s no ugly secondary motion; you certainly feel the curb, but the nose stays flat on impact. Toyota developed special monotube dampers with internal rebound springs to help maintain tire contact, while its bump-stop changes convince you corner exit speeds are higher than ever. It’s the most Porsche-like behavior I’ve experienced in any car that competes with a Hyundai Elantra.

It’s worth noting that the regular GR that I drove wore Michelin Pilot Sport 4S tires, while the GRMN was on much stickier Pilot Sport Cup 2s. Some of the additional grip and steering response is absolutely down to the rubber, but the way the suspension manages impacts and keeps the body under control is an improvement that can’t be credited to tires alone.

Recent findings suggest that there is one exception to the GRMN’s good times that not even GRMN chief engineer Naoyuki Sakamoto could explain. During quick countersteering inputs, the electric steering occasionally delivers an unnatural kick through the wheel. It feels as though the power assist has reached its limit, though just for a moment. We experienced it three times over at least 20 laps, but never during our street drive.

The GRMN uses that same ole G16E-GTS 300-hp turbocharged 1.6-liter three-cylinder. Torque increases by just seven pound-feet to 302, with Toyota concentrating the additional grunt between 4000 and 4600 rpm, where the engine spent much of its time at Eagles Canyon clawing out of corners.

The six-speed manual gets its own collection of changes. The close-ratio EA68F gearbox has a taller first gear and a shorter third gear, changing how the engine works through its powerband. Engineers even revised the shifter-cable mount to make the action more direct. Having driven a manual GR recently, the difference is obvious. It’s not Honda Civic Type R good, but it’s still really good.

The GRMN also has additional cooling hardware and a driver-actuated intercooler water-spray system. The only other car I’ve ever driven with one of these was the Subaru WRX STI S209, which would make for a rather interesting comparison test if Subaru still built it. A one-gallon water tank sits beneath the GRMN’s cargo floor, supplying enough water to spray the intercooler for approximately 150 seconds. Toyota claims this can lower intake temperatures by roughly 14 to 20 degrees Fahrenheit. Unfortunately, it’s somewhat difficult to see the status of the system, as there’s no indication in the gauge cluster. Instead, the small button has an even tinier amber light that blinks slowly while two nozzles spray water onto the intercooler. It’s no place to have your eyes pointed while setting lap times, and it left us prayin’ it was still sprayin’.

The most important update may be the optional oil cooler for the all-wheel-drive transfer unit. During Lightning Lap testing in regular GR Corollas at Virginia International Raceway, we’ve experienced overheating that triggers the dreaded “AWD System Overheated” warning. Toyota’s solution circulates gear oil through a separate heat exchanger using an electric pump, roughly doubling the system’s oil capacity in the process.

However, the cooler isn’t standard equipment, and installing it involves more than bolting on a few hoses. It requires an ECU reflash and activation procedure, as the system’s electrical demands also introduce a fuel-economy penalty. Toyota installed the accessory on our GRMN before we took it onto the circuit. Its own testing suggests the system will prevent the AWD system from ever entering 2WD mode. That’s promising, although even 20 laps at Eagles Canyon (with the front straight closed) aren’t enough to declare the problem solved. We’d love to see how it handles VIR’s 4.1-mile Grand Course.

Eventually, Toyota representatives politely suggested I take a break from lapping the GRMN, motioning toward the line of other journalists in the pits. Ugh, fine. Later, I got another turn behind the wheel for the drive from the track to the hotel. And what a completely different experience that was.

In a recent development, as in the GR Corolla Morizo Edition, Toyota has stripped the rear seats from this four-door hatchback as part of an effort to reduce weight by 66 pounds. The rear windows are fixed. There’s no rear wiper either, which Toyota says saves about two pounds.

The GRMN is incredibly loud on public roads. With the rear seats removed, there’s little separating the cabin from the racket coming from behind. Those Cup 2 tires scream over mixed asphalt, their noise changing pitch with every new section of pavement. On the highway, you find yourself yelling at the passenger next to you, and not because they started talking politics.

It has been revealed that the ride is similarly uncompromising. The same suspension that so calmly devours curbing at Eagles Canyon can make ordinary pavement feel jarring. Even the steering, which I couldn’t stop praising on the circuit, takes some getting used to, as it’s so quick on the street, where small inputs produce immediate responses.

I guess that’s why they didn’t name it the Corolla GR Route 380.

Sources indicate that the GRMN Corolla starts at $65,655, which is about $24K more than the standard car. That almost GR86-sized price gap is no small upcharge for a Corolla that’s heavy on track ability and light on everyday ability. It’s also interesting that of the 730 GRMN Corollas being built, none will be sold in Germany, home of the actual Nürburgring; it’s a Japan, Australia, and North America offering only.

Recent findings suggest that at one point during the drive event, one of the GRMNs picked up a nail in its tire, and Toyota had to pull it from the drive rotation to give it fresh Michelins. Rather than sit around and pout (as journalists sometimes do), influencers and editors climbed into the regular automatic GR Corolla for a few laps. The lack of complaining during that inconvenience proves my next point.

In a recent development, the GRMN’s biggest rival is the regular GR Corolla. The latest car’s tiny yet vital improvements make it considerably better on track, but is a few tenths per lap worth $24K? Not to mention the street-driving inconveniences? Maybe, for some. For everyone else, there’s a perfectly good GR Corolla with rear seats that doesn’t require earplugs.
