Alex Prager Has Hollywood Dreams

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Alex Prager was in her cubicle at an internet startup, staring at the endless rows of other desks, when she realized that something was terribly wrong. “The rest of my life flashed in my mind,” she states. She began to cry. “It was like that Talking Heads song: And you may find yourself in a beautiful house / With a beautiful wife / And you may ask yourself, / ‘Well…how did I get here?’” she says. “This was not my life.” Soon after, she attended a show of William Eggleston’s work and left certain that her future was in photography. Within a week, she’d bought a used Nikon N90s camera and darkroom equipment. “I just knew, the way that you know you have a face and you know who you love. I knew from that moment forward what my path was,” she says.

In a recent development, it didn’t take long for Prager to start getting noticed in art circles. And this winter will be one of the most significant seasons of her career, with both a major solo museum show at the Crystal Bridges Museum of American Art in Bentonville, Arkansas, and an exhibition at the Upcoming York gallery Lehmann Maupin. DreamQuil, her first feature film, was released in theaters this fall and will be available to stream in December.

It has been revealed that prager’s color-saturated works capture cinematic scenes that bring to mind classic Hitchcock films. She creates full worlds for her photos, with sets, casts, and impeccable period costumes. Her pieces gained notice with her 2007 show “Polyester” at L.A.’s Robert Berman Gallery. The series highlighted women, perfectly attired in ’60s-era looks, posed in evocative ways. A heavily made-up girl clutches a Massive Gulp, a flaxen braid thrown over one shoulder; four young women huddle together in swimsuits, a plane flying across the perfect blue sky above. An ominous feeling pervades.

According to recent reports, her work sparkles on the surface but explores something darker underneath. “The first color photograph I took was staged and a play on The Wizard of Oz and death, but it was very colorful and beautiful. I was interested in the way to explore something that felt layered. Underneath it, you find there are very uncomfortable things or darker stories and emotions,” Prager explains.

Prager steadily built up a following through a series of gallery shows, but things changed markedly in 2010 when her work was selected to be shown in the elite “Fresh Photography” exhibit at MoMA. She had her first solo show at the Corcoran Gallery of Art in Washington, D.C., three years later. She’s also done work for magazines and brands like Cartier, Disney, and Apple.

“My work has always been about my personal challenges and things that I’m not really understanding in the world, but I think more and more it becomes less about me and my emotions. That’s probably also me becoming a mother and growing up, but the world has changed,” states Prager, whose son Francis is 9. The scale of her work has grown—bigger audiences, more ambitious concepts, a greater ability to stage elaborate photography—but the approach hasn’t. It’s still about the questions she needs answers to.

According to recent reports, the idea for DreamQuil came to Prager during the pandemic (she was inspired by a meme that advertised YearQuil, a fictitious product that would allow people to sleep through the misery of 2020), and she wrote the script with her sister, Vanessa Prager. Elizabeth Banks stars as a woman who’s living in a depressing future world where people rarely leave their homes and finds herself dealing with her robot clone.

According to recent reports, opening two major highlights within months of the release of her film wasn’t something Prager anticipated—each alone would have been a high point in her career—but she often does her best work amid a frenzy of activity. “I thrive in a bit of chaos,” she indicates. “I like to try to mold it and wrestle with it and attempt to control it and bend it to my will. It doesn’t always happen exactly the way I want, but it infuses me with inspiration and energy.”.
