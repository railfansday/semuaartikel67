But the specific cultural forces shaping what we do and how we do it can change dramatically, even within a few decades.Take the 1990s, which many living humans today would not have experienced because they were too young. In the scheme of things, three decades isn’t that much time. Yet the differences between then and now are almost comically dramatic. In the ‘90s, work was dominated by fax machines, pagers, bulky early mobile phones, and painfully slow dial-up internet connections that occupied the telephone line. Email was only emerging, to the point that your exchanges were largely limited to other early adopters, and the content of messages was often less interesting than the process of exchanging electronic mail itself. Being online was something you actively did, rather than a permanent state of existence, and work took place almost entirely at work, with the office encapsulating our professional selves and much of life unfolding outside it. Leaving the office generally meant leaving work behind, and being unreachable was the norm rather than a sign of negligence, indulgence, or power. Technology was an add-on—a secondary part of work rather than something we ought to supervise by bot-sitting, being a meat proxy, or calling ourselves the “human in the loop.” Since there were few apps or websites through which our personal lives could infiltrate working hours, digital distractions and multitasking were relatively rare: no Amazon shopping, Expedia travel planning, LinkedIn humblebragging, or keeping up with what our neighbor’s cat had for breakfast on Facebook or Instagram. So, was all of that better or worse? Here are four ways in which it was arguably the former—and four in which it was perhaps the latter. Four things that were arguably better about work in the 1990s 1. More genuine human connection. Work was a fundamentally more social, in-person experience, with more face-to-face interactions, spontaneous conversations, and opportunities to build relationships beyond scheduled meetings. We lacked digital personas and our reputations were the result of what we actually do in the physical world. 2. Greater focus and fewer distractions. With fewer digital interruptions and less pressure to respond instantly, people had more opportunities for sustained attention, deep work, and uninterrupted concentration. Or at least, our distractions were purely human—which meant that you could actually withdraw from others by staring at your computer (as opposed to the reverse). 3. More independent thinking and intellectual effort. Knowledge workers had to rely more heavily on their own memory, reasoning, creativity, and problem-solving skills, rather than outsourcing cognitive tasks to algorithms or AI. General knowledge, as demonstrated by the art of storytelling and argumentation, as well as engaging others in deep thinking and logical reasoning, all occupied a more central role in our daily working days than they do today. 4. Greater psychological separation between work and life. Work had clearer temporal and physical boundaries, and employees were less likely to feel permanently connected, constantly monitored, or threatened by technologies capable of making their skills obsolete. There were few discussions about “bringing your whole self to work” and employers didn’t need to monitor our social media footprint to alleviate any concerns about reputation management or brand compromising. Oh, and work did not occupy the central part of our identity, unless you were a workaholic: someone who resembles the average worker today in the sense of never disconnecting and always thinking about work and their professional self. Four things that were arguably worse about work in the 1990s 1. Less flexibility and autonomy. Work was tied to specific locations and fixed schedules, with little opportunity for remote or hybrid working, even when physical presence added little value. This may sound great today to people who miss or like structure and demarcation, but there is a reason why online and hybrid work have become so popular, namely that people enjoy freedom and flexibility—and especially their employers’ trust—vis-à-vis how their work is done. 2. More rigid hierarchies and organizational bureaucracy. Authority was more concentrated, communication more top-down, and employees generally had less freedom to challenge decisions or influence how work was organized. Organizational designs are more fluid, agile, and networked, which has persuaded employers to shift beyond formal org charts and try to evaluate the value each individual brings to their teams and organizations. In essence, this is good news for those who work hard and have talent, and bad news for those who simply pretend to work. 3. Less access to information and expertise. Knowledge was harder to find, slower to circulate, and more dependent on formal education, organizational status, and proximity to influential people. In theory, any novice could produce much more proficient, competent, and high-quality output in any area of the knowledge economy—which of course doesn’t guarantee they will!—because there is no longer any distance or separation between any question and any answer. And the ability to learn and acquire a decent level of expertise in any topic has increased dramatically with AI. 4. Fewer opportunities for inclusion and global collaboration. Geographic barriers, traditional career paths, and less inclusive workplace norms limited opportunities for women, minorities, people with disabilities, and talent outside major economic centers. This means opportunities to thrive and succeed because of your social capital (a euphemism for privilege or “who you know”) were far more common than today. In essence, work has been evolving towards a more meritocratic and talent-centric model, though there’s still a great deal of progress to make. Does it matter? In short, there is an interesting paradox in that work in the 1990s offered less freedom over where and when we worked, but arguably more freedom from work when we weren’t working. And while today’s technologies have liberated us from many tedious tasks, they have also made it harder to escape work, focus on it, or feel valued, not least since our professional identity is being rewritten by AI’s impact on our jobs, roles, and skills, and the impact of that is yet to be determined. Importantly, since employee engagement levels tend to fluctuate very little across the years, it is safe to conclude that people were no more or less happy then than they are today. Which points to the idea that any objective changes or improvements in working conditions and habits are promptly absorbed by our expectations. Work had clearer temporal and physical boundaries, and employees were less likely to feel permanently connected, constantly monitored, or threatened by technologies capable of making their skills obsolete. There were few discussions about “bringing your whole self to work” and employers didn’t need to monitor our social media footprint to alleviate any concerns about reputation management or brand compromising. Oh, and work did not occupy the central part of our identity, unless you were a workaholic: someone who resembles the average worker today in the sense of never disconnecting and always thinking about work and their professional self.

In a recent development, 1. Less flexibility and autonomy. Work was tied to specific locations and fixed schedules, with little opportunity for remote or hybrid working, even when physical presence added little value. This may sound great today to people who miss or like structure and demarcation, but there is a reason why online and hybrid work have become so popular, namely that people enjoy freedom and flexibility—and especially their employers’ trust—vis-à-vis how their work is done.

2. More rigid hierarchies and organizational bureaucracy. Authority was more concentrated, communication more top-down, and employees generally had less freedom to challenge decisions or influence how work was organized. In essence, work has been evolving towards a more meritocratic and talent-centric model, though there’s still a great deal of progress to make.

In short, there is an interesting paradox in that work in the 1990s offered less freedom over where and when we worked, but arguably more freedom from work when we weren’t working. And while today’s technologies have liberated us from many tedious tasks, they have also made it harder to escape work, focus on it, or feel valued, not least since our professional identity is being rewritten by AI’s impact on our jobs, roles, and skills, and the impact of that is yet to be determined.
